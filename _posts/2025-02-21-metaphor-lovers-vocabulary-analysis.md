## A Private Language: The Verdict on the Lovers' Shared Metaphor

Metaphor in Romeo and Juliet is not a solo performance, and the play's most famous figures prove it. The pilgrim conceit at the Capulet feast has two authors. The sun at the orchard window is coined by one speaker and then argued over, rationed, and partly refused by the other. The sea of bounty in the same scene begins as a merchant's venture in Romeo's mouth and ends as an economy of infinite giving in Juliet's. Again and again, a figure enters the dialogue as one person's property and leaves it as joint property, altered by the passage. The verdict of this article is that the shared vocabulary is the marriage performed in advance, inside language itself: before any priest joins the lovers, their metaphors have already joined them, because a metaphor that two speakers coin, correct, and complete together is a small ceremony of union that no witness is required to validate. Intimacy in the play can be measured, almost mechanically, by watching who hands a figure to whom, and what the receiving speaker does with it.

![Romeo and Juliet exchanging the pilgrim conceit at the Capulet feast - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-96.webp)

The contrast that organizes the whole design is the contrast between answered and unanswered figures. Romeo's speeches for Rosaline are dense with metaphor, and no metaphor in them is ever taken up, because Rosaline never speaks. His figures circulate in a closed system of one: reported by friends, mocked by Mercutio, diagnosed by Friar Laurence, but never answered by the woman they describe. The result is a vocabulary that cannot grow, since growth requires a second mind to push back. At the feast, the system breaks open within fourteen lines. Romeo offers a shrine, a pilgrim, a sin to be purged; Juliet accepts the terms and immediately revises them, ruling that hands, not lips, are the pilgrims' proper instrument, and the figure that emerges belongs to neither speaker alone. From that moment the play keeps its accounts in a new currency. Figures are proposed, audited, amended, sometimes rejected outright, as when Juliet forbids the moon as an oath's guarantor, and sometimes extended past their coiner's intention, as when she takes the heavens Romeo offered as compliment and turns them into the stars of her wedding-night speech. The lovers' language converges because convergence is what their exchanges are for.

Verona's public language supplies the negative proof. The two houses share a great deal: a city, a prince, a code of insult, a liturgy of oaths, a legal vocabulary of fines and forfeits. What no Capulet and no Montague ever do, in five acts, is build a metaphor together. Their common speech is inherited, formulaic, and adversarial, a stock of phrases each side repeats without alteration, from the servants' rehearsed boasts in the opening scene to the fathers' closing competition in golden statues, where even reconciliation is phrased as rival expenditure rather than shared figure. The feud's language can be exchanged as blows are exchanged, but it cannot be co-authored, because co-authorship requires the one thing the feud forbids: taking another speaker's term seriously enough to change it and hand it back improved. The lovers do exactly that, in scene after scene, and the play marks the difference as the difference between a city that talks past itself and a household of two that talks itself into existence.

Three clarifications govern everything that follows. A metaphor, as the term is used here, asserts identity between two things: Juliet is the sun. A simile keeps the two things apart and compares them under a flag of truce, like or as: the contract is too like the lightning. A conceit is a metaphor placed under sustained rational pressure, argued rather than stated, its correspondences itemized until the comparison becomes a small machine for thinking. The lovers use all three instruments, and the distribution is itself evidence, since Romeo reaches for metaphor and conceit while Juliet, the pair's auditor, repeatedly reaches for the simile that keeps a saving distance between the thing and its image. Caroline Spurgeon counted the play's images and found the light cluster dominant; M. M. Mahood listened to the play's wordplay and heard figures doing dramatic work between speakers. Both accounts are true, and they are true of different objects. The count establishes which domains the lovers share. The exchange establishes what sharing does. This article maps the domains, then follows the handovers, and adjudicates the critics where they part.

## Metaphor, Simile, Conceit: Three Instruments, Three Kinds of Claim

Precision about the instruments matters because the lovers' intimacy is partly a story of which instrument gets used, and by whom, and with what result. A metaphor fuses. When Romeo, watching the window before Juliet appears, says "It is the east, and Juliet is the sun" (2.2.3), the sentence does not propose a comparison for inspection; it performs a substitution, abolishing the distance between woman and star so completely that the rest of the speech can reason from the substitution as from a fact: the sun can kill the envious moon, can be entreated, can rise. A simile negotiates. When Juliet warns that the contract is "too rash, too unadvised, too sudden; / Too like the lightning, which doth cease to be / Ere one can say 'It lightens'" (2.2.118-120), the like holds the contract and the lightning apart even while it judges one by the other, and the judgment is a caution rather than a fusion: the thing compared is found dangerous precisely because it resembles something that vanishes. A conceit litigates. The pilgrim exchange at the feast is a conceit because its central identification, the beloved as shrine and saint, the lover as pilgrim, is not merely asserted but pleaded, with premises, objections, and rulings, across fourteen lines and two speakers, until the comparison yields a verdict neither speaker held at the start: that lips may do what hands do.

### What is the difference between the tenor and the vehicle of a metaphor?

The tenor is the thing meant, and the vehicle is the thing whose name and properties are borrowed. In "Juliet is the sun," Juliet is the tenor and the sun is the vehicle, and everything the vehicle carries, radiance, centrality, life-giving heat, the power to kill the moon, is transferred to the tenor for the life of the figure.

The transfer is never free of charge, and the play knows the price list. A vehicle imports its whole system of associations, including the ones the speaker did not intend. The sun that makes Juliet supreme also makes her distant, daily, and fatal to look upon; the shrine that makes her holy also makes her an object of pilgrimage, approached by strangers under rules she is expected to enforce; the merchandise that makes her precious in Romeo's sea speech also makes her cargo, a thing adventured for by a pilot who risks himself to acquire her. Skilled speakers in the play audit these imports. Juliet's corrections are so often corrections of vehicle rather than of feeling: she does not refuse Romeo's devotion when she refuses his moon, she refuses the moon's inconstancy as its vehicle, and directs him to a guarantor without phases, his own gracious self. The distinction between tenor and vehicle is therefore not classroom scaffolding. It is the working tool by which one lover edits another, keeping the tenor, the love itself, while rejecting the vehicle that would misdescribe it.

Romeo's early practice shows the instruments in their unshared state. The oxymoron chain of the first scene, "O brawling love! O loving hate!" (1.1.176), is metaphor compressed to detonation, tenor and vehicle forced into single words; the Petrarchan machinery behind it, the siege, the wound, the devout religion of the eye that he still rehearses in the second scene (1.2.88), is conceit material operated by one pair of hands. None of it is audited, because the only auditors available are the wrong ones: Benvolio prescribes a rival beauty as medicine, Mercutio translates every figure into the body's plain terms, and the Friar hears rote reading where spelling should be. The full account of [how Romeo's Petrarchan voice changes once a real auditor replaces those false ones](/2022/05/27/romeo-petrarchan-language-transformation/) belongs to the study of his transformation; the point required here is narrower. Before the feast, Romeo's figures are finished objects, polished and inert, because no answering intelligence has ever taken them up. After the feast, his figures are drafts, and the play's figurative life begins.

Juliet's native instrument deserves its own emphasis, because it explains her editorial power. Her characteristic figure is the conditional simile and the provisional image, offered with its expiry date attached: the bud that may prove a beauteous flower when next they meet (2.2.121-122), the lightning whose danger lies in its brevity, the bounty whose infinity she states as a paradox of giving rather than a picture of a thing. Where Romeo's metaphors declare, hers stipulate. The difference is not timidity; it is a theory of language. A declared metaphor can be believed or disbelieved, but a stipulated one can be tested, and Juliet tests everything, including, in the name speech, the very relation between words and things. The shared vocabulary is built from the collision of the two styles: his fusions supply the lexicon's bold entries, her stipulations supply its definitions, its cautions, and its terms of use.

Two further instruments complete the inventory, and both are solo instruments, defined by the absence of an answering partner. The first is apostrophe, the address of something absent, inanimate, or abstract as though it could hear: night, fortune, the fiery-footed steeds of Juliet's wedding-night summons, "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds" (3.2.1). Apostrophe is the figure of a speaker whose real addressee is unavailable, and the play reserves its greatest apostrophes for the hours when the partners cannot reach each other, which is why the wedding-night speech and the tomb speech, for all their splendor, belong to the vocabulary's literature of absence. The second is hyperbole, the unaudited superlative, and its career in the play is a small history of the audit itself. Romeo hyperbolizes freely in solitude, where no one prices his claims; in dialogue, his excesses meet Juliet's accountancy, and the counting paradox of the wedding scene, in which only beggars can sum their worth, is hyperbole audited into a law of the lovers' economy. The instruments thus sort themselves by the same test as everything else in this article. Metaphor, simile, and conceit can be shared, because each can be answered. Apostrophe and hyperbole, in their pure forms, cannot, because each presumes a hearer who will not reply.

### Which metaphor domain do Romeo and Juliet trade more than any other?

Light and the heavens supply the most traded domain, the sun, moon, stars, torches, and lightning passing between the lovers from the feast to the tomb. Sacred pilgrimage runs second, sealed in a single scene yet never afterward abandoned, and the wealth of jewels, bounty, and merchandise runs third, the domain in which Juliet's corrections bite deepest.

A class working through the map row by row can turn the table into slides with the [ReportMedic PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), keeping each exchange beside the lines that carry it, since the map's claim is visible only when coinage and answer stand on the same page. The rows deliberately include two control cases. The torch and jewel figures of Romeo's first feast speech are coined in an aside, addressed to no one, and are therefore marked unanswered: they belong to the old economy even though their subject is the new love, proof that the vocabulary becomes shared only when a second speaker enters it. The lark and nightingale of the dawn parting are marked contested: the one exchange in which the lovers trade a figure and fail to agree on it, because the figure has become an argument about the world rather than an emblem of the pair. Between those poles, the standard transaction repeats with variations: proposal, audit, amendment, and a figure that returns to its coiner changed.

The table's deepest row is the one that looks least figurative. The Friar's "incorporate two in one" (2.6.37) is not a lover's coinage at all; it is liturgy, the Church's own term for what the ceremony does to two persons. Its presence in the map marks the single moment when the public world's language and the private vocabulary say the same thing, and the rarity of the moment is the measure of the feud's failure. Everywhere else, the public world speaks of the lovers in terms they never chose: the Chorus's death-marked love, the parents' merchandise of marriage, the Prince's legal arithmetic of fines and banishments. Only inside the exchanges does a language exist in which the lovers are what they call each other. The map is therefore also a census of citizenship. To be inside the shared vocabulary is to belong to the play's smallest state, population two, with the Friar admitted once, as officiant, and no one else admitted at all.

### What does the metaphor map record that a list of images cannot?

Direction. A list can show that light imagery saturates the play; the map shows a figure leaving one mouth and returning altered from another, the sun refused as an oath and kept as a name, merchandise answered by bounty. Direction turns imagery from atmosphere into action, and action is where the play's meaning is decided.

Two further patterns in the map deserve naming before the exchanges are examined in detail. First, the domains interbreed. Light is spoken in the language of worship when Juliet becomes a saint whose radiance teaches torches; worship is spoken in the language of wealth when a sin at a shrine is discharged by a fine; wealth is spoken in the language of the sea when bounty becomes boundless water. This cross-domain traffic is a signature of a living lexicon, since dead formulas stay inside their compartments, while a vocabulary in active joint use keeps borrowing across its own borders to say new things. Second, the map shows a shift of initiative. Romeo coins nearly everything before the wedding; Juliet coins nearly everything after it, the stars of the wedding night and the falconry of the parting already hers, until the tomb restores the imbalance in the saddest form, a single speaker spending the joint currency alone. The convergence of the lovers' language is thus not a merging into sameness. It is an equalization of authorship, completed at exactly the moment the plot begins to separate the authors.

The map's evidentiary standard should be stated, since the whole argument rests on it. An exchange is entered as a trade only when the answering speaker's words alter the figure's sense: striking a guarantor, repricing a cargo, attaching a season, reversing a direction. Mere response in kind does not qualify, which is why the servants' antiphonal boasts, exquisitely responsive and wholly unaltering, appear nowhere in the rows, and why echoes, puns returned for puns, and compliments answered by compliments are excluded even when they occur between the lovers themselves. The standard is deliberately strict, and it cuts both ways: it excludes some beloved passages, the tomb's defiance and the wedding-night apostrophes among them, from the evidence for sharing, because splendor is not the criterion. Alteration is. What survives the standard is a small, hard dataset, ten trades and near-trades across five acts, and the argument of this article is that the dataset, not the play's general saturation with imagery, is where the lovers' intimacy is stored. A map built on a looser standard would show more and prove less.

## The Pilgrim Conceit at the Feast: A Metaphor Built by Two Speakers

The feast exchange is the founding document of the shared vocabulary, and its structure deserves slow attention, because everything the lovers later do with figures is present here in miniature. Romeo opens with a completed conceit, polished in the Petrarchan manner: "If I profane with my unworthiest hand / This holy shrine, the gentle fine is this: / My lips, two blushing pilgrims, ready stand / To smooth that rough touch with a tender kiss" (1.5.92-95). The figure is a small legal-theological machine. Her hand is a shrine, his touch a profanation, his lips pilgrims who must pay a fine for the offence, the fine being a kiss. Every term is in place, and the whole is frankly a device for obtaining the kiss it pretends to apologize for. A Rosaline would have left the machine unanswered, as she leaves all his machines. Juliet instead steps inside it and starts repairing it from within, and the repairs are the point. The sonnet they build between them has been anatomized line by line in [the study of the shared sonnet as the lovers' first words](/2023/07/07/shared-sonnet-first-meeting-analysis/); the concern here is the conceit's workmanship, the way its terms change owners.

### How many speeches pass before one of Romeo's feast figures is first answered by Juliet?

Three. Romeo's torch speech and jewel speech are spoken aside, to the audience and to himself, and hang unanswered; his question about her identity goes to a servingman, not to her. Only when he takes her hand and offers the shrine does a figure of his receive an answer, and the answer rewrites it.

Juliet's first move is jurisdictional. "Good pilgrim, you do wrong your hand too much, / Which mannerly devotion shows in this; / For saints have hands that pilgrims' hands do touch, / And palm to palm is holy palmers' kiss" (1.5.96-99). She accepts the entire sacred frame, shrine, saint, pilgrim, devotion, and disputes only its application: the hand he calls profane has done nothing a pilgrim's hand may not do, since touch itself is the sanctioned rite. The correction is doctrinally exact and tactically devastating, because it grants the premise and denies the penalty. His fine is cancelled by her ruling; the kiss he angled for as payment is ruled out by the same theology that authorized the touch. Then, when he reopens the case, "Have not saints lips, and holy palmers too?" (1.5.100), she concedes the fact and fences its use: "Ay, pilgrim, lips that they must use in prayer" (1.5.101). Only at his final plea, "let lips do what hands do; / They pray, grant thou, lest faith turn to despair" (1.5.102-103), does she grant the transfer, and she grants it in the figure's own terms, with the stillness of saints as her condition: "Saints do not move, though grant for prayers' sake" (1.5.104). His closing line, "Then move not, while my prayer's effect I take" (1.5.105), is the first sentence in the play that neither lover could have written alone, since its wit depends entirely on the accumulated rulings of the exchange.

The form of the exchange enacts the same transfer socially. Two speakers who begin by trading whole speeches end by trading half-lines, the tempo of the rulings quickening as the constitution settles, until the final couplet's business, the kiss itself, is transacted in clauses too short for either voice to monopolize. The sonnet's internal anatomy, its quatrains and its couplet division, is treated in the companion study named above; what matters for the vocabulary is the social fact the anatomy houses. A form designed for a single meditating voice has been occupied by two voices and converted into a legislature. The conversion is the lovers' signature move, repeated on every instrument they touch: the oath becomes a negotiation, the compliment becomes a contract review, the serenade becomes a constitutional convention. Nothing in the play's Verona undergoes this conversion except in their hands. The fathers' council, the Prince's court, the Friar's cell all preserve the forms they inherit, which is why their proceedings reproduce the past, while the feast's fourteen lines, improvised inside a borrowed form, produce something the city has never contained: an institution founded by amendment.

The pilgrim and palmer machinery itself, the palm leaf of the returned crusader, the touching of shrines, the etiquette of the saint's hand, is set out in [the companion study of the first kiss and its pilgrims' and palmers' hands](/2023/07/14/first-kiss-pilgrim-imagery-analysis/). What the machinery does inside the shared vocabulary is more specific. It gives the lovers a constitution. Within the conceit, each speaker holds an office: he petitions, she adjudicates; he proposes extensions of the rite, she rules on their validity; the kiss, when it comes, is lawful because it has been legislated. The pattern recurs at every scale of their later exchanges. Romeo proposes the moon as guarantor and is overruled; Juliet proposes the bud's season as the contract's timetable and is obeyed; the bounty figure is amended in passage from his merchandise to her infinite giving. The feast sonnet is not simply the first thing the lovers build together. It is the template of their building, proposal and audit, coinage and correction, and its sacred domain is well chosen for a constitution, since the sacred is the one register in which a thirteen-year-old girl of the house may outrank a guest, a masked stranger, and her own inexperience, all at once.

Two details of the exchange repay notice as evidence of genuine co-authorship rather than mere repartee. The first is the migration of the word sin. It enters as his property, a transgression he confesses in advance and offers to pay for; after the kiss it becomes negotiable currency, purged, taken back, returned as a trespass sweetly urged, each transfer changing its value. A word that changes value as it changes hands is the exact signature of a shared vocabulary, as against a shared script, in which words keep the values they were assigned. The second detail is the disappearance of the conceit's scaffolding. By the parting couplets of the scene, the lovers no longer need the shrine between them; the figure has done its work and been put away, its terms absorbed into a private shorthand. Later figures show the same life cycle at larger scale: coined in ceremony, worked in argument, and finally available as allusion, a single word, stars, bounty, night, carrying the whole history of its trading. A vocabulary is shared, in the strict sense this article defends, when its words carry a history both speakers remember. By that test the pilgrim exchange is not the lovers' first conversation. It is their first act of joint memory.

The exchange also outlives its own frame, and the survival is further evidence. The sonnet proper closes with the kiss, yet the lovers immediately run the machine a second time without the sonnet's scaffolding: his sin is purged by her lips, she rules that her lips now hold the sin they took, he begs the trespass back as a favor sweetly urged, and she delivers the auditor's verdict on the whole performance, that he kisses by the book (1.5.106-109). The post-sonnet lines matter because they show the constitution operating in ordinary dialogue rather than in ceremonial verse. The conceit's terms, sin, purge, trespass, have become small change, spendable at conversational speed, and the final verdict is itself a figure about figures, a judgment of style delivered from inside the style it judges. The book image that closes the exchange will migrate through the play's other book readings, from Lady Capulet's volume of Paris to the Friar's registers, but its first life in the lovers' mouths is this one, as the name of a technique two speakers have just invented together and are already, delightedly, reviewing.

## Light at the Window: How the Orchard Scene Trades the Heavens

The orchard scene is the shared vocabulary's largest market day, and its opening shows the old economy and the new one side by side. Romeo's first speeches are asides and soliloquy, magnificent and unanswerable: the window breaks with light, Juliet is the sun, the moon is sick and pale with grief, her eyes are stars entreated to twinkle in their spheres (2.2.2-20). As figures, these belong with the feast's torch and jewel speeches, coined for a woman who does not yet know she is being figured. The moment Juliet speaks, unaware of her auditor, the economy changes, because her first great figure is about language itself: a name is not a hand or foot or any other part belonging to a man, and a rose by any other name would smell as sweet (2.2.42-44). The speech is usually read as the scene's philosophy, and it is also its charter of trade. Juliet announces, before she knows Romeo hears her, that words are instruments to be audited against things, that a term may be discarded when it misdescribes its object. She then spends the scene applying the doctrine to his figures one by one.

The application begins with the oath. Romeo, discovered, reaches for the heavens she has just seen him assign her to: "Lady, by yonder blessèd moon I swear / That tips with silver all these fruit-tree tops" (2.2.107-108). The moon is, in his private system, her subordinate, the envious maid he has already demoted beneath her sun. As a guarantor it is therefore doubly wrong, subordinate and inconstant, and Juliet strikes it from the contract: "O, swear not by the moon, th'inconstant moon, / That monthly changes in her circled orb, / Lest that thy love prove likewise variable" (2.2.109-111). The audit is precise about vehicles. She does not say his love is variable; she says the moon is, and that a vow sworn on a variable vehicle will import the variability. Her substitute guarantor, his gracious self, is the only vehicle in the scene whose properties she is prepared to trust, and even that she immediately hedges, directing him not to swear at all. The full radiance cluster, the torch, the jewel, the sun, the saint enthroned above them, is given its devotional reading in [the study of Juliet as sun and saint, where light becomes holiness](/2024/08/09/juliet-as-light-sun-saint-imagery/); the trading floor is the subject here, and on the trading floor the heavens are the most heavily negotiated goods the lovers handle.

For the negotiation is not one-sided refusal. Juliet refuses the moon and keeps the sun; refuses the oath and keeps the lover; and in her own coinages she outbids his heavens with heavens of her own devising. His stars were decorative, two fair stars deputizing for her eyes. Her stars, in the wedding-night speech, are constitutive: Romeo cut out in little stars will make the face of heaven so fine that all the world will be in love with night (3.2.21-24). The figure takes his domain, reverses its direction, since he had made her a body of heaven and she makes him the heaven's very fabric, and raises its stakes from compliment to cosmology. This is the uptake pattern at its most developed: not echo, not correction, but escalation under new management. A shared vocabulary, unlike a borrowed one, permits this. The later speaker owes the earlier the domain, and owes nothing else; the figure may be rebuilt entirely so long as the currency is honored. By the wedding night, Juliet is the currency's central bank.

One more audit in the scene converts figure into institution, and it is the audit from which the rest of the plot is paperwork. Having refused the moon and rationed the oath, Juliet takes up the evening's whole proceeding under the law's own figure and rules on its validity: the contract is too rash, too unadvised, too sudden, and her remedy is not annulment but procedure. If his bent of love is honourable and his purpose marriage, he is to send her word tomorrow, by a messenger she will procure (2.2.149-151). The sequence deserves to be read as the vocabulary's first official act. A figure, the contract, is proposed by circumstance, examined for defects of haste, and reissued with terms attached: a channel, a deadline, a condition of honorable intent. Everything the feud's lawyers and fathers will later do with documents, injunctions, settlements, and dates set for weddings, the lovers do first with a metaphor, in an orchard, in the dark. The difference is that their instrument is drafted by both parties and amended at the request of the party it most binds. When the wedding follows within a day, it follows the orchard contract's schedule, not the household's, and the Friar's office is engaged as the contract's executing officer rather than its author. The play's institutions all pass through the lovers' figures and come out altered; the contract is simply the case where the alteration can be watched clause by clause.

The orchard scene also contains the pattern's limit case, and the limit is instructive. When the parting comes, Juliet's falconry figure, the falconer's voice that would lure the tassel-gentle back, is spoken to a man already vanished below the wall (2.2.158-160). It receives no answer, and yet it does not fall back into the old economy, because its terms are now joint property; she can spend them alone and they still signify the pair. The distinction matters for the tomb. A figure coined in solitude, like the Rosaline machinery, means only its coiner. A figure coined in exchange and later used in solitude means the exchange, and carries the absent partner inside it. The orchard scene thus establishes both halves of the play's figurative law: figures become shared by being answered, and having become shared, they survive the answerer's absence. Act 5 will test the second half under the worst conditions the play can arrange.

## Bounty and Merchandise: The Wealth Vocabulary and Its Correction

The wealth domain is where the correction pattern shows its teeth, because wealth is the domain in which Verona's public language is strongest. The marriage market speaks fluent treasure: a daughter is a hopeful lady of her father's earth, a suitor is appraised like a volume lacking only a cover, dowries and jointures are the grammar of every negotiation the lovers never hear. When Romeo reaches for wealth figures in the orchard, he reaches, inevitably, for the market's dialect of it. Clinging to the wall's top, he prices the venture: he is no pilot, yet were she as far as the vast shore washed with the farthest sea, he would adventure for such merchandise (2.2.82-84). The figure is ardent and it is acquisitive. She is cargo; he is the venturer; love is a trading expedition whose risk dignifies its object. It is also, structurally, the same figure the marriage market uses, in which a woman is the goods and men are the parties to the contract. Juliet's answer rebuilds the domain from its foundations.

Her answer is the bounty speech: "My bounty is as boundless as the sea, / My love as deep; the more I give to thee, / The more I have, for both are infinite" (2.2.133-135). Every term corrects his. Merchandise becomes bounty, a gift rather than a cargo. The far shore of acquisition becomes the sea itself, no longer a distance to be crossed for gain but the substance of the giver. Most radically, the arithmetic changes: the market's wealth diminishes by being spent, while hers increases by being given, so that the figure refutes the economy it borrows its words from. Romeo does not answer the correction with a counter-figure. He accepts the new currency, and his later wealth figures, the feast-day spending of himself in the tomb, the gold he calls a worse poison than the drug it buys, are struck in her mint, wealth as expenditure and gift rather than as acquisition. The trade in this domain is therefore the play's clearest case of a figure changing its politics in passage. Whoever studies only the cluster, wealth images counted and filed, will find continuity: the lovers talk treasure from the jewel at the feast to the statues at the close. Whoever studies the exchange finds a coup: the domain was captured from the marriage market in a single speech and repopulated with contrary laws.

The jewel at the feast belongs to the prehistory of this capture, and its wording already contains the tension the bounty speech resolves. In his aside, Romeo hangs Juliet on the cheek of night as a rich jewel in an Ethiope's ear, "beauty too rich for use, for earth too dear" (1.5.44-46). The figure appraises even as it adores: rich, dear, priced beyond use, the vocabulary of a connoisseur before a cabinet piece. Nothing in the aside is ignoble, and everything in it is solo. Its afterlife is the measure of the vocabulary's growth. By the orchard, preciousness has migrated from the appraised object to the giving subject; by the wedding night, the treasure figure has become Juliet's own, the mansion of a love bought but not possessed (3.2.26-27), real property held in her name and on her terms; by the tomb, the only wealth left in the lovers' register is the gold of the apothecary's transaction, spoken by Romeo in bitterness as poison's price, and the fathers' answering gold, the statues, which belong to the public economy entirely and are phrased, tellingly, as competing bids. The domain begins in appraisal, is captured by gift, and ends by reverting to the market that spawned it, once the two speakers who held it in common are gone. No count of wealth images across the five acts can show that trajectory. Only the map of handovers can.

The apothecary scene supplies the domain's final audit, conducted by Romeo alone in Mantua, and its verdict is phrased in her currency's terms. The gold he pays for the poison is itself named the worse poison, a cordial to no man's soul, and the transaction is condemned in the act of being completed: wealth that buys death is wealth inverted, the anti-bounty, giving that diminishes the receiver to nothing (5.1.80-83). The speech's bitterness is usually read as world-weariness, and it is more specific than that. It is the doctrine of the orchard applied by its surviving student to a market transaction, the audit performed correctly by a speaker who has no one left to perform it with. He prices the ducats, finds the price obscene, and pays it anyway, because the system the audit served no longer exists to receive the appeal. The wealth domain thus ends as the light domain ends, in a solo use of joint law, exact in form and desolate in circumstance. What the fathers spend on statues, an hour later, is the same gold without the doctrine, the market's answer to the audit it never heard.

A smaller exchange in the same domain sharpens the point about who audits whom. At the wedding, the Friar warns in the market's own idiom of measure and store, and Juliet, arriving, answers Romeo's rapture with an auditor's paradox: those who can count their worth are beggars, but her love has grown to such excess that she cannot sum up half her wealth (2.6.32-34). The figure takes his hyperbole and subjects it to accountancy, finding the sum uncountable not because it is vague but because it is the wrong operation. Counting, in her system, is what poverty does. The line deserves its place in the wealth dossier because it states the domain's new law in its purest form, a law phrased as a figure about figures: in the lovers' economy, value is proved by the failure of enumeration. Spurgeon's method, which enumerates, will meet this line again when the critics are heard.

## Correction as Co-Authorship: Juliet Edits Every Figure She Receives

The pattern this article keeps finding, proposal and audit, has a politics that should be stated plainly. In the Verona of the play, a woman's speech is expected to be receptive: Lady Capulet delivers the marriage pitch as a prepared text, praises Paris as a book to be read, and receives her daughter's conditional answer, that she will look to like if looking can move liking, as the limit of permitted response. Juliet's practice with figures is the opposite of receptive. She treats every metaphor addressed to her as a draft contract, striking clauses, replacing guarantors, rewriting the arithmetic, reserving conditions. The bud of love figure is her signature instrument, an image with a maturity clause: this bud, by summer's ripening breath, may prove a beauteous flower when next they meet (2.2.121-122). Where Romeo's figures are declarations of a present state, hers are instruments with futures attached, and the shared vocabulary is shaped accordingly. It does not merely describe the love. It schedules it, conditions it, and insures it, all in figures, because figures are the only instruments the lovers are permitted to draft for themselves.

### Why does Romeo speak in finished metaphors while Juliet speaks in questions about them?

His figures arrive complete because they come from books and from solitude, polished before they are spoken, the Petrarchan stock rehearsed for Rosaline and never tested in dialogue. Hers arrive as questions because she is auditing in real time, testing each term against the thing it names, and the interrogative is the auditor's native mood.

The audit metaphor is not imposed on the scene; the scene supplies it. Juliet's orchard speeches are full of the language of proof, trial, and reckoning, and her corrections are consistently framed as rulings rather than counter-declarations. She does not answer the sun with a rival sun. She cross-examines the oath, confiscates the moon, stipulates the season, reprices the merchandise. Even her dissent from his figure-making in general takes the form of a procedural rule: farewell compliment, the dismissal of formal courtesies as inadmissible evidence (2.2.88). The lovers' shared vocabulary is therefore not best pictured as a common purse into which both drop the same coins. It is a mint and an assay office under one roof. He strikes the coin; she tests its metal, clips its edges, and stamps it for circulation, and the coin that passes bears both their marks. This is why the correction pattern, far from signaling her coldness, is the play's strongest evidence of her investment. Indifference lets figures pass. Only a partner audits.

The pattern also redistributes the play's intellectual honors in a way the criticism has not always registered. Romeo is the play's most quoted metaphorist, and the anthology pieces, the sun speech, the torch speech, the tomb's feasting presence, are his. Yet nearly every one of his set pieces is either addressed to an absent woman or spoken before the answering begins, and the figures that live in the dialogue, the ones the play itself keeps reusing, are the joint ones: pilgrim and saint, bounty and sea, stars and night. Juliet, who coins fewer anthology pieces, is the co-author of all the durable ones, including several, the pilgrim constitution above all, that anthologies credit to the scene rather than to her rulings within it. The shared vocabulary thus corrects the play's own reputation. Read by set pieces, the figurative genius is Romeo. Read by exchanges, it is the partnership, with Juliet holding the senior share in everything that lasts.

The audit skill also has a defensive use, and the mother scene of the third act displays it. Facing Lady Capulet's murderous grief and the Paris marriage, Juliet answers in constructions engineered to carry two tenors at once, satisfied with Romeo only when she beholds him dead, in a heart positioned so that both women hear a different sentence (3.5.93-103). The equivocation is usually praised as presence of mind, and it is better understood as the shared vocabulary's technique operating in hostile territory. A speaker trained to ask what a vehicle imports, which properties a term smuggles past its hearer, possesses exactly the skill required to build terms with controlled imports, one cargo declared and one concealed. The auditorship that made her a co-author at the feast makes her ungovernable at home. Verona's public language never develops the technique, because repetition has no use for double meanings held steady; her figures can hold two meanings the way her bounty held two directions of wealth, because holding contraries in a single term is what her half of the vocabulary was always for.

One further correction belongs in the dossier because it operates on the largest possible term. In the tomb, Romeo, at the threshold of the vault, finds Juliet's beauty making the place a feasting presence full of light (5.3.85-86). The figure fuses the vocabulary's two founding domains, the feast's light and the feast itself, in a single phrase, and it is built entirely from joint currency: the torches of the first meeting, the radiance of the window, the banquet at which a masked stranger was fed by his enemies. No editor is needed to hear the history in it, and no auditor survives to amend it. The line is the shared vocabulary's last utterance, a fortune spent in a room where the other signatory lies silent, and the play's figurative plot completes itself in the spending. What the feud's language does at the same hour, competition in statues, belongs to the feud's own hearing, which follows.

## The Wedding Exchange: Three Languages in One Room

The wedding scene is the vocabulary's public debut, the one occasion on which the lovers' language, the Friar's language, and the Church's language occupy the same room and can be compared under laboratory conditions. Romeo speaks first, and his opening figure is the proof that the orchard's reforms have held: come what sorrow can, it cannot countervail the exchange of joy that one short minute gives him in her sight (2.6.3-5). The terms are the market's, exchange, countervail, measure, and the doctrine is entirely hers. Value is computed as surplus of giving over cost, sorrow entered on the ledger and found insufficient against a minute of presence. A speaker still using the old economy would have priced the bride; this speaker prices his own endurance, and finds the trade profitable at any rate. The bounty reform, enacted two scenes earlier as a figure, is here functioning as an assumption, which is what reforms look like when they succeed.

The Friar's answer is the scene's counter-demonstration. His warning, that violent delights have violent ends and die in their triumph like fire and powder which, as they kiss, consume (2.6.9-11), is a fine figure and a public one, proverb-shaped, portable, addressed to the situation rather than to the man. Its most revealing feature is its vehicle: the kiss itself, the lovers' founding act, conscripted as an image of mutual destruction. Neither lover takes the figure up. Romeo does not amend it, Juliet does not rule on it, and the gunpowder passes out of the dialogue unspent, to be detonated by the plot rather than by the conversation. The non-uptake is the politest refusal the vocabulary permits, and the scene teaches the reader to hear it. In this play, silence about a figure is a verdict on it, from auditors trained to correct everything they accept. The Friar's counsel is heeded in the event and refused in the language, a distinction the tragedy will exploit: the lovers die of the warning's truth without ever having admitted its terms into their lexicon, as citizens may die under a law whose wording they never adopted.

Juliet's entrance completes the triangulation, because her first act in the scene is to state the vocabulary's aesthetic law in the vocabulary's own terms. Conceit, more rich in matter than in words, brags of its substance rather than its ornament (2.6.30): the doctrine distinguishes figures that carry freight from figures that display finish, and it is pronounced, fittingly, by the partner whose corrections always chose freight. Her counting paradox follows in the same speech and has been entered in the wealth dossier; the doctrine has not, and it deserves its place here because it is the nearest the play comes to a poetics spoken by a practitioner. By its standard, the pilgrim conceit passes, since its ornament was all substance, every rhyme a ruling. The Friar's gunpowder, for all its finish, is ornament in her sense, a figure whose matter is a commonplace about excess. The rite then proceeds in the Church's words, and the scene closes with the three languages unmerged: the liturgy has blessed the union, the counsel has warned it, and the vocabulary has gone on, in asides and doctrines of conceit, building the only account of the marriage that both spouses co-authored. It is the last scene in which all three can be heard at once. After it, the public languages and the private one separate for good, and the play's remaining trades are conducted, as the map records, under worsening conditions until they are conducted alone.

## Initiation, Adoption, Refusal: How a Domain Enters the Vocabulary

Not every figure offered to the lovers enters their vocabulary, and the admissions policy is as revealing as the coinages. A domain can arrive by three routes. It may be coined inside the pair, as pilgrimage and bounty are. It may be offered by an outsider and adopted under amendment, the rarest route, of which the Friar's incorporation is the standing example. Or it may be offered and refused, and the refusals map the vocabulary's border, since a lexicon is known by what it will not naturalize. The book is the cleanest case of refusal followed by capture. Lady Capulet introduces it as the marriage market's master figure: Paris is a volume to be read, delight written in his face with beauty's pen, a precious book of love that lacks only a cover, the cover being a wife (1.3.82-94). The figure assigns the roles exactly as the market requires, the man as text and treasure, the woman as binding and ornament, legible only in relation to the volume she completes. Juliet's answer refuses the reading contract itself, in the consent-gated formula by which she will look only as far as permission allows her eye to fly. The domain is not thereby banished. Hours later it reappears inside the lovers' exchange as her verdict that Romeo kisses by the book, the market's volume shrunk to a joke about technique, a term of the household's salesmanship naturalized as a term of intimate criticism. Refusal and adoption are the same act at two speeds: nothing enters unchanged.

The stars show refusal in its pure form, because the refusal is of the play's own frame. The Prologue's astrology, star-crossed lovers under adverse influence, is the most authoritative figure Verona possesses, pronounced by the Chorus with the play's whole machinery behind it, and the lovers never once take it up between them. Juliet's stars are Romeo's substance transfigured, a private astronomy with no fate in it; Romeo's one engagement with the hostile heavens is defiance, a posture he drops the moment he is back inside the pair's language at the tomb, where the operative figure is not the star but the feast. A vocabulary that can refuse its own prologue is a vocabulary with a constitution in the strict sense, a rule of recognition by which proposed terms are tested. The test, by now familiar, is answerability under amendment. The Chorus's terms fail it, since they are pronounced over the lovers' heads and admit no reply. The Friar's liturgy passes it once, because the pair kneel to a term and thereby make it, for one ceremony, jointly held. Benvolio's pharmacy fails it in the other direction: his cure by contrary fires, one pain lessened by another's anguish (1.2.45-46), is answered by Romeo only with counter-prescriptions of the same school, eye answering eye in a duel of remedies neither man believes, and the exchange ends where exchanges between non-lovers end, in the counsel being abandoned rather than amended. Advice can be rejected without cost. A shared figure cannot be rejected at all; it can only be corrected, which is why corrections, not rejections, fill the lovers' scenes.

Mercutio is the border's most interesting casualty, since his figures are offered constantly and adopted never. His idiom is a closed virtuosity, proverbs, bestiaries, and bawdy inventories that invite applause rather than amendment, and when Romeo does trade with him in kind, in the market scene's duel of puns, the product is a game resumed, not a lexicon founded: the terms are spent in the playing and banked by neither. The play marks the distinction in Mercutio's own verdict on the Rosaline period, when he conjures his friend by the mistress's bright eyes and scarlet lip, an imitation catalogue meant to shame the original out of circulation. Imitation is adoption's counterfeit; it reproduces a figure's surface to destroy its credit. The lovers' uptakes do the opposite, preserving the term by changing its law. Between Mercutio's parody and Juliet's audit lies the whole distance between Verona's wit and the pair's language, and it is the distance this article has been measuring from the start: whether a second speaker's treatment of a figure leaves it richer or leaves it ruined.

## The Vocabulary's Memory: Figures Recalled, Amended, and Spent Alone

A shared vocabulary proves itself by memory. When a figure coined by one speaker returns, acts later, in the other speaker's mouth, amended by everything that has happened between the coinage and the recall, the return is a deed of title: the term is common property, and both speakers know its history without needing to recite it. The light chain shows the mechanism at full stretch. At the feast, light is Romeo's aside property, torches taught and a jewel appraised, a domain of one. At the window, light becomes the medium of address itself, the sun assigned, the moon audited, radiance converted into the scene's constitution. At the dawn parting, light is the disputed fact, the thing both speakers see and cannot describe into agreement, the chain's stress test. In the tomb, light is fused with the feast that began the chain, the feasting presence, so that the final use of the domain cites its first use across four acts, and the citation is legible only to a hearer who holds the whole history. Juliet, who would have caught the allusion instantly, is the one person who cannot hear it. The chain's meaning is therefore stored between the speakers and nowhere else; remove either memory and the last link is a pretty phrase instead of a settlement of accounts.

The night chain runs in counterpoint, and its links are Juliet's. Night begins as backdrop, the cheek on which the jewel hangs in his aside, a mere condition of visibility. In the orchard it becomes the meeting's element and protector, the time when names can be discarded. On the wedding night Juliet addresses it as a person and a procurer, "Come, night; come, Romeo; come, thou day in night" (3.2.17), a line in which the chains cross: night summoned, Romeo named as day within it, the light vocabulary and the night vocabulary fused into a single vocative that no speaker outside the pair could parse, since it depends on knowing that he is her sun and that night is her ally. By the dawn parting, night has become the losing party in the argument, defended with a false meteor and surrendered with a ruling on the lark's music. The domain's biography is the relationship's biography told in one word's changing offices, backdrop, element, ally, defendant, and the biography is co-authored at every step, since each new office is conferred by the speaker who did not confer the last.

The failures of memory are as diagnostic as the successes. The Nurse, who carries more of the lovers' words than any other character, retains none of their figures: she can repeat an errand verbatim and forget a term's meaning entirely, praising Romeo in one breath as an honest gentleman and dismissing him in the next as a dishclout beside Paris, valuations from the market's scale applied to a man the vocabulary had long since repriced. Balthasar delivers his catastrophic report in unamended prose, exact and fatal, a message whose fidelity is the disaster. Even Romeo, in Mantua, falls out of the lexicon for a scene: his defiance of the stars and his purchase of the poison are conducted in the public registers of challenge and commerce, and the shared vocabulary returns to his mouth only as he descends into the vault and finds the feast waiting in the dark. The play's figurative memory is thus located with unusual precision. It does not live in either lover, ready to be carried into exile or widowhood. It lives between them, in the space their exchanges built, and it can be re-entered only by returning to that space, which is why the tomb, of all places, is where Romeo's language comes home.

## The Language the Houses Share: Insult, Oath, and Law, but Never a Metaphor

The feud has a language, and it is genuinely shared; the claim of this article is not that the houses lack common speech but that their common speech never once performs the operation the lovers perform constantly, the joint building of a figure. Consider the opening scene. Sampson and Gregory rehearse a routine of boasts and puns whose every line is formula, a script the pair have plainly performed before, and when the Montague servants arrive the same formulas serve as challenges, word given and word returned without alteration. The exchange is real exchange, turn and counterturn, yet nothing in it is coined, because coinage is not its purpose. Its purpose is the reproduction of a stance, and its success is measured by how exactly the inherited phrases are repeated. The thumb is bitten, the lie is given, the swords come out, and the language has done its office when the bodies take over. Set beside it the feast sonnet, in which a phrase's success is measured by how completely the answering speaker transforms it, and the two systems define each other. One language exists to be repeated; the other exists to be revised.

The houses' higher registers show the same law. The Prince speaks law: edicts, forfeits, the arithmetic of three civil brawls and a penalty in lives, a vocabulary of command that admits no reply except obedience or defiance. The fathers speak household: honor, disposal, the language of property and provision in which Juliet is an item of estate and Paris a purchaser of rank. Tybalt speaks the duel's liturgy, its challenges timed like fencing measures. Mercutio, the great apparent exception, speaks a virtuoso idiolect that he shares with no one, since his figures are performances for an audience of friends who admire without answering in kind; when Romeo finally does answer him in kind, in the punning duel of the market scene, the recovery of the shared game is read by Mercutio himself as a resurrection, the friend returned from the Petrarchan tomb. Even wit, in Verona, becomes communal only at the moment it touches the lovers' story. The one vocabulary in the play that is built by revision is built by the pair the city is trying to keep apart, and the play's design insists on the irony: the feud's parties, who meet constantly, never make a figure together, while the lovers, who meet four times, make a language.

The Prince's closing speech shows the public language arriving, at the very end, at a figure of its own, and the arrival confirms the rule by its lateness and its form. For five acts his register has been definition and price: offenses enumerated, penalties scheduled, mercy debated as policy and banishment computed as the lesser sum. Over the bodies, the register finally flowers into the famous superlative, a story of more woe than any other, sealed in the rhyming couplet that pairs the lovers' names as no living speaker had paired them in public. It is a genuine coinage, and it is unanswerable by design, since its subjects are past answering and its speaker is the law. The Chorus opened the play by imposing figures on the lovers from above; the Prince closes it the same way, and between those two impositions the entire shared vocabulary flourished in the only space Verona left unadministered, the space between two speakers who kept handing each other the pen.

The figures the public world does produce for the lovers are imposed from outside and never adopted. The Prologue calls them star-crossed and death-marked, cosmic and funerary terms of great power; the lovers themselves never use either phrase, and Romeo's only engagement with the star system on his own account is defiance of it. The fathers, at the close, translate the lovers into gold, statues raised in competitive grief, and the translation is final precisely because it is unanswerable: a statue cannot correct its coiner. Between imposition and statue lies the whole career of the shared vocabulary, and the contrast explains why the imposed figures ring hollow even when they ring beautiful. They describe the lovers the way the feud describes everything, from outside, in terms fixed in advance, with no mechanism for the described to answer back. Co-authored figures carry their history of consent inside them. Imposed figures carry only their maker's power, and the play measures the difference in the only coin it trusts, what survives its speakers. The Chorus's phrases survive as the play's fame; the lovers' phrases survive as the play's life, quoted inside the action by the partners themselves, the stars, the bounty, the palm-to-palm ruling returning in scene after scene, each return a small proof that the vocabulary, unlike its imposers, was built to be handed on.

Capulet provides the limiting case, a public speaker who does coin a great figure alone. Over his daughter's seeming corpse he renames the wedding as a funeral in a single sustained substitution: death is his son-in-law, death his heir, his daughter married to death (4.5.35-38). The coinage is powerful, and it is the anti-type of the lovers' work. It is coined over a silent body, by the man who holds the floor, in a register no one present may amend, and its function is disposal: the figure arranges the girl, the household, and the feast in a pattern that its speaker alone controls. Juliet's own figures for her situation, coined in soliloquy that same night, are all conditions and contingencies, a dagger kept ready against several futures. His are decrees. The difference between a metaphor imposed on a person and a metaphor built with a person is, at the last, a difference of government, and the play, which is everywhere concerned with who may speak and who must be silent, draws the line exactly there.

## Caroline Spurgeon Counts, M. M. Mahood Listens: The Critics Disagree

The critics mandatory to this subject divide along the very line the map is drawn to expose. Caroline Spurgeon, in Shakespeare's Imagery and What It Tells Us, assembled the play's images by patient enumeration and found the iterative cluster of light, set against darkness and joined with swiftness, to be the play's imaginative signature, a pattern she took to be largely unconscious, the deposit of the dramatist's mind rather than a device of his rhetoric. Her method has a famous blindness, which her critics named early: a count cannot tell who speaks an image, or to whom, or with what answer. Wolfgang Clemen answered the blindness from within imagery study itself, arguing that an image means by its development, its placement and transformation across the action, so that the unit of study should be the career of a figure rather than its frequency. M. M. Mahood, in Shakespeare's Wordplay, pressed further toward performance: the play's figures, its puns and metaphors alike, are events between speakers, engines of the drama's thinking, and their sense is completed in the hearing and the reply. The methods behind the counting, and the objections the count provoked across the century, are treated in full in the study of Spurgeon's cluster method; the adjudication required here concerns the lovers' vocabulary alone.

On that ground, the disagreement resolves with unusual cleanliness, because each method succeeds exactly where the other is blind. Spurgeon's count is vindicated on domains: the lovers do share the clusters she found dominant, and the map's rows confirm the census at the level of exchange, light and the heavens traded more heavily than any other goods. A critic who denied the census would have to deny the text. But the count is then exhausted, since everything that distinguishes the lovers' use of light from the play's general weather of light, the auditing, the escalation, the conversion of merchandise into bounty, lies in the handovers, where Spurgeon's instrument does not reach. Mahood's exchange model reaches it and explains it, yet it needs Spurgeon's census as its warrant, for without the count there is no proof that the traded domains are the play's central ones rather than a local habit of two scenes. The adjudication is therefore a division of jurisdiction with a verdict on the question that matters most. For what imagery the lovers share, Spurgeon stands. For what sharing means, Mahood and Clemen stand, and Mahood stands first, since the figure's meaning in this play is not complete when it is spoken. It is complete when it is answered.

The editor's testimony belongs in the same hearing, because editors mediate every figure a modern reader meets. René Weis, editing the play for the Arden third series, prints the text from which the quotations here are taken, and his notes perform on dead vehicles the service Juliet performs on live ones: they audit and restore. The palmer's palm, the Ethiope's ear, the tassel-gentle, the gentle fine itself, all reach a modern audience carrying editorial explanation without which the vehicle's freight is lost. Weis's edition keeps the second quarto's reading at the sonnet's hinge, where the first quarto's variant would alter the theology of the fine into the theology of sin, and the choice is not cosmetic; an entire account of the conceit, payment or pardon, hangs on which noun the editor prints. The editorial disagreements of the play's modern history, between the Oxford text of Jill Levenson and the Arden text among them, cluster precisely at figurative pressure points, since those are the places where a reading changes a sense. The lesson the apparatus teaches is the article's lesson at one remove. A metaphor is a transaction, and a text is a transaction too, between a dead author's coinage and a living editor's audit, with the reader cast, finally, in Juliet's office: receiving figures, testing their vehicles, and admitting only those that survive the test.

Clemen's contribution deserves separate statement, because the map is built on his insight as much as on Mahood's ear. In The Development of Shakespeare's Imagery, Clemen argued against the decorative account of the early plays, in which images were read as embroidery applied to a finished design. His images grow with the action, gathering associations as they recur, until a late occurrence carries the accumulated charge of every earlier one. The light domain in this play is his thesis in miniature. Decorative at the feast, where it ornaments an aside, it becomes constitutional in the orchard, evidentiary at the dawn parting, and elegiac in the tomb, each stage unintelligible without the stages before. Yet Clemen's unit remains the motif in the dramatist's hand, and the map requires one further turn of the screw: in the lovers' scenes, the development is not only wrought by the author behind the speakers. It is transacted between the speakers, on stage, in the open, each recurrence proposed by one and amended by the other. Clemen explains why the figures ripen. The exchange model explains who ripens them, and the adjudication stands: his developmental account is the necessary middle term between Spurgeon's census and the handover analysis this article defends.

Spurgeon deserves a final word in her own defense, because the lovers themselves seem to have anticipated the objection to her method. Juliet's wedding-day paradox, that only beggars can count their worth, is spoken against enumeration from inside the play, and yet the play that contains it is built, as Spurgeon showed, from enumerable recurrences whose patterning no reader's ear can fully track unaided. The resolution is to keep the count in its office, as the lovers keep the Friar in his: a servant of the design, not its master. Counted, the images show a mind saturated with light. Exchanged, they show two minds building a country of two out of the light they share. The first finding is Spurgeon's and it is permanent. The second is the one the play's dialogue keeps proving, and it is the one this article's map exists to display.

## The Last Trades: The Aubade Argument and the Soliloquy Spending

The dawn parting is the shared vocabulary's severest test, because it is the one exchange in which the machinery of trade is turned to disagreement about the world itself. Juliet opens with a figure stated as a finding of fact: it was the nightingale, and not the lark, that sang on the pomegranate tree (3.5.2-3). Romeo answers with the rival vehicle, the lark, herald of the morn, and the evidence of his eyes, night's candles burnt out and jocund day standing tiptoe on the misty mountain tops (3.5.6-9). For twenty lines the lovers do with a bird what they once did with a shrine: propose terms, examine them, rule on them. The difference is that the shrine's terms were theirs to legislate, while the bird's identity is not, and the scene's pain is the pain of a legislative body confronting a fact. Juliet's last amendment is the vocabulary's masterpiece of desperation, the invention of a meteor to explain the light away, a figure coined in full knowledge that it is false, offered anyway, because the shared language has become the only court in which the parting can be appealed. When she surrenders the amendment, "It is, it is," the surrender is phrased as a judgment on the figure rather than on the fact: the lark sings out of tune, its divisions divide (3.5.27-30). She concedes the world and keeps the lexicon, blaming the bird's music for the separation the bird merely announces. No other scene shows so clearly what the shared vocabulary is for. It cannot repeal the morning. It can ensure that the morning is described in terms the pair built together, and therefore suffered together, which is the whole of what language can do against dawn.

After the parting, the exchanges stop, and the vocabulary enters its third state: coined jointly, traded jointly, and now spent in solitude by speakers who can no longer reach each other. Juliet's wedding-night speech is the first great solo spending, and its splendor is the splendor of fluency in a learned tongue. She summons night as a sober-suited matron, bargains with fortune for Romeo's return, and coins, alone, the heavens figure that answers his window speech across an act's distance: Romeo taken and cut out in little stars (3.2.21-24). The coinage is hers, the currency theirs, and the speech knows the difference, since it is addressed to the night and to an absent husband precisely because the answering voice is unavailable. Romeo's tomb speech completes the pattern from the other side. The feasting presence full of light spends the feast's domain and the window's domain in a single fusion, addressed to a woman who cannot hear it, in a room filling with the dead. Both speeches are among the play's summits, and both are, in the ledger of the shared vocabulary, unbalanced entries: all expenditure, no reply. The play's critics have usually treated them as the lovers' figurative triumphs. The map reads them as triumphs under erasure, the vocabulary functioning perfectly after its world has ended, like law recited in a dissolved state.

Paris at the tomb is the control case the play has been keeping in reserve, the would-be lover whose figures are never answered, exhibited at the moment of maximum contrast. He strews the bridal bed with flowers, addresses the seeming corpse in the vocabulary of courtship, sweet flower, bride, the whole bridal register performed over a woman who cannot hear it (5.3.12-16), and the performance is flawless and null. Everything the feast scene established is here inverted. The beloved does not rule, amend, or grant; the figures do not change hands; the speaker's tenderness has no more conversational standing than the Chorus's astrology, and for the same reason: it is pronounced over a silent person by a speaker whose terms admit no reply. Paris is not mocked for this, and the scene's pity for him is real, but the play's figurative law is unsparing. His flowers mark the exact distance between the marriage the market arranged and the marriage the vocabulary built. One consists of figures spoken at a woman. The other consisted of figures spoken with her, and the tomb scene, by placing the two modes minutes apart in the same vault, delivers the comparison no critic needs to argue.

Juliet's potion speech is the vocabulary's grammar turned inward, and it belongs in this ledger of last trades. Alone in her chamber, she conducts the audit on herself, in the conditional mood that was always her instrument: what if the mixture does not work at all, what if the Friar has given her a poison in subtlety, what if she wakes before Romeo comes (4.3.21-43). Each hypothesis is a figure under examination, a scenario proposed and tested against its consequences, and the enforcement clause is kept ready in the dagger laid beside the vial, the auditor's power to void the whole proceeding rather than accept a corrupted term. The speech is the feast scene inverted: one speaker supplying petition, objection, ruling, and remedy in rotation, because the partner who once held half the offices is beyond reach. Its courage is usually praised, and its form deserves equal notice. It is the shared vocabulary operating at half strength and still recognizably itself, proof that what the pair built was not a set of phrases but a procedure, portable enough to be run by one mind in an empty room, at the cost of running it without appeal.

The Friar's letter scene and the apothecary's shop, the machinery of the ending, are conducted in the public language of errands, statutes, and prices, and the contrast is the play's final comment on its two tongues. Everything that fails in Act 5 fails in the language of repetition: a letter carried by formula, a report repeated without examination, a law cited and a price named. Everything that had succeeded between the lovers succeeded in the language of revision, where a message was expected to be changed by its receiver. Balthasar delivers his news exactly as received, and the exactness kills; Juliet would have audited it. The play does not state this moral, since stating morals is the public language's habit. It enacts the moral in the difference between a vocabulary that could be corrected and a set of messages that could not.

## What the Shared Vocabulary Reveals about the Love

The verdict can now be given its full form. The lovers' shared vocabulary reveals that the love is an act of joint authorship before it is anything else the criticism has called it: not appetite, since appetite does not audit; not idolatry, since idolatry does not accept correction; not escape, since the vocabulary does the hardest work of the play, the negotiation of consent, timetable, guarantor, and price, inside its figures. A couple that can build a language can build the institutions a language implies, and the tragedy is legible in those terms as an institutional murder: a household of two, with its own constitution drafted at a feast, its own currency reformed in an orchard, its own ceremonies ratified by one sympathetic official, is dismantled by a city whose language admits no revision, where every phrase must mean tomorrow what it meant before the lovers were born. The feud cannot update its terms; the lovers can update nothing else. Their speed, so often arraigned, is the speed of speakers who must coin, audit, and ratify a world in the intervals of a hostile one, and their figures have the haste of founding documents because that is what they are.

The fathers' reconciliation deserves a colder look than it usually gets, because it is conducted, uniquely in the feud's history, in the lovers' own grammar. Montague offers a statue in pure gold (5.3.299); Capulet answers with a matching figure, Romeo's statue to lie as rich beside his lady, and seals the exchange by naming the dead the poor sacrifices of their enmity (5.3.303-304). Proposal, escalation, a figure returned enlarged: the trades of the orchard, performed at last by the two men who never traded anything but blows. The grimness is the point. The feud learns co-authorship only from the lovers, only over their bodies, and only in the one material where escalation needs no answering mind, expenditure. Gold is bounty with the partner removed, giving that cannot be corrected because it cannot be heard. Verona's first jointly built figures are monuments, which is to say figures from which the answering voice has been structurally excluded, and the play leaves them gleaming as the public language's confession that it arrived at the lovers' method a lifetime late and understood everything about it except the listening.

The cliché hears pretty figures, interchangeable ornaments on a story that would stand without them. The map shows the figures bearing the structure's weight. Remove the pilgrim rulings and the first kiss loses its law; remove the moon's rejection and the vow loses its guarantor; remove the bounty's arithmetic and the love loses its economics; remove the stars' escalation and the wedding night loses its cosmology. What remains would still be a plot. It would not be this play, in which two people become married in the only register fully available to them, the register of figures proposed and figures answered, until the answering stops and the figures, spent in solitude over a sleeping girl and a silent vault, are gathered up by other hands and cast in gold. The golden statues are the vocabulary's opposite and its monument: the lovers' words, which lived by being changed, preserved by being fixed. Every reader who takes up the play reopens the exchange, auditing the vehicles, amending the terms, and in that sense the conversation the feud interrupted has never quite been closed. It waits, as the figures themselves wait, for the next answering voice.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch metaphor map, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch metaphor map is the table in this article that charts the lovers' figurative exchanges rather than their images. Each row records one trade: the shared figure and its domain, the speaker who coined it, the speaker who took it up and what the uptake did, and the consequence for the pair. The pattern the rows expose is a law of the dialogue. After the feast, no major figure between Romeo and Juliet is left unanswered; every coinage is audited, corrected, completed, or contested by the receiving speaker. Set beside that pattern stand the controls, figures coined in asides that stay in the old solo economy, and the closing entries, figures spent in solitude after the answering has stopped. The map thus turns imagery from atmosphere into biography, the record of a language being built by two people in real time.

### Q: How do metaphor, simile, and conceit differ as instruments in the lovers' exchanges?

The three instruments make three different kinds of claim. A metaphor asserts identity, as when Romeo declares that Juliet is the sun, abolishing the distance between the woman and the star so the speech can reason from the substitution. A simile compares under a flag of truce, keeping its terms apart with like or as, as when Juliet finds the contract too like the lightning, a judgment that cautions precisely because the compared things remain separate. A conceit litigates: it sustains an identification under argument, itemizing correspondences until the comparison yields a ruling, as the pilgrim exchange does across fourteen lines. The lovers' distribution of the instruments is itself evidence. Romeo reaches for metaphor and conceit, the fusions and the machines. Juliet reaches for the simile and the conditional figure, the instruments that let an auditor keep a saving distance while the claim is tested.

### Q: What makes a metaphor shared rather than merely repeated by two speakers?

Alteration in passage is what makes a metaphor shared. When two speakers merely repeat a figure, as the servants repeat their rehearsed boasts or the mourners repeat the commonplaces of lament, the figure keeps the value it arrived with and belongs to the tradition that issued it. A figure becomes shared property when the second speaker changes it under pressure of reply: ruling that pilgrims touch with hands rather than lips, refusing the moon while keeping the oath, answering merchandise with bounty. The changed figure thereafter carries both speakers' marks, and later uses of it recall the whole negotiation. Repetition is the feud's mode, in which phrases succeed by surviving unaltered. Revision is the lovers' mode, in which a phrase succeeds by surviving its audit. The distinction is the article's working test, applied row by row across the metaphor map.

### Q: At what moment does a figure of Romeo's first become a figure belonging to both lovers?

The transfer happens at the feast, in the pilgrim exchange, at the instant Juliet stops merely answering Romeo's conceit and starts ruling on it. His opening quatrain offers a complete machine: her hand a shrine, his touch a profanation, his lips two blushing pilgrims ready to pay the gentle fine. Her reply accepts the frame and reverses its law, ruling that saints have hands pilgrims' hands do touch and that palm to palm is itself the palmers' kiss. From that ruling the figure has two authors, and every later term in the exchange, sin purged, sin returned, prayer granted, is negotiated in a currency both have issued. Earlier figures of his, the torches and the jewel of his aside speeches, never undergo the transfer, because no answer reaches them. The moment of sharing is therefore datable to a single ruling inside a single sonnet.

### Q: Which speaker supplies each term of the pilgrim conceit at the feast, and how does the answering speaker change its meaning?

Romeo supplies the conceit's premises and Juliet supplies its law. His terms are the shrine, the profaning hand, the pilgrim lips, and the fine, a complete theology of approach in which a kiss is owed as payment for a touch. Her answering terms are the palmer, the sanctioned touch of hands, and prayer, and each one changes the meaning of the stock she inherited. The palmer replaces the pilgrim as the figure's licensed actor, shifting the rite from journeying to greeting. The sanctioned touch converts his profanation into devotion already permitted. Prayer converts his demand for lips into a petition that may be granted or refused at her discretion. By the closing couplet the conceit no longer says that a lover pays for a kiss. It says that a saint grants one, under conditions the saint has legislated, which is the opposite doctrine carried in the same words.

### Q: Why does Juliet correct Romeo's figures so often instead of simply receiving them?

Correction is her form of authorship, and the play builds it into her office from the first exchange. Receiving a figure unchanged would make her its object, the silent tenor of someone else's vehicle, as Rosaline is the silent object of figures she never hears. Juliet instead audits every vehicle for the properties it smuggles in: the moon's inconstancy, the merchandise's implication that she is cargo, hyperbole that would price her love by counting it. Each correction keeps the tenor, the devotion itself, while replacing the vehicle that misdescribes it, so the revisions are acceptances conducted by other means. The pattern also distributes the play's figurative genius justly. His set pieces dominate the anthologies, but the durable figures, pilgrim and saint, bounty and sea, are the audited ones, and the audit is hers. Indifference lets figures pass unexamined. Only a partner corrects them.

### Q: How can the metaphors alone tell a reader whether Romeo is speaking of Rosaline or of Juliet?

The test is answerability. The Rosaline figures form a closed system: oxymoron piled on oxymoron, the devout religion of the eye, the siege and the wound, all of it polished, quotable, and addressed to a woman who never replies, reported instead by friends who mock or diagnose it. Nothing in that system can develop, because development requires resistance, and the speeches show it, recycling the same Petrarchan stock from scene to scene. The Juliet figures, by contrast, bear the marks of a second mind: terms struck out, guarantors replaced, arithmetic rewritten, conditions attached. A reader given two passages without names could sort them by this test alone. Figures that end where they began, however brilliant, belong to the first love. Figures that travel, change value in passage, and return amended belong to the second, because amendment is the signature of a listener who answers.

### Q: Why do Capulet and Montague never build a single metaphor together, though they share a city and a prince?

Joint building requires each party to take the other's term seriously enough to change it, and the feud's discipline exists to prevent exactly that act of attention. The houses share plenty: a street code of insult whose formulas both sides repeat verbatim, a liturgy of challenges, the Prince's legal arithmetic, the marriage market's prices. All of it is inherited stock, and its rule is repetition without revision, since to amend an enemy's phrase would be to collaborate with him. Even their reconciliation is phrased competitively, statue bid against statue, expenditure answered by larger expenditure rather than by a figure made in common. The one apparent exception proves the rule, since Capulet's great figure over his daughter's body, death as son-in-law and heir, is coined alone, over a listener who cannot reply. Where the lovers' speech creates, the houses' speech administers, and administration is the enemy of coinage.

### Q: Why does Mercutio's imitation of Romeo's figures never turn into a shared figure between them?

Imitation aims at exposure, not continuation, and Mercutio's figures are built to end conversations rather than to extend them. His conjuring of the absent Romeo by Rosaline's bright eyes, high forehead, and scarlet lip reproduces the Petrarchan catalogue in order to ridicule its owner by inventory, and his Queen Mab flight turns Romeo's dream into a bestiary that its dreamer is invited to disown. A shared figure requires the answering speaker to invest in the term, to hold part of it as his own; Mercutio invests in nothing, holding every figure at the distance of parody, where its value can be enjoyed without being honored. The single moment the friends do trade in kind, the punning duel of the market scene, is registered by Mercutio as a resurrection, the old Romeo restored, which confirms the rule by its rarity. Wit between them is a game resumed, never a currency founded.

### Q: Why can the Nurse carry the lovers' words faithfully yet never enter their vocabulary?

The Nurse is a carrier by office, and the vocabulary admits only authors. Her own speech is rich in figures, the lamb and the ladybird, the wormwood on the dug, proverbial earth that she owns outright and that no one corrects because no one is auditing her. But the lovers' lexicon is closed to transmission: she brings Romeo's message and Juliet's ring without ever being asked to amend a term, and her attempts to speak inside the love's register, praising Paris as a man of wax or weighing Romeo as a dishclout beside him, are valuations in the market's dialect, not coinages in the pair's. The boundary is marked at the break, when Juliet dismisses her with a courtesy that conceals everything, and thereafter conducts her figurative life alone. A go-between may hold the words of an exchange and hold none of its meaning, which is why the play's letters and errands fail while its conversations succeed.

### Q: What does Spurgeon's counting of images miss about the handing of a metaphor from one speaker to another?

The count misses direction, and direction is where the drama is. Caroline Spurgeon's enumeration establishes, permanently, that light against darkness is the play's dominant imaginative weather, and the metaphor map confirms the census at the level of exchange. But a tally of light images cannot register that the sun is coined by Romeo as compliment and then rationed by Juliet as oath, that merchandise enters from the market and leaves as bounty, that a figure's meaning in this play is completed by its answer rather than by its utterance. Wolfgang Clemen's developmental account and M. M. Mahood's hearing of figures as events between speakers supply what the count cannot, while depending on the count for proof that the traded domains are the central ones. The verdict is jurisdictional: Spurgeon for what the lovers share, Mahood for what sharing does.

### Q: How do the Chorus's figures for the lovers differ from the figures the lovers coin for each other?

The Chorus's figures descend from outside and cannot be appealed, while the lovers' figures are appealable by design. Star-crossed and death-marked are magnificent impositions, cosmic and funerary verdicts pronounced before the evidence, and the lovers never adopt either term; Romeo's only transaction with the star system is defiance of it, and Juliet conducts her love in defiance of every prognosis the frame supplies. Imposed figures describe their objects the way the feud describes them, in terms fixed in advance by a speaker who will not be answered. Coined figures carry their history of consent inside them, each term bearing the amendment of the partner who accepted it. The difference is finally one of government. The Chorus rules by proclamation, like the Prince. The lovers legislate in committee of two, and their figures survive inside the action, requoted and revised, long after the proclamations have faded into the play's fame.

### Q: What happens to the lovers' shared figures at the dawn parting, when they disagree about the light they are both describing?

The machinery of trade survives and its product changes: for the first time, a traded figure becomes a dispute about fact rather than an emblem of the pair. Juliet proposes the nightingale as a finding; Romeo counters with the lark and the evidence of the sky; amendments follow, the invented meteor offered to explain the light away, until Juliet concedes with the judgment that the lark sings out of tune and its divisions divide. The scene shows the vocabulary doing the hardest work language can do, not repealing the morning but ensuring that the morning is described in terms the pair built together. Earlier trades legislated a private world; this one negotiates with a public fact, and its partial failure is the measure of the world's strength. After it, the figures continue in soliloquy, spent by speakers who can no longer reach each other, fluent and unanswerable at once.

### Q: Why do the lovers' figures keep crossing domains, so that light is spoken of in the language of worship and worship in the language of wealth?

Crossing is the signature of a living lexicon, since only a vocabulary in active joint use borrows across its own borders. Juliet's radiance is stated as sanctity, a saint who teaches torches; the shrine's offense is discharged as a fine, worship audited in the language of payment; bounty becomes the sea, wealth dissolving into water and infinity. Dead formulas stay in their compartments, as the houses' phrases do, each insult and oath filed under its occasion. The lovers' figures migrate because each exchange licenses the next speaker to extend a term past its home domain, and extension is how the pair says what neither domain could say alone: that the beloved is at once light to see by, a shrine to approach under law, and a treasure whose law is giving. The map's domain column is therefore always double-entry. A figure's home is recorded, and so is its destination in the partner's mouth.

### Q: How does the shared vocabulary register the marriage before any ceremony takes place?

The feast exchange performs, in figures, every act the ceremony will later ratify in words. Two parties meet under a governing rite, negotiate terms, strike clauses, grant petitions, and seal the bargain with the rite's prescribed gesture, all inside the pilgrim conceit, a scene before they have exchanged names. Consent, which the marriage service will ask for aloud, is here rehearsed as a sequence of rulings: his petition, her jurisdiction, his amended plea, her grant under conditions. By the orchard scene the pair are amending each other's figures with the ease of partners executing a standing agreement, and the Friar's liturgical phrase about two incorporated in one arrives as a caption to a union language has already effected. The ceremony remains necessary, and the play insists on it. But the register of the marriage, the book in which it is first written, is the vocabulary, and its first entry predates the wedding by two acts.

### Q: Why do modern editors have to rebuild the lovers' metaphors in their notes, and what has died in the figures since the first performances?

The vehicles have died while the tenors live, and the notes are resurrection work. A palmer's palm leaf, an Ethiope's ear as the period's standard of darkness against which a jewel shows, a tassel-gentle's training, the gentle fine as a pilgrim's payment: each vehicle carried freight for the first audiences that a modern reader cannot recover unaided, and without the freight the figure's argument, not merely its color, is lost. René Weis's Arden notes perform the audit openly, restoring the systems of pilgrimage, falconry, and devotion inside which the lovers' terms were negotiable currency. Editors even decide the theology at pressure points, printing the second quarto's fine where the first quarto's variant would make the sonnet's hinge a matter of sin rather than payment. The editorial task thus repeats the lovers' own pattern at a distance of centuries, receiving figures and testing what their vehicles can still carry.

### Q: How can two actors show an audience the moment a metaphor changes owners?

The transfer is physical before it is verbal, and the feast sonnet supplies the choreography. The actor offering a figure leans on its props, the hand extended, the term presented like an object for inspection; the answering actor takes the term the way the hand is taken, holding the pause a fraction longer than repartee requires, so the audience hears a ruling being considered rather than a cue being caught. Thereafter the amended term should be spoken with a small emphasis of possession, the palmer, the bounty, the stars, pronounced as property now jointly held. In the orchard the ownership markers reverse, since the correcting actor stands still while the coining actor circles, until the bounty speech stops the movement altogether. In the dawn parting both actors handle the same figure and cannot settle it, and the unsettling should be visible in competing gestures toward the light. Ownership, played this way, needs no underlining: an audience watches a word change hands exactly as it watches a ring change hands.

### Q: How should an essay use the metaphor map to argue that the lovers' intimacy is made of language before it is made of anything else?

The essay should argue from the handovers, not from the images. Begin with the founding trade, the pilgrim conceit, showing consent negotiated inside a figure before the lovers have exchanged names, and take the sonnet's sequence of petition and ruling as the template. Proceed through the map's central rows, the merchandise corrected into bounty, the moon struck from the oath, the stars escalated on the wedding night, quoting coinage and answer in pairs so the argument never rests on a figure quoted alone. Concede the census to Spurgeon, since the shared domains are the play's dominant ones, then claim the exchange for Mahood, where the meaning is completed by reply. Close with the solo spendings of the last acts, where the vocabulary persists after its world ends, as proof that the intimacy outlasted every other form the pair were permitted. The thesis writes itself from the evidence: the marriage was drafted in figures before it was solemnized in church.

### Q: Why does Friar Laurence, who marries the lovers, keep to his own figures and never borrow theirs?

His figures belong to a different system, the herb garden's doctrine of virtue and vice in single natures, and a system that complete has no need of imports. The Friar's metaphors are a philosophy, stated in soliloquy, applied to cases, never submitted for amendment; when Romeo answers his counsel it is with rejection or with tears, not with a counter-figure the Friar then adopts. Even his one phrase that enters the lovers' story, the incorporation of two in one, is liturgy rather than borrowing, the Church's term imposed on the union from outside, however welcome. The boundary matters because it defines the vocabulary's citizenship. The Friar is admitted as officiant, the single outsider whose figure the pair kneel to, and not as a member; his system frames their language without joining it. His tragedy is partly this exteriority, a man whose doctrine describes the lovers exactly and whose words never once become theirs.

### Q: How do the lovers' joint figures differ from the ready-made figures of the love poetry Romeo had memorized?

Ready-made figures arrive with their conclusions built in, while joint figures are built to reach conclusions neither speaker holds at the start. The Petrarchan stock Romeo rehearses for Rosaline, the cruel mistress, the siege, the wound, the eyes as stars of a distant heaven, is a closed kit: it assigns the lover his posture and the lady her silence in advance, and its speeches could be delivered, word for word, to any unresponsive beauty in Europe. The joint figures begin as the same stock, a shrine, a sun, a treasure, and are then broken open by reply, so that their final forms, a saint who legislates, a bounty that grows by giving, a heaven rebuilt from a husband's body, exist nowhere in the tradition he memorized. The difference is testable in the text. A memorized figure sounds identical whenever it recurs. A joint figure never recurs unchanged, because each return carries the amendments of the partner who last held it.
