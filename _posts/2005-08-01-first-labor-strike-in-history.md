In the twenty-ninth year of Ramesses III, around 1157 BCE, the men who cut and painted the tombs of Egypt's kings stopped working, walked off the job, and sat down behind the walls of a royal mortuary temple. Their grain rations were roughly eighteen days late. They stayed there, moved to other temples over the following days, called out that they were hungry, and refused to return until they were paid. A village scribe named Amennakht recorded what happened, day by day, on a papyrus that survives.

That document is the earliest known account of a labor strike anywhere in the world, and it is the reason this episode matters far beyond Egyptology.

![The first recorded labor strike in history at Deir el-Medina under Ramesses III, explained - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-83.webp)

The claim this guide defends can be named so it can be argued with: call it the wages-as-obligation thesis. The strike happened because the workmen understood their rations not as a gift from a generous king but as a wage owed under a standing arrangement, and because they believed that a failure to deliver broke an obligation they were entitled to protest. That is a specific and testable claim about how ordinary Egyptians understood their relationship to the state, and it runs against the assumption that people in ancient societies were powerless subjects with no concept of entitlement.

The evidence for it is unusually good, because the community that struck happens to be the best-documented working population in the ancient world. Deir el-Medina, the purpose-built village that housed the royal tomb workers, produced tens of thousands of written fragments covering work schedules, absences, wages, lawsuits, marriages, divorces, debts, medical complaints, letters, and complaints about neighbors. Nothing else from antiquity approaches it for depth of detail about how ordinary people actually lived.

The strike is therefore two stories at once. It is a specific event in a specific year with a specific cause, and it is a window into a whole community whose daily life can be reconstructed in detail that historians of most periods can only envy.

## The Village in the Desert

Understanding the strike requires understanding the place, because the community's unusual structure explains both why it kept such extensive records and why it was in a position to withhold its labor.

### Why was the village built where it was?

Deir el-Medina was a purpose-built, state-supported village on the Theban west bank housing the craftsmen who cut and decorated royal tombs. The Egyptians called it Set Maat, the Place of Truth. Founded early in the Eighteenth Dynasty and occupied until the end of the Twentieth, it was walled, closely supervised, and dependent on the state for food and water.

The village sits in a dry valley enclosed by hills, deliberately positioned away from the cultivated floodplain and close to the royal burial grounds it served. That placement was not accidental. The men who lived there knew the locations, layouts, and contents of the tombs of Egypt's kings, which made them both indispensable and a security concern. Housing them in a controlled settlement under state supervision solved a problem.

The site's physical layout is well recorded because it has been extensively excavated. A wall enclosed a compact grid of houses along a central street, with the settlement expanding over the centuries to roughly seventy dwellings within the enclosure at its largest, plus additional structures outside. Estimates of population vary with period and method, but a working figure of several hundred people, comprising the workmen, their wives, children, and dependents, gives the right scale.

### The isolation and its consequences

The location carried a cost that shaped everything about how the community functioned. There was no water. The village had no well, and every drop had to be carried in by donkey from the valley below, delivered by water carriers assigned to the task by the state. There was no agricultural land. The residents grew nothing and could not have fed themselves if the deliveries stopped.

This is the fact that makes the strike intelligible. A farming village whose payment fails can eat its own produce while it complains. Deir el-Medina could not. Its dependence on state provisioning was total, which made a delay in rations not an inconvenience but a direct threat to the household. It also, and this is the crucial point for the argument, gave the community both an urgent motive to act and a form of leverage, since the work they performed was work no one else could do.

### The organization of the crew

The workforce was organized on a pattern the documents describe in detail. The men formed a gang, divided into two sides conventionally rendered as the right side and the left side, corresponding to the two flanks of the tomb they were cutting. Each side had a foreman and a deputy. A scribe of the tomb kept the records for the whole operation, and the position carried considerable authority, since the scribe controlled the documentation on which everything else depended.

Above this local hierarchy stood the vizier, the highest official in the Egyptian administration, who was responsible for the royal tomb and who visited, issued instructions, and adjudicated. The chain running from a stonecutter in a corridor of the Valley of the Kings to the vizier of Egypt was very short, which is one reason the strike escalated as quickly as it did.

### The trades that made up the crew

They were the specialist craftsmen who excavated and decorated the royal tombs: stonecutters, plasterers, draftsmen, sculptors, and painters, titled Servants in the Place of Truth. They were state employees paid in rations, organized into a gang of two sides each under a foreman, supervised by a scribe and ultimately by the vizier.

The composition of the workforce is worth stating precisely because popular accounts often blur it. These were not laborers in any general sense. They were skilled tradesmen at the top of their professions, several of whose specialties required years of training, working on the most important construction project the state undertook in any given reign. Draftsmen who laid out relief scenes to the proportional canon and painters who executed them were not interchangeable with unskilled labor, and the state knew it.

Positions tended to run in families. Sons followed fathers into the crew, and particular families held the foremanships and the scribal post across generations. This produced a hereditary community of specialists with a strong internal identity, its own patron deities, its own cemetery on the slope above the houses, and its own accumulated sense of what it was owed.

## How the Work Actually Ran

The rhythm of the job explains a good deal about the community's life and about the mechanics of the dispute.

### What did the working rhythm look like?

The Egyptian week ran ten days, and the crew normally worked eight of them with two days off. During working periods the men stayed in huts on the col between the village and the Valley of the Kings rather than returning home each night, going back to their families for the rest days and for festivals.

The ten-day week, which Egyptologists call a decade, structured the calendar. Three of them made a month, and the pattern of eight days on and two days off gave a working rhythm broadly comparable to a modern arrangement, though the total holiday allowance was considerably more generous than that comparison suggests, since festivals were numerous and the records show a great deal of additional time off.

The working day itself was divided into two shifts with a break in the middle, and water was delivered to the work site as it was to the village. Tools were issued from state stores, counted out and counted back in, with copper chisels weighed to track wear, since the metal was valuable and the accounting was meticulous.

### The absence registers and what they record

Absence records from the village list reasons in plain language: illness, eye trouble, scorpion sting, brewing beer, building or plastering a house, embalming or burying a relative, attending a festival, offering to a god, and being detained by a superior. The records were kept as administrative fact rather than as accusation.

This body of material is one of the most humanizing survivals from the ancient world, and it also carries a serious analytical point. Absences were recorded, tolerated, and evidently not punished in most cases. A man who missed days for a family funeral or because he was ill remained on the roll and continued to be paid. That is not the profile of a coerced workforce. It is the profile of an employment relationship with recognized entitlements, which is exactly what the wages-as-obligation thesis requires.

### The pay

Wages came as rations, principally grain, and the arithmetic of the system determined how a household lived.

### Rations, ranks, and what a wage consisted of

Payment came chiefly in monthly grain rations of emmer wheat and barley, the raw materials of bread and beer, with amounts scaled by rank. Supplementary deliveries included fish, vegetables, water, firewood, pottery, and oil, with occasional issues of meat, salt, natron, and clothing, plus bonuses on royal festivals.

Grain functioned as the base currency of the arrangement. A workman received a monthly allotment sufficient to feed a household with a surplus that could be traded, and the surplus mattered, since it was the mechanism by which the villagers acquired everything the state did not supply. The extensive documentation of sales, loans, and debts within the community shows a lively internal economy operating on grain, commodities, and copper valued by weight, without coinage, which did not yet exist.

Rank determined amount. Foremen and the scribe received substantially more than ordinary workmen, and the differential was formalized rather than discretionary. Deliveries were made by outside personnel drawn from the temple estates and state granaries, which is a detail with direct bearing on the strike, since it means the people who failed to deliver were not the villagers' immediate supervisors but a separate administrative arm.

### The literacy that changed everything

The single most unusual feature of this community, and the reason it can be studied at all, is that a substantial proportion of its members could read and write.

### How literate was this community?

Literacy in the village was far above the Egyptian norm, with a significant proportion of the workmen able to read and write at least at a functional level. They produced tens of thousands of ostraca, flakes of limestone and potsherds used as cheap writing surfaces, covering work records, letters, contracts, lawsuits, accounts, sketches, and copies of literary texts.

Estimates of general literacy in ancient Egypt run very low, typically in the low single digits as a percentage of the population, with writing confined to a professional scribal class. Deir el-Medina was different because its work required it. Draftsmen laying out tomb decoration had to read the texts they were transcribing. Foremen had to handle written instructions. And the community's dense internal legal and economic life generated constant documentation.

The consequence for historians is enormous. Papyrus was expensive and reserved for important documents, but limestone flakes were free and lay everywhere on the Theban hillsides. The villagers used them for everything, and then threw them away, and the dry conditions preserved them. What survives is the discarded paperwork of an entire community across several centuries: not the official version, but the working version, including the parts nobody intended anyone to keep.

## The Strike Itself

With the community described, the event can be narrated, and it is worth following closely because the sequence is more interesting than the summary.

### Dating the stoppage

The strike took place in the twenty-ninth regnal year of Ramesses III, in the second month of the growing season, conventionally dated to around 1157 BCE. It is the earliest documented work stoppage in recorded history, and the record of it was written by a village scribe as the events unfolded.

### The document that records it

The Turin Strike Papyrus is the document recording the events, written by the scribe Amennakht of Deir el-Medina and preserved in the Egyptian collection at Turin. It gives a dated, sequential account of the stoppages, the workmen's movements, their statements, and the responses of officials, making it the earliest surviving narrative of industrial action.

The document's character deserves comment before its content. This is not a monument, a royal inscription, or a piece of official propaganda. It is an administrative record kept by a working scribe for internal purposes, recording what happened because recording what happened was his job. That makes it a very different kind of evidence from the temple walls that supply most Egyptian history, and a considerably more candid one.

### The sequence of events

Rations had failed to arrive. The papyrus records that they were roughly eighteen days overdue, which for a community with no alternative food source was a serious matter rather than a bureaucratic irritation.

The crew responded by leaving the work site. They passed the walls of a royal mortuary temple on the west bank and sat down behind it. The choice of location was deliberate and it was clever. Mortuary temples were operating institutions with staff, stores, and revenues, functioning as economic centers as well as religious ones. Sitting in one meant occupying the place where the grain actually was, in front of the people whose job it was to distribute it, in a manner that could not be ignored.

Officials came out to speak with them. The workmen stated their grievance and did not move. Over the following days the crew relocated to other temples in the necropolis, including the great mortuary temple of Ramesses II, continuing the demonstration. The papyrus records them calling out that they were hungry, and stating that they had come to this place because of hunger and thirst, with no clothing, no oil, no fish, and no vegetables.

The escalation continued. The workmen made clear that they would take their case to higher authority, and at one point the record indicates they went to the necropolis administration and pressed their case directly. The chief of police became involved. Officials attempted to persuade them to return with promises. Partial deliveries were made.

The dispute was not settled cleanly. Rations came, then failed again, and further stoppages followed within the same year and in subsequent years, with the pattern recurring into the following reigns. What began as a single incident turned out to be the first instance of a recurring problem.

### How the dispute ended

It was resolved by partial payment rather than by any structural fix. Officials negotiated, deliveries were made, and the men returned to work. Because the underlying supply failure was never corrected, stoppages recurred repeatedly over the following years and into later reigns, making the first strike the beginning of a pattern rather than an isolated episode.

### Once, or a pattern?

Yes. The twenty-ninth year of Ramesses III produced multiple stoppages, and the records document further actions in subsequent years and reigns as ration deliveries continued to fail. The recurrence is significant evidence: a single incident might reflect an unusual accident, while a repeating pattern indicates a systemic breakdown in state provisioning.

## Why the Rations Failed

Explaining the cause matters because the strike is often presented as a story about worker militancy when it is at least as much a story about state failure.

### The immediate trigger and the deeper causes

They struck because their grain rations were roughly eighteen days overdue and their households had no other source of food. The village had no farmland and no well, so state deliveries were the only supply. The immediate cause was a failure of provisioning; the underlying causes were administrative breakdown and economic strain in the later Twentieth Dynasty.

Several pressures converged. Egypt in this period had lost its Levantine empire and the tribute that came with it, following the upheavals examined in the account of [the Sea Peoples and the Late Bronze Age Collapse](/2005/07/01/sea-peoples-bronze-age-collapse/). Grain prices recorded in the village documents rose substantially across the later Twentieth Dynasty, with fluctuations indicating genuine scarcity rather than ordinary variation. The temple estates from which the necropolis workers were supplied were themselves under pressure. And the administrative machinery that moved grain from granary to village had multiple points at which it could fail, including simple corruption, which the documents elsewhere show was a live problem.

There is no evidence that anyone deliberately withheld the rations to discipline the workforce. The picture is of a system under strain failing to perform a routine function, with the consequences landing on the people least able to absorb them.

### The wider economic context

The strike belongs to a period whose economic difficulties are unusually well documented for the ancient world, precisely because Deir el-Medina kept records.

Grain price data from the village ostraca allow price movements to be tracked across decades, and they show a substantial rise concentrated in the later Twentieth Dynasty. Interpretation requires care, since the records measure exchange values in a non-monetary economy and the sample is uneven, but the general direction is not in doubt.

Alongside price inflation came the phenomenon that would eventually destroy the necropolis: systematic robbery of the royal tombs, documented in a body of legal papyri recording investigations, confessions, and trials, with evidence of official complicity reaching high into the administration. The relationship between unpaid workers with intimate knowledge of tomb locations and the plundering of those tombs is not a coincidence, and the fuller treatment of that connection belongs to the account of [tomb robbery and the fall of the New Kingdom](/2005/09/01/tomb-robbery-fall-of-new-kingdom/).

The overall trajectory of the dynasty, its declining royal authority, and the rise of the Theban priesthood are set out in the overview of [Ramesside Egypt and the age of the Ramesses kings](/2005/01/01/ramesside-egypt-complete-guide/), which is the pillar this article sits beneath. The strike is one symptom of that decline, visible earlier and more clearly than most because the people it affected wrote things down.

## What the Strike Actually Demonstrates

The interpretive question is what the episode proves about Egyptian society, and it is easy to get wrong in both directions.

### Stating the claim precisely

The first documented labor strike occurred in Egypt around 1157 BCE, when the royal tomb workers of Deir el-Medina stopped work and occupied mortuary temples in protest at grain rations that were roughly eighteen days overdue. The events were recorded contemporaneously by a village scribe on the papyrus now held in Turin.

### The wages-as-obligation thesis

The core claim is that the workmen understood their rations as an entitlement rather than as a benevolence, and the evidence for it is embedded in how they behaved.

They did not petition humbly and wait. They withdrew their labor, which is an act that presupposes the labor was being exchanged for something. They occupied economic institutions rather than symbolic ones, which indicates a clear understanding of where the obligation sat and who could discharge it. They stated their grievance in terms of what they lacked rather than in terms of royal mercy. They repeated the action when the failure repeated. And they were not, so far as the record shows, punished for any of it.

That last point carries considerable weight. A state that regarded a work stoppage as rebellion had ample means to respond with force, and Egyptian administration was not squeamish. Instead officials negotiated, made partial payments, and brought the men back to work. The response treats the grievance as legitimate and the remedy as payment, which is precisely how one would expect a contractual obligation to be handled.

### Was this a labor movement or something narrower?

It shows that Egyptian workers held a clear conception of rations as an owed wage and believed that non-delivery justified collective action. The state's response, negotiating and paying rather than punishing, indicates that officials shared this understanding at least in practice. It does not show a legal right to strike or any institution resembling a union.

That distinction is where careful treatment separates from careless. There was no Egyptian law establishing a right to withhold labor. There was no organization representing workers as a class, no collective bargaining framework, no strike fund, and no ideology of labor solidarity extending beyond the community itself. The workmen were not asserting a principle applicable to Egyptian workers generally. They were asserting that they, specifically, had not been paid.

What the episode demonstrates is something more modest and more interesting than a proto-union: the existence of a widely shared and practically enforced understanding that the state owed its workers, that the debt was real, and that failure to pay it entitled the unpaid to act. That understanding was customary rather than codified, and its enforcement depended on the leverage the workmen happened to hold. But it existed, it was mutual, and it was strong enough that the administration honored it under pressure.

## The Texture of Daily Life

Because the strike is inseparable from the community that carried it out, and because that community is the best-documented working population in the ancient world, the rest of this guide reconstructs how these people actually lived.

### Inside a village house

Houses were long and narrow, built of mudbrick on stone footings and sharing walls along a central street. A typical dwelling ran through four or five spaces in sequence: an entrance room, a main living room with a raised platform and a column supporting the roof, one or two smaller rooms behind, and a kitchen at the rear, open to the sky, with a stairway to the roof.

The entrance room is the most discussed feature. Many houses contain in this space a plastered brick structure raised above floor level, enclosed on three sides and reached by steps, painted in some cases with figures associated with fertility and childbirth. Its function has been argued over extensively. Proposals include a birthing bed, a space for a woman's confinement after delivery, a household shrine, and an altar for domestic cult. The painted decoration supports a connection with fertility and protection, but no text explains it, and honest treatments present the interpretation as unresolved.

The main room served as the household's principal space, with a raised bench along one wall for sitting and sleeping and, in many houses, a niche or false door associated with the cult of household and ancestral spirits. Behind lay storage and sleeping rooms, with cellars beneath some floors. The rear kitchen held grinding equipment, an oven, and storage jars, and the roof was usable space for sleeping in hot weather and for storage.

Houses were small by any standard, and the community was dense. Walls were shared, the street was narrow, and privacy in any modern sense was unavailable. That physical closeness is visible throughout the documentary record, which is full of disputes between neighbors, accusations, gossip, and litigation over small matters.

### Food and drink

Diet centered on bread and beer, both made from the grain that constituted the wage, with bread baked in domestic ovens and beer brewed at home. Beer in Egypt was a thick, low-alcohol staple closer to a liquid food than to a recreational drink, and it supplied a meaningful share of daily calories.

Beyond the staples, the state supplied fish in regular deliveries, brought up from the river by assigned personnel, along with vegetables. Dates, figs, and other fruit appear. Meat was occasional rather than routine, issued as a festival bonus or acquired through trade, and its presence in the records generally marks a special occasion. Oil for cooking, lighting, and personal use was part of the delivery schedule, and its absence is one of the complaints recorded during the strike.

The internal market handled everything else. Villagers bought, sold, lent, and bartered constantly, and the surviving accounts record transactions in linen, furniture, livestock, tools, coffins, and services, valued against grain and against copper measured by weight.

### Health and the body

Skeletal remains from the village cemetery, combined with the written record of illness and treatment, give an unusually complete picture of what the work did to the people who performed it.

The physical evidence shows the signature of heavy manual labor: degenerative joint disease, spinal problems, and the wear patterns associated with repetitive strain and with carrying loads. Cutting rock in confined spaces with hand tools, often in awkward postures and poor air, was punishing work, and the bodies record it. Life expectancy was low by modern standards, with high infant and child mortality, though those who survived childhood could reach a considerable age.

The written record adds a dimension the bones cannot. Workmen reported sick and were recorded as absent for illness without apparent penalty. A physician served the community, supported by the state, and the documents mention treatment and the supply of medical materials. Eye complaints appear frequently, which is unsurprising for men working by lamplight in dusty enclosed spaces. Scorpion stings recur.

The existence of state-supported medical care and paid sick leave for a working community in the twelfth century BCE is one of the details that most surprises readers, and it belongs squarely within the wages-as-obligation argument. A state that pays a man while he is too ill to work has accepted an obligation extending beyond the transaction of labor for grain.

### Family, marriage, and the life course

Households were nuclear in structure, typically a couple with children, sometimes with an elderly parent or an unmarried relative attached. Marriage appears to have been a matter of establishing a household rather than of any recorded ceremony, and the documents treat it as a social and economic fact rather than a legal event requiring registration.

Property arrangements are documented in detail because they generated disputes. Property brought into a marriage remained identifiable, and division on divorce or death followed rules the community understood and litigated over. Wills and transfers appear in the record, including cases where a woman disposed of property in her own name.

Children entered the workforce through their fathers, with sons inheriting positions on the gang. The documents record the training of the next generation and the negotiation of places, and the hereditary character of the community is visible across the genealogies that can be reconstructed from the names.

Death was a major economic event as well as a personal one. These were people whose profession was building tombs, and they built their own on the slope above the village, small chapels with pyramidal superstructures over decorated burial chambers. The quality of their private tombs is high, since the men who decorated them were the same men who decorated the tombs of kings, working for themselves.

### Women as property holders and litigants

Women in the village could own, inherit, buy, and sell property in their own names, bring cases before the local court, testify, and initiate divorce. They ran households, engaged in trade, made loans, and appear in the documents as economic actors rather than as dependents, though they held no positions on the work gang itself.

The legal capacity of Egyptian women was genuinely unusual by the standards of the ancient Mediterranean, and Deir el-Medina supplies the best documentation of it in practice rather than in theory. Women appear as plaintiffs and defendants, as parties to sales, as creditors, and as testators. A woman could hold property independently of her husband and dispose of it as she chose.

The limits should be stated with equal clarity. Women did not hold positions in the crew and did not receive the workman's ration in their own right, though widows received support. The formal administrative and scribal roles were male. Economic independence coexisted with exclusion from the institutional structures that generated income and authority.

Women's work in the village included spinning and weaving, food preparation and brewing, and the management of household resources, which in a community whose wage arrived as raw grain was substantial and skilled labor. The conversion of a monthly grain delivery into daily bread and beer was itself a significant productive task, and it was performed by women.

One female role in the community stands out. The documents refer to a wise woman consulted on matters including illness, misfortune, and the causes of trouble, whose function appears to have combined divination and counsel. The role is attested but thinly described, and reconstructions of what she actually did go beyond the evidence.

### Religion in the village

The community's religious life was intense, local, and unusually well documented, and it centered on figures with a direct connection to the villagers' own situation.

Their principal patrons were Amenhotep I, an early Eighteenth Dynasty king credited in local tradition with founding the settlement, and his mother Ahmose-Nefertari. Both received cult in the village across centuries, with festivals, chapels, and processions. A community venerating a long-dead king and queen as its own founders is a community with a strong institutional identity and a long memory.

The goddess Meretseger, whose name carries the sense of one who loves silence, was associated with the peak that dominates the necropolis and was invoked as a protector of the region and a punisher of wrongdoing. Texts from the village record personal appeals to her, including expressions of contrition from individuals who believed they had been afflicted for a transgression.

These personal religious texts are among the most valuable material the site produced, because they show ordinary people describing their own relationship to a deity in their own terms, which the monumental record never does. They present a religion of personal responsibility, divine attention to individual conduct, illness understood as consequence, and appeal for mercy, and they are the best evidence available for what Egyptian religion felt like from below rather than from the temple.

Household religion operated alongside the public cults, focused on ancestors, on protective figures associated with childbirth and the home, and on the niches and false doors built into the houses themselves.

## Law, Disputes, and the State's Reach

The village had its own machinery for settling arguments, and watching it operate tells us how Egyptian justice worked at ground level rather than in theory.

### The local court

Disputes were heard by a court composed of members of the community itself: the foremen, the scribe, and senior workmen, sitting to hear cases brought by villagers against one another. Its jurisdiction covered debts, property, sales gone wrong, inheritance, accusations of theft, and personal grievances. Serious matters and cases involving the state went to the vizier.

The proceedings recorded in the ostraca have a strikingly informal quality. Parties spoke, witnesses were called, oaths were sworn in the name of the king and the gods, and judgments were given. Cases could drag on across years, with the same dispute reappearing, which suggests enforcement was imperfect and that a judgment did not always settle matters.

What the court demonstrates is a community handling its own affairs through recognized procedure without external supervision in most cases. That capacity for self-management is relevant to the strike, since a population accustomed to organizing hearings, recording decisions, and holding one another to obligations is a population equipped to organize a collective action.

### Oracles

Alongside the court, the villagers used oracular consultation to settle questions. The procedure involved the statue of the deified Amenhotep I, carried in procession by bearers on festival days, which answered questions put to it by moving forward or backward, or by responding to written alternatives placed before it.

Oracles were used for genuine disputes, including questions of ownership, accusations of theft, and matters where evidence was contested. The record treats the outcomes as binding, and cases appear in which an oracular judgment settled a matter the court had not resolved.

Modern readers reach immediately for the question of manipulation, and it is a reasonable question, since the statue was carried by men who had views. The documents do not settle it. What they do show is that the procedure was taken seriously, used repeatedly, and accepted as an authoritative mechanism, which makes it a functioning legal institution regardless of what was happening at the level of the bearers' shoulders.

### Crime and its handling

The documents record theft within the community, adultery, assault, and disputes escalating into violence. They also record accusations against officials, including complaints about a foreman who was accused of misappropriating goods and abusing his position, which led to proceedings against him.

That last category is significant. A community in which an ordinary workman could bring charges against his own foreman and have them heard is a community with functioning internal accountability. The specific case, which involved a long list of allegations compiled and pressed by villagers, shows the mechanism being used against a person with power over the accusers.

### The state's presence

The vizier's authority over the necropolis was real and exercised. Vizierial visits are recorded, along with instructions issued, appointments made, and disputes adjudicated. The chief of police and the necropolis administration maintained a presence. Deliveries were made by external personnel on a schedule the state set.

What is absent from the record is routine coercion. There is no evidence of guards compelling attendance, of physical punishment for absence, or of the apparatus one would expect around forced labor. Supervision existed, and it was administrative rather than custodial. The men were employees of the state, subject to its authority, and free within the terms of that relationship in ways the popular image of ancient labor does not accommodate.

## What the Evidence Shows and What It Hides

Deir el-Medina is the richest source available for ancient daily life, and its richness creates a specific analytical trap that deserves naming.

### The exceptionality problem

This village was not typical of anything. It was purpose-built, state-supported, unusually literate, physically isolated, occupationally specialized, and hereditary. Its members were skilled craftsmen at the top of their trades working on the most prestigious project in the country, paid reliably for most of their history, provided with medical care, and permitted a degree of self-management.

The overwhelming majority of Egyptians were agricultural laborers who could not read, who worked land they did not own, who paid taxes in produce and labor, and who left no written record of themselves whatsoever. Nothing about Deir el-Medina describes their lives.

The trap is that because the evidence for this village is so good, it inevitably supplies the mental image of ancient Egyptian daily life, and that image is drawn from the least representative community in the country. Every general statement about how Egyptians lived that rests on Deir el-Medina material is a statement about a state-supported elite artisan enclave.

### The survival bias within the site

Even within the village, the record is skewed by what happened to survive. Ostraca preserve the writing that was thrown away on durable material in dry conditions. Papyrus documents survive in far smaller numbers because papyrus was expensive, reused, and more fragile. Oral communication, which carried most of daily life, left nothing.

The documentary record is therefore weighted toward matters that generated writing: disputes, accounts, work records, and formal transactions. Ordinary contentment produces no paperwork. A community whose surviving archive is full of quarrels was not necessarily a quarrelsome community; it was a community whose quarrels required documentation.

### The chronological unevenness

The village was occupied for roughly four centuries, and the documentation is not evenly spread. The great bulk of surviving material comes from the Nineteenth and Twentieth Dynasties, with the earlier Eighteenth Dynasty phases far less well represented. Statements about the community that draw on the rich later material and apply it across the whole occupation are extending the evidence beyond its range.

### What is missing entirely

We do not know what the workmen thought about the kings whose tombs they cut. We do not know how they regarded the theology embedded in the decoration they painted. We have their letters and their lawsuits and almost nothing that could be called reflection.

We also lack the perspective of the least powerful people in the village. Servants and dependents attached to households appear in the documents as items in accounts rather than as voices. Children appear as names and as future workmen. The record is written by literate adult men, and it shows their world.

### How much can be built on the strike papyrus itself

The document that anchors this whole subject is a single account by a single scribe. It is detailed, contemporaneous, and administrative rather than rhetorical, which makes it a strong source. It is nonetheless one man's record, produced within an institutional role, describing events in which he was a participant with his own position in the hierarchy.

There is no independent corroborating narrative. Supporting evidence comes from the wider body of village documentation showing ration failures, price rises, and further stoppages, which establishes the context and the pattern. The specific sequence of days and statements rests on Amennakht.

That is a good evidential position by the standards of ancient history and it is not an unassailable one, and the difference matters for how confidently the episode should be described.

## Was This Really the First Strike?

The claim that Deir el-Medina produced the first strike in history is repeated everywhere, and it needs qualifying in two directions before it can be defended.

The first qualification concerns the word first. What the evidence supports is that this is the earliest documented strike, not the earliest strike. Organized labor withholding is not a complicated invention, and there is no reason to suppose that no group of workers anywhere had previously stopped work over non-payment. What makes Deir el-Medina exceptional is not the behavior but the record, and the record exists because this particular community happened to be literate and to write on material that survives. Earlier stoppages, if they occurred, produced no documentation or produced documentation that has not survived.

This is a distinction worth internalizing, because the pattern recurs constantly in ancient history. Claims of the form the first X in history almost always mean the earliest X for which evidence survives, and the difference between those two statements is the difference between a fact about the past and a fact about preservation.

The second qualification concerns the word strike. Applying a modern industrial term to a twelfth-century BCE ration dispute imports assumptions that do not fit. A modern strike typically involves an organized union, a formal dispute procedure, a defined bargaining relationship, and a legal framework establishing what is and is not permitted. None of that existed. What happened at Deir el-Medina was a collective withdrawal of labor by a specific workforce in pursuit of payment owed, sustained over days, using occupation of premises as leverage.

Strip away the institutional apparatus and that description matches what a strike fundamentally is. The term is defensible provided the modern furniture is not smuggled in with it, and the responsible formulation is that this is the earliest recorded instance of workers collectively withholding labor to enforce payment.

### What earlier evidence exists for labor disputes?

Very little, which is the point. Earlier Egyptian and Mesopotamian records document labor organization, ration schedules, and occasional absconding by workers, but no comparable account of a collective refusal survives. Flight was the more usual response to bad conditions, and running away is individual, unorganized, and available to anyone with legs.

A strike requires something else: a group able to act together and holding something the employer needs. Withholding labor collectively demands solidarity and leverage that most ancient workforces did not possess, which is why the response to hardship was normally departure rather than organized refusal.

Deir el-Medina had both. The community was small, hereditary, cohesive, and physically concentrated. Its skills were irreplaceable in the short term. And its members were literate enough to coordinate and to document. Those conditions are why the action was possible there and not in a village of agricultural laborers, and they explain the strike better than any appeal to unusual courage.

## The Strike in Comparative Perspective

Setting the episode against later labor history clarifies both what is genuinely comparable and what is not.

### What is comparable

The mechanics translate well. Workers withheld labor because payment failed. They acted collectively rather than individually. They chose their location for maximum pressure, occupying the institutions holding the resources. They escalated when initial responses were inadequate. They accepted partial settlement and returned to work. And the dispute recurred because the underlying problem was structural rather than incidental.

Any labor historian would recognize that sequence. The strategic logic of a strike does not depend on modern institutions; it depends on the withholding of something the other party needs, and that logic is available to any workforce with leverage in any period.

The response is also comparable. Officials negotiated rather than suppressing, made partial payments to end the immediate action, and did not address the structural cause. That pattern of buying peace without solving the problem is thoroughly familiar and explains why the stoppages recurred.

### What is not comparable

The absence of institutions is fundamental. Modern labor action operates within a framework of recognized organizations, legal protections, and established procedures built up through long political struggle. The Deir el-Medina workmen had a community, a grievance, and leverage. They had no organization outside the crew structure the state itself had created, no legal standing, and no conception of workers elsewhere as people with shared interests.

The ideological dimension is likewise absent. Modern labor movements were built on ideas about class, about the relationship between labor and value, and about political rights. Nothing of that kind appears in the Egyptian material. The workmen were not challenging the system that employed them or advancing a claim about how society should be organized. They were insisting that a specific arrangement be honored.

The employment relationship itself differed. These men were not free laborers selling their work in a market; they were state servants attached to a project, born into their positions, paid in kind, and unable to seek alternative employment. Their grievance was about performance of an obligation within a fixed relationship rather than about the terms of a negotiable contract.

### The useful conclusion from the comparison

What the comparison establishes is that the Deir el-Medina action was a real strike in the functional sense and not a proto-union in the institutional sense, and that conflating the two produces bad history in both directions. Treating it as merely a food riot understates the organization, the sustained duration, and the strategic use of location. Treating it as the birth of the labor movement overstates the ideology and the institutions and projects three thousand years of subsequent development backward onto a ration dispute.

## The Village's Wider Documentary Legacy

The strike is the most famous thing Deir el-Medina produced, and it is not the most important. The site's contribution to knowledge of the ancient world is broad enough to deserve separate treatment.

The absence records are the earliest known attendance registers of any workforce, listing men by name against days and reasons. They establish the working calendar, the tolerance for absence, and the range of circumstances that a state employer accepted as legitimate.

The price data allow the reconstruction of an ancient economy in unusual detail: what things cost relative to one another, how values moved over time, and how a non-monetary exchange system actually functioned in practice rather than in theory. Few periods before the classical world permit anything comparable.

The legal material documents Egyptian law as applied rather than as prescribed. Egypt left no law code of the Mesopotamian type, and what is known about Egyptian legal practice depends heavily on records of actual cases, of which Deir el-Medina supplies the largest concentration.

The literary ostraca preserve copies of Egyptian literary works made by villagers, sometimes as writing exercises, which has contributed to the recovery of texts that would otherwise be known only from fragmentary copies or not at all. A community of tomb decorators copying poetry onto discarded limestone has materially improved the surviving corpus of Egyptian literature.

The personal religious texts, discussed above, supply the best available evidence for lived religion at a non-elite level.

And the tombs of the villagers themselves, decorated by professionals for their own families, preserve painting of high quality in a private rather than a royal context, which allows comparison between what the same craftsmen produced for kings and what they produced for themselves.

## The End of the Village

Deir el-Medina did not survive the New Kingdom, and how it ended is part of the same story as the strike.

### Abandonment and relocation

The village was abandoned toward the close of the Twentieth Dynasty, around the end of the New Kingdom, as security in the Theban necropolis deteriorated. The community relocated within the fortified enclosure of the mortuary temple at Medinet Habu, and royal burial in the Valley of the Kings ceased, removing the reason for the settlement's existence.

The proximate cause was insecurity. The later Twentieth Dynasty saw incursions by Libyan groups into the Theban region, disorder in the necropolis, and the systematic tomb robbery documented in the legal papyri. An unwalled or lightly walled village in an isolated valley was not defensible, and the population moved behind the massive enclosure wall of a mortuary temple that was.

The underlying cause was the end of the institution the village served. Royal burial in the Valley of the Kings stopped. The kings of the following period were buried in the north, and the Theban necropolis ceased to be an active royal project. A community of specialist royal tomb builders has no function once kings stop building royal tombs there, and the crew dissolved into the general population.

There is a final chapter that closes the circle rather grimly. The same priests who administered Thebes in the following period systematically entered the royal tombs, removed the bodies of the kings, stripped the burials of their valuables for state use, and reburied the mummies in caches. The men who had cut those tombs were gone, and the tombs themselves were emptied by the authorities.

## The Ration Arithmetic

Because the strike was fundamentally about grain, it is worth working through what the wage actually amounted to and how a household converted it into food.

Egyptian grain was measured in units conventionally rendered as the sack and its subdivisions, and the ration schedule assigned quantities by rank. The commonly cited figures give an ordinary workman a monthly allowance of several sacks of emmer wheat together with a smaller quantity of barley, with foremen and the scribe receiving a substantially larger allocation. The exact figures vary across the records and across periods, and any single number quoted as the ration is a simplification of a schedule that moved.

The two grains had different destinations. Emmer was the bread grain, processed by grinding, sieving, mixing, and baking into loaves. Barley was primarily the beer grain, converted through a process involving lightly baked loaves, mashing, and fermentation into the thick, nutritious, low-alcohol beer that Egyptians drank in quantity. A household therefore received not food but the raw materials of food, and the conversion required substantial daily labor, performed by the women of the household.

The quantities were generous relative to subsistence, which is the crucial economic point. A workman's allocation exceeded what his household needed to eat, and the surplus was the mechanism by which the villagers acquired everything the state did not supply. Furniture, coffins, clothing, livestock, services, and luxuries were bought with grain, and the extensive documentation of sales and loans shows the surplus circulating constantly within the community.

That surplus also explains the severity of an eighteen-day delay. A household with a margin can absorb a short interruption. A community whose entire economy, including its debts and its ongoing transactions, was denominated in a commodity that had stopped arriving faced a cascade: no food, no means of purchase, and no ability to meet obligations already entered into. The strike was not only about hunger, though hunger is what the men shouted. It was about the collapse of the medium in which the whole community's economic life was conducted.

Payments were made by external personnel drawn from the granaries and temple estates rather than by the village's own supervisors, and the deliveries were recorded. This division is worth noting because it means that when rations failed, the foremen and scribe of the crew were on the same side of the dispute as the workmen. The strike was not a confrontation between labor and local management; it was a confrontation between an entire community and a distant administrative system that had stopped functioning.

## The Politics of the Village

A community of a few hundred people living in shared-wall houses, holding hereditary positions, and competing for a limited number of senior posts generates politics, and the documents record them in detail.

The two foremanships and the scribal post were the positions of consequence. They carried larger rations, control over work assignments, influence over the distribution of state-supplied goods, and standing in the local court. They also tended to remain within particular families across generations, which produced both continuity and resentment.

One case dominates any discussion of village politics. A foreman of the Nineteenth Dynasty named Paneb is the subject of an extended document compiling accusations against him, brought by a member of the family whose claim to the foremanship he had displaced. The charges are numerous and severe, covering theft of state property and materials from royal tombs, misuse of crew labor for his own purposes, violence and intimidation, sexual misconduct with women of the village, and corrupt acquisition of his office.

The document requires careful handling, and it is a good exercise in source criticism. It is an accusation, not a verdict. It was compiled by a man with a direct interest in Paneb's removal and a family claim to his position. Its charges are unverified, its rhetoric is hostile, and the outcome of the case is not recorded in what survives.

What it establishes regardless of Paneb's guilt is that the mechanism existed: a villager could compile detailed charges against the most powerful man in his community, address them to higher authority, and have them entered into the record. That is a functioning accountability procedure, and its existence tells us something about the community's expectations even if the specific case cannot be resolved.

The scribal families produced the other pole of village authority. The office of scribe of the tomb carried responsibility for the records on which rations, work assignments, and legal proceedings all depended, and control of documentation is control of a great deal. Several generations of one scribal family appear in the record, and members of it were literary figures as well as administrators, composing texts that circulated beyond the village. The scribe who recorded the strike belonged to this world: an educated, senior, well-connected member of the community whose account is therefore both exceptionally informed and entirely internal.

Beneath the senior posts, ordinary workmen competed over house allocations, over places on the crew for their sons, over debts, and over reputation. The court records and the letters are full of it. This was a small, intense, hereditary community in a confined space, and the documents preserve its frictions with unusual completeness because writing material was free.

## Tools, Techniques, and Conditions

The physical work these men performed shaped their bodies, their calendar, and ultimately their grievance, and it can be described in some detail.

Excavation of a royal tomb began with the establishment of the entrance and the axis, laid out by measurement, after which the crew cut inward and downward through the limestone. Stonecutters worked the face with copper and later bronze chisels struck by wooden mallets, supplemented by heavy stone pounders for rough removal and by wedges for splitting along planes. Copper dulls quickly against limestone, so tools were issued from state stores, returned, weighed to assess metal loss, resharpened, and reissued, with the whole cycle recorded by the scribe.

Waste rock had to be carried out in baskets, an unending task that consumed a large share of the labor as the tomb went deeper. Lighting came from lamps burning oil with twisted wicks, and Egyptian practice included adding salt to the fuel to reduce smoke, a necessary refinement in a sealed corridor where the air was already thick with limestone dust.

Once the chambers were cut, the finishing sequence began. Plasterers covered the rough rock where the stone was too poor to carve, using a coarse layer to level and a fine layer to receive decoration. Draftsmen laid out the scenes using grids and guidelines and drew the outlines. Sculptors carved relief where the stone permitted. Painters applied the colors, and final outlining sharpened the forms.

Each of these was a distinct trade with its own training, and the gang contained all of them. The division into a right side and a left side allowed the two halves of a corridor or chamber to be worked simultaneously, which is why the organizational structure took the form it did: it mirrored the physical shape of the job.

The conditions were hard in ways the skeletal evidence confirms. Dust, poor light, restricted postures, heavy loads, and repetitive strain produced the joint and spinal damage visible in the remains. Eye complaints recur in the absence records. Accidents happened. And the men spent eight days out of every ten away from their families, living in stone huts on the ridge above the valley.

Set against those conditions, the terms of employment look considerably better than the popular image of ancient labor allows: reliable pay well above subsistence, medical support, tolerated absence, a substantial holiday calendar, hereditary security of employment, and a community with legal and social institutions of its own. That combination is what makes the failure of the ration system so significant. The state had established a relationship with real terms, and then it stopped meeting them.

## Reading the Ostraca

The material that makes this community knowable deserves description in its own right, because the nature of the evidence shapes everything that can be said from it.

An ostracon is a flake of limestone or a fragment of pottery used as a writing surface. The Theban hills shed limestone chips constantly, and tomb excavation produced them in industrial quantities, so the villagers had an unlimited supply of free writing material lying at their feet. Papyrus, by contrast, was manufactured, expensive, and reserved for documents that mattered enough to justify the cost.

The economics of that distinction produced the archive. Because ostraca were free, people wrote on them without hesitation: work registers, letters, shopping lists, contracts, receipts, sketches, practice exercises, complaints, and copies of literary works. Because they were disposable, people threw them away. Because they were stone and pottery in a desert, they survived. Tens of thousands have been recovered, principally from rubbish deposits and from a great pit near the village.

The script is hieratic, the cursive form of Egyptian writing used for everyday purposes, and reading it is a specialist skill. Hands vary enormously in quality, from the practiced fluency of professional scribes to the labored efforts of men writing with difficulty, and that variation is itself evidence about the distribution of literacy in the community.

Several limitations attach to the material. Ostraca are frequently broken, so texts break off. They are rarely dated in a way that permits precise placement, so building chronologies requires prosopography, the tracing of named individuals across documents to establish who was contemporary with whom. And because they were discarded, they arrive without context: a rubbish heap preserves what was thrown away without recording when or by whom.

The methodological consequence is that the picture of Deir el-Medina is assembled from tens of thousands of fragments by cross-referencing names, titles, and internal references, and that the resulting reconstruction is a genuine achievement of scholarship rather than something the evidence delivers ready-made. Where accounts of the village state facts confidently, those facts generally rest on a long chain of inference from scattered scraps.

## How the Site Came to Be Known

The recovery of Deir el-Medina has its own history, and knowing it helps explain why the evidence is shaped the way it is.

The site attracted attention early because tombs on the hillside were visible and productive, and nineteenth-century activity in the area was directed at those tombs rather than at the settlement. Objects and papyri left the site through the antiquities trade during this period, which is why documents from a single community are now dispersed across museums in several countries, including the strike papyrus itself, held in Turin.

That dispersal has consequences that are still being worked through. Fragments of a single document can sit in different collections. Reconstructing texts requires collaboration across institutions, and material that entered the market without recorded provenance cannot be placed within the village stratigraphy.

Systematic excavation of the settlement came later, most extensively through a long-running French mission that cleared the village, the cemetery, the chapels, and the rubbish deposits over a period of decades. That work produced the plans of the houses, the stratigraphic sequence, and the great bulk of the ostraca now available.

Publication has been a very long project, and a substantial quantity of material recovered from the site has been slow to appear in print. This is not unusual in archaeology and it does mean that the picture continues to develop, with new readings and newly published texts modifying details of the established account.

## The Reign of Ramesses III From Below

The strike offers something rare in Egyptian history: a view of a famous reign from the perspective of people at the bottom of it.

The official version of Ramesses III's reign is inscribed on the walls of his mortuary temple. It presents a king who defeated Libyan invasions, destroyed a great seaborne coalition in linked land and naval battles, maintained order, and endowed the temples of Egypt with enormous benefactions recorded in an exceptionally long administrative papyrus produced after his death. On that evidence the reign is a success, and the king is the last great ruler of the New Kingdom.

The Deir el-Medina material describes the same reign differently. In its twenty-ninth year the state could not deliver grain to the men cutting the king's own tomb. Prices were rising. Deliveries were unreliable. The stoppage recurred. And the reign ended with the king killed in a conspiracy originating in his own household, an event documented in the trial records of the conspirators.

Neither account is false. Both are Egyptian, both are contemporary, and the difference between them is a difference of vantage point and genre. Monumental inscription reports what the institution of kingship required to be reported. Administrative records report what happened to the grain.

Holding the two together is the historian's job, and the result is a considerably better understanding of the period than either supplies alone: a state still capable of winning its battles and no longer capable of paying its workers, presenting triumph on its temple walls while its provisioning system failed behind them.

This is also why the strike deserves the attention it receives. Not because a work stoppage in a village of a few hundred people changed the course of Egyptian history, which it did not, but because it is one of the very few points at which the ancient record lets us hear from the people the monuments were built by rather than from the people they were built for.

## The Chain of Supply and Where It Broke

Tracing the route grain took from the field to a workman's household identifies the points at which the system could fail, which is the most direct way to understand the strike's cause.

At the top sat the vizier, the senior official of the Egyptian administration, responsible among much else for the royal tomb and its workforce. The vizier issued instructions, appointed to positions, adjudicated disputes, and periodically visited. He did not personally handle grain.

Beneath him operated the necropolis administration, a body of officials responsible for the west bank establishment as a whole, including security, supply, and the coordination of the various institutions on that side of the river. The chief of police of the necropolis, who appears in the strike account, belonged to this level.

The grain itself came from institutional estates: the granaries of the great mortuary temples and other state holdings, whose agricultural production supported the necropolis operation. These were substantial economic enterprises with their own personnel, land, and records, and the workmen's rations were a charge against their output.

Delivery was performed by assigned personnel who brought the grain, along with water carriers, fishermen, woodcutters, potters, and gardeners attached to the village's support. These people were not members of the crew. They constituted a separate service establishment whose job was to keep the specialists supplied so that the specialists could work.

At the receiving end, the scribe of the tomb recorded what arrived and the foremen distributed it according to the schedule.

The chain therefore ran through at least four institutional levels, each with its own records, personnel, and competing obligations. Failure at any point produced the same outcome at the village: no grain. And the workmen, standing at the end of the chain, had no way of knowing which link had broken.

That structural fact explains their tactic. Occupying the mortuary temple precincts placed them at the institutional level where the grain actually sat, in front of the officials who could authorize its release, bypassing the intermediaries who had failed to deliver it. It was not a symbolic protest at a palace gate. It was a physical relocation to the granary.

The wider question of why the estates were failing to produce or release the grain belongs to the economic history of the period. Contributing factors included the loss of imperial tribute, the permanent transfer of an increasing share of agricultural land to temple endowments, administrative corruption documented elsewhere in the same body of records, and the general strain examined in the treatment of [tomb robbery and the fall of the New Kingdom](/2005/09/01/tomb-robbery-fall-of-new-kingdom/).

## What the Strikers Said and How to Read It

The papyrus preserves the substance of what the workmen stated during the dispute, and the character of those statements supports the argument of this guide.

Their case was made in terms of deprivation and entitlement rather than of appeal to mercy. They described what they lacked: food, oil, fish, vegetables, and clothing, the specific components of the ration schedule. They stated that hunger and thirst had brought them to the place they were occupying. They referred to the length of the delay.

Two features of this are worth drawing out.

The first is the specificity. The men did not complain of general hardship; they enumerated categories of supply that were contractually due and had not arrived. That is the language of an accounting grievance, and it presupposes a schedule both parties recognized.

The second is the absence of deference formulae. Egyptian communication upward was normally elaborate, with extended expressions of humility and praise. The recorded statements of the strikers are direct. They are addressed to officials by men asserting a claim, not by petitioners hoping for favor.

A necessary caution attaches to all of this. The papyrus is a scribe's record, written in his own words as a summary of what occurred, not a transcript. What survives is Amennakht's rendering of the workmen's position, produced by a senior member of their own community who shared their interest in the grain arriving. It is a sympathetic account from inside, which makes it credible about the substance and unreliable as a verbatim record of anyone's speech. No responsible treatment should present quoted words from this episode as the actual sentences spoken.

The escalation is also informative. The men made clear their willingness to take the matter higher, and the account indicates they pressed their case with the necropolis authorities directly rather than waiting for a response to come to them. That readiness to go over the heads of the immediate officials, in a society organized around hierarchy, is a further sign that they regarded their claim as securely grounded.

## Common Misconceptions

Several claims about this episode circulate widely enough to deserve direct correction.

That the strikers were slaves rebelling. They were paid state employees with property, legal standing, and hereditary positions, and the entire logic of the episode depends on their being wage earners.

That the strike was the birth of the labor movement. There was no organization beyond the state-created crew, no ideology, no claim about workers generally, and no successor institution. It was a specific workforce enforcing a specific debt.

That the workmen were punished. Nothing in the record indicates reprisal. Officials negotiated and paid.

That the strike succeeded. It obtained payment and changed nothing structural, which is why it recurred repeatedly over the following years and reigns.

That it was a single event. Multiple stoppages occurred within the same regnal year and further actions followed in later reigns.

That Deir el-Medina shows how ordinary Egyptians lived. It shows how an unusually privileged, literate, state-supported artisan community lived, which is close to the opposite of ordinary.

That the exact words of the strikers survive. A scribe's summary survives. Treating his rendering as transcription overstates what the document is.

And that this was beyond question the first strike ever to occur. It is the earliest for which evidence survives, which is a claim about preservation rather than about human behavior.

## Workers' Settlements Across Egyptian History

Deir el-Medina was not the only community of its kind, and the comparisons available establish that state-provisioned worker settlements were a standing Egyptian institution rather than a Ramesside innovation.

At Giza, excavation south of the pyramid plateau has revealed a substantial settlement associated with the Fourth Dynasty pyramid projects, including bakeries capable of production on an industrial scale, breweries, storage facilities, and structures interpreted as barracks or galleries for accommodating workers. Faunal remains indicate the delivery of cattle, sheep, and goats in quantity, which means the workforce was eating meat regularly. A cemetery of workers has also been identified, containing burials with grave goods and, in some cases, evidence of healed fractures and of medical intervention including set bones.

The Giza material is the single most effective refutation of the enslaved-masses image, and it predates Deir el-Medina by more than a millennium. A state that maintains bakeries, breweries, meat supply, medical treatment, and burial provision for its construction workforce is operating an employment system, not a slave camp.

At Lahun, a Middle Kingdom town associated with a pyramid complex has been excavated, showing a planned settlement with a clear division between larger and smaller housing and producing a substantial body of papyri covering administration, medicine, law, and daily affairs. It demonstrates that purpose-built, state-planned settlements for project workforces were already standard practice a thousand years before the Ramesside period.

At Amarna, a workmen's village serving the royal tomb workforce of Akhenaten's short-lived capital has been excavated. Its position, layout, and function parallel Deir el-Medina closely enough that the two can be compared directly, and the resemblance indicates a standing institutional model applied wherever a royal tomb project required a resident specialist crew.

Placed in this series, Deir el-Medina looks less like a unique phenomenon and more like the best-documented example of a recurring arrangement. That reframing strengthens rather than weakens the argument of this guide, because it means the employment relationship visible in the strike was not a peculiarity of one village but a version of how the Egyptian state normally organized skilled project labor.

What Deir el-Medina alone supplies is the writing. The other settlements give us buildings, bones, and bread ovens. Only Deir el-Medina gives us the workers' own documents, and only there can we hear a workforce state its grievance.

## What the Strike Changed

The honest answer to this question is: within Egypt, almost nothing.

No reform followed. The ration system continued to operate on the same basis and continued to fail. No new institution was created, no rule was written down, and no protection was established. The men were paid and went back to work, and when the grain failed again they walked out again. Measured by structural outcome, the first strike in history achieved a delivery of grain and no more.

The reason is worth stating because it is instructive about the limits of leverage without institutions. The workmen had power in the moment because their skills were irreplaceable and their work was urgent. They had no power over the system, because they had no standing within it beyond the crew and no means of converting a temporary advantage into a permanent arrangement. Each stoppage started from nothing, and each settlement resolved only the immediate arrears.

That pattern held until the village ceased to exist. The community's leverage disappeared entirely once royal burial in the Theban necropolis ended, which demonstrates how contingent its position had been. The workmen were strong only as long as the state needed exactly what they could do.

What the episode changed is our understanding rather than Egypt's arrangements. It established, for anyone willing to read the evidence, that ancient working people held concepts of entitlement, that they could organize, that they could articulate a claim, and that an ancient state could recognize and settle such a claim rather than crushing it. That is a substantial correction to a widespread picture of the ancient world, and it is the strike's real legacy.

## Studying This Topic

For readers approaching this material for coursework or examination, a few points of emphasis carry the most weight.

Name the document. An answer that cites the Turin Strike Papyrus and identifies the scribe Amennakht demonstrates command of the evidence rather than of a summary.

Date it properly. The twenty-ninth regnal year of Ramesses III, conventionally around 1157 BCE, with the qualification that Egyptian absolute dates carry a margin.

State the cause at two levels. Immediate: rations roughly eighteen days overdue in a community with no alternative supply. Underlying: economic strain in the later Twentieth Dynasty following the loss of empire and imperial tribute.

Explain the tactic. The occupation of mortuary temple precincts was strategic, placing the men at the institutions holding the grain rather than at a symbolic location.

Distinguish the claim from its exaggeration. Earliest recorded strike, not earliest strike; workers with a recognized entitlement, not a labor movement; a functional strike, not a proto-union.

Flag the exceptionality of the source community. Any general conclusion about Egyptian workers drawn from Deir el-Medina requires the qualification that this was the most privileged working population in the country.

And connect it outward. The strike belongs with rising grain prices, systematic tomb robbery, and the assassination of Ramesses III as evidence of a New Kingdom state in decline, and an answer that makes that connection is doing history rather than reciting an anecdote.

## The Festival Calendar and Time Away From Work

The generosity of the Egyptian working year surprises readers who expect relentless toil, and the documentation from the village allows it to be described concretely.

Beyond the two rest days in every ten, the crew observed a substantial calendar of festivals. Some were national occasions in the cults of the great gods. Others were local to Thebes, above all the festivals in which the divine images processed between temples on the two banks, drawing the whole west bank population into participation. Others still were specific to the community, centered on its own patron figures.

Festivals meant more than a day off. They involved distributions of food and drink, processions, music, and in some cases the oracular consultations through which disputes were settled. The state issued bonuses on royal occasions, and the records note the arrival of extra rations, meat, and other goods at such times.

Added to festivals were personal absences of the kind the registers record, and the aggregate can be considerable. Reconstructions of individual working years from the attendance documents show men present for well under the theoretical maximum, sometimes far under, without any indication that this was regarded as a problem requiring correction.

Two conclusions follow. The first is that the Egyptian state was buying a defined quantity of skilled output rather than a maximum quantity of time, and it accepted a working pattern that would strike a modern industrial manager as extraordinarily relaxed. The second is that the calendar itself was part of the compensation. A position on the royal tomb crew carried leisure as well as grain, and it was leisure the state deliberately provided.

Against that background, the failure of the rations reads differently again. The state had constructed a relationship generous in its terms and reliable across generations. When it stopped delivering, it was breaking an arrangement its own conduct had established as dependable, and the workmen's readiness to act reflects the confidence that a long-honored arrangement produces.

## Death and the Village Cemetery

The men who built tombs for kings built tombs for themselves, and the cemetery on the slope above the village is among the most informative parts of the site.

Village tombs consisted of a small courtyard, a chapel, and a shaft or passage descending to burial chambers, with many surmounted by a small brick pyramid. The chapels and burial chambers were decorated, and the decoration was executed by the same hands that worked in the Valley of the Kings.

The quality is high, which produces one of the more interesting comparisons available anywhere in Egyptian art. The same craftsmen can be studied working under royal commission and working for their own families, and the differences and continuities between the two bodies of work illuminate what was convention, what was direction, and what was individual preference.

The contents of these burials, where recovered, show households of some means: coffins, furniture, funerary equipment, personal possessions, and figurines intended to perform labor for the deceased in the next world. The presence of such equipment is itself a statement about status, since a proper burial was expensive and its provision indicates disposable resources.

The skeletal material from the cemetery supplies the physical record of the working life already described, and the demographic pattern it shows is the ordinary premodern one: high infant and child mortality, substantial risk in childbirth, and a meaningful number of individuals reaching advanced age.

There is a final observation the cemetery supports. These were people who spent their working lives on the machinery of Egyptian death and who applied it to themselves with evident conviction. Whatever they thought about the kings whose tombs they cut, and the record does not tell us, they believed in the system those tombs served enough to buy into it with their own resources.

## The Question of Coercion Revisited

One objection to the wages-as-obligation thesis deserves a direct answer, because a careful reader will raise it.

The objection runs as follows. The workmen could not leave. They were born into their positions, assigned to a state project, housed in a controlled settlement, dependent on state deliveries for food and water, and unable to seek other employment. Whatever the terms of their treatment, they were not free labor in any modern sense, and calling their rations a wage imports a market relationship that did not exist.

The objection is substantially correct as description and does not defeat the thesis. Two things can be true at once: the relationship was unfree in the sense that exit was not available, and the relationship carried terms that both parties recognized as binding.

Medieval and early modern history is full of arrangements of exactly this shape, in which people occupied hereditary positions they could not leave while holding customary rights their superiors were expected to honor and which they would defend when infringed. The absence of a labor market does not imply the absence of entitlement. It implies a different mechanism for establishing what is owed: custom and precedent rather than negotiated contract.

That is precisely what the Deir el-Medina evidence shows. The ration schedule was established practice, honored across generations, recorded in writing, and understood by everyone involved. Its authority came from having always been done, not from an agreement anyone had signed. When it failed, the workmen invoked the practice, and the officials responded by restoring it rather than by disputing its existence.

The thesis should therefore be stated with the qualification built in. The workmen understood their rations as an owed wage within a relationship they could not exit, and the obligation they asserted was customary rather than contractual. Stated that way it is defensible against the objection, and it is also a more historically precise claim than the version that quietly imports modern employment into the twelfth century BCE.

There is one further point the objection actually strengthens. If the men could not leave, then withholding labor was their only available instrument. Flight, the ordinary recourse of the discontented in the ancient world, was not really open to a hereditary specialist community in a controlled valley whose members had nowhere else to practice their trade. The strike was not one option among several. It was the single form of pressure the situation allowed, which makes its use a considered choice rather than an impulsive one.

## The Slavery Question

No treatment of Egyptian labor can avoid the assumption most readers arrive with, and Deir el-Medina is the best available place to address it because the evidence is so direct.

The workmen who cut the royal tombs were not slaves. They were state employees who received wages, held property, engaged in trade, brought lawsuits, took days off for family reasons, received medical care, passed their positions to their sons, decorated their own tombs, and on at least one well-documented occasion withheld their labor and were paid rather than punished. Every element of that list is incompatible with enslavement.

The broader picture of labor in Egypt requires more nuance than either the slavery image or its simple denial allows. Egyptian society did include unfree people. Prisoners of war were assigned to state and temple estates and their status was genuinely unfree. Household servants appear in the documents, including at Deir el-Medina, in positions of dependency, and some could be transferred between owners. Debt could produce forms of bondage.

Alongside this ran a system of compulsory labor obligation on the general population, under which people could be called up for state work projects for defined periods. That obligation was real, enforceable, and unwelcome, and evasion was punishable. It was also not slavery: those subject to it were free persons with property, families, and legal standing who owed a service, comparable to a tax paid in work rather than in grain.

The reason this matters for the strike is that the assumption of a slave workforce forecloses the entire question the episode raises. Slaves do not have wages to be owed. If Egyptian workers were chattel, the strike is unintelligible, and the wages-as-obligation thesis has nothing to explain. It is only because these men were paid employees with a recognized entitlement that their action makes sense, and their action is among the strongest pieces of evidence that they were.

The same correction applies to the monuments generally. The pyramids, the temples, and the royal tombs were built by organized workforces combining permanent state-supported specialists with seasonally conscripted labor, provisioned by an administration that recorded what it delivered. Evidence for the housing, feeding, and medical treatment of such workers has been recovered at multiple sites. The image of masses driven under the whip is a nineteenth-century construction with a literary rather than an archaeological pedigree, and the documentary record from Deir el-Medina is its most direct refutation.

## Why This Episode Endures

The strike has entered general awareness far beyond Egyptology, appearing in labor histories, in general accounts of the ancient world, and in popular writing, and it is worth asking why a ration dispute in a village of a few hundred people three thousand years ago should have that reach.

Part of the answer is priority. The earliest recorded anything attracts attention, and a documented strike predating the industrial revolution by more than three millennia is arresting on its face.

Part of it is recognizability. Most of what survives from the ancient world is alien: divine kingship, mortuary theology, iconographic conventions that require training to read. A group of workers who have not been paid, who stop work, who sit down in the place where the money is, and who shout that they are hungry requires no translation at all. The situation is immediately legible to anyone who has ever been owed wages.

Part of it is the reversal it performs. The dominant image of ancient Egypt is of pharaohs, gods, and monuments, with the people who built them present only as an anonymous mass. This episode inverts the frame. For a few days in the twenty-ninth year of a famous reign, the monuments recede and the builders speak, and what they say is about grain.

And part of it is the corrective it supplies. The assumption that ancient working people were passive, powerless, and without any conception of what they were owed is widespread and comfortable, and it makes the ancient world safely remote. The Deir el-Medina evidence dismantles it, not through argument but through documentation: attendance registers, sick leave, property deeds, lawsuits, and a scribe's account of men refusing to work until they were paid.

That is why the episode belongs in a general history of Egypt rather than in a footnote to it. It is the clearest surviving evidence that the relationship between an ancient state and the people who worked for it had terms, that those terms were known, and that ordinary people were prepared to enforce them.

## The Honest Picture

Several conclusions follow from the evidence, and stating them precisely is more useful than any celebration of the episode.

Before the conclusions, one framing note. This episode is small. A few hundred people in one valley withheld their labor over a delivery of grain, and the Egyptian state did not notice in any way that registered on its monuments. Its importance is entirely evidential: it is not that the strike mattered to Egypt, but that it survives to tell us what Egypt was like at a level the monuments never show.

The strike was real, it was organized, and it worked in the short term. The men achieved payment. They did not achieve reform, and the failure repeated, which is the most common outcome of industrial action in any period.

The workmen understood their rations as owed. The wages-as-obligation thesis is supported by their behavior, by their stated grievance, by the state's response, and by the surrounding documentation showing paid sick leave, tolerated absence, and a functioning system of recorded entitlement. This is the article's central claim and the evidence carries it.

The action was not a labor movement. There was no organization beyond the crew, no ideology, no conception of workers as a class, and no legal right being asserted. Reading modern labor politics into the episode distorts it.

Deir el-Medina was not typical. The community that struck was the most privileged working population in Egypt, and its capacity to act derived from advantages almost no other Egyptian workers possessed: irreplaceable skills, physical concentration, hereditary cohesion, and literacy. Concluding from this episode that Egyptian workers generally had leverage would be a serious error.

The episode is a symptom of decline. The strike happened because the New Kingdom state was failing to perform a routine function it had performed for centuries, in a period of lost empire, rising prices, and administrative breakdown. It belongs with the tomb robberies and the political disorder of the later Twentieth Dynasty as evidence of a system running down.

The community's leverage was contingent and temporary. It rested on irreplaceable skills, physical concentration, and a project the state urgently wanted finished, and it vanished entirely the moment royal burial in the Theban necropolis ceased. Power without an institution to hold it does not outlast the circumstances that created it.

And the record exists by accident. Everything known about this comes from a community that happened to be literate, that happened to write on limestone, and that happened to live somewhere dry. Innumerable comparable episodes elsewhere in the ancient world left nothing at all.

What the strike finally demonstrates is that the relationship between an ancient state and its workers was a relationship with terms, that the terms were understood on both sides, and that ordinary people were willing and able to enforce them. That is a more modest claim than the one usually made about this episode, and it is better supported and more interesting than the alternative. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the first labor strike in history?

The earliest documented labor strike took place in Egypt around 1157 BCE, in the twenty-ninth regnal year of Ramesses III, when the craftsmen who cut and decorated the royal tombs stopped work over grain rations that were roughly eighteen days overdue. They left the work site, occupied the precincts of royal mortuary temples on the Theban west bank, remained there across several days, and called out that they were hungry. A village scribe named Amennakht recorded the sequence of events as they happened, on the papyrus now held in Turin. Officials negotiated and made partial deliveries rather than punishing the men.

### Q: Who went on strike at Deir el-Medina?

The strikers were the specialist craftsmen of the royal tomb crew, titled Servants in the Place of Truth: stonecutters, plasterers, draftsmen, sculptors, and painters who excavated and decorated the tombs in the Valley of the Kings and the Valley of the Queens. They were organized into a gang divided into a right side and a left side, each under a foreman with a deputy, with a scribe keeping the records and the vizier exercising ultimate authority. These were skilled tradesmen at the top of their professions, not general laborers, and their positions typically passed from father to son across generations.

### Q: Why did the workers go on strike?

Their monthly grain rations, the basis of their entire food supply, were roughly eighteen days overdue. Deir el-Medina had no farmland and no well, so state deliveries were the only source of food and water, and a delay of that length was a direct threat to households rather than an inconvenience. The immediate cause was a breakdown in provisioning. The underlying causes lay in the wider strain of the later Twentieth Dynasty: the loss of Egypt's Levantine empire and its tribute, rising grain prices documented in the village's own records, pressure on the temple estates that supplied the necropolis, and administrative failure.

### Q: What was the village of Deir el-Medina?

Deir el-Medina was a purpose-built, state-supported settlement on the Theban west bank that housed the workmen who built the royal tombs. Its Egyptian name was Set Maat, the Place of Truth. Founded early in the Eighteenth Dynasty and occupied until the end of the Twentieth, it consisted of a walled grid of roughly seventy narrow mudbrick houses along a central street, set in a dry valley away from the cultivated floodplain and close to the burial grounds it served. The location was deliberate: the men knew the layouts and contents of royal tombs, which made housing them under supervision a security decision.

### Q: Who were the tomb workers of Deir el-Medina?

They were state-employed specialist craftsmen responsible for excavating and decorating royal tombs, paid in rations, organized into a two-sided gang under foremen and a scribe. They were not slaves, and the evidence against that reading is direct: they held property, traded, brought lawsuits, took recorded days off for illness and family matters, received state-supported medical care, decorated their own tombs on the hillside above the village, and on at least one documented occasion withheld their labor and were paid rather than punished. Positions ran in families, producing a hereditary community with a strong internal identity.

### Q: When did the first strike happen?

It occurred in the twenty-ninth regnal year of Ramesses III, in the second month of the growing season, conventionally dated to around 1157 BCE. The dating rests on the regnal year recorded in the papyrus combined with the conventional chronology of the Twentieth Dynasty, and carries the usual margin that attaches to Egyptian absolute dates. The stoppage was not a single event confined to those days: further actions followed within the same year and in subsequent reigns as ration deliveries continued to fail, making this the first instance of a recurring pattern rather than an isolated episode.

### Q: How was the Deir el-Medina strike resolved?

It ended in partial payment rather than structural reform. Officials came out to speak with the men, the chief of police became involved, promises were made, and grain was eventually delivered, after which the crew returned to work. Nothing was done about the underlying supply failure, which is why the same dispute recurred repeatedly over the following years. The response is itself significant evidence: the administration negotiated and paid rather than suppressing an action it could easily have treated as insubordination, which indicates that officials regarded the grievance as legitimate.

### Q: What does the strike tell us about workers' rights?

It shows that Egyptian workers held a clear and practically enforced understanding that rations constituted an owed wage, and that non-delivery justified collective action. The state's response, negotiating and paying rather than punishing, indicates officials shared that understanding in practice. It does not show any legal right to strike, any organization resembling a union, any collective bargaining framework, or any ideology of labor solidarity extending beyond the community. The entitlement was customary rather than codified, and its enforcement depended entirely on the leverage this particular workforce happened to possess.

### Q: What is the Turin Strike Papyrus?

The Turin Strike Papyrus is the document recording the events, written by the Deir el-Medina scribe Amennakht and preserved in the Egyptian collection at Turin. It provides a dated, sequential account of the stoppages, the crew's movements between mortuary temples, their statements about hunger and lack of supplies, and the responses of officials. Its value lies partly in its character: this is an internal administrative record kept by a working scribe for practical purposes, not a monument or a piece of royal propaganda, which makes it a considerably more candid witness than most surviving Egyptian texts.

### Q: How were the Deir el-Medina workers paid?

Wages came principally as monthly grain rations of emmer wheat and barley, the raw materials of bread and beer, with amounts scaled by rank so that foremen and the scribe received substantially more than ordinary workmen. Supplementary deliveries covered fish, vegetables, water, firewood, pottery, and oil, with occasional issues of meat, salt, natron, and clothing, and bonuses on royal festivals. Grain functioned as the base currency: the surplus beyond household consumption was traded within a lively internal economy that operated on commodities and copper measured by weight, since coinage did not yet exist.

### Q: Could the workers of Deir el-Medina read and write?

Literacy in the village was far above the Egyptian norm, where writing was generally confined to a small professional scribal class. A significant proportion of the workmen could read and write at least functionally, because the work demanded it: draftsmen had to read the texts they transcribed onto tomb walls, and foremen handled written instructions. The community produced tens of thousands of ostraca, flakes of limestone and potsherds used as free writing material, covering work records, letters, contracts, lawsuits, accounts, sketches, and copies of literary works, then discarded them where the dry conditions preserved them.

### Q: What did a Deir el-Medina house look like?

Houses were long, narrow, and built of mudbrick on stone footings, sharing walls along a central street. A typical dwelling ran through four or five spaces: an entrance room, a main living room with a raised platform and a roof-supporting column, one or two smaller rooms behind, and an open-air kitchen at the rear with grinding equipment, an oven, and a stairway to the roof. Many entrance rooms contain a raised, enclosed, plastered structure reached by steps and sometimes painted with figures associated with fertility, whose purpose remains debated among proposals including a birthing bed and a household shrine.

### Q: How many days a week did Egyptian tomb workers work?

The Egyptian week ran ten days, and the crew normally worked eight with two days off. During working periods the men did not return home each night but stayed in huts on the col between the village and the Valley of the Kings, going back to their families for rest days and festivals. The working day was divided into two shifts with a break between them, and water was carried to the work site as it was to the village. Total time off was considerably more generous than the eight-and-two pattern suggests, since festivals were numerous and the records document extensive additional absence.

### Q: What reasons did workers give for missing work?

Surviving absence registers list reasons in plain administrative language: illness, eye trouble, being stung by a scorpion, brewing beer, building or plastering a house, embalming or burying a relative, attending a festival, making an offering to a god, and being detained by a superior. These are recorded as facts rather than as accusations, and men who missed days remained on the roll and continued to be paid. That tolerance is significant evidence about the nature of the employment relationship, since it is not the profile of a coerced workforce but of one with recognized entitlements including what amounts to sick leave.

### Q: What rights did women have at Deir el-Medina?

Women in the village could own, inherit, buy, and sell property in their own names, appear before the local court as plaintiffs or defendants, give testimony, make loans, dispose of property by will, and initiate divorce. They appear throughout the documents as economic actors rather than as dependents, and their legal capacity was genuinely unusual by the standards of the ancient Mediterranean. The limits were equally real: women held no positions on the work gang, did not receive the workman's ration in their own right, and were excluded from the administrative and scribal roles through which income and authority flowed.

### Q: What happened to Deir el-Medina in the end?

The village was abandoned toward the close of the Twentieth Dynasty as security in the Theban necropolis deteriorated under Libyan incursions and widespread tomb robbery. The community relocated inside the massive fortified enclosure of the mortuary temple at Medinet Habu, which was defensible in a way an isolated valley settlement was not. The deeper cause was the end of the institution the village existed to serve: royal burial in the Valley of the Kings ceased, later kings were buried in the north, and a community of specialist royal tomb builders had no remaining function.

### Q: Did the Deir el-Medina strike happen more than once?

Yes, and the recurrence is analytically important. Multiple stoppages took place within the twenty-ninth year of Ramesses III alone, with the crew returning to work after partial payment and walking out again when deliveries failed once more. Further actions are documented in subsequent years and into later reigns. A single incident might reflect an unusual administrative accident, but a repeating pattern across reigns indicates systemic breakdown in state provisioning, and it places the strike alongside rising grain prices and systematic tomb robbery as evidence of a New Kingdom state running down.
