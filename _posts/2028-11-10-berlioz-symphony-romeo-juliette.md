Hector Berlioz's symphony on Romeo and Juliet makes the boldest single decision in the whole adaptation history of the play: the lovers never sing. In *Roméo et Juliette*, Op. 17, the dramatic symphony completed in 1839 to a text by Émile Deschamps, Romeo and Juliet possess no voices at all. A contralto commentator sings in the Prologue, a tenor spins the Scherzetto, choruses fight and mourn and swear, and Friar Laurence steps forward in the finale to demand an oath over two bodies. The pair at the center of the tragedy, the pair whose shared sonnet at the Capulet ball stands among the most quoted exchanges in English drama, are entrusted to the orchestra alone. Berlioz defended the choice in the preface to the score with an argument that still governs hearings of the work: the sublimity of the love in question made verbal setting dangerous, and instrumental language, richer, more varied, less finite than sung words, gained power from imprecision itself. The verdict argued through every section that follows is plain. The gamble succeeds. *Roméo et Juliette* is the strongest concert adaptation of the tragedy because the wordless center is not an evasion but a reading, a claim about where the play keeps its meaning, tested against the play scene by scene and against the rival settings that chose the opposite path.

The claim needs its evidence laid out in order. A composer who had never heard a word of English in the theatre carried the 1827 Paris performances of an English company in memory for twelve years, then spent a virtuoso's gift of 20,000 francs on seven months of freedom to build a form that had no shelf waiting for it: neither opera nor cantata, by the preface's own insistence, but a choral symphony. Inside that form, voices perform every task except the central one. The Prologue's fourteen singers outline the action in choral recitative, following the example of the play's own Chorus. The ball blazes in pure orchestra. The garden scene, the love duet that operatic tradition would have crowned the evening, unfolds as an adagio for instruments. Mercutio's Queen Mab speech, the most verbal flight in the play's first act, shrinks to a scherzo of gossamer strings and winds. The tomb, where the play gives Romeo a long soliloquy and Juliet a waking recognition, plays out as orchestral pantomime so specific that the score labels each stage of the catastrophe. Only at the end, when private feeling gives way to public consequence, do words return in force, in the Friar's narrative and the reconciliation oath sworn by two full choruses. Form and interpretation are the same gesture throughout: speech governs the feud's world, and music without speech governs the lovers' world, and the tragedy lives in the border between the two.

## The Verdict on the Berlioz Symphony: A Reading, Not a Reduction

Adaptations of the tragedy usually compete on fidelity of incident or beauty of setting, and *Roméo et Juliette* wins on a different field altogether: interpretive nerve. Berlioz cut the Nurse entirely, compressed the street fighting into a fugal introduction of a few minutes, and refused voices to the two figures an audience most expects to hear. Each cut follows from a single analytical conviction, stated in the preface and enacted in the score, that the tragedy's essence sits in states of feeling too large and too swift for the literal meaning of sung words. The play supports the conviction more often than polite opinion admits. The lovers' first exchange is a shared sonnet, a form whose perfection matters more than its paraphrase: "If I profane with my unworthiest hand / This holy shrine, the gentle sin is this" (1.5, Arden Third Series lineation) begins an exchange in which pilgrim and saint trade conceits inside fourteen lines whose rhyme scheme completes at the kiss. A composer setting those words competes with the sonnet's own music. A composer discarding the words and keeping the encounter's shape, its approach, suspension, and release, competes instead on the ground where the scene's power actually resides.

The symphony also reads the feud correctly, as machinery rather than backdrop. Shakespeare's Prologue opens with "Two households, both alike in dignity" (Prologue.1) and spends its fourteen lines on grudge, mutiny, and burial, naming the lovers inside a civic frame. Berlioz mirrors the proportion. The Introduction's combats and tumult occupy the foreground before any lover appears, the Prince intervenes in trombone unison like a voice of law entering a riot, and the choral Prologue then narrates a story whose ending the audience already holds, exactly as the play's Chorus surrenders suspense in favor of attention. Adaptation here means structural sympathy: the symphony thinks the way the tragedy thinks, in public violence framing private intensity, in foreknowledge pressing down on every tender passage that follows.

Against that standard, the famous complaint that the work lacks unity dissolves. The heterogeneity is the argument. Choral recitative, solo strophes, scherzetto, symphonic allegro, adagio, scherzo, funeral march, orchestral melodrama, operatic finale: the sequence looks miscellaneous on a contents page and reads as a map of the play's own mixed modes, sonnet and brawl, sermon and banter, potion soliloquy and civic lament. Berlioz had absorbed from the 1827 performances a Shakespeare who broke French classical rules of genre separation, and the symphony honors the lesson by refusing a single settled genre for itself. The sections ahead take the work in the order of its making: the Odeon nights that fixed the subject in a young composer's imagination, the gift that bought the time, the form the preface defends, the movements one by one, the revisions that hardened the design, and the critical quarrel the work still provokes. ## An Odeon Night in 1827: Berlioz, Smithson, and Shakespeare Without English

The symphony's first movement was an evening at the theatre twelve years before a note of the score existed. In September 1827 an English company under Charles Kemble brought Shakespeare to the Odéon in Paris, and Harriet Smithson, Irish, striking, and trained in a declamatory school foreign to French stages, played Ophelia in *Hamlet* and then Juliet. Berlioz attended, and the *Mémoires* describe a seizure rather than an appreciation: by the third act of the tragedy he could scarcely breathe, an iron hand gripped his heart, and he knew himself lost. The confession that frames the whole episode deserves emphasis, because the symphony grows from it. Berlioz states plainly that at the time he knew no English at all, glimpsing the playwright dimly through the Letourneur translation, with the poetry's splendor largely lost to him. What reached him was acting: the flow of scenes, expression, voice, gesture. The drama arrived as patterned feeling carried by bodies and sounds whose verbal content stayed half sealed.

Hold that experience against the finished symphony and the design stops looking eccentric. A listener who first received Juliet through gesture and vocal color, without semantic access, built a setting in which Juliet's meaning again travels without words, and in which the audience is placed in the composer's own 1827 seat: understanding everything essential while the literal text stays out of reach. The wordless love scene is autobiography converted into method. Where other composers set the play from the page outward, Berlioz set the memory of a performance inward, and the performance he remembered had already demonstrated that the tragedy's passion survives the loss of its language. Smithson's Juliet proved the premise in a Paris theatre before the symphony existed to argue it.

The personal entanglement is documented and inseparable, though the article keeps it in proportion. Berlioz fell in love with Smithson across the footlights, pursued an actress who did not know his name through years of obsession, poured the fixation into the *Symphonie fantastique* of 1830 with its idée fixe of the beloved, and married her in 1833 after her career faltered and debts mounted. By 1839 the marriage was strained and cooling, a fact biographers note when the husband chose the play of ideal young love for his grand statement. The symphony is no diary, however. Smithson herself, by Berlioz's side during composition and a living authority on the role within the household, contributed an understanding of the part that no library supplies. The work's Juliet descends from a performance tradition embodied in the composer's own home, filtered through a dozen years of rereading the play in translation and, increasingly, in English he taught himself for the purpose.

### What did the Odeon stage give Berlioz that a printed page could not?

The Odeon stage gave Berlioz the tragedy as physical event before verbal artifact: Smithson's gesture and vocal color carried passion across a language barrier, the Garrick-shaped action moved at theatrical speed, and the tomb awakening landed as spectacle. Print later supplied study and line-by-line knowledge, but the symphony's wordless lovers preserve that first transmission, feeling understood before words.

A second Odeon inheritance matters as much, and honest scholarship insists on it. The text Berlioz saw was not Shakespeare unmediated. The English company played the tragedy in the version descended from David Garrick's mid-eighteenth-century alteration, the version that had ruled English stages for generations. Garrick cut, rearranged, and, in the tomb, rewrote: his Juliet wakes before Romeo dies, granting the lovers a final conscious exchange that Shakespeare denied them. Berlioz carried that staging in memory as the tragedy's shape, and Deschamps built the libretto with Garrick's arrangement in the foundation. The symphony's tomb movement follows the Garrick disposition: an awakening, a shared frenzy of recognition and despair, then the double death. Purists who charge the symphony with betraying the play aim at the wrong target. The work adapts the play as the nineteenth-century stage actually transmitted it, and that transmission, Garrick's hand included, forms part of the tragedy's real history. The finale likewise expands a gesture the play keeps brief: where Shakespeare's Prince extracts promises of statues and a rate of sorrow in a short closing movement, Deschamps and Berlioz build a full operatic oath, because the remembered theatre of the period habitually enlarged civic reconciliation into spectacle.

Berlioz's Shakespeare devotion, once kindled, ran through his whole career and lends the symphony its authority as one station in a lifelong project. The *King Lear* overture followed in 1831, the *Symphonie fantastique* channeled Smithson worship into program music, *Béatrice et Bénédict* would eventually draw an opera from *Much Ado About Nothing*, and the *Mémoires* return to the playwright with a reverence Berlioz grants no other artist, ranking Shakespeare as the supreme creator after God in a famous private formulation. French Romanticism supplied the movement around the man: Hugo, Delacroix, and the young generation battering at classical unities all seized on the 1827 season as liberation. Berlioz's symphony, arriving a dozen years later, stands as the season's most durable monument in music, the work in which the Odeon thunderbolt finally found large-scale form.

## Paganini's Francs and the Seven Months That Bought a Symphony

Great forms need financing, and the financing here is a matter of exact record. On 16 December 1838 Berlioz conducted *Harold en Italie* at the Paris Conservatoire. Niccolò Paganini, the era's defining virtuoso, attended, dragged the composer back onto the platform in public view, knelt, and kissed his hand, hailing Berlioz, in the account the *Mémoires* preserve, as Beethoven's heir. Two days later a letter arrived with 20,000 francs deposited at Rothschild's, a gift framed as homage from a musician who recognized a successor. The sum equaled roughly twice Berlioz's annual earnings from journalism and teaching, the grind that ordinarily consumed his composing hours. Paganini asked for no viola concerto this time and offered no advice on subject, answering Berlioz's letters from the south of France, where illness had driven him, with the reply the *Mémoires* quote in substance: the choice belonged to the composer, who knew best what suited him.

Berlioz spent the gift with a bookkeeper's conscience and an artist's single-mindedness. Debts came first, and the *Mémoires* record that after settling accounts a handsome remainder survived, enough to suspend the feuilleton trade that fed him. For seven months he worked on the symphony, halting only three or four days a month for other obligations, a stretch of concentration his career never again afforded on this scale. The prose draft of all vocal text came from the composer's own hand; Deschamps, a fellow Shakespeare enthusiast who had championed the playwright in French verse translation, rendered the draft into singable lines with fluency Berlioz praised. Composition ran from the spring of 1839 to 8 September 1839, the completion date the autograph carries, and the dedication went to Paganini, the only possible name for the title page.

The gift carries a shadow the article does not soften. Paganini never heard the work he funded. Health kept him in Nice, Berlioz withheld the score until revision and publication should perfect it, and the virtuoso died in May 1840 with the symphony unheard and unread by its patron. A letter of January 1840 from Nice, quoted in the *Mémoires*, speaks of envy reduced to silence and leaves the composer's regret permanently on record. The dedication thus functions as memorial as much as thanks. There is also a colder professional point: the money bought independence from operatic institutions. *Benvenuto Cellini* had failed at the Paris Opéra in 1838, and a composer without a theatre owed his large dramatic statement to the concert hall instead. The choral symphony's hybrid shape answers an institutional fact. Berlioz built the drama for the forces he could command himself, orchestra, choruses, and soloists in a hall he could hire, with his own baton guaranteeing the premiere. Necessity and aesthetic conviction arrive at the same form, and the preface's pride makes no apology for either.

### How did a violinist's gift change the kind of work Berlioz could write?

Paganini's 20,000 francs cleared debts and replaced journalism income for seven months, freeing Berlioz from the feuilleton grind and from operatic gatekeepers after the failure of *Benvenuto Cellini*. The gift bought sustained concentration and institutional independence together, so the large Shakespeare statement took the shape of a concert work under the composer's own direction rather than a stage production.

The chronology deserves a compact statement because inflated claims circulate. Berlioz and Deschamps had sketched a plan for a Shakespeare symphony in the wake of the 1827 and 1828 Odeon seasons, by Deschamps's own later account, years before the gift. The *Symphonie fantastique* and *Harold en Italie* each developed tools the new work needed: the idée fixe technique of recurring thematic identity, the solo protagonist woven through orchestral scenes, the program that guides hearing without staging. A lost early attempt at scenes from the tragedy may date from 1828 to 1830, absorbed later into other scores, though the evidence permits no firm reconstruction and responsible accounts label the episode probable rather than proven. What the gift changed was not the idea's existence but its scale and finish. A plan nursed for a decade became, in seven funded months, a score of some ninety-five minutes for an orchestra of one hundred and choruses to match, copied, rehearsed in sectional sessions Berlioz pioneered for the occasion, and performed three times at the Conservatoire on 24 November, 1 December, and 15 December 1839 before audiences Balzac described as the brain of Paris. Among the listeners sat a young Richard Wagner, newly arrived and nearly penniless, whose testimony about the experience belongs to the critical record examined in a later section.

## Neither Opera nor Cantata: The Choral Symphony Berlioz Claimed as a New Genre

The preface opens with a border dispute. "There should be no misunderstanding about the genre of this work," Berlioz writes, in Michel Austin's translation for the Hector Berlioz Website. "Although voices are frequently used in it, it is neither a concert opera, nor a cantata, but a choral symphony." Every clause of the defense that follows maps onto the score's construction. Singing appears almost from the start, the preface explains, for two reasons: to prepare the listener's mind for dramatic scenes whose feelings the orchestra will express, and to introduce the choral masses gradually, since a late and abrupt arrival of huge vocal forces would undermine unity. So the Prologue makes do with fourteen voices. A male chorus of Capulets sings offstage after the ball. The funeral ceremony adds the Capulet women. The finale finally assembles two full choruses, Capulets and Montagues, with Friar Laurence, and the closing pages unite three choral bodies in the oath. Vocal mass grows across the evening like a tide chart, and the growth itself enacts the tragedy's widening circles: private pair, household, city, solemn public settlement.

Beethoven's Ninth Symphony stands behind the conception, a debt Berlioz never hid. The Ninth had shown that a symphony could admit voices at its climax and turn instrumental argument into communal utterance. Berlioz radicalizes the precedent by distributing voices through the whole span and by assigning them narration and commentary rather than a single hymn. The model from the theatre matters equally: the Prologue follows, in the preface's phrase, the example of Shakespeare's own drama, where the Chorus outlines the action in advance. Berlioz's chorus, though, does more than outline. In choral recitative, a texture between speech rhythm and song, the fourteen singers recount the feud, the ball, the secret marriage, the deaths standing ahead, compressing five acts into minutes of heightened report. The device turns exposition into drama in its own right, voices leaning on orchestral punctuation, the story racing under the pressure of foreknowledge the play's Prologue creates with its own spoiler: "A pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life" states the ending before the action starts, and Berlioz keeps faith with that cruelty of structure.

The generic claim also protects the work's center. Because the piece announces itself as symphony, the long instrumental movements need no apology and carry the argument's weight; because the piece admits chorus and soloists, the public dimension of the tragedy, the dimension opera would assign to ensembles and crowds, stays present without staging. Opera comparison clarifies the trade. Gounod's sung setting of the same tragedy, treated in the companion article on the opera, gives the lovers a sequence of duets culminating in a tomb scene of full-throated exchange, everything Berlioz declined. The decline was principled rather than timid, and the preface states the principle in the passage every account of the work must quote and every rival reading must answer.

### Why does the preface call instrumental language more powerful for this love?

The preface argues that the love's very sublimity made verbal depiction dangerous, since sung words fix a literal meaning and fence the imagination. Instrumental language, richer, more varied, and less finite, draws strength from imprecision in such a case. The claim crowns three reasons together: symphonic genre, the wish to try another mode after vocal masters, and sublimity itself.

The passage deserves full quotation, kept short as the series rule requires, from the preface in Austin's translation: the love duet and the duet of despair are entrusted to the orchestra because "the very sublimity of this love made its depiction so dangerous for the composer that he needed to allow his imagination a freedom which the literal meaning of the words sung would have denied him. Hence the resort to instrumental language, a language which is richer, more varied, less finite, and through its very imprecision incomparably more powerful in such a situation." Two supporting reasons precede the famous one. First, the work is a symphony and not an opera, a justification Berlioz calls sufficient on its own. Second, vocal duets of the kind had been treated countless times by the greatest masters, making another mode of expression wise as well as interesting. Read whole, the preface offers a ladder: genre permits, novelty invites, sublimity compels. Critics who quote only the third reason make Berlioz sound mystical; the ladder shows a craftsman reasoning from form and precedent toward an aesthetic he then states at full stretch.

## Deschamps and the Libretto: Verse Built to Serve the Orchestra

Émile Deschamps occupies a curious position in the work's authorship, at once indispensable and deliberately subordinate. Berlioz drafted the prose of every vocal passage himself, fixing the content, order, and dramatic function of the sung text before a line of verse existed. Deschamps then rendered the draft into singable French with the fluency Berlioz praised in the *Mémoires*, calling his collaborator obliging as ever and crediting the exceptional ease of the versification. The arrangement tells a listener how to weigh the libretto's literary quality, a point on which hostile critics have always feasted. The verses make no claim to rival Shakespeare, translate no line of the play directly, and pursue clarity of narration over poetic distinction. Judged as poetry, the text invites condescension. Judged as engineering, the text performs exactly the task the design assigns: rapid, intelligible report in the Prologue, a meditative flowering in the Strophes, a brisk miniature in the Scherzetto, a funeral chant of ritual plainness, and a closing narrative and oath with the gravity of civic ceremony. The words stay serviceable so that attention flows past them toward the orchestral center where the composer placed the tragedy's soul.

The collaboration also had a history that distinguishes the libretto from a commission. Deschamps was a leading figure of French Romanticism's Shakespeare campaign, a translator and advocate who had pushed the playwright's cause in print while French classical taste still resisted. By his own later account, he and Berlioz sketched the plan of a Shakespeare symphony in the wake of the 1827 and 1828 Odeon seasons, when the English performances had set Paris arguing. The 1839 libretto thus realizes a scheme nursed for a decade by two devotees, not a text ordered from a professional versifier after the music's conception. The distinction matters for the Garrick question. Deschamps, like Berlioz, knew the tragedy through the stage tradition the Odeon company embodied, and the libretto's disposition, funeral spectacle enlarged, awakening lovers in the vault, reconciliation expanded into ceremony, reflects the received performance text of the period rather than a scholar's quarto. Later editors and translators of the sung text have sometimes apologized for the divergences from Shakespeare; the apology mistakes the document's nature. The libretto records what the nineteenth-century French imagination held when the name of the tragedy was spoken.

Cuts within the collaboration reveal the design's priorities as clearly as the inclusions. The Nurse vanishes entirely, and with her goes the play's great conduit of messages, gossip, and earthy counsel. Mercutio survives only as Mab's occasion, his death unshown and unsung, absorbed into the Prologue's rapid report. Paris dies offstage in the narration's economy, his vault fight with Romeo compressed into the tomb movement's opening agitation. The Prince never sings, his authority delegated to trombones, and the parents appear only as choral households, Capulet voices and Montague voices massed against one another until the oath merges them. Personhood in the libretto follows function: the Friar sings because explanation is his office, the contralto comments because commentary is hers, the tenor flashes through the Scherzetto because Mercutio's world is speed and glitter. Everyone else dissolves into chorus or orchestra, a distribution no opera libretto of the period would have dared, and the daring originates in the prose draft's author, the composer, before the versifier touched a line.

One textual episode from the revision years shows the machinery of collaboration under strain and repair. The Friar's closing narrative, as Deschamps first versified the composer's draft, ran long, and the effect in performance struck Berlioz as cold. The *Mémoires* attribute the corrective advice to Joseph d'Ortigue, the critic and friend whose ear Berlioz trusted: an important cut fell in the narrative where excessive verses from the librettist had slowed the finale's approach to the oath. The episode deserves emphasis because hindsight often credits or blames Deschamps alone for the finale's proportions. The record shows a composer auditing his own design in performance, accepting outside counsel on a specific passage, and cutting his collaborator's verse to protect the architecture. Berlioz's loyalty in print, praising Deschamps's fluency while silently recording the cut, models the working relation: devotion shared, authority retained.

## The Forces of the Berlioz Symphony: An Orchestra of a Hundred, Choruses in Three Divisions

Scale in *Roméo et Juliette* is a meaning, not a budget line. The score calls for an orchestra of one hundred: piccolo and paired flutes, oboes with English horn, clarinets, four bassoons, four horns, trumpets and cornets, three trombones with ophicleide, the ancestor of the tuba in the bass, four timpani, a percussion battery including antique cymbals tuned in F and B flat, tambourines and bass drum, harps in numbers the score allows to rise as high as ten, and full strings. The Boston Symphony's program documentation lists the complement in that detail because the complement itself instructs: antique cymbals, small tuned discs associated with ancient rite, enter for the funeral and tomb atmosphere, color chosen for meaning rather than brilliance. Against the hundred players stand the voices, roughly a hundred at the premiere: three soloists, the fourteen-voice Prologue semi-chorus, and the choral bodies that divide into Capulets, Montagues, and the combined strength of the finale. The symmetry of the forces, orchestra and voices at parity, stages the work's thesis in personnel. Neither medium outnumbers the other; the drama lives in the allocation of tasks between equals.

Berlioz's *Treatise on Instrumentation*, published a few years after the symphony, reads in places like a commentary on the score's decisions, and the Romeo music supplies the treatise's spirit with examples at every turn. The Prince's trombone unison draws on the instrument's associations with law, ceremony, and judgment. The love scene's layered strings, divided into shimmering strands, create a sonority of suspended time that the treatise's doctrine of combination explains as blended timbre in service of a single affect. The Queen Mab scherzo pushes the opposite pole: individual instruments atomized into points of sound, muted strings, flickering winds, harp harmonics, antique cymbals struck softly, an orchestra of a hundred impersonating a coach the size of a hazelnut. Contemporary accounts of the 1839 performances stress the precision of the execution, and Berlioz's sectional rehearsals, strings drilled apart from winds, winds apart from brass, were the technology that made such extremes reliable. The composer demanded, in the preface's tradition and the *Mémoires*' later insistence, preparation comparable to a new opera mounted in a good house, performers studying almost to the point of memory.

The choral writing observes the same discipline of allocation. Three choral divisions exist because the drama needs three vocal states: the small narrating body of the Prologue, agile enough for recitative at speed; the household choruses, Capulet men at the ball's edge and Capulet men and women at the funeral, each a family acting in public; and the full double chorus of the finale, where a third body joins for the oath and the two feuding masses discover a single harmony. Friar Laurence's bass stands outside the divisions as officiant, and the two Prologue soloists stand outside as commentators, so the vocal plan distinguishes five functions with the clarity of an organizational chart. Conductors approaching the score learn the lesson Berlioz built in: balance problems in performance usually trace to function confusion, a semi-chorus swollen into a mass or an oath underweighted against the orchestra's blaze. The composer calibrated mass to meaning, and the calibration survives as the work's most practical teaching for the ensembles that still find the score, in the *Mémoires*' phrase, immensely difficult to perform.

Spatial thinking completes the picture. Offstage voices drift after the ball, a Capulet chorus heard through garden darkness, and the score's directions for placement turn the concert hall into a shallow stage of distances: near and far, public square and private garden, procession approaching and receding. Berlioz had learned from the Odeon performances how placement shapes feeling, Juliet's voice reaching a hidden listener in the orchard darkness, and the symphony reproduces the geometry in sound alone. Even the Introduction observes spatial logic, the fugato brawl surrounding the listener before the trombones' proclamation arrives like authority entering from a palace door. Analysis that confines itself to themes and harmonies misses the dimension audiences feel first. The symphony is choreographed hearing, and the choreography descends from a stage the composer never forgot.

Read down the forces column and the architecture announces itself. Three divisions belong to the orchestra without assistance, and those three hold the meeting, the love scene, and the catastrophe, the tragedy's beating heart. Every passage where the social world acts, narrates, mourns, or settles receives voices. The division of labor also solves a practical problem of scale: a ninety-five-minute concert work cannot stage-manage dozens of characters, so Berlioz assigns personhood selectively. Friar Laurence, the play's great explainer, sings because explanation is his function; the Prince speaks through trombones because law in the tragedy arrives as proclamation rather than personality; Mercutio dissolves into the scherzo because his Queen Mab flight already behaves like music, a virtuoso riff whose content matters less than its velocity. [Music in Performance History of the Play](/2028/02/25/music-in-performance-history/) traces how stage productions deploy sound inside the drama; the Berlioz case inverts the relation, extracting the drama into sound and leaving the stage behind entirely.

One further row hides inside the map and rewards attention. The lovers possess a theme. Juliet's melody, first broadened in brass over the ball's fever, returns transformed across the work, and Romeo's longing figure in the solitude movement supplies the love scene's substance. The technique descends from the idée fixe of the *Symphonie fantastique*, where a beloved's theme recurs through a life in episodes. In the tragedy the recurrence does narrative work words would otherwise perform: recognition, memory, foreboding. When the tomb movement restates the love music in frenzy and collapse, a listener who has tracked the themes understands the catastrophe's emotional grammar without a syllable of text. Program music at this level is not picture painting. The score argues in recurrences, and the map's rows are chapters of a single thematic sentence.

## The Prologue: Fourteen Voices Doing the Work of Shakespeare's Chorus

The Introduction throws the listener into the feud before any frame can soften it. Strings launch a fugato, entries chasing entries like drawn swords multiplying across a square, the counterpoint itself enacting the Prologue's "ancient grudge break to new mutiny" (Prologue.3). Trombones then halt the scramble with a unison recitative of unmistakable authority, the Prince's intervention rendered as a single massed voice that no faction answers. Berlioz the orchestrator signs the passage: where a conventional composer might pictorialize a brawl with percussion noise, the fugue makes conflict structural, voices of the orchestra interrupting and pursuing one another under strict law, until a higher law, the trombone line, imposes silence. The Prince in the play threatens death to further disturbers of the peace; the trombone theme's later returns keep that threat hanging over festivities the audience is about to enjoy.

Then the temperature drops and the Prologue proper begins, the symphony's most original texture. Fourteen singers, a semi-chorus rather than a mass, deliver the story in choral recitative over spare orchestral support, the words by Deschamps summarizing feud, ball, balcony, marriage, exile, potion, and tomb. The effect defies the period's habits: neither aria nor ensemble, the passage behaves like a Greek chorus drilled in operatic timing, reportage with the pulse of drama. Inside the Prologue sit two solo flowerings. The contralto's Strophes, beginning "Premiers transports que nul n'oublie," first transports that nobody forgets, widen the narration into meditation, a commentator's voice dwelling on young love's first rapture with a warmth the recitative withholds. The tenor's Scherzetto then compresses the Queen Mab matter into a bright miniature with semi-chorus, a foretaste in vocal form of the great orchestral scherzo waiting later. Berlioz plants his motifs like seeds: Mab appears early in words so that the later wordless scherzo lands on prepared ground, and Juliet's theme, introduced as foreshadowing strengthened during the 1846 revisions, lets the Prologue promise the love music before the ball has even begun.

Compare the device with the play's own proportions. Shakespeare's Chorus speaks two sonnets and vanishes after Act 2 begins, a frame abandoned once the story ignites. Berlioz's Prologue absorbs the Chorus function and distributes its energy forward: commentary migrates into the contralto's meditation, into the Friar's finale narration, into thematic recurrence itself. The fourteen voices also solve the preface's stated problem of gradual introduction, letting choral sound establish its credentials at chamber scale before the finale's masses. Nothing here is filler between instrumental showpieces. The Prologue is a thesis about how stories survive: a community tells the tale first, in words, and only then does the orchestra presume to show what the telling cannot contain. Listeners who know the ending from the first minutes listen the way the play's audience listens, attention freed from outcome and fixed on manner, on the how of each approach toward a known grave.

## Romeo Alone and the Ball: Solitude Swallowed by a Feast

The second number opens the tragedy's private register. Romeo Alone begins in reverie, a long-breathed orchestral meditation on the young Montague before the ball, sadness marked in the score's own heading, and the passage earns its place in the play's psychology. The Romeo of Act 1 is a performer of melancholy, sighing for Rosaline in borrowed Petrarchan coin, "O brawling love! O loving hate!" (1.1) among his oxymorons, a young man in love with a posture. Berlioz's opening pages catch the posture's genuine undertow: the theme is sincere and slightly self-regarding at once, a melody admiring its own sorrow. Then distance intervenes. Sounds of a concert and ball drift in from far off, the Capulet festivity tuning up across the city, and solitude loses its monopoly on the young man's ear. The movement's design, reverie eroded by approaching dance music, dramatizes the hinge of Act 1 better than any literal scene setting could: a private fiction of love dissolving into the public event where the real encounter waits.

The Great Festivity at the Capulets follows without a break, and Berlioz unleashes the full brilliance of his orchestra: waltzing splendor, percussion glitter, the ballroom's heat rendered in massed sound. Inside the blaze, the score performs its most consequential act of characterization. Juliet's theme rises in broad statement, brass carrying the melody over the dance's fever, at the moment the action implies the first sight across the room. Romeo's theme and the new melody entwine as the festivity roars on, and the meeting that the play conducts through a shared sonnet happens here as thematic recognition: two musical identities find one another inside a crowd's noise. The device respects the sonnet's secret. In the play, the lovers' exchange creates a private chamber in public space, fourteen lines sealed against the ballroom around them. In the symphony, the themes create the same chamber by standing out from the dance texture in a broader, slower gait, a pocket of lyric time inside social time. Analysis that calls the ball a mere orchestral showpiece misses the chamber entirely.

### Why does the ball music reach Romeo from a distance first?

Distance performs the drama of Act 1 in miniature. Romeo begins sealed inside a performed melancholy for Rosaline, and the Capulet festivity arrives faintly, as rumor and temptation, before swallowing the solitude whole. Hearing the ball approach lets the audience feel the hinge where a private fiction of love gives way to the public event that destroys and remakes it.

What the movement omits speaks as clearly as what it stages. Tybalt's recognition of Romeo's voice, the quarrel suppressed by Capulet's hospitality, the seed of the duel, receive no separate episode. Berlioz trims the ball to encounter alone, leaving menace to the feud music of the Introduction and its returns. The choice has a cost, acknowledged in the critics section: the tragedy's causal chain, in which the ball directly breeds the street fight, loosens when the ball sheds its threat. The choice also has a defense internal to the symphony's reading. Berlioz consistently externalizes causality into the feud frame and interior states into the orchestral center, keeping the connective plotting for the Prologue's narration and the Friar's closing account. A listener following the map knows why Tybalt rages; the ball itself is reserved for the one thing narration cannot give, the felt instant of mutual discovery. The movement runs some twelve minutes in performance, the symphony's first full proof that orchestral narrative can sustain a scene, and it sets the terms on which the love scene will ask for even greater trust.

## The Love Scene: Why the Orchestra Keeps the Garden Wordless

The third number is the symphony's heart and the passage by which Berlioz himself, in the *Mémoires*, reported the verdict of Europe's musicians: three quarters of those who knew the adagio placed the love scene above everything else he wrote. The movement follows the ball after an interval of night. Its full heading in the score reads like a stage direction: serene night, the Capulet garden silent and deserted, the young Capulets leaving the festivity while singing snatches of the ball music. That departing chorus, men's voices offstage with fragments of dance melody dissolving into darkness, frames the adagio in one of the score's most admired strokes. Public music exits humming itself away, and the garden empties for the pair the chorus never names in song. Then the orchestra begins the long love scene, strings and winds exchanging phrases of rising ardor over a hushed accompaniment, the lovers' themes conversing with an intimacy no staging could guarantee.

The scene rendered is the play's orchard exchange, Act 2, Scene 2, the balcony scene in the stage's later vocabulary. Shakespeare gives the pair language that alternately soars and checks itself: Romeo's "But, soft! what light through yonder window breaks? / It is the east, and Juliet is the sun" (2.2.2-3) opens a soliloquy of cosmic address, Juliet's asides puzzle over names and Montague identity, and the dialogue proper negotiates vows, danger, and parting. Berlioz's adagio does not translate these beats one by one. The music instead renders the scene's emotional syntax: wonder, approach, avowal, swell of mutual certainty, lingering reluctance at the edges. Because the preface has explained the refusal of words, the listener completes the scene from memory, projecting known lines onto wordless phrases, and the projection is the experience Berlioz designed. Half a century of readers have called the result the finest love music of the nineteenth century, and the claim survives because the adagio exploits a resource singing cannot match: two melodic lines can intertwine simultaneously, neither waiting for the other to finish, enacting mutuality in the texture itself. A sung duet alternates and harmonizes by turns; the orchestral lines here overlap, answer mid-phrase, and merge, a conversation freed from the etiquette of speech.

The adagio also anchors the series thesis this article carries: the cliché needs the words, and Berlioz restores the love scene as pure music. Popular memory reduces the orchard scene to quotation fragments printed on cards, "wherefore art thou" detached from sense, the balcony detached from any early text. The symphony strips the fragments away and discovers that the scene's structure survives intact, longing, risk, vow, and dawn pressure all legible in sound. The love-death tradition that Wagner would raise to its summit in *Tristan und Isolde* passes through this garden: [The Liebestod: Love and Death Entwined](/2024/01/05/love-and-death-liebestod-analysis/) examines that entwining across the tradition, and the Berlioz adagio belongs in the genealogy as the moment love music learned to sustain a whole scene without a libretto. Wagner heard the symphony in Paris in 1839 and carried the lesson into his own harmonic language, a debt the critics section documents from his own hand.

Performance facts keep the praise honest. The adagio asks for an orchestra capable of pianissimo control across a long span, strings divided and layered, wind solos exposed without cover, and a conductor willing to let ardor accumulate rather than peak early. Berlioz's own rehearsal doctrine, sectional preparation followed by patient assembly, was invented partly for music of such exposure. The movement's length, near seventeen minutes in the Gergiev recording's measure and similar in most complete performances, makes it the symphony's largest single span of pure orchestra, a symphonic slow movement that also behaves as an operatic scene with the singers removed. Removing the singers was the risk; the adagio's sustained line is the vindication. Even listeners skeptical of program music's narrative claims concede the passage as absolute music of the first rank, melody and harmony sufficient without any story attached, which produces the elegant paradox at the work's center: the movement most dependent on a verbal scene for its conception stands most securely when the scene is forgotten.

### How do the departing Capulet guests frame the love scene?

The departing guests sing fragments of the ball music offstage as the garden empties, their voices fading into the serene night the score describes. The frame clears the social world away in sound, melody dissolving bar by bar, so the adagio begins inside an emptied space where the orchestra alone can stage an encounter no crowd may witness or interrupt.

Set the adagio beside the tragedy's dawn parting and the reading sharpens. The play gives the lovers a second great duet at daybreak in Act 3, Scene 5, the lark and nightingale debate, "Wilt thou be gone? It is not yet near day," where avowal curdles into strategy and grief. Berlioz folds the parting's pressure into the tomb movement rather than granting it a separate scene, keeping the garden adagio unshadowed. The purity is a Romantic choice and a falsification a rigorous account must name: Shakespeare's love music in the play is always shadowed, by the Nurse's calls, by the feud's timetable, by dawn. The symphony's garden suspends the shadow for one movement so completely that the return of darkness in the funeral and tomb sections lands with redoubled force. Structure forgives the falsification and even explains it. A reading that brightens the center to deepen the surrounding dark follows the play's own alternation of lyric plateaus and violent descents, the pattern Susan Snyder's comedy-into-tragedy account taught readers to see.

## Queen Mab: Mercutio Shrunk to a Scherzo

Mercutio's Queen Mab speech is the play's first great set piece, a forty-line flight in Act 1, Scene 4, in which the fairies' midwife gallops across sleeping faces in a coach built from an empty hazelnut, drawn by a team of atomies, visiting lovers who dream of love, courtiers who dream of fees, soldiers who dream of throats cut in ambush. The speech begins "O, then, I see Queen Mab hath been with you" (1.4.53) and accelerates until Romeo halts it: peace, Mercutio, talk of nothing. Berlioz heard the speech in Rome years before the symphony, telling Mendelssohn during an 1831 ride, as the *Mémoires* recall, his astonishment that nobody had written a scherzo on Shakespeare's sparkling little poem, and instantly regretting the suggestion for fear Mendelssohn would seize it. The scherzo that resulted is the symphony's fourth number and its most famous excerpt, a miniature of orchestral gossamer: muted strings skittering at the edge of audibility, winds flickering, harp and antique cymbals glinting, the whole apparatus of a hundred players disciplined to a whisper.

The scherzo owns its territory in this article as score, while the speech itself belongs to the play articles of the series, and the score's achievement is compression with character intact. Mab's coach stays microscopic in the music: no theme swells to human scale, the dynamics hug pianissimo, and the scherzo's fleetness renders the speech's defining paradox, enormous imaginative energy spent on a subject its speaker calls nothing, the product of an idle brain. Berlioz even preserves the speech's darkness at the edges. Mercutio's catalogue curdles as it proceeds, dreams sliding toward corruption and violence, and the scherzo's trio-like episodes admit fleeting shadows before the gossamer resumes. The 1846 revision history sharpens the ending: a listener named Frankoski, secretary to the violinist Ernst, told Berlioz in Vienna that the scherzo's abrupt close dissatisfied, and the composer wrote the coda the score still carries, a tail of vanishing sound that lets Mab's coach dwindle out of earshot rather than stop dead.

Reception split over the scherzo from the first performances, and the split instructs. Berlioz records in the *Mémoires*, with undimmed fury, that one Paris paper heard in Queen Mab nothing but a grotesque little noise resembling badly lubricated syringes, while the composer counted the movement among his most delicate inventions. Posterity ruled for the composer so completely that the scherzo became the symphony's calling card, extracted for concerts alongside the love scene and the ball, sometimes to the work's detriment: a symphony reduced to its sparkle misrepresents a design in which the scherzo's placement, between the garden's ardor and the funeral's tread, does structural work, the last gleam of the comic world before the catastrophe closes over it. In the play the Mab speech performs the same placement duty, Mercutio's brilliance flaring one scene before the ball and two acts before his death under Romeo's arm. The scherzo, heard in sequence, carries that mortality inside its lightness for any listener who knows the story, and Berlioz, who built the Prologue to guarantee such knowledge, counted on exactly that double hearing.

## How the Love Scene Builds: Theme, Timbre, and the Long Orchestral Line

A closer pass through the adagio rewards the listener who tracks its construction, because the movement's reputation for effortless ardor conceals deliberate architecture. The movement grows from the materials of the solitude and ball number: Romeo's longing theme, first heard as private reverie, and Juliet's melody, first broadened in brass above the dance. In the garden the two strands meet in conditions opposite to the ball's. Where the festivity layered theme over glittering motion, the adagio strips the accompaniment to hushed pulses and lets the melodic lines themselves carry the event, winds answering strings in phrases that lengthen as confidence grows. The technique is conversational in a strict sense. A phrase proposed by one timbre is completed by another, overlaps replace alternation at the movement's climaxes, and the ear perceives two presences thinking together rather than a single narrator describing a pair. Opera composers achieve the effect with voices in duet; Berlioz achieves the effect with orchestral choirs, and the substitution carries the preface's argument into audible fact.

Harmonic patience distinguishes the movement from the period's ordinary love music. The adagio sustains long spans on slowly shifting foundations, deferring resolution the way the orchard scene defers parting, and the deferrals accumulate a pressure that melody alone could not generate. Shakespeare's scene observes the same economy: the exchange stretches against repeated farewells, Juliet calling Romeo back at the moment of departure, the Nurse's voice intruding from within, dawn deferred by argument over lark and nightingale in the later parting scene the symphony folds into the tomb. Berlioz's long line translates the scene's temporal experience, the feeling of minutes dilating inside an embrace while a hostile timetable waits outside the garden wall. Analysis in terms of theme spotting misses the translation's essence. The adagio renders duration itself, love's dilation of time, and the rendering explains why listeners who know no French and forget every heading still report the movement as the tragedy's center of gravity.

Timbre performs the characterization that voices decline. Romeo's strand tends toward the ardent middle voices, cellos and winds in their singing registers, while Juliet's melody rides higher, luminous against the night texture, and the assignment, though never labeled in the score, emerges from the ball movement's precedent and governs the tomb movement's frenzy, where the strands collide in recognizable distortion. A listener educated by the earlier movements reads the catastrophe without narration because the timbres have become persons. The device descends from the idée fixe of the *Symphonie fantastique*, where a single theme embodied the beloved across a life's episodes, and the tragedy gave Berlioz a second figure to embody, doubling the technique into dialogue. Later composers of program music, Liszt with thematic transformation, Strauss with his tone poem protagonists, inherited the method, and the line of descent runs through the garden adagio as surely as through any score of the century.

The movement's ending deserves separate notice because endings expose a composer's real convictions. The adagio refuses the operatic love duet's conventional crown, no ecstatic unison blaze ratified by applause. Instead the music subsides into the night from which the departing guests' song had cleared a space, ardor banked rather than discharged, the themes left suspended in an atmosphere the funeral procession will violate soon enough. The restraint honors the play, where the orchard scene ends not in triumph but in a whispered contract and a parting full of foreboding: Juliet's own summary, "this bud of love, by summer's ripening breath," looks forward to a flowering the audience knows will be cut. Berlioz's quiet close performs the same foreboding in sound. Ecstasy that ends softly, with the machinery of death already audible in the work's larger design, is the tragedy's authentic emotional signature, and the adagio's refusal of a blaze is the surest sign that its composer understood the scene from inside.

## Funeral, Tomb, and Oath: The Wordless Catastrophe and the Sung Reconciliation

The symphony's third part descends into the tragedy's machinery of error. A second Prologue once stood at the part's head in the 1839 version, a lengthy choral preparation Berlioz deleted during the 1846 revisions, and the cut tightens the design: narration has done its work, and the final span moves from rite to catastrophe to settlement with symphonic directness. The funeral procession for Juliet comes first, the *Convoi funèbre*, in which the Capulet chorus, men and women together as the preface's scheme prescribes, chants over the seemingly dead daughter in a ceremony of muffled tread and tolling color. The passage renders Act 4's mourning, the household that demanded the Paris marriage keening over its consequence, and the choral writing keeps grief formal, a family's public face, because private grief in this symphony belongs to instruments. A fugal tread under the voices suggests the procession's steps and the fate mechanism grinding forward at once.

Then the tomb. The sixth number, Romeo at the Tomb of the Capulets, is the boldest orchestral narrative Berlioz ever wrote, a sequence the score labels stage by stage: Romeo's arrival and struggle, the invocation before the seemingly dead beloved, the poison, the awakening, the frenzied final scene, the double death. The disposition follows the Garrick-shaped action Berlioz saw in 1827, in which Juliet wakes while Romeo still lives, granting the pair a last shared span of consciousness that Shakespeare's text refuses. Within the movement, the orchestra performs feats of specificity that test the preface's doctrine to its limit. The Invocation slows time around a solo line of stunned adoration, Romeo contemplating a beauty death appears not to have touched, the play's own wonder rendered in the soliloquy's spirit: Shakespeare's Romeo marvels that death has not conquered Juliet's crimson, asking why beauty keeps such color in the vault. The poison arrives in descending cellos, a detail program annotators have cited for generations. The awakening flickers in a clarinet figure. The final scene restates the love music at frantic pressure, themes colliding and collapsing, until the double death stills the movement on a chord of exhausted quiet.

Skeptics of program music choose the tomb movement as their exhibit, and the article concedes the ground partially before reclaiming it. A listener ignorant of the story hears agitation, lyric suspension, renewed agitation, collapse: a legible emotional arc without narrative particulars. The particulars, Paris at the vault entrance, the fight, the letter that never arrived, exist only in the score's headings and the listener's memory. Berlioz accepted the dependency and built the whole apparatus of Prologue, preface, and headings to service it, wagering that a known story changes the terms: the audience for this tragedy always knows the ending, in the theatre as in the concert hall, so illustrative precision plays to foreknowledge rather than substituting for plot. The wager descends from the play's own Prologue, which spends its sonnet giving the ending away. Judged as melodrama for orchestra, the tomb movement succeeds on its own terms, pacing and contrast shaping a catastrophe whose beats a prepared listener follows without strain. Judged as absolute music, the movement remains the symphony's most vulnerable span, a fact the revisions acknowledge: Berlioz trimmed and clarified here across the 1840s, sharpening a sequence he knew walked the border of his art.

The awakening deserves its own paragraph of judgment, because Garrick's alteration concentrates the whole question of adaptation ethics into a single stage picture. Shakespeare's disposition is pitiless and perfect: Romeo drinks, speaks his last lines over a wife he believes dead, and dies; Juliet wakes minutes later to a corpse, and the tragedy's irony completes itself in her solitude with the dagger. Garrick, writing for a century that preferred its pathos shared, slowed the poison and woke Juliet in time for recognition, farewells, and a death in her arms. Berlioz, receiving the play through that frame, composed the shared version, and the frenzied final scene of the tomb movement, love music restated at breaking pressure by two presences at once, depends on the alteration for its entire design. A purist hearing must therefore choose between condemning the movement's foundation and accepting the movement as a document of the play's stage life. The article's position follows the theatre historians: the Garrick tomb ruled the English stage for generations, Smithson played within its tradition at the Odéon, and the symphony memorializes a performance culture, not a reading text. Fidelity to the received play of 1827 is fidelity nonetheless, and the frenzy Berlioz builds on the awakening honors the emotional logic Garrick's audiences wept over, however firmly modern editors restore Shakespeare's colder sequence.

The Invocation at the movement's center offers the counterweight, a passage where Berlioz follows the spirit of Shakespeare's text with uncanny closeness. Romeo's tomb soliloquy in Act 5, Scene 3, mixes terror and tenderness in a single breath: the speaker addresses death as a rival suitor, marvels at the color death has failed to conquer, and kisses the seemingly dead Juliet before drinking. Berlioz slows the orchestral narrative around a solo line of stunned adoration, time dilating as it dilated in the garden, and the parallel is the point. The garden adagio and the tomb Invocation are the same music of contemplation at two stations of the story, desire in its dawn and desire at its extinction, and the recurrence binds the symphony's wordless center into a single arc. A listener tracking the strands hears the tragedy think. The poison's descending cellos and the awakening's clarinet flicker then do their illustrative work inside a structure already secured by thematic argument, which is why the movement survives the Hanslick test better in full context than in excerpt: the labels matter least where the recurrences matter most.

### What changes when Friar Laurence finally sings in the finale?

The Friar's recitative and air narrate the catastrophe to the assembled families, converting private disaster into public knowledge, and the bass voice carries an authority the orchestra never claimed. Singing returns because the tragedy's last business is civic, a settlement among the living, and settlements require words, witnesses, and an oath.

The finale then performs the tragedy's closing settlement at a scale Shakespeare compresses. Friar Laurence, the bass soloist, unveils the mystery in recitative and air, "Pauvres enfants que je pleure," poor children whom the speaker mourns, recounting the secret marriage, the potion, the failed letter, the deaths, while the two choruses react as the households they represent. Berlioz and Deschamps cut the Friar's narrative substantially on the advice the *Mémoires* attribute to Joseph d'Ortigue, who found the original draft cold and long-winded under the librettist's excess of verses. The surviving air balances account and lament: the Friar in the play ends as a supplicant offering his life to the law, "and, if aught in this miscarried by my fault, / Let my old life be sacrific'd" in spirit, while the symphony's Friar stands as officiant, presiding over the reconciliation his schemes failed to prevent and his testimony finally enables. The transformation flatters the character and fits the concert frame, where a narrator-officiant holds the closing architecture together.

The Oath of Reconciliation crowns the design. "Jurez donc, par l'auguste symbole," swear then, by the august symbol, the Friar commands, and Capulets and Montagues, two full choruses that have glowered past one another in the score's scheme, unite with a third choral body in a solemn ensemble over the bodies. The play's ending distributes grief into promises: Capulet's golden statue, Montague's counter-gift, the Prince's summary that a glooming peace attends the morning. The symphony converts promises into sacrament, an oath sworn in massed voices, because the preface's vocal scheme has saved its full choral weight for exactly this expenditure. Words return at maximum force only when the subject becomes collective obligation. The lovers, silent in death as in love, lie beneath a settlement spoken by everyone who failed them, and the irony is structural rather than stated: the social world that could not hear the pair in life finds its voice, at last, over their grave. A performance that underplays the finale's gravity betrays the architecture; the oath must feel earned by ninety minutes of withheld mass.

## Revisions, Performances, and the Long Road from 1839 to Print

Berlioz revised *Roméo et Juliette* with a persistence that amounts to a second composition. The three Conservatoire performances of late 1839 convinced him, by his own account, that many parts needed re-examination from every aspect, and the work then waited seven years for its next complete hearing. A Viennese performance on 2 January 1846 presented a revised version, the first performance since 1839 and the first abroad, and a complete hearing in Vienna later that January triggered the major overhaul undertaken before the Prague performance of April 1846. The documented changes repay listing because each one tightens the original conception rather than altering it. The Queen Mab scherzo gained its coda after Frankoski's objection to the abrupt ending. The Friar's narrative in the finale shrank after d'Ortigue's complaint about cold length. The second Prologue at the head of the final part disappeared entirely. The first Prologue gained musical foreshadowing, themes planted earlier so the design's recurrences bind tighter. Full publication followed in 1847, once the composer judged the search for blemishes complete, and a second edition in 1857 fixed the text that stands as the reference version for performance and study.

The performance tradition that followed is a history of excerpts interrupted by acts of faith. Complete performances demand an orchestra of one hundred, choruses in three divisions, three soloists, and rehearsal patience of operatic scale, and Berlioz's lifetime heard few: after Paris, Vienna, and Prague in the 1840s came St. Petersburg in 1847 and Weimar in 1852, where Liszt's advocacy kept the composer's music before German audiences. Berlioz himself extracted the instrumental movements for his own concerts, Parts Two and Three with the Queen Mab scherzo, a practice concert life adopted wholesale: the love scene, ball, and scherzo circulated as orchestral favorites while the choral frame waited for festivals and recording projects to restore the whole. The Boston Symphony's first Berlioz Romeo excerpts arrived in February 1888 under Wilhelm Gericke, a typical date for the work's piecemeal conquest of orchestral repertory. The recording era reversed the imbalance. Complete sets under conductors from Monteux and Munch through Colin Davis, whose Berlioz cycle treated the symphony as a cornerstone, to Gardiner and Gergiev made the full design, Prologue and oath included, the standard object of study, and the excerpts lost their status as substitutes.

A stubborn fact of the tradition deserves plain statement because program notes often blur it. The symphony has never belonged to the theatre. Berlioz called the reconciliation scene the one passage belonging to the genre of opera or oratorio and noted that the scene had never been performed in any theatre since Shakespeare's time, a remark about the play's finale that doubles as a manifesto for his own concert frame. Staged productions of the score as ballet or pageant remain curiosities. The work's home is the concert hall, its drama enacted by forces facing the audience rather than by impersonation, and that home explains both its survival and its recurring neglect: subscription programs built on overture, concerto, and symphony must bend to admit a ninety-five-minute hybrid with choruses, and many seasons decline the bend. When the bend happens, the payoff matches the 1839 reports, capacity audiences and the sense of an event, Balzac's brain of Paris replaced by any city's listeners discovering that a symphony can hold a tragedy entire.

The soloists of the premiere fix the work's vocal expectations in the record: Emily Widemann, whose name appears in variant spellings, sang the contralto Strophes; Alexis Dupont took the tenor Scherzetto; Louis Alizard, a bass-baritone from the Paris Opéra, sang Friar Laurence, with the Prologue chorus drawn largely from Opéra ranks and prepared, in a logistical feat Berlioz recounts, during intermissions of performances there. Sectional rehearsals, separate sessions for strings, winds, and brass before full assembly, were the composer's innovation for mastering the score's difficulties, a method orchestras treat as routine in the modern rehearsal room. The detail matters for assessment of the work's demands: *Roméo et Juliette* effectively required a new rehearsal technology, and the smooth surface of a fine modern performance conceals labor the score still exacts from every ensemble that undertakes the whole.

## The Critics Disagree: Program Music on Trial in the Berlioz Symphony

Three named witnesses frame the standing quarrel, and a fourth supplies the verdict of practice. Richard Taruskin, the music historian whose *Oxford History of Western Music* treats the nineteenth century at full stretch, locates the symphony's historical weight in a single auditor: Wagner, who received in Paris in the fall of 1839 an impression Taruskin calls all-important, a revelation of a new world of possibilities in the handling of the orchestra and the transmutation of drama into instrumental music, the dramatic concretization of textless music. Wagner's own later conduct corroborates the historian: a presentation copy of the *Tristan und Isolde* score carried the inscription, to the great and dear author of *Roméo et Juliette*, from the grateful author of *Tristan*, and a letter from the *Lohengrin* period counted Berlioz, Liszt, and Wagner as the three who belonged together. On Taruskin's reading, the symphony is a hinge of music history, the door through which opera's future walked into the concert hall and back again.

Eduard Hanslick, the Viennese critic whose 1854 treatise *On the Musically Beautiful* founded the formalist opposition, supplies the counter-doctrine against which all program music had to fight. Hanslick argued that music's content is tonally moving form, that definite feelings and narratives lie outside what tones themselves can present, and that a work leaning on a story mistakes literature for musical value. Applied to the symphony, the Hanslick test asks a brutal question of each movement: strip the headings, the preface, the remembered play, and what remains? The test acquits the love scene, whose melodic and harmonic substance needs no program, and convicts, or at least indicts, the tomb movement, whose labeled stages ask memory to supply what the tones dramatize only in outline. Hanslick never wrote the symphony's definitive prosecution, and honest attribution stops at doctrine rather than invented review; the doctrine nonetheless organized the hostile hearing the work received from formalist quarters for generations, the same climate in which a Paris paper could hear Queen Mab as badly lubricated syringes and another could charge the composer with failing to understand Shakespeare, jibes Berlioz preserved in the *Mémoires* with a contempt that suggests the wounds stayed open.

Jeffrey Langford, the scholar of Berlioz's dramatic symphonies, answers from inside the score. Langford judges the work, in the formulation quoted in modern program literature, Berlioz's most perfect synthesis of operatic and symphonic elements, the apotheosis of the notion of dramatic symphony. The judgment rests on structure rather than atmosphere: the vocal and instrumental forces alternate under a calculated plan, the choral Prologue states the argument, the finale comments on and resolves the tragedy, and descriptive writing intensifies toward the tomb in preparation for the sung settlement. Where Hanslick sees dependency, Langford sees distribution, each medium assigned the labor it performs best, voices for the communal and narrative, orchestra for the interior and ineffable. The Hugh Macdonald edition for the New Berlioz Edition underpins such claims textually, giving scholars a reliable score against which the 1839, 1847, and 1857 states can be distinguished, and D. Kern Holoman's catalogue work fixes the work's identity as H 79, Op. 17, amid the composer's crowded output.

Adjudication, as the series requires, must end in a ruling rather than a survey. The ruling here sides with Langford and Taruskin, with one Hanslick clause retained. The synthesis claim stands because the alternation follows a legible law, the law the InsightCrunch Berlioz map tabulates, and laws of distribution are formal facts, not program notes. The historical claim stands because Wagner's inscription and career constitute evidence no doctrine can dissolve: textless drama learned in 1839 reshaped the century's central opera. The retained clause concerns degree. The tomb movement's reliance on headings and foreknowledge is a genuine exposure, and performances that treat the sequence as self-explanatory sound episodic; the movement succeeds when a listener arrives prepared, which is to say the symphony's contract includes the Prologue's spoiler and the play's fame as working parts. A work may fairly build on the best-known plot in European drama. Berlioz built on nothing else, and the building stands.

One more disagreement, quieter and closer to the text, deserves its sentence. Berlioz's dependence on the Garrick-shaped action has drawn fire from Shakespeare scholars who treat the awakening lovers as a corruption the symphony should not perpetuate, while theatre historians answer that Garrick's version was the play for the audiences Berlioz knew, a transmission as historically real as the quarto text. The historians hold the stronger ground. An adaptation answers to the work as received in its moment, and the 1827 Odeon performances, Smithson's Juliet blazing through a Garrick frame, are precisely the reception the symphony memorializes. To demand quarto purity from a concert work of 1839 is to demand that Berlioz adapt a book he had not yet read in place of the performance that changed his life.

## Berlioz's Other Shakespeares: The Symphony Among a Life's Work

The Romeo symphony gains depth when set inside the composer's lifelong Shakespeare shelf, because the devotion was systematic rather than occasional. The Odeon season of 1827 struck a musician of twenty-three whose formal education had barely begun, and the plays became his private university. *Hamlet* haunted him through Smithson's Ophelia; the *Symphonie fantastique* poured the obsession with the actress into program music within three years. The *King Lear* overture of 1831 turned another tragedy into orchestral argument, storm and madness rendered without voices, a rehearsal for the Romeo design's confidence that instruments can carry Shakespearean states. The funeral march for *Hamlet*, composed for a Paris production project of the 1840s, extended the shelf into ceremonial music for the Danish prince's burial, Ophelia's madness never far from the composer's imagination. Late in the career, *Béatrice et Bénédict* drew a comic opera from *Much Ado About Nothing*, proof that the devotion survived disillusion, age, and the collapse of the marriage that had begun under Juliet's sign.

Letters and essays fill out the record with a critic's energy. Berlioz wrote about Shakespeare across decades of journalism, defended the plays against French classical prejudice, and measured other composers against the standard the plays set for dramatic truth. The *Mémoires* return to the playwright at the life story's hinges, the Odeon thunderbolt, the Roman rides with Mendelssohn, the Paganini chapters, as if the biography organized itself around successive encounters with a single author. Even the great *Requiem* and *Les Troyens* breathe the same air of monumental literary ambition, works conceived as cathedrals for texts and nations. Inside such a career the Romeo symphony occupies the exact center: the first full-scale attempt to house the supreme subject in a new form, funded by the century's most famous act of musical patronage, and revised across two decades with a tenacity Berlioz granted few other scores. The work is the devotion's monument, and hearings that treat the symphony as a curiosity of program music mistake a life's axis for an experiment.

The marriage shadow belongs in the record without melodrama. Berlioz married Harriet Smithson in 1833, after years of pursuit conducted through concerts, letters, and public rumor; the union deteriorated under money strain, touring absences, and temperamental mismatch, and by the years of the symphony's composition the couple lived in evident unhappiness, separating formally in the following decade. Biographers note the poignancy of the husband composing the century's great monument to young love while his own marriage to Juliet's embodiment cooled. The poignancy should not be inflated into a program. The score shows no trace of bitterness, and the love scene's ardor reads as recovered memory rather than present-tense confession: the Juliet of 1827, preserved in the amber of a young man's seizure, addressed by a master of thirty-five with full command of his orchestra. Smithson's presence in the household during composition, her authority on the role available across a dinner table, remains the strangest and most human fact of the work's making, the original Juliet consulting, however indirectly, on her own orchestral afterlife.

French Romanticism supplies the movement around the monument. The Odeon season had detonated inside a generation already straining against classical unities: Hugo's preface to *Cromwell* brandished Shakespeare as the banner of Romantic drama in the same year, Delacroix painted Shakespearean scenes, and the *bataille d'Hernani* of 1830 turned theatrical taste into street argument. Berlioz's symphony arrived after the battle's heat had cooled into institutions, and the work's hybrid form carries the campaign's principles into music with a completeness no manifesto achieved: mixture of genres, history and lyric in one span, the sublime and the grotesque as neighbors, Mab's gossamer beside the funeral's tread. A French audience of 1839 heard the symphony as the musical wing of a settled revolution claiming its monuments. Later French composers inherited the claim, and the line from the dramatic symphony through the symphonic poem to the tone poems of the century's end runs through Paris as surely as through Weimar.

## Hearing the Whole Work: A Listener's Path Through the Three Parts

Guidance for a first complete hearing can be brief because the work teaches its own method, and the Prologue functions as built-in instruction. The practical counsel reduces to preparation and patience. Preparation means holding the play's outline firmly, the feud's frame, the ball, the orchard, the potion, the vault, and the settlement, since the symphony spends its narration early and trusts memory afterward. A reader who knows the shared sonnet's occasion, the aubade's pressure, and the Prince's closing summary will follow every orchestral decision without strain. Patience means resisting the excerpt habit during the choral spans. The Prologue's recitative can seem austere beside the ball's blaze, and the funeral's tread beside the scherzo's sparkle, yet the austerity is the price of the design's payoff, vocal mass saved and spent according to plan. Listeners who grant the frame its minutes receive the finale's oath as architecture fulfilled rather than as a choral appendix.

Attention pays its highest dividends at the joints between numbers. The ball's music dissolving into offstage song at the love scene's threshold, the scherzo's vanish into silence before the funeral, the tomb's collapse into the Friar's first recitative: each joint performs a change of world, public to private, comic to mortal, private to public again. Berlioz composed the joints with the care of a dramatist, and program notes that list movements without remarking transitions undersell the craftsmanship. Conductors shape the evening at the joints most of all, pacing silences, tuning the offstage distances, letting the garden's quiet register before the adagio dares its first phrase. The 1839 audience, Balzac's brain of Paris, sat for the whole span across three concerts' worth of evenings and returned, a discipline modern festival formats recover when the symphony anchors a program entire.

The work also rewards a second hearing devoted to the chorus as protagonist. Track the voices alone and a shadow drama emerges: narration hardening into meditation, household grief formalized in procession, enmity massed in two camps, enmity dissolved into a single oath. The chorus travels farther than any individual in the score, from fourteen reporters to a city's whole population swearing peace, and the journey mirrors the tragedy's civic lesson, the movement from private catastrophe to public knowledge. Shakespeare ends with a prince instructing survivors to go hence and have more talk of these sad things; Berlioz ends with the talk accomplished and the oath sworn, the talk set to the largest sound the evening permits. Hearing the chorus as the work's collective character clarifies why the lovers had to stay silent. The symphony tells two stories at once, a pair who feel and a city that learns, and only one of the stories can be sung.

Preparation can begin with the play itself, read aloud where possible, because Berlioz's orchestra thinks in the play's rhythms of alternation: brawl and sonnet, sermon and banter, procession and vault. A listener who has heard the Prologue's sonnet spoken, who has felt Mercutio's catalogue accelerate under a reader's breath, who has sat with the Friar's closing account of the miscarried letters, carries into the concert hall exactly the equipment the composer assumed in 1839. The symphony then performs its rarest service for the play's students: the familiar words fall silent, and the structures underneath the words, proportion, recurrence, foreknowledge, the civic frame closing over private fire, become audible as form. Returning to the text afterward, a listener reads with ears the symphony has trained, alert to the Chorus in the Prologue, to music cues in the ball scene's directions, to the long orchestral silences Shakespeare builds around his lovers at the moments of highest pressure.

## Why the Berlioz Symphony Still Tests Orchestras: Difficulty as Meaning

Berlioz warned performers plainly, and the warning has never expired. The *Mémoires* call the work immensely difficult to perform, presenting problems of every kind arising from form and style, soluble only through long and patient study under perfect supervision, with artists of the first rank in every chair and a preparation comparable to a new opera mounted in a good theatre, studied almost to the point of performance by heart. The sentence is sometimes quoted as a composer's vanity. Orchestras that schedule the complete score discover the sentence operating as a technical manual. The difficulties begin with sheer coordination: a hundred players, three choral divisions entering at widely separated points, soloists who must float over textures of gossamer in the scherzo's neighborhood and command a hall in the finale, offstage effects whose distances need rehearsal in the actual room. Sectional preparation, the method Berlioz pioneered for the 1839 performances, remains the practical answer, strings and winds and brass drilled separately until the full assembly can spend its time on meaning rather than survival.

Balance poses the subtler test. The score's extremes, a scherzo held at the threshold of audibility against a finale of massed choruses and full brass, defeat any single rehearsal dynamic. Conductors must protect the quiet movements from the orchestra's instinct to fill a hall, and protect the oath from the opposite instinct, choral weight squandered by an accompaniment playing at concert brilliance rather than ceremonial support. The love scene exposes intonation across long spans of exposed wind writing, and the tomb movement exposes ensemble precision in music that lurches between stillness and frenzy without a singer's breath to cue the changes. Even the Prologue's choral recitative, modest in forces, tests a choir's diction at speed, French text racing over orchestral punctuation in a texture with no operatic precedent to imitate. Every generation of performers relearns the lesson of 1839: the work's radicalism lives in execution as much as in conception, and a routine performance, however polished, betrays the score more surely than a rough one that honors the stakes.

The difficulty also explains the work's programming ecology across two centuries. Subscription orchestras plan in halves and pairs, and a ninety-five-minute hybrid with choruses fits neither slot; the symphony gravitates toward festivals, opening nights, recording projects, and the tenures of conductors with a Berlioz brief, Monteux and Munch in Boston and London's tradition, Colin Davis across a career-long cycle, Gardiner with period forces, Gergiev with the London Symphony's choral strength. Each complete performance therefore carries an institutional statement, an orchestra declaring capacity and intent, and audiences respond with the event atmosphere the 1839 reports describe. The excerpt tradition persists alongside, the love scene and scherzo welcoming listeners who will graduate to the whole, and the healthiest sign in the work's modern life is the frequency with which excerpt audiences convert: a listener captured by seventeen minutes of garden music eventually demands the Prologue that explains the silence and the oath that answers it.

For the play's students, the performance demands carry a final interpretive dividend. Shakespeare's tragedy likewise depends on execution virtues that summaries cannot convey, verse speaking at speed, a Juliet of genuine youth, a Mercutio whose brilliance must flare and vanish, and generations of readers have mistaken a dull production's failure for the text's weakness. The symphony teaches the parallel lesson in another medium. Heard in a cautious, under-rehearsed account, the work can seem episodic, its joints loose and its tomb movement melodramatic; heard at full commitment, the architecture locks and the episodes reveal themselves as movements of one argument. Judgment of Berlioz, like judgment of the play, must discount for performance before condemning design. The critics section's ruling assumed strong performance throughout, and the assumption deserves the emphasis this section gives it: the synthesis Langford praises is a property of the score realized, not of the score merely read.

## Berlioz Among the Rival Romeos: Opera, Overture, and the Concert Hall

The symphony's choices sharpen against the rival settings the series treats in companion articles. Opera, the tragedy's expected musical home, hands the lovers voices and lets them sing their meetings at full length: [Gounod's Opera Romeo et Juliette (1867)](/2028/10/27/gounod-opera-romeo-et-juliette/) crowns its structure with a chain of duets and a tomb scene of sung recognition, the direct antithesis of Berlioz's orchestral garden and vault. The comparison is not a contest of quality but of premise. Gounod trusts the singing voice as the natural vessel of lyric feeling and writes accordingly, duet upon duet of mounting ardor. Berlioz distrusts the vessel for this particular feeling, arguing that words pin love to literal statement, and the preface's doctrine makes the two works permanent counter-arguments, each exposing the other's wager every time the garden scene arrives.

The concert hall produced its own rival a generation later. [Tchaikovsky's Fantasy-Overture and Its Theme](/2028/11/17/tchaikovsky-fantasy-overture/) compresses the whole tragedy into a single sonata-form span of some twenty minutes: Friar's hymn, feud allegro, love theme of legendary breadth, catastrophe, transfigured close. Tchaikovsky, who revered Berlioz and met him in 1867, learned the older composer's lesson that themes can carry characters, then disciplined the lesson inside classical form where Berlioz had sprawled across seven numbers and three parts. The fantasy-overture solves the program problem by concentration, trusting one love theme to bear the lovers entire. Berlioz solves the problem by distribution, trusting an evening's architecture. Concert life, preferring twenty minutes to ninety-five, programmed Tchaikovsky's solution far more often, and the Berlioz symphony's relative rarity beside its Russian descendant measures practicality rather than worth: the fantasy-overture fits a half, the dramatic symphony demands a festival.

Ballet completes the triangle of wordless Romeos, Prokofiev's full-evening score proving that orchestral narrative can sustain the complete plot when dancers supply the bodies Berlioz withholds. The comparison flatters Berlioz in one respect: Prokofiev's audience watches the story, eyes confirming every musical gesture, while Berlioz's audience hears the story with eyes free, the boldest wager of all. Song settings, from Bellini's opera, which Berlioz had heard and declined to imitate, to the thousands of ballads trading on the names, fill out a field in which the symphony remains the outlier that thought hardest about medium. The through-line of the series holds: every adaptation is an argument about the play, and Berlioz's argument, that the center of the tragedy exceeds language, was made by the one composer whose first encounter with the play had arrived without language at all. The circle from the Odéon in 1827 to the Conservatoire in 1839 closes with a completeness rare in adaptation history, a work whose form remembers its origin in every bar of the garden adagio.

What remains for a listener approaching the whole score is a discipline of preparation the work itself teaches. Read the play's Prologue and the orchard scene, hold the feud's outline and the sonnet's shape in mind, then let the symphony withhold the words and feel the knowledge do the singing. The Prologue's fourteen voices will narrate, the trombones will threaten, the ball will blaze and recede, the garden will open in a silence the departing guests leave behind, Mab will vanish down her microscopic road, the funeral will tread, the vault will flare and collapse, and two choruses will swear an oath over the stillness. Ninety-five minutes later the tragedy's shape stands complete, carried by everything except the lovers' voices, and the absence at the center will have become the loudest presence in the hall. That inversion, silence as fullness, is Berlioz's gift to the play's long afterlife, and the reason the symphony outranks every rival setting that played safe with song.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What kind of work is Berlioz's Romeo et Juliette?

Berlioz's Romeo et Juliette is a dramatic symphony, Op. 17, for orchestra, three choruses, and three vocal soloists, completed in 1839 to a text by Émile Deschamps after Shakespeare's tragedy. The composer insisted on the symphonic label: voices serve narration, commentary, and public ceremony, while the central dramatic scenes, the ball, the love scene, the tomb catastrophe, belong to the orchestra alone. Seven numbers in three parts carry the design across roughly ninety-five minutes, from a fugal street fight to a closing oath of reconciliation sworn by massed choruses over the lovers' bodies.

### Q: Why does Berlioz call Romeo et Juliette a choral symphony rather than an opera or cantata?

The preface to the score opens with the distinction: although voices are used frequently, the work is neither a concert opera nor a cantata, but a choral symphony. The label governs the design. Singing enters early to prepare the listener's mind for scenes the orchestra will express, and the choral masses grow gradually, from fourteen voices in the Prologue to the united choruses of the finale, so vocal weight accumulates across the evening. An opera would impersonate the lovers on a stage; a cantata would narrate and stop. The choral symphony claims a third contract, symphonic argument enriched by communal voice.

### Q: Why are Romeo and Juliet given no singing voices in the symphony?

Berlioz gives the lovers no singing voices because the preface argues that words would diminish the love being depicted. Three reasons stack together: the work is a symphony rather than an opera; vocal masters had treated such duets countless times, inviting another mode of expression; and the love's sublimity made literal sung meaning a danger, fencing an imagination that needed freedom. Instrumental language, richer, more varied, and less finite, draws power from imprecision in such a case. The orchestra therefore plays both lovers throughout, at the ball, in the garden, and in the vault, while soloists and choruses handle every other function.

### Q: What happens in the Prologue of Berlioz's Romeo et Juliette?

The Prologue follows an orchestral Introduction of street fighting halted by the Prince's trombone intervention. Fourteen singers then deliver the whole story in choral recitative after the manner of the play's own Chorus: feud, ball, secret marriage, exile, potion, and deaths, all reported in advance. A contralto soloist widens the report into meditation in the Strophes, dwelling on first love's transports, and a tenor Scherzetto compresses the Queen Mab matter into bright miniature with semi-chorus. Themes heard here, including Juliet's melody as foreshadowing strengthened in revision, seed the instrumental movements that follow, so narration and symphonic argument interlock from the start.

### Q: How does the Queen Mab scherzo relate to Mercutio's speech in the play?

The scherzo translates Mercutio's Queen Mab speech in Act 1, Scene 4, into pure orchestral miniature. Shakespeare's flight describes the fairies' midwife galloping over sleeping faces in a hazelnut coach drawn by atomies, planting dreams of love, fees, and violence. Berlioz keeps the scale microscopic: muted strings, flickering winds, harp, and antique cymbals hold a pianissimo surface that renders the speech's velocity and gossamer without a syllable. Berlioz had imagined the scherzo as early as 1831, confiding the idea to Mendelssohn in Rome and fearing a rival setting. The 1846 revision added the vanishing coda after a listener found the original ending too abrupt.

### Q: What does the love scene in Berlioz's symphony depict without words?

The love scene depicts the orchard exchange of Act 2, Scene 2, the meeting in the Capulet garden under a serene night, rendered as a long orchestral adagio. Departing guests first sing fragments of the ball music offstage until the garden empties, and then the lovers' themes converse across strings and winds in rising ardor. The music renders the scene's emotional syntax rather than its lines: wonder, approach, avowal, mutual certainty, reluctant lingering. Listeners complete the scene from memory of the play, projecting known words onto wordless phrases, a collaboration Berlioz designed through the preface's argument that instrumental imprecision strengthens rather than weakens the depiction.

### Q: How did Paganini's gift make the symphony possible?

Niccolò Paganini heard Berlioz conduct Harold en Italie at the Paris Conservatoire on 16 December 1838, publicly knelt and kissed the composer's hand in homage as Beethoven's heir, and followed the gesture with a gift of 20,000 francs deposited at Rothschild's. Berlioz paid debts first, then used the remainder to suspend the journalism that ordinarily consumed his hours. Seven months of near-unbroken work followed, from the spring of 1839 to the score's completion on 8 September 1839, with Émile Deschamps versifying the composer's prose draft. The symphony carries the dedication to Paganini, who died in 1840 without hearing a note of the work his money freed.

### Q: When and where was Berlioz's Romeo et Juliette first performed?

The symphony received its first performance on 24 November 1839 in the hall of the Paris Conservatoire, with Berlioz conducting an orchestra of one hundred and choruses to match. Two further performances followed on 1 December and 15 December 1839 before capacity audiences drawn from the city's artistic and intellectual elite, a gathering Balzac described as the brain of Paris. The soloists were Emily Widemann, contralto, Alexis Dupont, tenor, and Louis Alizard, the bass-baritone who sang Friar Laurence. Berlioz prepared the demanding score through sectional rehearsals, separate sessions for each orchestral family, a method the performances helped establish as standard practice.

### Q: Who wrote the words for the vocal sections of Romeo et Juliette?

Émile Deschamps wrote the words, versifying a prose draft Berlioz supplied for every vocal interlude between the instrumental movements. Deschamps, a poet and a fellow champion of Shakespeare in France, had collaborated with Berlioz on the plan for a Shakespeare symphony in the wake of the 1827 and 1828 Odeon seasons, by the librettist's own later account. The verses never reproduce Shakespeare's lines directly. The text follows the play as the English stage transmitted it, Garrick's arrangement included, omits the Nurse entirely, and expands the closing reconciliation into the full operatic oath that crowns the finale.

### Q: What does Friar Laurence do in the finale of the symphony?

Friar Laurence, sung by the bass soloist, steps forward in the finale to unveil the mystery before the assembled families. His recitative and air, beginning with the lament for the poor children he mourns, recount the secret marriage, the sleeping potion, the letter that failed to reach Mantua, and the double death in the vault. Berlioz shortened the narrative during revision after Joseph d'Ortigue found the original draft cold and long-winded. The Friar then presides as officiant, commanding the two households to swear the oath of reconciliation that closes the symphony in massed choral solemnity.

### Q: Why does the symphony end with an oath of reconciliation?

The oath ends the symphony because the preface's vocal scheme saves its full choral mass for the tragedy's public consequence. Shakespeare closes with promises: statues of gold, a prince's glooming summary, grief converted into civic gesture. Berlioz and Deschamps enlarge the gesture into a sacrament, two full choruses of Capulets and Montagues uniting with a third choral body as Friar Laurence commands the households to swear by the august symbol. Words return at maximum force only when the subject becomes collective obligation. The silent lovers beneath the oath sharpen the irony: the social world finds its voice over the pair it failed to hear in life.

### Q: How long does a performance of Berlioz's Romeo et Juliette last?

A complete performance lasts roughly ninety-five minutes, the span reference accounts and orchestral program notes consistently report for the full score with its seven numbers in three parts. The love scene alone runs near seventeen minutes, the largest single span of pure orchestra, and the ball and solitude movement runs about twelve. Length explains the work's concert history: the complete symphony demands a festival scale of commitment, an orchestra of one hundred, three choruses, and three soloists, so the instrumental movements long circulated as excerpts while complete performances remained events. Recordings under conductors from Monteux to Gardiner restored the whole design to regular study.

Read down the forces column, the map exposes the work's governing law: the orchestra alone carries the meeting at the ball, the garden love scene, and the tomb catastrophe, while voices narrate in the Prologue, mourn in the funeral procession, and settle the feud in the finale's oath. ### Q: Did Berlioz revise Romeo et Juliette after the first performances?

Berlioz revised the symphony extensively. The 1839 performances convinced him that many parts required re-examination, and after a revised version reached Vienna on 2 January 1846, a complete hearing later that month prompted the major overhaul completed before the Prague performance of April 1846. The Queen Mab scherzo gained its coda, the Friar's narrative in the finale shrank, the lengthy second Prologue at the head of the final part disappeared, and the first Prologue gained thematic foreshadowing. The full score appeared in print in 1847, and a second edition of 1857 fixed the reference text that performers and scholars use.

### Q: What did Wagner learn from Berlioz's Romeo et Juliette?

Wagner heard the symphony in Paris in the fall of 1839, and the historian Richard Taruskin calls the impression all-important: a new world of possibilities in orchestral handling and in the transmutation of drama into instrumental music, the dramatic concretization of textless music. The debt stayed acknowledged. Wagner inscribed a presentation copy of the Tristan und Isolde score to the great and dear author of Roméo et Juliette from the grateful author of Tristan, and a letter from the Lohengrin period counted Berlioz, Liszt, and Wagner as the three who belonged together. The love scene's wordless ardor stands behind the younger composer's own love-death music.

### Q: What happens in the tomb scene movement of the symphony?

The tomb movement, Romeo at the Tomb of the Capulets, plays the catastrophe of Act 5, Scene 3, as labeled orchestral pantomime. The score's headings mark the stages: Romeo's arrival and struggle at the vault, the Invocation as he contemplates the seemingly dead Juliet, the poison rendered in descending cellos, the awakening flickering in a clarinet figure, a frenzied final scene restating the love music at breaking pressure, and the double death. The disposition follows the Garrick-shaped action Berlioz saw in 1827, in which Juliet wakes while Romeo still lives, granting the pair a last shared span of consciousness.

### Q: Why is there a funeral procession for Juliet in the symphony?

The funeral procession renders the mourning of Act 4, the Capulet household keening over a daughter believed dead after the sleeping potion. Berlioz assigns the rite to the Capulet chorus, men and women together under the preface's scheme of gradually expanding vocal mass, because household grief is a public and formal act, and public acts belong to voices in the symphony's division of labor. A fugal tread beneath the chanting suggests procession steps and the fate mechanism grinding forward together. The ceremony also descends from the Garrick-shaped stage tradition, which had enlarged Juliet's funeral into a spectacle generations before Berlioz set it.

### Q: What role does the contralto soloist play in the Prologue of the symphony?

The contralto soloist sings the Strophes, the Prologue's meditation on first love, beginning with the transports that nobody forgets. Within the choral recitative's rapid narration, the Strophes pause the report and widen it into commentary, a single voice dwelling on young love's rapture with warmth the fourteen-voice outline withholds. The role establishes the symphony's pattern for solo voices: commentators and officiants rather than impersonated characters. Emily Widemann sang the part at the 1839 premiere. No soloist in the work plays Romeo or Juliet, a casting fact that states the whole conception in miniature.

### Q: Is Berlioz's Romeo et Juliette program music, and why does the label matter?

The symphony is program music in the strong sense: a score whose movements follow a known story scene by scene, guided by headings, a preface, and thematic recurrences that track characters and events. The label matters because the work became a test case in the century's quarrel over musical meaning. Eduard Hanslick's formalist doctrine held that music presents tonally moving form and cannot carry definite narrative, while the symphony's defenders, Jeffrey Langford among them, show a calculated distribution of labor between voices and orchestra. Wagner's career supplied the practical verdict: the program experiment of 1839 reshaped opera itself, whatever doctrine concluded.

### Q: Which movements of the symphony are heard most often on their own?

The instrumental movements circulate most often: Romeo Alone with the Capulet ball, the love scene, and the Queen Mab scherzo. Berlioz set the pattern himself, extracting those spans for his own concerts in Paris and abroad when complete performances, with their hundred players, three choruses, and three soloists, exceeded the occasion. Orchestras adopted the practice wholesale; the Boston Symphony's first Berlioz Romeo excerpts arrived in February 1888 under Wilhelm Gericke. The excerpts made the love scene famous across Europe, yet the extraction carries a cost the complete score corrects: without the Prologue's narration and the finale's oath, the tragedy's public frame vanishes and pure lyric remains.
