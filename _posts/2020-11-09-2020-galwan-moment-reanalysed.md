The Galwan Valley clash 2020, fought on the night of 15 and 16 June 2020 along the Line of Actual Control in eastern Ladakh, killed twenty Indian soldiers, among them Colonel B. Santosh Babu, the commanding officer of the 16 Bihar Regiment, and produced the first fatalities on that contested boundary since October 1975. A clash of that scale invites two familiar readings. The first treats the night as a military event: a disengagement gone wrong at Patrol Point 14, a melee fought with clubs and stones at altitude, a tactical episode to be reconstructed from terrain, orders and timings. The second treats the night as a strategic event: a signal sent by Beijing, a probe of Indian resolve, a move inside the larger contest between China, Pakistan and the United States. Both readings capture part of the record. Neither captures what the Galwan moment operationalised inside Indian politics. Between June 2020 and October 2024, the Government of India banned hundreds of Chinese applications by ministerial order, screened Chinese investment through a press note that predated the clash by two months, excluded Chinese vendors from the 5G future of Indian telecommunications, accelerated the Quadrilateral Security Dialogue to summit level, poured concrete along the northern border at wartime pace, and negotiated a staged disengagement that closed, four years and four months after the clash, with an announcement on Depsang and Demchok and a formal meeting between Narendra Modi and Xi Jinping at Kazan. That sequence is not a series of reactions. It is a pattern, and the pattern has an architecture: a post-China decoupling carried out domain by domain, order by order, through institutions rather than declarations. This article defends that reading as the closing argument of a pillar that has treated foreign policy as domestic politics from non-alignment forward.

The claim deserves precision before the evidence. Decoupling, as the word is used here, does not mean the severing of the India China economic relationship. Bilateral trade between India and China crossed 125 billion dollars in the calendar year 2021 and rose further in 2022 by the Ministry of Commerce and Industry figures, a fact that critics of the decoupling reading cite at every opportunity and that this article confronts directly in its adjudication section. Decoupling here names something narrower and more institutional: a deliberate, documented programme of removing Chinese presence from sectors the Indian state classified as sensitive, of raising barriers where none had existed, and of building alternative alignments and infrastructure whose stated rationale was the reduction of dependence on China. The programme operated through gazette notifications, press notes, ministerial orders under Section 69A of the Information Technology Act 2000, and summit communiques. Each instrument is datable, signed and archived. The argument of this article is that the Galwan clash supplied the political condition under which those instruments became usable at scale, and that the sequence from June 2020 to October 2024 is best read as one architectural act spread across three domains: economic decoupling, strategic repositioning, and a disengagement architecture that managed the border without dissolving the other two domains. Read that way, the Galwan moment belongs to the same analytical family as the moments this pillar has examined since 1947: foreign policy decisions whose deepest content was domestic, taken by named governments, contested by named opponents, and recorded in documents a reader can find.

### What made Galwan a turning point rather than a border skirmish?

The Galwan clash became a turning point because twenty Indian soldiers died in a single night on a boundary that had produced no combat fatality for forty five years, because the dead included a commanding officer, and because the government answered across economic, strategic and diplomatic domains rather than at the point of contact alone.

The forty five year interval matters to the argument. On 20 October 1975, a patrol of the Assam Rifles was ambushed at Tulung La in the eastern sector, and four Indian personnel were killed. Between that date and 15 June 2020, the Line of Actual Control saw standoffs, transgressions, a month long confrontation at Depsang in April and May 2013, a seventy three day standoff at Doklam in the summer of 2017, and repeated face-offs that both armies contained without a fatality. The agreements of 1993 and 1996, the Agreement on the Maintenance of Peace and Tranquillity signed on 7 September 1993 and the Agreement on Confidence Building Measures signed on 29 November 1996, had built a regime whose central achievement was precisely the absence of killing: firearms were restricted near the line, and both sides patrolled a boundary neither had agreed on paper while refraining from shooting across it. Galwan broke the regime's core result while respecting its letter, since the deaths of 15 and 16 June 2020 were inflicted with improvised weapons rather than firearms. A reading that treats the night as one more skirmish must explain why a state that had absorbed Doklam without a single economic measure answered Galwan with an app ban within fourteen days. The answer this article gives is that the deaths converted an abstract boundary dispute into a domestic political fact with a casualty list, a commanding officer's name, and funeral processions in Bihar, Telangana, Punjab, Jharkhand and other states from which the twenty soldiers came. Foreign policy acquired a constituency of grief, and the government moved to give that constituency a programme.

## May 2020: The Crisis That Preceded the Galwan Valley Clash 2020

The clash of June 2020 closed a crisis that had opened six weeks earlier, and the chronology of May 2020 is the first body of evidence any reading must master. In the first week of May 2020, Indian and Chinese patrols confronted each other at multiple points in eastern Ladakh: at Pangong Tso on the night of 5 and 6 May, near the Galwan confluence in the same period, and at other friction points along a line where the two armies hold different perceptions of where the boundary runs. On 9 May 2020, soldiers clashed at Naku La in Sikkim, in the eastern sector, an incident both sides acknowledged through brief statements. The Indian Army's public references through May 2020 were restrained, describing transgressions and face-offs resolved through established mechanisms. Behind that restraint, both armies moved reinforcements forward. By late May 2020, Chinese troops had crossed at several points in eastern Ladakh and occupied positions the Indian side regarded as lying on its side of the line, a characterisation the Chinese Ministry of Foreign Affairs disputed in its statements of the same weeks, which described Chinese border troops as defending Chinese territory and accused Indian forces of trespass and of building facilities across the line. The two accounts of May 2020 were never reconciled, and the dispute over who moved first remains a disputed point in the record, marked as disputed here: the Indian government's statements, including the Ministry of External Affairs statement of 17 September 2020, hold that Chinese actions in contravention of the 1993 and 1996 agreements caused the crisis; Chinese statements of June and September 2020 hold that Indian infrastructure work and patrol conduct created the friction. What is settled is the sequence of meetings that followed. On 6 June 2020, Lieutenant General Harinder Singh, commander of the Leh based XIV Corps, met Major General Liu Lin, commander of the South Xinjiang Military District, at the Chushul Moldo meeting point. The two commanders agreed a phased disengagement, and through the following week both sides reported initial pullbacks at some points, including a partial thinning in the Galwan Valley itself.

The Galwan Valley carries a weight in Indian memory that the geography alone does not explain. The valley takes its name from Ghulam Rasool Galwan, the Ladakhi explorer and caravan leader whose party traversed the river route in the nineteenth century, and in October and November 1962 the valley was a battlefield of the war that the domestic political history of this pillar has already examined through [the domestic-political-event framework](/2020/09/26/1962-china-war-domestic-political-event/). In 1962, Indian posts in the Galwan Valley were overrun in the war's opening phase, and the name entered the Indian political vocabulary as shorthand for Himalayan unpreparedness. When Indian and Chinese soldiers began confronting each other along the Galwan river in May 2020, every Indian newspaper reader of a certain age recognised the dateline. Bertil Lintner, whose China's India War of 2017 reconstructed the 1962 conflict from declassified and Chinese sources, returned to the subject in commentary through 2020 and argued that Beijing's Himalayan pressure formed part of a longer pattern of territorial assertion timed to moments of Indian distraction. The article returns to Lintner's reading in the adjudication section; here the point is narrower. The May 2020 crisis was not a sudden storm. It was a managed confrontation between two armies operating inside a thirty year old regime of agreements, handled by corps commanders at a recognised meeting point, with a disengagement formula agreed on 6 June 2020. The deaths of 15 and 16 June 2020 occurred inside that management process, during a verification of the agreed pullback, which is why the Indian official reaction carried a charge of betrayal that a battlefield death in open war would not have carried, and why the Chinese official reaction carried a counter claim of Indian provocation at the same verification. The management regime survived the clash in form. Its political meaning did not survive, and the remainder of this article traces what replaced it.

The Indian government's own framing of the crisis evolved across documented statements, and the evolution is itself evidence for the domestic politics reading. Through June and July 2020, official statements spoke of disengagement and de-escalation as the governing objectives. On 3 July 2020, Narendra Modi travelled to Ladakh unannounced, visited troops at Nimu, and addressed soldiers with a formulation about the age of expansionism being over, a phrase delivered without naming China and understood by every listener in its address. On 15 September 2020, Defence Minister Rajnath Singh made the government's fullest statement to the Lok Sabha, describing Chinese attempts to transgress the line at multiple points, stating that India had counter deployed, and declaring that no force in the world could stop Indian soldiers from patrolling their traditional points. The statement named the agreements of 1993 and 1996, charged China with violating them, and rejected any restriction on Indian patrols. Each of these formulations was a domestic political act as much as a diplomatic one, made before Parliament, the electorate of Bihar approaching its October and November 2020 assembly election, and a national press corps that published the casualty figures and the satellite photographs in the same editions. Foreign policy was being conducted in the open, for an audience that voted.

## The Night of 15 and 16 June 2020: The Clash and the Official Record

The settled chronology of the clash rests on official statements, and the article states it from them. On the evening of 15 June 2020, a party of the 16 Bihar Regiment led by its commanding officer, Colonel B. Santosh Babu, moved to Patrol Point 14 in the Galwan Valley to verify the pullback agreed at the corps commander meeting of 6 June 2020. Indian accounts, carried in the Indian Army's statements and in the government's subsequent parliamentary answers, record that a Chinese observation post and structures stood at the point in contravention of the agreed disengagement, that an argument followed the Indian party's demand for their removal, and that a violent melee developed across the evening and into the night on the ridge above the Galwan river. The fighting was conducted with stones, clubs and improvised weapons, at an altitude above fourteen thousand feet, and soldiers of both sides fell into the river and onto the rocks below the ridge. The Indian Army's statement of 16 June 2020 first reported three Indian deaths, naming Colonel Babu among them, and was revised the same day as the toll among the critically injured rose: twenty Indian soldiers had died, including the commanding officer. The Army statement recorded that casualties had occurred on the Chinese side as well, without a figure. Indian officials, speaking in briefings reported across the Indian press in the days that followed, attributed a figure of forty three Chinese casualties, killed and seriously injured combined, to intercepts and assessments; the figure was never issued as a formal statement with a sourcing note, and this article records it as an attributed Indian official assessment rather than a settled number.

China's accounting followed a different timetable, and the difference is part of the political record. Through the second half of 2020, the Chinese Ministry of Foreign Affairs and the People's Liberation Army declined to publish a casualty figure, describing questions about numbers as unhelpful to de-escalation. On 19 February 2021, eight months after the clash, China acknowledged four dead: the PLA Daily and the Central Military Commission honoured four soldiers killed at Galwan, naming a battalion commander and three soldiers, and awarded a posthumous honorary title to a regimental commander who had been seriously injured. Chinese state media at the same moment published an account of the clash attributing its outbreak to Indian violations of the consensus reached on 6 June 2020. In February 2021, the Russian news agency TASS reported, citing sources, that forty five Chinese servicemen had died, a figure the Chinese embassy in Moscow and Chinese state media rejected. American intelligence assessments, reported by American and Indian newspapers during 2020, placed Chinese deaths substantially above the four later acknowledged, in the range of thirty five or more. The honest summary is that twenty Indian deaths are officially stated and individually named, four Chinese deaths are officially acknowledged by China, and every higher Chinese figure belongs to a named holder whose claim the reader can weigh: Indian official briefings, TASS reporting, American intelligence assessments. An article that printed any of those figures as settled fact would be laundering attribution into certainty, and this pillar's charter forbids the laundering.

What the Chinese statements of June 2020 did settle was the diplomatic frame Beijing chose. On 17 June 2020, Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi spoke with External Affairs Minister S. Jaishankar by telephone, and the two readouts diverged in the manner that became characteristic: the Indian readout recorded agreement that the situation would be handled in a responsible manner and that the disengagement understanding of 6 June 2020 would be implemented; the Chinese readout recorded Wang Yi's demand that India punish those responsible and his statement that Indian frontline troops had crossed the line and attacked Chinese personnel. The Galwan Valley sovereignty question sat beneath the exchange. In the days after the clash, Chinese statements asserted that the Galwan Valley lay on the Chinese side of the Line of Actual Control, a formulation the Indian Ministry of External Affairs rejected on 20 June 2020 as an untenable and exaggerated claim inconsistent with China's own past positions. The sovereignty assertion mattered domestically in India out of proportion to its cartographic content, because it arrived in the same week as the casualty list. A government that had been charged by its opposition with conceding territory was handed, by Beijing's own spokesman, a claim that converted patrol friction into annexationist language. The political space for a quiet, purely military management of the crisis, of the kind that had contained Depsang in 2013, narrowed with each such statement. The space for an architectural answer, one that punished through markets and alignments rather than through escalation at Patrol Point 14, widened correspondingly. The decoupling reading does not require the implausible claim that ministers sat in New Delhi designing a three domain programme in the last week of June 2020. It requires only the documented sequence: a political shock, a sovereignty claim that deepened the shock, and then a series of instruments, each signed and dated, that together built the architecture the programme later displayed.

### What did the corps commander talks achieve between 2020 and 2024?

The corps commander talks, opened at Chushul Moldo on 6 June 2020 and continued through twenty one rounds by February 2024, converted a confrontation that had killed twenty soldiers into a managed process: the talks produced each staged pullback, at Pangong Tso, Gogra, Hot Springs and finally Depsang and Demchok, and kept the line free of further fatalities.

The talks deserve their own paragraph in any account of the moment, because they are the institutional spine of the third domain this article maps. The first round preceded the clash; the second, on 22 June 2020 at Moldo, met a week after it, with the Indian side led from the third round onward by Lieutenant General Harinder Singh and later by his successors at XIV Corps. Fourteen rounds had been held by January 2022, sixteen by the middle of that year, and the twenty first round met on 19 February 2024. Between rounds, the Working Mechanism for Consultation and Coordination on India China Border Affairs, the diplomatic channel created in 2012, met in parallel. The joint statements issued after several rounds used a vocabulary of mutual and equal security, phased and verified disengagement, and the maintenance of peace in the border areas as the basis for restoring the wider relationship. The Indian government's consistent public position, stated by External Affairs Minister Jaishankar in interviews and in his book The India Way of 2020 and restated across 2021, 2022 and 2023, was that the state of the border would determine the state of the relationship: normalisation of trade, investment and political contact was made formally conditional on disengagement and de-escalation at the line. That conditional formula is the diplomatic expression of the decoupling architecture. It converted the app bans and the investment screening from improvised retaliation into a stated doctrine with a stated exit condition, and it held through four years of negotiation until the October 2024 announcement declared the condition, for the remaining friction points, met.

## The Domestic Contest of June 2020: An All Party Meeting and a Sentence

The Galwan moment was made in Parliament and in press conferences as much as in the valley, and the domestic contest of June 2020 is the clearest evidence that the pillar's framework, foreign policy as domestic politics, is the right lens for the episode. On 19 June 2020, four days after the clash, Narendra Modi chaired an all party meeting held by video conference, attended by the leaders of the principal national and regional parties. The Prime Minister assured the meeting that Indian forces were fully capable of defending the country's territory, and he made a formulation that governed the politics of the following months. As the Prime Minister's Office published the remarks, Modi stated that neither had anyone intruded into Indian territory, nor was anyone inside Indian territory, nor had any Indian post been captured. The opposition's response was immediate and turned on the sentence's plain reading. The Indian National Congress, through statements by Rahul Gandhi in the days before and after the meeting and through the party's formal questions, charged that the formulation contradicted the premise of the clash itself: if no intrusion had occurred, the Congress asked, why had twenty soldiers died, and had the government conceded the Chinese claim that the Galwan Valley was Chinese territory? Sonia Gandhi, speaking at the all party meeting, had asked the government when Chinese troops had occupied Indian territory and in what numbers, questions the published exchange left publicly unanswered. The Prime Minister's Office issued a clarification on 20 June 2020 stating that the remarks had referred to the situation at the specific points following the bravery of the armed forces, that attempts at transgression had been foiled, and that a mischievous interpretation was being given to the Prime Minister's words by a section of opinion. The clarification did not retire the sentence. It entered the permanent record of the controversy, cited by the opposition through the monsoon session of Parliament in September 2020, when Congress members demanded a discussion on the border situation and the government offered, and delivered, the Defence Minister's statement of 15 September 2020 instead of a debate with voting.

The contest establishes two things for the adjudication that closes this article. First, the government's China policy after Galwan was made under sustained domestic interrogation, and the instruments of the decoupling programme can be read partly as answers to that interrogation: each app ban order, each statement on disengagement, addressed a public that included grieving families, an opposition asking whether territory had been lost, and a governing party whose nationalist credentials were the asset under challenge. A purely strategic reading, which explains Indian behaviour as a signal to Beijing, cannot explain the timing and publicity of the domestic instruments. An app ban is a poor signal to a general staff; it is an excellent answer to a television debate. Second, the opposition's strongest case deserves statement at its strongest, because the charter of this series requires it and because the case identifies the genuine limit of the decoupling reading. Rahul Gandhi's charge, made in statements through June and July 2020, was that the Prime Minister had surrendered Indian territory to Chinese aggression and had misled the country about the situation at the line. The government's answer, made at its strongest, was that disengagement was being pursued from a position of counter deployment, that the deceased soldiers had died preventing a change in the status quo at Patrol Point 14 rather than after conceding one, and that the Prime Minister's sentence described the position after the clash, in which no Chinese force sat on a captured Indian post in the Galwan Valley. Which reading of the sentence the record supports remains a disputed point: the words as published, the clarification as issued, and the two parties' glosses are all on the record, and the article marks the dispute rather than settling it. What is not disputed is the consequence. From June 2020 onward, every Indian government action toward China was calibrated for a domestic audience that had been promised, in the Prime Minister's own words, that nothing had been lost. The decoupling architecture was built under that promise, and its instruments were chosen to be visible.

The governing party's own machinery amplified the visibility. Ministers described the app bans in the language of a digital strike, a phrase used by senior Bharatiya Janata Party figures in the weeks after 29 June 2020, borrowing the vocabulary of the September 2016 cross Line of Control operation and of the February 2019 Balakot air strike to place an economic instrument inside a military frame. Boycott calls against Chinese goods circulated through trade bodies; the Confederation of All India Traders announced campaigns against Chinese imports in June 2020, and customs clearances for Chinese consignments slowed at Indian ports in the same weeks, widely reported as an administrative consequence of the crisis. None of these episodes appears in a gazette. All of them belong to the domestic political record of the moment, because they show the constituency the government's formal instruments addressed. The earlier episodes of this pillar ran on smaller publics: non-alignment was contested in Parliament and in the press of the 1950s, the 1971 war was ratified by an election fought months before it, the 1998 tests were defended before a Parliament that had installed the testing government weeks earlier. Galwan ran on the largest public any of these moments had faced, a public connected by the same Chinese owned platforms whose banning became the programme's first instrument. The irony was noted at the time and belongs in the record: TikTok, banned on 29 June 2020, had been one of the principal channels through which the ruling party's own messaging reached young voters in small town India. The ban's cost fell partly on the government's own communication ecology, which is one reason scholars of the episode treat the ban as evidence that the signal was meant seriously. A government does not disable its own megaphone for a gesture.

## The Funerals and the Public: Mourning as a Political Fact

Between the all party meeting and the first app ban order lay a week of funerals, and no account of the Galwan moment's domestic mechanics is complete without them. The twenty dead were returned to their home districts in the third week of June 2020, in the middle of the pandemic's first wave and its restrictions on gathering, and the funerals were nevertheless public events: state honours, crowds in defiance or in relaxation of the restrictions, ministers and chief ministers in attendance, and an unbroken presence in the regional press of a dozen states. Colonel Babu's funeral at Suryapet drew thousands. The government announced ex gratia payments and the states added their own; the names of the twenty entered the record in a form unusual for Indian military casualties on this frontier, individually published, individually mourned, attached to villages and regiments and families whose interviews ran for weeks. The political effect requires neither exaggeration nor theory. A border crisis with unnamed costs can be managed in the grammar of diplomacy. A border crisis whose costs have faces, home districts and televised cremations creates a public that expects an answer proportionate to its grief, and that audits the government against the expectation. The opposition understood the audit and conducted its campaign in its terms, asking whether the dead had been sent unprepared; the government understood it and conducted its programme in the same terms, presenting each instrument as a reply. The decoupling architecture was the reply that a modern regulatory state could give at scale without firing a shot: visible, punitive in direction, costless in Indian lives. Read the sequence backwards from the funerals and the choice of instruments loses its mystery. The question the government had to answer was never only what would deter Beijing. It was what would satisfy Suryapet.

The mourning also set the episode apart from the pillar's earlier foreign policy moments in a way the synthesis must register. The dead of 1962 were mourned in an India without private television; the names of the Kargil dead of 1999, in the conflict this pillar's periodisation treats within the Vajpayee chapters, were the first to be carried into Indian homes by satellite news, and the Kargil precedent is the Galwan public's true ancestor. Galwan extended the Kargil pattern by one decisive element: the same handset that carried the funeral pictures carried the applications of the adversary's platform economy, and the citizen who watched a cremation on a Chinese owned application in June 2020 was offered, on 29 June, the state's removal of the application itself. The intimacy of that sequence, grief and remedy arriving through the same screen, is the domestic political texture that distinguishes the Galwan moment from every earlier case in this pillar, and it is invisible to both the military and the strategic framings. The architectural framing exists to make it visible.

## Domain One: The App Bans and the Architecture of Economic Decoupling

On 29 June 2020, fourteen days after the clash, the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology issued an order under Section 69A of the Information Technology Act 2000 prohibiting fifty nine mobile applications, the overwhelming majority of them Chinese owned or Chinese developed, on grounds the order stated as the protection of the sovereignty and integrity of India, the defence of India, the security of the state and public order, supported by findings that the applications were engaged in activities prejudicial to those interests, including the transmission of user data to servers outside India. The list named TikTok, the short video platform whose Indian user base the company estimated at two hundred million, WeChat, the Tencent messaging service, UC Browser, the Alibaba owned browser with a commanding share of the Indian mobile browser market, Helo, Likee, Shein and Club Factory. The order was the first instrument of the post Galwan programme, and its legal form matters to the argument. Section 69A empowers the central government to block public access to information through a computer resource, through a procedure with an examination committee and recorded reasons, and the same section had been used against individual items of content for a decade. Its use against an entire category of foreign owned platforms, in a single order of fifty nine applications, converted a content moderation power into an instrument of economic statecraft. The conversion was deliberate and was defended as such: the Minister for Electronics and Information Technology, Ravi Shankar Prasad, described the June 2020 bans in Parliament and in public statements as a measure for data sovereignty, and the government's stated case rested on the data practices of the banned applications rather than on the border situation alone, a framing that gave the order a legal durability an openly retaliatory measure would have lacked.

The first order was not the last. On 27 July 2020, forty seven further applications, largely clones and variants of the banned fifty nine, were prohibited. On 2 September 2020, a third order banned one hundred and eighteen applications, including PUBG Mobile, the Tencent associated game whose Indian player base ran into tens of millions and whose competitive circuit had become a fixture of Indian youth culture. On 24 November 2020, a fourth order banned forty three more, including Alibaba's AliExpress retail application. The four orders of 2020 therefore prohibited two hundred and sixty seven applications in total, a figure this article states from the arithmetic of the orders themselves, and in February 2022 a further order banned fifty four applications on the same statutory ground, carrying the programme into its second year. The February 2022 order matters to the reading because it defeats the interpretation of the bans as a spasm of June 2020 grief. A spasm does not issue its fifth order twenty months later, on a Tuesday, to modest press attention. A programme does. The bans also survived a change in the factual basis of the original complaint: several banned applications restructured, divested or sought re-entry through Indian partners, and the prohibitions held, with the government treating the orders as settled policy rather than as bargaining counters to be traded at the corps commander table. When disengagement advanced at Pangong Tso in February 2021, no application returned. When the October 2024 disengagement was announced, no application returned. The separation between the border track and the economic track, once established, was itself a structural feature of the architecture, and it is the feature the strategic framing most often misses.

### Which law authorised the app bans, and why does the legal form matter?

The app bans were issued by the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology under Section 69A of the Information Technology Act 2000, the provision for blocking public access to information in the interest of sovereignty, defence, security and public order; the statutory form gave a retaliatory impulse the durability of law.

The commercial consequences were large enough to constitute a domestic political fact of their own. TikTok's exit ended the Indian operations of ByteDance's flagship product in its largest foreign market and displaced an economy of creators whose incomes had been built on the platform; Indian competitors, including Josh, Moj and Chingari, raised capital and users on the vacancy, a substitution pattern the government cited as vindication. PUBG Mobile's ban produced the episode's most instructive corporate sequel: the game's publisher restructured its Indian presence, and a reworked version, Battlegrounds Mobile India, launched in July 2021 under Krafton, the South Korean parent, with Tencent's publishing role removed, before facing its own temporary removal in July 2022 on the same statutory ground and returning in 2023. The sequence shows the architecture operating with precision rather than with a blunt nationalism: the objection was never to a game, or to foreign ownership as such, but to a data and control nexus traced to China, and the remedy demanded was the severing of that nexus. Scholars of Chinese platform power made the complementary point from the other side: the bans demonstrated that market access, granted in the decades of engagement, could be withdrawn by a host state at speed when the security framing changed, and Chinese commentary of 2020 said so plainly, with the Chinese Ministry of Commerce protesting the measures as discriminatory and the Chinese embassy in New Delhi charging that the bans violated World Trade Organization rules and the interests of Indian consumers. Both protests are on the record and are recorded here; the adjudication section weighs them.

The orders also generated a documentary trail that future historians of the decoupling will mine, and the trail is unusually institutional. Each Section 69A order passed through the committee procedure the rules prescribe, and the correspondence between the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology, the Ministry of Home Affairs and the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade through the second half of 2020, preserved in the two ministries' archives, records the coordination by which a border crisis became an economic programme: which applications were listed, on what intelligence inputs, with what treatment of Indian employees and Indian data. Readers who work with such scanned ministerial files in bulk will know the practical point that the archive's value depends on legibility, and a researcher assembling the Galwan orders alongside the investment screening files is well served by [an office file viewer for Excel, DOCX and PPTX documents](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) when the correspondence tables arrive in mixed formats. The point is not clerical. The decoupling reading stands or falls on documents, and the documents exist in unusual density for an episode of this kind, because every instrument of the programme had to be written, reasoned and signed. That density is what separates the architectural reading from the commentary that surrounded the episode, and it is why this article treats the programme as a text to be read rather than a mood to be described.

## Press Note 3 and the Screening of Chinese Investment

The second instrument of Domain One predates the clash, and its chronology carries an argument of its own. On 17 April 2020, the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade issued Press Note 3 of 2020, amending the consolidated foreign direct investment policy to provide that an entity of a country sharing a land border with India, or whose beneficial owner is situated in such a country, could invest in India only under the government approval route, with prior security clearance where applicable; transfers of ownership that brought beneficial ownership within the rule were brought under the same requirement. India shares land borders with Pakistan, Afghanistan, China, Nepal, Bhutan, Bangladesh and Myanmar. Pakistan and Bangladesh already faced approval requirements in substance; Nepal, Bhutan and Myanmar generated negligible investment flows of the kind the note addressed; the note's operative target, understood by every ministry, market and editorial page that received it, was China. The immediate trigger on the record was the disclosure, days earlier, that the People's Bank of China had raised its holding in the Housing Development Finance Corporation above one percent during the market collapse of March 2020, an acquisition executed within the automatic route that the note then closed. The government's stated rationale was the prevention of opportunistic takeovers of Indian companies whose valuations had fallen in the pandemic. Chinese official reaction was swift: the Chinese embassy in New Delhi stated on 20 April 2020 that the measure violated World Trade Organization principles of non discrimination and called for its revision, and Chinese commentary named the note a protectionist turn.

Press Note 3 was issued seven weeks before the first May confrontations in Ladakh and two months before Galwan. The decoupling architecture therefore did not begin at Galwan; the screening state was already under construction when the People's Bank of China bought its HDFC shares in a falling market, in the first shock of the pandemic. What Galwan changed was the note's political weather. Before June 2020, Press Note 3 was a contested trade measure, criticised by Indian startup founders whose funding rounds ran through Chinese venture capital, Tencent and Alibaba money having been present in a generation of Indian unicorns, and defended by the government as a pandemic safeguard. After June 2020, the note became untouchable. No Indian political formation of consequence proposed its dilution while soldiers stood eyeball to eyeball in Ladakh, and the approval route hardened into a near freeze: reporting by Indian business newspapers through 2021 and 2022, based on ministry data, counted Chinese investment proposals worth several billion dollars held in the approval process, with clearances granted selectively, including for some component manufacturers, and refused or left pending for others. The note also acquired descendants. The government's production linked incentive schemes, announced from March 2020 onward and extended across fourteen sectors by 2021, carried eligibility and security conditions that operated alongside the screening regime, and the trusted sources directive for telecommunications, announced in December 2020 and effective from June 2021 under the National Security Directive on the telecom sector, subjected network equipment procurement to a national security clearance in which Chinese vendors did not appear. Each instrument was general in form and Chinese in incidence, a drafting pattern that Indian administrative law made available and that the political conditions of the Galwan moment made durable. Domain One's feature, to state the article's analytic point plainly, is institutional: the decoupling was executed through the ordinary machinery of the Indian regulatory state, by named ministries, under existing statutes and policy instruments, and it is therefore reversible by the same machinery, a fact whose significance the October 2024 section examines.

Domain One acquired a third instrument in the same season, in the least visible corner of the state: its own purchasing. On 23 July 2020, the Department of Expenditure in the Ministry of Finance amended the General Financial Rules to insert a provision under Rule 144 requiring that bidders from countries sharing a land border with India be registered with a competent authority, with political and security clearance, before they could participate in public procurement, and the requirement was extended to the Government e-Marketplace through which ministries buy. On 3 July 2020, the Ministry of Power had already ordered that power sector equipment imports from prior reference countries, the administrative category under which China and Pakistan fell, would require prior permission and would be tested in designated Indian laboratories for malware and concealed communications capability. The procurement instruments lack the drama of an app ban, and no minister called them a digital strike. Their significance is architectural in the strictest sense: they rewired the default settings of the Indian state's own demand, so that exclusion no longer required a decision in each case but operated as the standing rule from which exceptions had to argue their way out. A programme that reaches the procurement manual has passed the point of gesture. It has also acquired the property that most distinguishes architecture from announcement, which is that reversing it requires the same tedious paperwork that built it, undertaken by officials who must sign their names to the reversal. The durability the programme displayed through the October 2024 thaw was manufactured in precisely these unglamorous amendments of July 2020, and any future account of the decoupling that confines itself to the headline bans will miss the load bearing walls.

### Why were Huawei and ZTE absent from India's 5G trials?

Huawei and ZTE were absent from the 5G trials the Department of Telecommunications authorised in May 2021 because the trusted sources regime, announced in December 2020, routed network equipment through a national security designation in which the two Chinese vendors were not listed, completing their exclusion from India's next generation networks.

The 5G exclusion deserves a paragraph of its own because it shows Domain One extending from the visible economy of applications into the invisible economy of infrastructure. Indian telecommunications networks had been built substantially on Chinese equipment in the 3G and 4G generations; Huawei and ZTE supplied gear to private operators, and the state owned BSNL's procurement had long been contested on security grounds. Through 2019, the government's position had remained formally open: a committee under the Principal Scientific Adviser considered the 5G question, and Huawei was permitted a demonstration presence at industry events. The Galwan moment closed the question without a formal ban. No order prohibited Huawei by name. Instead, the trusted sources architecture of December 2020 created a designation process, the May 2021 trial authorisations named Ericsson, Nokia, Samsung and Indian equipment makers as the vendors with whom operators could conduct trials, and the commercial 5G auctions and rollouts of 2022 and 2023 proceeded on non Chinese equipment across the private networks. A scholar reading only formal prohibitions would find no 5G ban. A scholar reading the procurement record finds a complete exclusion. The gap between the formal record and the procurement record is exactly the kind of evidence the architectural reading exists to catch, and it recurs across the decoupling programme: the instruments are frequently facially neutral, procedurally ordinary and substantively categorical. Chinese scholars and officials named the pattern discrimination in their protests of 2020 and 2021. Indian officials named it security screening. The effect, which is the article's concern, is not in dispute.

## Domain Two: The Strategic Shifts, the Quad and the Border Build Up

If Domain One removed Chinese presence from Indian markets, Domain Two built the alternative geometry within which a post Galwan India would stand. Its central institution is the Quadrilateral Security Dialogue, the grouping of India, the United States, Japan and Australia whose revival from 2017 this pillar has traced through [the maritime-architectural-substantive-domestic-political-content framework](/2020/10/29/india-indian-ocean-strategy-explained/). The Quad existed before Galwan; foreign ministers of the four countries had met in New York in September 2019. What Galwan changed was the tempo and the level. In March 2020, before the Ladakh crisis, Quad officials had already held an exceptional consultation on the pandemic. In October 2020, the four foreign ministers met in Tokyo, with S. Jaishankar stating publicly that the four countries shared a vision of a free, open and inclusive Indo Pacific, language the Chinese foreign ministry answered by dismissing the grouping as an exclusive clique. On 3 November 2020, the Malabar naval exercise opened in the Bay of Bengal with the Australian Navy participating for the first time since 2007, completing the four nation naval format that India had declined to restore for thirteen years, in part from sensitivity to Chinese objection. On 12 March 2021, the Quad met at leaders level for the first time in its history, virtually, with Narendra Modi joining Joseph Biden, Yoshihide Suga and Scott Morrison; the leaders issued a joint statement, The Spirit of the Quad, and announced a vaccine partnership whose production centre was India. On 24 September 2021, the four leaders met in person in Washington, the first in person summit, issuing a joint statement that addressed supply chains, technology standards and maritime security. The sequence from ministerial to virtual summit to in person summit occupied eighteen months. No comparable acceleration exists in the Quad's earlier record, and the acceleration's dates track the Ladakh standoff month by month: each summit step was taken while Chinese forces remained deployed at the friction points, and Indian official statements of the period linked the border situation to the wider relationship in terms that made the linkage available to any reader.

The causation question must be handled with the care the charter requires. Quad officials of all four countries denied, in statement after statement, that the grouping targeted any single country, and the grouping's expanded agenda on vaccines, climate and critical technology was real. Australian participation in Malabar had been sought by Canberra for reasons of its own deteriorating relationship with Beijing, which by late 2020 included Chinese trade measures against Australian exports. American policy had its own trajectory under two administrations. The article's claim is therefore not that Galwan created the Quad's ascent. The claim is that Galwan removed the Indian hesitation that had been the ascent's principal brake. Indian governments of both major formations had rationed their Quad commitment for a decade, balancing the grouping against the BRICS and Shanghai Cooperation Organisation memberships and against the management of Beijing; the documentary trace of that rationing runs from India's refusal of an Australian Malabar invitation in 2018 to the formal statements that kept the Quad at officials level through 2019. After June 2020, the rationing ended in public view, and it ended without a parliamentary revolt, an opposition campaign or a media reckoning of the kind that had attended earlier Indian alignments. The domestic political condition for the ascent, a public that had buried twenty soldiers, was created by the clash. That is the sense in which a foreign policy framework explains a strategic shift: the shift's external logic belonged to the region, but its Indian political possibility belonged to Galwan.

Domain Two's second element ran along the border itself. The Border Roads Organisation, the Ministry of Defence agency that builds India's strategic roads, received budgets and deadlines of a new order after 2020: the defence budget documents of 2021 and 2022 recorded increased allocations for border infrastructure, and the organisation's own reporting counted dozens of bridges and road stretches inaugurated by the Defence Minister in the frontier states through 2021, 2022 and 2023. The Darbuk Shyok Daulat Beg Oldie road, completed in 2019 after two decades of construction, acquired feeder roads and bridges; the Atal Tunnel beneath the Rohtang Pass, opened by the Prime Minister on 3 October 2020, gave the Manali Leh axis an all weather link whose strategic value every commentary of the day recorded; the Sela Tunnel in Arunachal Pradesh, inaugurated on 9 March 2024, extended the same logic to the eastern sector; airfields and advanced landing grounds in Ladakh and the northeast received upgrades recorded in the Ministry of Defence annual reports. India also raised a new mountain strike formation's deployment orientation toward the northern borders and, by the Army's own statements, rebalanced a strike corps from the Pakistan front to the China front during 2021, a reallocation reported at the time and confirmed in subsequent official remarks. The domestic significance of the build up is easily missed because roads do not hold press conferences. Yet the infrastructure programme was the domain of the post Galwan settlement with the longest physical life: the app bans can be lifted by a notification, the Quad can drift, but a tunnel under the Sela Pass will carry Indian divisions for a century. Chinese statements through the period protested Indian infrastructure work near the line as a cause of friction, returning the compliment of 1962, when Indian forward posts in unroaded country had dramatised the gap between a claim and the logistics behind it. The comparison with 1962, argued fully in the synthesis, begins here: the state that answered the Galwan clash built roads first and fastest among its long term instruments.

The strategic domain also carried a quieter element, the management of India's other memberships. India attended BRICS and Shanghai Cooperation Organisation summits through the standoff years, hosted both in 2023, and received Xi Jinping at neither, the two leaders meeting only on multilateral margins, at Bali in November 2022 for a brief exchange and at Johannesburg in August 2023 for a conversation whose readouts again diverged in emphasis. The duality was the point. India's post Galwan posture was never a defection from one camp to another of the kind the Cold War vocabulary supplies; it was a reweighting, executed by a government that described its doctrine as multi alignment in the pages of the External Affairs Minister's own book, and the reweighting's direction was measurable in summit levels, exercise rosters and procurement files. The pillar's long arc makes the measurement intelligible. Non-alignment in its founding form, as the account of [the foreign-policy-as-domestic-politics framework](/2020/09/21/non-alignment-indian-domestic-politics/) showed, was a domestic settlement before it was a foreign doctrine: it protected a poor republic's development budget and a ruling party's ideological centre. The post 2020 reweighting served a comparable domestic function for a different settlement, protecting a manufacturing ambition expressed through the production linked incentives and a ruling coalition whose nationalism required visible firmness toward Beijing. The instruments differ across seventy years. The structure of the explanation does not.

## The Domestic Geography of the Border: Ladakh After October 2019

The border that produced the Galwan moment had been redrawn on the Indian side eight months before the clash, and the redrawing belongs to the domestic record of the episode. On 31 October 2019, under the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act passed in August 2019, Ladakh became a Union Territory administered directly from New Delhi, separated from the erstwhile state of Jammu and Kashmir and governed without a legislature of its own. The reorganisation was welcomed in Leh, whose Buddhist majority had demanded Union Territory status for decades, and received with reserve in Kargil, whose Shia majority had closer ties to the Kashmir Valley's politics. When Chinese forces moved at the friction points in May 2020, they confronted a frontier whose Indian administration had passed, months earlier, from Srinagar to the Union Home Ministry, and the crisis was managed accordingly: the Lieutenant Governor at Leh, the XIV Corps at the same station, and the ministries in New Delhi formed the chain through which the standoff's civil consequences were handled. The Galwan dead included soldiers recruited from across India, but the ground they died on had a new constitutional description, and the description was itself one of the decisive acts of the governing formation's first year. The pillar's framework insists on the connection: a foreign policy crisis landed on a domestic constitutional settlement that was eleven months old, and the settlement's defenders and critics read the crisis through it. Leh's political leadership, through the period of the standoff, pressed the government on the security of the grazing grounds used by Changpa nomads near the line, a constituency level detail that national commentary rarely reached; Indian press reporting through 2020 and 2021 recorded Changpa herders describing reduced access to pastures in the areas facing Chinese deployments, a claim the Army answered by describing facilitation measures. The herders' pastures are the smallest unit of the decoupling story: sovereignty, at the line itself, is measured in grazing seasons.

Ladakh's own politics then organised around the standoff's long duration. In 2020, the Leh Apex Body, a coalition of religious, political and trade organisations, was formed to press demands including statehood for Ladakh, inclusion in the Sixth Schedule of the Constitution, a public service commission and parliamentary representation; the Kargil Democratic Alliance carried a parallel platform from the other district, and the two bodies arrived at a shared four point agenda. The Ministry of Home Affairs constituted a high powered committee under the Minister of State for Home Affairs in January 2023 to examine the demands, and talks between the ministry and the Ladakhi bodies proceeded through 2023 and 2024, inconclusively by the public record. The sequence matters to this article because it completes the domestic circuit of the Galwan moment. The clash nationalised Ladakh: a region of under three hundred thousand people became, for the Indian public, the named frontier of a continental rivalry, its district towns datelines in a four year crisis. Nationalisation brought the infrastructure programme described in Domain Two, whose roads and tunnels run through Ladakhi valleys, and it brought an intensified Ladakhi politics of constitutional demand, whose leaders negotiated with the same Home Ministry that administered the territory. The Vibrant Villages Programme, announced in the Union Budget of February 2022 and approved by the Cabinet in February 2023 with villages along the northern border as its first field, made the logic explicit in policy language: the development of border villages, their roads, housing, telecommunications and livelihoods, was framed by the government as a component of border management, with the border population described in official statements as part of the country's first line of awareness. A programme that treats villagers as a strategic asset is a foreign policy instrument written in the grammar of rural development. The pillar exists to notice such instruments. Galwan's longest domestic shadow may fall here, in the conversion of India's Himalayan periphery from a neglected margin into a subsidised, connected, strategically described frontier, a conversion whose Ladakhi subjects are, by the record of their own organisations, determined to negotiate its terms rather than merely receive them.

## Domain Three: The Disengagement Architecture from Pangong to Depsang

The third domain managed the border, and its architecture was a sequence of dated stages, each negotiated at the corps commander table and each leaving the other two domains untouched. The first stage came at Pangong Tso. In the summer of 2020, Chinese forces had occupied the ridge lines between Finger 4 and Finger 8 on the lake's north bank, in the area where Indian patrols had traditionally moved to Finger 8, and in late August 2020 Indian troops occupied heights on the south bank, at Rechin La and Rezang La, positions whose tactical value Indian official accounts and the press of the period recorded as leverage. On 10 February 2021, Defence Minister Rajnath Singh announced in Parliament that the two sides had agreed a disengagement at Pangong Tso: Chinese forces would withdraw east of Finger 8 on the north bank, Indian forces would return to their permanent base near Finger 3, tanks and mechanised elements on the south bank would pull back, and patrolling in the contested stretch would be suspended by agreement until a further settlement. The withdrawal was completed in days, verified by both sides, and photographed. The formula was significant beyond the lake: disengagement created buffer arrangements in which patrolling rights were held in abeyance rather than surrendered, a device that allowed both governments to claim that no territory had been conceded in law while accepting that men would no longer stand where they had stood. The opposition's critique, voiced by the Congress through 2021, held that the buffer zones lay largely in areas India had patrolled before 2020 and therefore institutionalised a loss; the government's answer held that the arrangements were temporary, local and reciprocal. The dispute is recorded here as a dispute, with each side's strongest form; the documentary fact beneath it is the statement of 10 February 2021 and the pullback that followed.

The stages that followed Pangong were slower and smaller, and their tempo is part of the architecture's meaning. At Gogra, in the Kongka La sector, disengagement was completed in August 2021 after the twelfth round of corps commander talks, with a buffer arrangement on the Pangong model. At Hot Springs, Patrol Point 15, disengagement was announced on 8 September 2022, days before the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation summit at Samarkand, after the sixteenth round of talks. Each stage removed one friction point from the active list and left the hardest points, the Depsang Plains in the north, where Chinese deployments had blocked Indian patrol access to several patrolling points since 2020 by the Indian account, and Demchok in the south, for a final negotiation that took two more years. The Indian position through those years, restated by the External Affairs Minister in interviews and by the Foreign Secretary in briefings, was that disengagement at the remaining points was the precondition for any wider normalisation; the Chinese position, restated by the Chinese foreign ministry and by Ambassadorial statements, was that the border question should be placed in its appropriate position in the relationship and should not define the whole of it. The two formulas describe the same standoff from opposite ends: New Delhi made the border the gatekeeper of the relationship, Beijing asked that the relationship be allowed to proceed around the border. The decoupling programme lived inside the Indian formula. As long as the gate held, the app bans, the screening and the 5G exclusions required no fresh justification; they were the standing expression of a stated doctrine, renewable by silence. Domain Three thus performed a function for Domains One and Two that pure confrontation could not have performed: it gave the architecture time.

The endpoint of the sequence arrived in the autumn of 2024. On 21 October 2024, Foreign Secretary Vikram Misri announced in New Delhi that India and China had reached an agreement on patrolling arrangements along the Line of Actual Control in the border areas, leading to disengagement and a resolution of the issues that had emerged in 2020; the agreement covered the remaining friction points at Depsang and Demchok, and the Ministry of External Affairs stated that patrolling would resume in those areas on the pattern that had obtained before the 2020 standoff. On 23 October 2024, Narendra Modi and Xi Jinping met formally at Kazan, on the margins of the BRICS summit, their first bilateral meeting in the formal format since the October 2019 informal summit at Mamallapuram; the Indian readout recorded agreement that the Special Representatives on the boundary question would meet to stabilise and rebuild the relationship, and that the two leaders had affirmed that stable ties served regional and global peace. Chinese readouts recorded Xi's formulation that the two countries should be partners rather than rivals and should handle differences properly. Verification of the Depsang and Demchok pullbacks proceeded in the following weeks by the two armies' established mechanisms, and Indian statements through November 2024 described the disengagement as complete and patrolling as resumed. The disengagement architecture had closed its list: Pangong in February 2021, Gogra in August 2021, Hot Springs in September 2022, Depsang and Demchok in October 2024. Four years and four months after Galwan, the border returned, formally, to a managed condition.

### Did the October 2024 disengagement reverse the decoupling programme?

The October 2024 disengagement did not reverse the decoupling programme: the agreement of 21 October 2024 addressed patrolling and troop positions at Depsang and Demchok alone, lifted no app prohibition, amended no investment screening rule, restored no Chinese vendor to any Indian network, and left the strategic reweighting in place.

The question is the strongest counter to this article's reading, and the brief for the article requires the counter to be faced rather than waved past. The counter runs as follows. If the post 2020 instruments were an architecture of decoupling, the October 2024 settlement and the Kazan meeting should, on the reading's own logic, mark the architecture's dismantling or at least its embarrassment: the two governments agreed to stabilise and rebuild, the boundary mechanism was revived, direct flights and visa facilitation entered discussion in the following months by official accounts, and trade, which had never fallen, remained the relationship's ballast. A reading that predicted permanent estrangement would stand refuted by the handshakes. But the decoupling reading as this article has stated it predicts nothing about estrangement. It predicts that the instruments built between 2020 and 2024 are institutional rather than atmospheric: they exist as orders, notes, designations and summit formats, and they persist until other instruments of the same kind amend them. By that test, the autumn of 2024 is the reading's confirmation rather than its refutation. The disengagement was negotiated inside the architecture's own terms: India had stated since 2020 that border disengagement was the precondition for movement elsewhere, the movement elsewhere began only when the disengagement list closed, and the economic instruments were not placed on the table at Depsang or at Kazan. Engagement resumed, in other words, on a foundation the decoupling had poured. The relationship that reopened in October 2024 was not the relationship of 2019: it reopened without the two hundred and sixty seven banned applications, under a standing investment screening regime, on non Chinese telecommunications equipment, and with a Quad at leaders level. Whether that foundation proves permanent is a question the record cannot answer in advance, and the article does not pretend otherwise. What the record answers is the question of what the Galwan moment built. It built the foundation.

Shyam Saran, the former Foreign Secretary whose essays on the China relationship through 2020 to 2024 form one of the mandatory commentaries for this article, read the trajectory in a compatible spirit from within the strategic community. Saran argued across his columns and in his 2022 book How China Sees India and the World that the 2020 transgressions had altered the relationship's premises durably, that the assumption of a managed border underpinning economic engagement had been falsified by Beijing itself, and that India's response had to proceed on the premise that the old compact, prosperity in exchange for tranquillity, would not be restored in its earlier form. His reading differs from this article's in emphasis: Saran wrote as a strategist about China's intentions and India's options, where this article writes as a political history about India's domestic instruments. The difference is the pillar's difference. But the convergence matters for the adjudication, because Saran cannot be recruited as a partisan of the governing formation; his assessment that the premises changed in 2020 came from a former Foreign Secretary of a Congress led government, and it corroborates, from an independent position, the factual core of the decoupling reading: that after Galwan, the relationship's operating assumptions were rebuilt rather than restored.

## The Negotiation in Public: Readouts, Formulas and the Information Contest

The disengagement architecture was built in a negotiation that both governments conducted partly in public, and the public record of the negotiation is a primary source in its own right. From the telephone call of 17 June 2020 onward, the pattern of divergent readouts repeated with a regularity that scholars of the episode treat as data. After the Jaishankar Wang Yi meeting in Moscow on 10 September 2020, which produced a five point consensus on easing the situation, the two capitals published statements that agreed on the five points and differed on their meaning: the Indian statements emphasised disengagement as the first step and the restoration of peace as the condition for the relationship's repair, the Chinese statements emphasised that the boundary question should not be allowed to define the whole relationship and that differences should be managed through dialogue. The five point consensus is a genuine documentary artefact of the crisis, signed in effect by both foreign ministers in the margins of a Shanghai Cooperation Organisation meeting, and its subsequent history instructs: the consensus announced principles in September 2020 that the corps commanders required seventeen further months to translate into the Pangong pullback, and four further years to complete at Depsang and Demchok. Process, in Gokhale's grammar, was doing the work that communiqués could not.

The public formulas hardened into doctrines that the two sides then defended as positions of principle. The Indian formula, stated by Jaishankar in interviews through 2021 and 2022, in his remarks at international forums, and in the formulation he repeated with the least variation, held that the state of the border would determine the state of the relationship: peace and tranquillity in the border areas was the basis, in the language of the 1993 agreement itself, for the development of relations in other domains. The Chinese formula, stated by the foreign ministry's spokespersons and by Wang Yi, held that the border question was one part of a large relationship, should be placed in an appropriate position within it, and should not be permitted to hold the whole relationship hostage; Chinese statements through the period paired the formula with calls to resume exchanges, flights and trade facilitation. Each formula was addressed to three audiences at once: to the other capital, to third countries weighing their own China exposure, and to the domestic public whose support the formula's authors required. The Indian formula's domestic audience included an opposition that charged the government with conceding ground; the formula answered by making every concession of normalisation formally dependent on the other side's conduct at the line. The Chinese formula's domestic audience included a commercial establishment for whom the Indian market's partial closure carried measurable cost; the formula answered by presenting Beijing as the party of normalisation. An adjudication between the formulas on their merits belongs to strategy. A political history records what the formulas did: they converted a military standoff into a competition of public doctrines, conducted in the idiom of the 1993 and 1996 agreements both sides accused the other of violating, and they gave the decoupling programme its juridical cover. As long as the Indian formula stood as stated doctrine, the app bans and the screening required no separate defence; they were the doctrine's standing illustration.

The information contest ran underneath the formulas. Satellite imagery of the friction points, produced by commercial providers and published by Indian, Australian and American research institutions and newspapers, gave the public a visual record of deployments that an earlier generation of standoffs had denied it; the Australian Strategic Policy Institute's reporting on Chinese positions and the Indian press's publication of imagery from Pangong and Depsang made evasion of the factual questions, where forces stood and in what strength, progressively harder for both governments. The Chinese state media's February 2021 publication of its Galwan account, with video material, arrived eight months after the event and was read in India as an attempt to fix the narrative for a domestic audience at the moment of the Pangong settlement; Indian official responses restated the Army's account. The contest matters to the closing synthesis because it marks the episode's place in the history of Indian foreign policy's public. Non-alignment was argued in editorials. The 1971 war was argued on the radio and in the refugee camp reports. The 1998 tests were argued in the first years of Indian private television. Galwan was argued in satellite photographs, short video and the trending lists of platforms, two of which the Indian government then banned. The medium of the argument was itself one of the episode's stakes, and a government that banned the medium while winning the argument at home had demonstrated, to itself and to observers, a theory of information sovereignty that the app bans merely applied.

The analytical reading defended in this article is made citable by reducing it to a map. The map is labelled here so that a reader can cite it by name: Table 1, the Three-Domain Post-China Decoupling Pattern Map, sets out each domain of the post Galwan programme with its named instruments, its operating institutions and its dated record. A reader who disputes the reading can dispute the map row by row, which is the use an artifact exists to serve.

Three features of the map carry the argument. The first is that every cell names an institution rather than a sentiment: ministries, corps headquarters, a roads organisation, a dialogue format. A military clash framing can describe the first row's occasion but not its content; a strategic framing can describe the second row's direction but not its domestic possibility; the map is the article's evidence that a third description is required, and that the third description is the one the pillar's framework supplies.

## Adjudicating the Three Framings: Clash, Signal and Architecture

The named disagreement the article must adjudicate sets three framings against one another. The military clash framing, represented in the journalism of June 2020 and in the tactical literature that followed, reads Galwan as an encounter battle produced by a failed verification, local initiative and the lethal dynamics of a melee at altitude; its evidence is the terrain, the sequence at Patrol Point 14 and the two armies' statements. The strategic framing, represented in the work of scholars and former officials who read the episode as a Chinese coercive signal inside a continental rivalry, reads Galwan as a message about the boundary and about India's alignment choices; its evidence is the simultaneity of the May 2020 intrusions at several points, the Galwan Valley sovereignty assertion, and the pattern Bertil Lintner documented across his 2017 book and his post 2020 commentary, in which Chinese pressure on the Himalayan frontier recurs at moments chosen in Beijing. The decoupling framing, defended here, reads Galwan as the political condition under which an Indian government built a three domain architecture of economic removal, strategic reweighting and managed disengagement; its evidence is the documentary sequence set out in the map.

Each framing explains a residue the others leave. The military framing explains why the deaths occurred where and as they did, and no honest adjudication can dissolve the melee into policy: men died at a specific ridge for reasons that include the orders, the tempers and the terrain of that night. The strategic framing explains the simultaneity of May 2020, which the domestic record cannot: whatever New Delhi later built, the crisis began with coordinated Chinese movements at separated points, and the Indian literature that treats Beijing's timing as reactive must answer for the scale of the deployments that preceded any Indian instrument. Vijay Gokhale, the former Foreign Secretary and ambassador to China whose The Long Game of 2021 reconstructed Chinese negotiating practice across six episodes from 1949 to the Doklam settlement, supplies the adjudication's most useful discipline at this point. Gokhale's argument, stated at its strongest, is that Chinese negotiation proceeds by studied informality, by the exploitation of process, and by an asymmetry of patience, and that Indian negotiators err when they mistake a Chinese negotiating pause for a settlement of intent. Read through Gokhale, the four year disengagement sequence appears neither as Indian failure nor as Chinese retreat but as a negotiation conducted in the grammar his book describes, in which each stage was purchased with time and the final stage arrived when Beijing's own calendar, including the BRICS summit it sought to stabilise, made closure useful. The strategic framing, disciplined by Gokhale, survives the adjudication as the best account of Chinese conduct. It does not survive as a sufficient account of the episode, because the episode includes the Indian programme, and the programme is not explained by Chinese conduct at all. A framing of Galwan that cannot accommodate two hundred and sixty seven app bans is a framing of half the event.

Lintner's contribution to the strategic framing deserves separate statement, because his is the longest view any of the three framings can claim. In China's India War, published in 2017 from Chinese documents, declassified material and his own decades of reporting on the Himalayan frontier, Lintner argued that the 1962 war was prepared in a Chinese strategic culture that treated the frontier as a theatre for teaching lessons, and that the border question would recur as China's power grew. His post 2020 commentary applied the thesis directly: the May 2020 movements, in his reading, belonged to a pattern of calibrated pressure, timed to an India distracted by the pandemic and governed by a leader whose strongman image Beijing sought to puncture. The reading has the strength of documentary depth and the weakness of its own completeness: a framework that predicts pressure at every moment explains any moment at the cost of explaining the timing of none, and Lintner's account of Chinese conduct, like the strategic framing generally, falls silent at the Indian programme, which was not China's to design. The adjudication therefore assigns his work its proper jurisdiction. On the question of why Chinese forces stood where they stood in May 2020, the Lintner tradition and Gokhale's negotiating grammar together carry the strongest available account. On the question of what the Galwan moment did inside Indian politics, which is the question this pillar exists to ask, the strategic framing is a witness rather than a verdict.

The trade figures are the decoupling framing's hardest test, and the article puts them at full strength. India's imports from China grew through the decoupling years: by the Ministry of Commerce and Industry figures, bilateral trade reached approximately 125 billion dollars in 2021 and approximately 136 billion dollars in 2022, with the Indian deficit widening, driven by electronics components, active pharmaceutical ingredients, machinery and solar equipment. Chinese commentary and Indian critics of the government made the same point from opposite directions: a decoupling that leaves the deficit larger than before is, on this view, a performance for domestic galleries, a theatre of bans over a substrate of deepening dependence. The point deserves its force. It refutes a version of the decoupling claim that this article has not made, the claim that India separated its economy from China's. It does not refute the claim as stated, which concerns the sectors the state classified as sensitive and the instruments it built to govern them. Data, platforms, network equipment and investment entry were treated as security questions after June 2020 in a way they had not been treated before, and the treatment was executed in law. The substrate of dependence in components and pharmaceuticals was acknowledged by the government's own production linked incentive design, which targeted precisely those dependencies for reduction over a longer horizon, an admission in policy form that the decoupling was architectural and prospective rather than accomplished and total. The honest verdict is therefore a bounded one: the decoupling pattern is real as a structure of state action in named domains, incomplete as a description of the whole economic relationship, and misdescribed by both its celebrants, who call it a rupture, and its dismissers, who call it a performance. Between rupture and performance stands the documented middle: a screening state, built in four years, that changed the terms on which Chinese capital, code and equipment could enter India, and that survived the diplomatic thaw of its fourth year intact.

The opposition's economic critique adds a domestic dimension to the same test, and it should be stated in its strongest form before the verdict. Through 2021, 2022 and 2023, Congress spokesmen and the party's leadership put the trade figures to the government in Parliament and in press conferences: imports from China had risen to record levels, the deficit had widened past one hundred billion dollars on some presentations of the figures, and the government that banned applications had presided, on the opposition's charge, over a deepening dependence dressed as defiance. The charge was electorally pointed, because it turned the government's own nationalist standard against its commerce record, and it was pressed again during the parliamentary exchanges that followed the Tawang sector clash of December 2022 at Yangtse, when opposition members demanded a discussion the government declined to schedule in the form sought. The government's answer, given by ministers in the houses and by the Commerce Ministry in its data releases, rested on composition: the import basket was dominated by capital goods, components and pharmaceutical inputs that fed Indian production and exports, the deficit's growth reflected the growth of Indian manufacturing demand, and the production linked incentives were the instrument by which the dependencies would be reduced over time. Both the charge and the answer are recorded here without a verdict on their economics, because the article's question is architectural rather than econometric. What the exchange proves for the reading is that the decoupling programme was contested on its results in the domestic arena throughout its life, and survived the contest unamended. Programmes that are mere theatre are usually abandoned when the theatre is heckled for three consecutive years. This one was extended, in February 2022 and in the continuing operation of the screening regime, under the heckling.

One further test belongs to the record. The decoupling framing would be embarrassed if the instruments had been traded away at the negotiating table, since a programme exchanged for disengagement would reveal itself as leverage rather than architecture. The documentary sequence shows the opposite traffic. Disengagement stages in 2021 and 2022 coincided with no relaxation in the app prohibitions, no amendment of Press Note 3, and no restoration of Chinese vendors to network planning; the February 2022 app order, the programme's fifth, was issued in the same season as the fifteenth round of corps commander talks was being scheduled. The two tracks ran in parallel by design, and the Indian formula, the state of the border determining the state of the relationship, was honoured in its breach proof form: the border track moved when the border track moved, and the economic track did not move at all. Architecture is the word for a structure that bears loads without being renegotiated at every contact. The programme bore the load of a four year standoff and of the thaw that followed it. The framing stands.

## Closing the Pillar: Foreign Policy as Domestic Politics from 1947 to Galwan

A closing article owes its pillar a synthesis, and the synthesis here is a measurement across seventy three years. The pillar opened with [the foreign-policy-as-domestic-politics framework](/2020/09/21/non-alignment-indian-domestic-politics/), which read non-alignment as a settlement among Indian needs: a development budget protected from alliance costs, a ruling party's ideology given world historical dress, a postcolonial state's dignity performed before its own citizens. The pillar then followed the framework through the moments where foreign policy and domestic politics fused most completely: the 1962 war read through [the domestic-political-event framework](/2020/09/26/1962-china-war-domestic-political-event/), the 1971 war and its Indira moment, the 1971 Indo Soviet Treaty, the 1998 tests, the 2008 nuclear deal crisis, [the three-vertex architectural-substantive-content framework](/2020/10/24/china-pakistan-us-triangulation-india/) that organised the triangular contest, the Indian Ocean strategy, and the diaspora's conversion into a constituency through [the diaspora-as-foreign-policy-and-domestic-political-constituency framework](/2020/11/04/diaspora-foreign-policy-constituency/). Galwan closes the sequence by completing its education. In 1962, foreign policy failure destroyed a domestic settlement: the war broke the Nehruvian consensus, ended a defence minister, and began the long revision of the founding order. In 2020, foreign policy shock was converted, within weeks, into domestic construction: the same state that could not prevent a melee at Patrol Point 14 built, from the materials of its regulatory machinery, the most extensive programme of economic exclusion in the republic's history, and carried the programme through an election cycle, a parliamentary siege and a four year negotiation without surrendering any of its instruments. The contrast is the pillar's closing evidence for its framework. Foreign policy is domestic politics not only when it fails, and not only in the founding decades; it is domestic politics in the administrative state of the twenty first century, where a press note can do the work a mobilisation order once did.

The Galwan moment also sits inside the wider architecture of the Modi era that this series has mapped in other pillars: it was executed through [the post-2014 PMO architecture context](/2019/08/11/pmo-centralisation-question-india/), in which strategic and economic instruments of consequence are coordinated from the centre; it was narrated to the electorate formed by [the post-2019 election political-architectural context](/2019/08/16/2019-election-verdict-explained/), whose mandate the government read as licence for firmness; it belongs to the sequence of decisive state acts that includes [the post-2019 Article 370 architectural moment](/2019/07/31/article-370-abrogation-august-2019/), sharing with that act a signature of preparation, surprise and irreversibility by design; and it tested the resilience of the constitutional order that [the most-consequential-constitutional-moment framework](/2020/07/28/basic-structure-most-consequential-doctrine/) organised, since emergency powers were never invoked and every instrument was ordinary law. Read with [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/), the moment renegotiated all three of the republic's permanent tensions at once: between state and market, in the screening of investment and the banning of platforms; between centre and region, in a Ladakh administered from New Delhi since October 2019 whose border became the republic's central strategic fact; and between India and the world, in a reweighting that no government since 1991 had attempted at this speed. A pillar that began with the proposition that foreign policy is made at home ends with a case in which the proposition needs no defence counsel. The app bans were debated in the language of data sovereignty, the screening in the language of the small investor and the falling market, the Quad in the language of a free and open region whose audience sat in Indian living rooms. The Galwan moment operationalises post-China architectural decoupling across multiple domains; the three domain framework captures the architectural content; and the framework's deepest claim is the series' own: that the republic argues with itself, in the idiom of foreign policy, about what kind of state it intends to be. The argument continues into the economic pillar that follows, where the instruments Galwan normalised, the incentive, the screen and the ban, meet the longer history of reform as politics.

A closing measurement can be taken from the episode's paper trail alone. The founding generation's foreign policy survives in debate volumes and in the correspondence of prime ministers. The Galwan moment survives in gazette orders under a technology statute, in a two page amendment to an investment policy, in designation lists for network procurement, in joint statements negotiated line by line between corps headquarters, in a parliamentary statement by a defence minister, in the clarification issued by a prime minister's office on a Saturday, and in the honours list of a February morning in Beijing that admitted four dead eight months after they fell. The archive is the argument's final exhibit. A republic that conducts its gravest border crisis since 1962 through instruments of this kind has not diminished the crisis; it has disclosed the kind of state it has become, one whose power runs through regulation as naturally as through mobilisation. The twenty soldiers whose deaths began the sequence were honoured by that state in the oldest grammar it possesses, with gallantry awards and state funerals. The reply the state constructed was written in its newest grammar. Holding the two grammars in one frame, the funerals and the press notes, the ridge at Patrol Point 14 and the summit table at Kazan, is the work this pillar set out to do, and the Galwan moment, reanalysed, is where the frame holds widest.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: When did the Galwan clash occur?

The Galwan clash occurred on the night of 15 and 16 June 2020, in the Galwan Valley of eastern Ladakh, along the Line of Actual Control between India and China. A party of the 16 Bihar Regiment, led by its commanding officer Colonel B. Santosh Babu, had moved to Patrol Point 14 to verify the pullback agreed at the corps commander meeting of 6 June 2020. Indian official accounts record that a Chinese observation post and structures stood at the point in contravention of the agreed disengagement. A violent melee developed through the evening and into the night, fought with stones and improvised weapons at high altitude, on the ridge above the Galwan river.

### Q: Who was Colonel B. Santosh Babu?

Colonel B. Santosh Babu was the commanding officer of the 16 Bihar Regiment, the Indian Army unit holding the Galwan sector in June 2020. A native of Suryapet in Telangana, Babu led the party that moved to Patrol Point 14 on 15 June 2020 to verify the disengagement agreed with the Chinese side on 6 June 2020, and he was killed in the melee that followed, one of the twenty Indian soldiers who died that night. He was posthumously awarded the Maha Vir Chakra, India's second highest gallantry decoration, in the honours announced for Republic Day 2021, and his name heads the Indian casualty list of the clash in every official account.

### Q: How many Indian soldiers died at Galwan?

Twenty Indian soldiers died at Galwan, including the commanding officer of the 16 Bihar Regiment, Colonel B. Santosh Babu. The Indian Army's statement of 16 June 2020 first reported three deaths and was revised the same day as the toll among the critically injured rose, settling at twenty. The twenty were individually named in the official record and their funerals were held in their home states across India. The deaths were the first fatalities on the Line of Actual Control since October 1975, when four Assam Rifles personnel were killed at Tulung La, ending an interval of forty five years without a combat death on that boundary.

### Q: What apps did India ban after Galwan?

India banned fifty nine applications by the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology order of 29 June 2020, including TikTok, WeChat, UC Browser, Helo, Likee, Shein and Club Factory, under Section 69A of the Information Technology Act 2000. Further orders followed: forty seven applications on 27 July 2020, one hundred and eighteen on 2 September 2020 including PUBG Mobile, and forty three on 24 November 2020 including AliExpress, bringing the 2020 total to two hundred and sixty seven applications. A fifth order in February 2022 banned fifty four more. The government's stated grounds were the sovereignty and integrity of India, the defence of India, the security of the state and public order, supported by findings on the applications' data practices.

### Q: What is the post-2020 FDI Press Note 3?

Press Note 3 of 2020 was issued by the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade on 17 April 2020, amending India's foreign direct investment policy so that an entity of a country sharing a land border with India, or whose beneficial owner is situated in such a country, may invest only through the government approval route with prior clearance. The stated rationale was the prevention of opportunistic takeovers of Indian companies weakened by the pandemic, following the People's Bank of China's disclosed purchase of a stake above one percent in the Housing Development Finance Corporation. The note's operative incidence fell on Chinese investment, and after the Galwan clash it hardened into a standing screening regime.

### Q: What is the October 2024 disengagement?

The October 2024 disengagement is the agreement on patrolling arrangements along the Line of Actual Control announced by Foreign Secretary Vikram Misri on 21 October 2024, covering the remaining friction points of the 2020 standoff at Depsang and Demchok in eastern Ladakh. The Ministry of External Affairs stated that the agreement would lead to disengagement and a resolution of the issues that had emerged in 2020, with patrolling resuming in those areas on the pattern obtaining before the standoff. It closed a staged sequence that had begun at Pangong Tso in February 2021 and continued at Gogra in August 2021 and Hot Springs in September 2022, and it preceded the Modi Xi meeting at Kazan on 23 October 2024.

### Q: How did Galwan shift the QUAD?

Galwan shifted the Quadrilateral Security Dialogue by removing the Indian hesitation that had rationed its ascent. The four foreign ministers met in Tokyo in October 2020, Australia rejoined the Malabar naval exercise in November 2020 for the first time since 2007, and the grouping rose to leaders level with a virtual summit on 12 March 2021 and a first in person summit in Washington on 24 September 2021. Each step was taken while Chinese forces stood at the Ladakh friction points. The four governments denied that the grouping targeted any country, and its agenda widened to vaccines, supply chains and technology; the article's finding is narrower, that the clash created the domestic political condition under which India's rationing of the format ended.

### Q: What did the Indian Army statement of 16 June 2020 say?

The Indian Army statement of 16 June 2020 recorded that during the de-escalation process in the Galwan Valley a violent face-off had taken place the previous night with casualties on both sides, and that the loss of lives on the Indian side included an officer and two soldiers, a figure the Army revised the same day to twenty as critically injured soldiers succumbed. The statement recorded that senior military officials of the two sides were meeting at the venue to defuse the situation. It gave no Chinese casualty figure. The statement's two stage release, an initial toll of three followed within hours by the settled figure of twenty, shaped the first day of the domestic controversy that followed.

### Q: What happened at the all party meeting of 19 June 2020?

At the all party meeting of 19 June 2020, held by video conference four days after the clash, Narendra Modi assured the leaders of the principal parties that Indian forces were fully capable of defending the country's territory, and stated, in the remarks published by the Prime Minister's Office, that neither had anyone intruded into Indian territory, nor was anyone inside Indian territory, nor had any Indian post been captured. Sonia Gandhi asked when Chinese troops had occupied Indian territory and in what numbers. The opposition charged that the Prime Minister's formulation contradicted the premise of the clash; the Prime Minister's Office issued a clarification on 20 June 2020 rejecting what it called a mischievous interpretation.

### Q: When did China acknowledge its Galwan dead?

China acknowledged its Galwan dead on 19 February 2021, eight months after the clash, when the People's Liberation Army Daily and the Central Military Commission honoured four soldiers killed in the Galwan Valley, naming a battalion commander and three soldiers, and awarded a posthumous honorary title to a regimental commander who had been seriously injured. Through the second half of 2020, Chinese officials had declined to publish a casualty figure. Higher figures belong to named holders rather than to any Chinese acknowledgment: Indian official briefings in June 2020 attributed forty three Chinese casualties, killed and seriously injured, to assessments; the Russian agency TASS reported forty five Chinese dead in February 2021, a figure Chinese state media rejected.

### Q: What did the Pangong Tso disengagement of February 2021 involve?

The Pangong Tso disengagement, announced by Defence Minister Rajnath Singh in Parliament on 10 February 2021, required Chinese forces on the lake's north bank to withdraw east of Finger 8, Indian forces to return to their permanent base near Finger 3, and the mechanised elements deployed on the south bank heights in August 2020 to pull back, with patrolling in the contested stretch suspended by agreement until a further settlement. The withdrawal was completed within days and verified by both sides. The opposition held that the buffer arrangement lay largely in areas India had patrolled before 2020; the government held that the arrangement was temporary, local and reciprocal. The formula became the template for the Gogra and Hot Springs stages.

### Q: What did Rajnath Singh tell the Lok Sabha on 15 September 2020?

In his statement to the Lok Sabha of 15 September 2020, Defence Minister Rajnath Singh described Chinese attempts to transgress the Line of Actual Control at multiple points in eastern Ladakh, stated that India had made appropriate counter deployments, charged China with violating the bilateral agreements of 1993 and 1996, and declared that no force in the world could stop Indian soldiers from patrolling their traditional and customary patrolling points. The statement was the government's fullest parliamentary account of the crisis. It came during a monsoon session in which the opposition had demanded a discussion on the border situation, and it set out the position the government held through the four years of negotiation that followed.

### Q: Who announced the October 2024 disengagement for India?

Foreign Secretary Vikram Misri announced the October 2024 disengagement for India, briefing the press in New Delhi on 21 October 2024. Misri stated that an agreement had been reached on patrolling arrangements along the Line of Actual Control in the India China border areas, leading to disengagement and a resolution of the issues that had emerged in 2020, the product of discussions through diplomatic and military channels in the preceding weeks. The remaining friction points covered were Depsang and Demchok. Two days later, on 23 October 2024, Narendra Modi and Xi Jinping held their first formal bilateral meeting since October 2019, at Kazan in Russia, on the margins of the BRICS summit.

### Q: What happened at Hot Springs in September 2022?

At Hot Springs, designated Patrol Point 15 in the Chang Chenmo sector of eastern Ladakh, Indian and Chinese troops completed a staged disengagement announced on 8 September 2022, following the sixteenth round of corps commander talks. The joint statement recorded that troops of both sides had begun to disengage in a planned manner from the Gogra Hot Springs area, the third friction point settled after Pangong Tso in February 2021 and Gogra in August 2021. The announcement came days before the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation summit at Samarkand. The settlement left Depsang and Demchok, the two oldest and hardest friction points of the 2020 standoff, for the final negotiation concluded in October 2024.

### Q: How did Vijay Gokhale read Chinese negotiating behaviour?

Vijay Gokhale, the former Foreign Secretary and former ambassador to China, read Chinese negotiating behaviour in his 2021 book The Long Game: How the Chinese Negotiate with India, a study of six negotiations from 1949 to the Doklam settlement of 2017. Gokhale argued that Chinese negotiation proceeds through studied informality, the careful management of process, and superior patience, and that Indian negotiators err when they mistake a negotiating pause for a settled intention. Read through his framework, the four year disengagement sequence after Galwan appears as a negotiation in the grammar the book describes, each stage purchased with time, with closure arriving when Beijing's wider calendar made it useful.

### Q: What did Shyam Saran argue about the post-2020 China relationship?

Shyam Saran, the former Foreign Secretary, argued in his essays of 2020 to 2024 and in his 2022 book How China Sees India and the World that the Chinese transgressions of 2020 had altered the premises of the India China relationship durably. On Saran's reading, the compact underpinning the earlier decades, under which economic engagement proceeded on the assumption of a managed and tranquil border, had been falsified by Beijing's own conduct, and India's response had to proceed on the premise that the compact would not be restored in its earlier form. His assessment carries weight in this article's adjudication because it came from a former Foreign Secretary of a Congress led government, independent of the governing formation whose programme the decoupling reading describes.

### Q: When did India and China last lose soldiers on the LAC before 2020?

India and China last lost soldiers on the Line of Actual Control before 2020 in October 1975, when a patrol of the Assam Rifles was ambushed at Tulung La in the eastern sector and four Indian personnel were killed. Between that incident and the night of 15 and 16 June 2020, the two armies confronted each other repeatedly, at Depsang in 2013, at Chumar in 2014 and at Doklam for seventy three days in 2017, without a combat fatality. The agreements of 1993 and 1996, which restricted the use of firearms near the line, underwrote the interval. The Galwan deaths, inflicted with stones and improvised weapons rather than firearms, broke the interval while respecting the letter of those restrictions.

### Q: What was the Working Mechanism for Consultation and Coordination?

The Working Mechanism for Consultation and Coordination on India China Border Affairs is the diplomatic channel between the two foreign ministries created by an agreement of January 2012, designed to handle border affairs consultation below the level of the Special Representatives. During the 2020 to 2024 standoff, the mechanism met repeatedly in parallel with the corps commander talks, handling the diplomatic track while the military commanders handled disengagement on the ground. Its meetings through the standoff years issued statements recording agreement on phased and verified disengagement and on maintaining peace in the border areas as the basis for restoring the wider bilateral relationship, the formula both governments carried into the October 2024 settlement.

### Q: How many rounds of corps commander talks followed the clash?

Twenty one rounds of corps commander level talks were held between the first meeting at Chushul Moldo on 6 June 2020, which preceded the clash, and the twenty first round on 19 February 2024. The Indian side was led initially by Lieutenant General Harinder Singh, commander of the Leh based XIV Corps, and subsequently by his successors in that command; the Chinese side was led by the commander of the South Xinjiang Military District. The rounds produced the staged disengagements at Pangong Tso, Gogra and Hot Springs, and the process they built carried the negotiation of the remaining points at Depsang and Demchok to the agreement announced on 21 October 2024.

### Q: What did the trusted sources directive do to telecom procurement?

The trusted sources directive, announced in December 2020 and effective from June 2021 under the National Security Directive on the telecommunications sector, subjected the procurement of network equipment by Indian telecom operators to a national security designation process administered through a designated authority. Equipment and vendors were to be drawn from trusted sources as notified; Chinese vendors Huawei and ZTE did not appear in the designations, and the 5G trial authorisations issued by the Department of Telecommunications in May 2021 named Ericsson, Nokia, Samsung and Indian equipment makers as the vendors for trials. No order banned Huawei by name. The procurement record of the 5G rollout that followed was, in effect, entirely non Chinese.
