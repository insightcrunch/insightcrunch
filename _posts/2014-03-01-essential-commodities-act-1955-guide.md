The Essential Commodities Act 1955 is the strangest statute in Indian economic law, because a person who reads it from the first section to the last learns almost nothing about what the law forbids, requires, or punishes in any actual market. The Act fixes no price, sets no stock limit, names no licence condition, and prohibits no transaction. What it does instead is confer power: a single wide power on the Central Government to make orders, and a machinery for enforcing whatever those orders say. Every operative rule that has ever governed the trade in foodgrains, sugar, edible oils, drugs, fertilisers, kerosene or jute textiles under this statute has lived not in the Act but in a control order, a licensing order, or a Gazette notification issued under it, and every such rule could be, and frequently was, rewritten overnight without Parliament being asked. That design is the whole subject of this article, because it explains both why governments of every persuasion have kept the Act for seven decades and why traders, economists and courts have argued about it for just as long.

The claim this article defends is a simple one about where law lives. The Essential Commodities Act is a statute made entirely of delegated power: scarcely any rule in it binds anyone directly, its whole content is a permission for the executive to make rules that do, and therefore the record of orders is the real law and the Act itself is a heading under which that law is filed. A trader who wants to know what the law requires must read the Gazette, not the statute book. A researcher who wants to understand the controlled economy must read the orders, because the Act will tell them only who held the pen. And a citizen who wants to know why a government can cap the stock of onions in a warehouse within days of a price spike, while a change to the Act itself would take a parliamentary session, has to understand that this asymmetry was not an accident of drafting. It was the deliberate constitutional and legislative choice of 1954 and 1955, made by a state that had known famine, war rationing and scarcity, and that wanted a standing emergency power in permanent form.

## The problem the Act was built to solve

The statute was written for an economy of shortage. In the early 1950s India imported foodgrains to feed its cities, industrial raw materials were scarce, the memory of the Bengal famine of 1943 was a decade old and politically raw, and the governing assumption across parties was that the private grain trade, left alone in a scarcity, would hoard, speculate and starve the ration shops. Whether that assumption was fair to the trade is one of the arguments this article takes up later. What matters for the statute's design is that the assumption was held with complete sincerity by the government that drafted it, and that the legal problem it faced was not how to write good permanent rules for commodity markets, since nobody believed permanent rules could anticipate the next shortage, but how to give the executive a lawful instrument that could be pointed at any commodity, in any district, within days.

The inheritance was physical as well as legal. Wartime rationing had built a network of fair price shops, a cadre of supply inspectors, and a district-level habit of treating the grain trade as a public utility to be supervised rather than a market to be left alone, and all of it survived the war, the transfer of power and the coming of the Constitution. The public distribution system that the Act would go on to feed for the rest of the century grew out of that network, and the licensing of foodgrain dealers grew out of the wartime registration of traders. When the drafters of 1955 sat down, they were not inventing a system; they were deciding whether the system that already existed, staffed, housed and functioning, should be dismantled, renewed year by year, or given a permanent legal home. The political cost of dismantling it in a decade of food imports and urban ration queues was prohibitive, and nobody in the Parliament of 1955 seriously proposed to pay it. That context explains a feature of the Act that puzzles readers who meet it fresh: it contains no commencement machinery, no new authority, no transitional apparatus of any size, because on the day it came into force nothing on the ground was supposed to change.

A legislature confronting that problem has three broad options. It can legislate commodity by commodity, writing a wheat Act, a sugar Act and a drugs Act, each debated and each slow to change. It can legislate emergency powers that lapse and must be renewed, forcing periodic parliamentary reconsideration of whether the emergency continues. Or it can do what India did, which is to enact a permanent framework statute whose only real content is a power to make orders, so that the emergency instrument never lapses and never needs renewal, and the decision to control or decontrol a commodity becomes an executive act rather than a legislative one. The first option was too slow for a government managing food imports ship by ship. The second was tried, in effect, through the temporary wartime and post-war legislation described below, and the experience of repeatedly extending a temporary Act for nearly a decade taught the government to prefer permanence. The third option is the Essential Commodities Act, and its permanence is the feature critics keep returning to: a power justified by emergency was drafted without a sunset, and so the emergency instrument outlived every emergency that produced it.

The second design choice is subtler and matters just as much. The Act does not merely delegate; it delegates twice. The Central Government holds the order-making power, and it may pass that power down to state governments and their officers, who in practice issued most of the licensing and stock orders that traders actually dealt with. The statute thus created a two-storey structure in which the Centre set the framework and retained override, the states ran the machinery, and the district administration, through the Collector and the civil supplies staff, touched the commodity. Almost every later controversy about the Act, including the litigation over whether a state order exceeded the delegation, and the political argument about whether stock limits are a central or a state responsibility, is a controversy generated by that two-storey design.

## What existed before: war controls, a temporary Act, and a constitutional bridge

The Act of 1955 was the third vessel for substantially the same power. The first was the Defence of India Act, 1939 and the rules made under it, through which the colonial government controlled prices, movement and stocks of foodgrains and other supplies during the Second World War. When the war ended, the controls were considered too necessary to dismantle in the middle of post-war scarcity, so the power was carried into peacetime by the Essential Supplies (Temporary Powers) Act, 1946, a statute whose very name recorded the understanding that it was a bridge and not a destination. The 1946 Act contained the scheme that the 1955 Act would inherit almost intact: a list of essential commodity classes, a central power to make orders regulating or prohibiting production, supply, distribution and trade, licensing as the characteristic instrument, and an overriding effect for orders. The jurisprudence on delegation and price fixation that still governs the 1955 Act was first worked out on the 1946 Act's text, which is why the foundational cases described later in this article, including Harishankar Bagla and Nath Mal, are formally decisions about the predecessor statute.

The bridge had a constitutional problem. Under the Constitution as adopted, trade and commerce in foodstuffs and several other commodities sat in the State List or at the margins of the Concurrent List, and Parliament's competence to run a permanent all-India control regime was not secure. The Constitution-makers, who had the wartime experience in front of them, had inserted a temporary bridge of their own: Article 369, which for five years from the commencement of the Constitution treated specified subjects, including trade and commerce in and the production, supply and distribution of foodstuffs including edible oilseeds and oils, cattle fodder, raw cotton and cotton seed and raw jute, as if they were in the Concurrent List, so that Parliament could legislate and the 1946 Act could continue. Article 369 was due to expire on 25 January 1955, five years after the Constitution commenced, and when it expired the legal foundation of the control regime would expire with it.

The 1946 Act deserves a closer look, because its scheme is the template and some of its history explains the template's durability. It was enacted in the shadow of the Bengal famine and the collapse of wartime procurement, and it covered the commodities of a siege economy: foodstuffs, cotton and woollen textiles, paper, iron and steel, coal, petroleum products and the rest of the list the 1955 Act would inherit. Its central provision empowered the government to make orders for regulating or prohibiting production, supply and distribution, and its overriding clause gave those orders primacy, exactly as Sections 3 and 6 would later do. Being temporary, it required repeated extension, and each extension debate rehearsed the same exchange, the government pleading continuing scarcity, the critics answering that a temporary power renewed for a decade is a permanent power in disguise. The exchange is worth remembering because it has never really ended; it simply moved its venue, first into the constitutional debate over Article 369, then into the Third Amendment, and finally into the periodic parliamentary argument about the 1955 Act itself. The defenders of permanence won every round for a structural reason: each time the question was put, a harvest had just failed, a price had just spiked, or a war, in 1962, 1965 and 1971, had just reminded the state of what a supply emergency feels like from inside. A temporary emergency power is easy to let lapse in calm years. India arranged its constitutional business so that the lapse question for this power never arose in a calm year, and by the time calm decades arrived, the power had become part of the furniture of government.

Two things were therefore done in quick succession, and the order in which they were done is the examinable point. First, the constitutional foundation was made permanent. The Constitution (Third Amendment) Act, 1954, introduced in the Lok Sabha by the Commerce and Industry Minister, T. T. Krishnamachari, in September 1954 and assented to on 22 February 1955 after ratification by the states, substituted a new Entry 33 in the Concurrent List. The substituted entry covers trade and commerce in, and the production, supply and distribution of, the products of any industry whose control by the Union is declared by Parliament by law to be expedient in the public interest, together with imported goods of the same kind, and then, in its own right and without any need for an industries declaration, foodstuffs including edible oilseeds and oils, cattle fodder including oilcakes and other concentrates, raw cotton whether ginned or unginned and cotton seed, and raw jute. The working of that entry in the federal scheme is traced in the series article on the [Seventh Schedule lists explained](/2018/09/15/seventh-schedule-lists-explained/), and the point to hold here is that the enlargement was deliberate and specific: Parliament did not stumble into competence over foodstuffs, it amended the Constitution to acquire that competence permanently, weeks before the statute that would use it.

Second, the statute itself was carried across the bridge. As Article 369 ran out, the Essential Commodities Ordinance, 1955 (Ordinance 1 of 1955) was promulgated to keep the regime alive, and Parliament then enacted the Essential Commodities Act, 1955 (Act 10 of 1955), dated 1 April 1955, which repealed the Ordinance and, by Section 16, every state law controlling the production, supply and distribution of essential commodities, while saving the orders already in force by deeming them to have been made under the new Act. The continuity is worth pausing on. No trader's licence lapsed, no control order fell, and no price line moved on the day the permanent Act replaced the temporary one, because Section 16 carried the entire operative content across. The change of 1955 was a change of legal foundation, from a wartime power on a constitutional bridge to a permanent power on a widened Concurrent List entry, and it was engineered so that the governed would notice nothing at all.

## The architecture: a Schedule, a power, and an enforcement tail

The Act is short, and its weight is distributed in a way no reader expects. Section 2A, inserted by the Essential Commodities (Amendment) Act, 2006 (Act 54 of 2006) with effect from 12 February 2007, provides that an essential commodity means a commodity specified in the Schedule to the Act, and that the Central Government may amend the Schedule by notification. Section 3 confers the order-making power, which is analysed clause by clause in the next section. Section 4 allows a Section 3 order to confer powers and impose duties on governments and officers. That modest provision is a federal lever of some force: a central order may not only regulate traders but direct the state machinery itself, assigning duties to state governments and their officers as to the exercise of powers and the discharge of functions under the order, so that the Centre can conscript the district administration into a control operation without any state legislation or even a state order intervening. Section 5 allows the Central Government to delegate the order-making power itself. Section 6 gives every Section 3 order effect notwithstanding anything inconsistent in any other enactment. Then, from Section 6A to the end, the Act is an enforcement tail: confiscation, penalties, attempts and abetment, false statements, offences by companies, cognizability, presumptions, cognizance, summary trial, presumptions about orders, burden of proof, and protection for action taken in good faith. The substantive middle of the Act, the part that tells anyone what to do, is empty, and the emptiness is filled from outside, by orders.

The small print of Section 2 repays a glance, because two definitions do quiet work. Sugar is defined exhaustively, as any form of sugar containing more than ninety per cent sucrose, including sugar candy, khandsari, bura and crushed sugar and sugar in process in a vacuum pan factory, a definition written into the Act because the levy and price machinery for sugar turned on exactly what counted as sugar and could not be left to orders. Food-crops are defined to include crops of sugarcane, which is what allows cane, and orders addressed to cane pricing at the field level, to be reached through the foodstuffs power. And the Collector, the officer on whom the confiscation code rests, is defined to include an Additional Collector and any officer not below the rank of Sub-Divisional Officer whom the Collector authorises, the statutory seed of the state variations in enforcement personnel described later in this article.

Two features of this architecture deserve emphasis because they recur throughout the Act's career. The first is that the boundary of the Act's reach is set by a list the executive itself can rewrite. Parliament fixed the categories in the Schedule, but it handed the Centre the pen: under Section 2A the Central Government may, if satisfied that it is necessary in the public interest, for reasons recorded in the notification and in consultation with the state governments, add a commodity to the Schedule or remove one, and it may make an addition for a stated period not exceeding six months, extendable for reasons recorded. Every such notification must be laid before both Houses of Parliament. The second feature is that the Act's penalties attach not to anything the Act says but to the contravention of an order, so the criminal law in this field is entirely parasitic on executive instruments. Section 7 does not punish hoarding; it punishes contravening an order made under Section 3, and whether a given act of storage is a crime depends wholly on whether an order happens to cover it, in that district, on that day. A legal system in which the boundary between lawful warehousing and a seven-year offence is a Gazette notification is a system whose real constitution is administrative, and the courts, as will appear, understood this from the beginning and chose, with narrow exceptions, not to disturb it.

## The Schedule: seven entries and a long contraction

The Schedule as it stands contains seven entries. They are drugs, a word which carries the meaning given to it by the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940; fertiliser, whether inorganic, organic or mixed; foodstuffs, including edible oilseeds and oils; hank yarn made wholly from cotton; petroleum and petroleum products; raw jute and jute textiles; and seeds, divided into seeds of food-crops and seeds of fruits and vegetables, seeds of cattle fodder, and jute seeds. That is the whole territory of the Act: seven heads, several of them very wide, foodstuffs above all, which the courts have read to cover everything eaten, while holding in S. Samuel, M.D., Harrisons Malayalam v. Union of India that tea is not a foodstuff because it lacks nutritive value, a reminder that even the widest head has edges that litigation must draw.

The seven entries are the residue of a much longer list, and the contraction is the statute's biography in miniature. As enacted in 1955, the Act contained no Schedule at all. Section 2(a) defined an essential commodity by eleven classes written into the Act itself, which included cattle fodder, coal and its derivatives, component parts and accessories of automobiles, cotton and woollen textiles, drugs, foodstuffs, iron and steel and its products, paper including newsprint, petroleum and petroleum products, raw cotton and cotton seed, and raw jute, together with a residuary power for the Central Government to declare any other class of commodity essential by notified order, provided Parliament had power to legislate on it under Entry 33. Over the decades the residuary power was used freely, and commodity after commodity, from cement to tyres to soap, was declared essential as prices rose and governments reached for the instrument. The declared list at its widest ran to dozens of commodities, most of them controlled in name far more than in fact, and the widening itself became an argument against the Act: an instrument that covered everything disciplined nothing.

The pruning came in stages and by different hands. Liberalisation after 1991 made the control of industrial goods untenable, and declarations lapsed or were withdrawn through the 1990s. In February 2002 the Central Government issued the Removal of (Licensing Requirements, Stock Limits and Movement Restrictions) on Specified Foodstuffs Order, 2002 (G.S.R. 104(E), dated 15 February 2002), which stripped licensing, stock limits and movement restrictions from wheat, paddy and rice, coarse grains, sugar, edible oilseeds and edible oils, and provided that states could reimpose such controls on those commodities only with the prior concurrence of the Centre; the Task Force on Agricultural Marketing Reforms recorded that the same notification deleted eleven items in full and one in part from the commodities under the Act. The final structural step was the 2006 amendment, which abolished the old Section 2(a) definition altogether and replaced it with the Schedule mechanism of Section 2A, so that the list moved out of the definitional section and into an amendable Schedule, and the number of heads settled at the seven that remain. The movement from eleven statutory classes plus an open residuary power to seven scheduled heads amendable only with reasons recorded and consultation with the states is a real tightening of form, though it left the substance, the breadth of the foodstuffs head and the order power beneath it, untouched.

The edges of the scheduled heads have generated a small, instructive case law of their own, because everything depends on them. Seeds illustrate the point. In Rashu Seeds and Farms v. Union of India (AIR 1994 SC 533), the Supreme Court upheld the Centre's competence over seeds of foodstuffs, reasoning that seed bears directly on the production of foodstuffs and falls within the legislative field over them; seeds have their own sub-entries in the Schedule, for food-crops and for fruits and vegetables, for cattle fodder and for jute. Cotton seed was subsequently brought within the seeds entry by notification, to support the Cotton Seeds Price (Control) Order, 2015, by which the Centre fixed the maximum sale price of cotton seed, including the trait value charged for Bt cotton technology, a use of the price clause in an input market that generated its own litigation and shows the Schedule mechanism reaching a commodity whose price politics concern technology rents rather than food. The foodstuffs head itself has been read by the nutritive-value test, under which what is eaten for nourishment is in and what is consumed without nourishment, tea in the Harrisons Malayalam case, is out, a line that keeps stimulants and luxuries outside the Act while sweeping in everything from grain to edible oil. These boundary decisions are sometimes dismissed as pedantry. They are the opposite: in a system where the whole apparatus of licensing, seizure and seven-year punishment is switched on by classification, classification is the merits, and the pedantry is the rule of law doing its work at the only point where the statute gives a court something hard to hold.

### Does the Act reach a commodity that is not in the Schedule?

No, and the negative is the one hard boundary the Act contains. Section 2A makes specification in the Schedule the condition of the Act's application, so a commodity outside the seven heads is outside the order power, outside the confiscation machinery and outside the penalties, whatever its price does.

The government's route to a new commodity is therefore not interpretation but amendment of the Schedule by notification under Section 2A, with reasons recorded, consultation with the state governments, and laying before Parliament, and where speed matters the notification may declare the commodity essential for a stated period of up to six months at a time. The boundary matters in litigation as well as in administration: prosecutions have failed at the threshold because the commodity seized was held to fall outside the scheduled head invoked for it, which is why the first question in any proceeding under this Act is always a Schedule question, and why the definition disputes, whether tea is a foodstuff, whether paddy is distinct from rice, whether a seed is a food-crop seed, are fought with a seriousness that the small sums in individual cases would not otherwise explain.

The practical consequence is that the Schedule is where the politics of the Act is most visible, because adding a commodity is a public signal that the government has decided a market is misbehaving. In March 2020, as the Covid-19 pandemic produced shortages and profiteering in protective goods, the Central Government amended the Schedule by order dated 13 March 2020 to declare masks, specified as two-ply and three-ply surgical masks and N95 masks, and hand sanitisers essential commodities until 30 June 2020, and followed the declaration with price orders: the Ministry of Consumer Affairs told a press conference on 21 March 2020, recorded in a Press Information Bureau release of that date, that the retail price of hand sanitiser had been capped at Rs 100 for a bottle of 200 millilitres, that a three-ply surgical mask was not to sell above Rs 10 and a two-ply mask not above Rs 8, and that the melt-blown fabric used in mask manufacture had been capped at the price prevailing a month earlier. The episode is the Schedule mechanism working exactly as designed: a commodity nobody had regulated in February was priced, capped and policed by the end of March, and released from the Schedule when the stated period ran out, all without Parliament amending anything.

## Section 3 of the Essential Commodities Act 1955: the engine of the Act

Section 3(1) is the provision under which every control order in Indian history has been made, and its text should be understood precisely. If the Central Government is of opinion that it is necessary or expedient to do so for maintaining or increasing supplies of any essential commodity, or for securing its equitable distribution and availability at fair prices, or for securing an essential commodity for the defence of India or the efficient conduct of military operations, it may by order provide for regulating or prohibiting the production, supply and distribution of the commodity and trade and commerce in it. Three features of the formula carry the analysis. The trigger is the government's opinion, not an objective condition that a court can readily re-find. The purposes are stated at a level of generality, supplies, equitable distribution, fair prices, that almost any market intervention can be fitted inside. And the instrument is an order, which Section 2(c) tells us includes a direction issued under an order, so the power cascades downward into individual directions as well as general rules.

Section 3(2) then illustrates, without limiting, what an order may do, and its clauses are the catalogue of the controlled economy. Clause (a) permits regulation by licences, permits or otherwise of the production or manufacture of an essential commodity. Clause (b) permits an order to bring waste or arable land under cultivation for food-crops, a clause that betrays the famine-era origins of the scheme and has slept almost undisturbed since. Clause (c) permits controlling the price at which an essential commodity may be bought or sold. Clause (d) permits regulation by licences, permits or otherwise of the storage, transport, distribution, disposal, acquisition, use or consumption of a commodity, and it is under clause (d), read with sub-section (1), that stock limits and movement restrictions are imposed. Clause (e) permits prohibiting the withholding from sale of a commodity ordinarily kept for sale, the anti-hoarding clause in its purest form. Clause (f) permits requiring a person holding stock, or engaged in production or in the business of buying or selling, to sell the whole or a specified part of the stock, or of what he produces or receives, to the Central or a State Government, to an officer or agent of a government, to a government corporation, or to such other persons as the order specifies: this is the requisition or levy power, the legal root of procurement. Clause (g) permits regulating or prohibiting classes of commercial or financial transactions relating to foodstuffs which the authority considers detrimental to the public interest. Clause (h) permits collecting information and statistics. Clause (i) permits requiring persons in the trade to maintain and produce for inspection books, accounts and records and to furnish information. Clause (ii), inserted later, deals with the grant of licences, the charging of fees, security deposits and their forfeiture. Clause (j) covers incidental and supplementary matters, including entry, search and seizure of commodities, conveyances and books by authorised persons.

The price-compulsion machinery that clause (f) sets in motion is completed by sub-sections (3) to (3E), which most summaries skip and which repay attention because they show the drafter working out, in the Act itself, what a fair compulsory purchase looks like. Where a person sells a commodity in compliance with a clause (f) order, Section 3(3) requires payment of the agreed price if one can be agreed consistently with any controlled price, failing that the price calculated with reference to the controlled price, and failing both, the price calculated at the market rate prevailing in the locality at the date of sale. Section 3(3A) lets the Centre, by a notification lasting not more than three months, substitute an average of market rates over the preceding three months in a locality where it wishes to control a price rise or prevent hoarding. Section 3(3B) provides that where foodgrains, edible oilseeds or edible oils are sold under a clause (f) order to a government or its agent, the seller is paid an amount equal to the procurement price specified by the State Government with the previous approval of the Central Government, having regard to any controlled price, the general crop prospects, the need to make the commodity available at reasonable prices to consumers and particularly to vulnerable consumers, and the recommendations of the Agricultural Prices Commission. Section 3(3C) does the same work for sugar with its own factors, and Sections 3(3D) and 3(3E) give the Centre standing powers over the release, removal and disposal of sugar from factory godowns, the statutory skeleton of the sugar release mechanism. In these sub-sections, exceptionally, the Act does contain substantive law, and it is no accident that the substance concerns the price the state must pay when it takes: even a drafter conferring unlimited power over traders felt bound to write the terms of the state's own purchases into the statute.

### Can the Centre take over the running of an undertaking under the Act?

Yes. Section 3(4) provides that where the Central Government considers it necessary for maintaining or increasing the production and supply of an essential commodity, it may by order authorise a person, called an authorised controller, to exercise functions of control over the whole or any part of an undertaking engaged in producing or supplying that commodity.

While such an order is in force the undertaking must be carried on in accordance with the controller's directions, and every person having functions of management in it must comply with those directions, although the controller himself must act on the Central Government's instructions and may not direct anything inconsistent with the enactments governing the undertaking except as the order specifically provides. The clause is the deepest reach of Section 3, the point at which regulation becomes administration, and its rarity in practice is itself informative. Governments have generally preferred to control what an undertaking sells and at what price rather than to run the undertaking, because the order power over trade achieves the supply objective without transferring the commercial risk to the state, and because a failed mill under a controller is the government's failure in the public eye in a way that a failed mill under a price order is not.

Two closing features of Section 3 complete the picture. An order of a general nature must be notified in the Official Gazette, and an order directed to an individual must be served on that individual, under Section 3(5); and every order made by the Central Government or its officers must be laid before both Houses of Parliament under Section 3(6). Laying is the one point at which Parliament touches the operative law of this field, and it is a weak touch: the order takes effect on notification, laying is subsequent, and the Act prescribes no procedure by which a House annuls an order it dislikes. The accountability for the real law under this statute is therefore political rather than parliamentary, a matter of questions, debates and press scrutiny after the event, which is precisely the arrangement the 2020 amendment briefly tried to change for agricultural foodstuffs and precisely what its repeal restored.

## How a control order is built, and how a trader reads one

Because the operative law is the orders, the pillar owes the reader an account of how an order is put together, since the drafting conventions of control orders are stable across commodities and decades and a reader who has mastered one licensing order can read any of them. A typical foodgrain licensing order made by a state government under delegated Section 3 power opens with definitions, of dealer, of foodgrains, of the licensing authority, usually the Collector or the district supply officer. It then prohibits dealing in the commodity without a licence, prescribes the form of application, the fee, the security deposit authorised by clause (ii) of Section 3(2), and the conditions that will be endorsed on the licence: the places of business covered, the obligation to maintain true accounts of purchases, sales and stocks, the obligation to issue cash memoranda, the obligation to display a list of prices and stocks at the premises, the obligation to submit periodical returns, and the obligation to permit entry and inspection. It then states the licensing authority's powers to suspend or cancel the licence and to forfeit the security for contravention of conditions, with an appeal to a named appellate authority, and it closes with the penal reminder that contravention of the order is punishable under Section 7 of the Act and that the stock is liable to seizure and confiscation under Section 6A. A stock limit order is shorter and sharper: definitions, the maximum holding for each class of holder, a clause requiring declaration of stocks above a stated quantity, a commencement and expiry date, and the same penal reminder. A price order is shortest of all, a schedule of commodities and maximum prices, wholesale and retail, with an explanation of how transport and taxes are to be treated.

Three practical consequences follow from the anatomy. First, the trader's obligations live in the conditions of his licence and the clauses of the order, not in his general duty to behave well, so compliance is a documentary discipline: the register, the return, the displayed list and the cash memorandum are the points at which the system touches the business every day, and they are therefore the points at which prosecutions most often begin, a register not maintained being easier to prove than a hoard ever is. Second, an order binds of its own force from its notification or its stated commencement, without individual service except where it is directed to a named individual, so ignorance of a Gazette notification is no answer, and the burden of surveillance lies on the trade, which is the practical meaning of the article's claim that obligations can change overnight. Third, an order made by one authority can be overtaken by another: a central removal order frees a commodity despite a state licensing order, a central stock order overrides inconsistent state arrangements, and the hierarchy of instruments, Act, central order, delegated state order, individual direction, is the map by which apparent contradictions between two documents in force are resolved. A reader who can place a document on that map can answer most questions the Act generates without opening a commentary.

## Delegation: how the power reaches the states

Section 5 provides that the Central Government may, by notified order, direct that the power to make orders or issue notifications under Section 3 shall, in relation to such matters and subject to such conditions as the direction specifies, be exercisable also by a subordinate central officer or authority, or by a State Government or its subordinate officers. The word to note is "also": delegation under Section 5 is cumulative, not a transfer, so the Centre never empties its own hands and may act directly whenever it chooses, as it did when it imposed onion stock limits across the country on 29 September 2019 rather than leaving the matter to the states. In practice the great mass of orders under the Act, the foodgrain dealers' licensing orders, the stock and display orders, the rationing orders, have been state orders made under Section 5 delegation, enforced by state civil supplies departments, and the statute book a trader must master in, say, Uttar Pradesh is a state book written under a central power.

Delegation on this scale generates its own litigation, and the leading case is K. Ramanathan v. State of Tamil Nadu, decided by the Supreme Court on 27 February 1985 and reported at (1985) 2 SCC 116. Tamil Nadu, acting under a Central delegation notified as G.S.R. 800 on 9 June 1978, had banned the movement of paddy out of Thanjavur district and specified taluks of South Arcot and Tiruchirapalli, the rice bowl the state wished to protect for its own procurement and distribution. The challenge was elegant: the delegation spoke of regulating storage, transport and distribution under clause (d) of Section 3(2), and regulation, it was said, does not include prohibition, so a total ban on movement exceeded the delegated power. The Court dismissed the appeal. Section 3(2), it held, confers no fresh power but merely illustrates the general power in Section 3(1), and the general power to regulate or prohibit, which had itself been delegated, travelled with the delegation; the word regulating, in the setting of an Act aimed at equitable distribution and fair prices, can in an appropriate case include prohibiting movement. The practical lesson has governed since: a state order under this Act is tested against the breadth of Section 3(1), not against the narrower words of the clause it happens to invoke, and a movement ban is within the power where the supply purpose is made out.

The volume of law this delegation produces deserves a moment's respect. Each state maintains its own family of orders for foodgrains, edible oils, sugar, kerosene and other commodities, amended at different times, expiring and reviving on different dates, and the families diverge in detail as much as in the enforcement texts discussed below: licence fees, security amounts, stock limits for the same commodity and the very definition of a wholesaler can differ across a state border, so that a consignment lawful in one state becomes an offence in the next without the goods changing at all. The Centre has periodically tried to compress this thicket, by model orders, by the concurrence fence of 2002 and by the removal orders, and the thicket has periodically grown back, because the underlying conditions, harvests, politics and administrative traditions, are state conditions. A national market for food, of the kind reformers have sought through marketing legislation, has to be built against this background of divergent state instruments under a single central power, which is why the legal question in this field is so often not whether a control is wise but whose control it is.

The federal balance under the Act is therefore asymmetric by design. Entry 33 is a Concurrent List entry, so state legislatures are not incompetent in the field, but the operative machinery is a central Act whose orders override inconsistent enactments under Section 6, whose delegations define what state executives may do, and whose 2002 removal order went further and required states to obtain the Centre's prior concurrence before reimposing licensing, stock or movement controls on the principal foodstuffs. A state that wishes to control a commodity the Centre has freed cannot simply legislate its way back; it works inside a permission structure. That asymmetry is the concrete meaning of the seed question whether the Act overrides state laws, and it is developed in the enforcement and assessment sections below.

## The controlled economy in operation

Between the mid-1950s and the end of the 1980s the order power built the administered economy that older Indians remember as a physical fact: the licensed fair price shop, the levy, the movement permit, the zonal boundary a truck could not cross with grain in it. The characteristic instruments were four. Licensing, under clauses (a) and (d), made the right to deal in a commodity a permission, revocable for breach of its conditions, and thereby made every dealer an enforcement agent of the distribution policy. Levy, under clause (f), obliged millers and producers to sell a stated proportion of their output to the government at the procurement price worked out under Sections 3(3B) and 3(3C), and it was levy, more than any purchase in the open market, that filled the godowns of the Food Corporation of India and fed the public distribution system; the price at which the state buys and the machinery of procurement are the subject of the series article on the [MSP and procurement legal framework](/2014/07/01/msp-procurement-legal-framework-india/). Movement control, under clause (d), divided the country at various times into zones, most rigidly for foodgrains in the 1960s, so that surplus grain could not lawfully leave a surplus state for a deficit state except on government account, a policy defended as the only way to run a national pool and attacked, with increasing success, as a device that depressed prices to farmers in surplus regions. Price control, under clause (c), fixed maximum prices for commodities from cement to drugs, most durably in pharmaceuticals through the Drugs (Prices Control) Orders.

Sugar was the most completely administered commodity of all, and its regime shows the order power at full stretch. Under the Sugar (Control) Order, 1966, made under Section 3 and supported by the sugar-specific sub-sections 3(3C) to 3(3E), the government operated a dual pricing system for decades: a levy portion of every mill's output was taken for the public distribution system at a price fixed under Section 3(3C), and the balance was released for free sale in monthly or periodic quotas under the release mechanism, so that the state decided both what a mill received for part of its sugar and how much of the rest it could sell in any month. The levy obligation and the release mechanism survived liberalisation by a generation, and were dismantled only in 2013, when the levy obligation was abolished and the regulated release ended, following the recommendations of the committee on the sugar sector chaired by C. Rangarajan. That a single commodity's marketing could be run from a ministry for forty-seven years under one section of one Act, without the Act ever mentioning a quota, is the cleanest demonstration available of the article's opening claim about where the law under this statute lives.

The wheat trade takeover of 1973 marks the outer limit the system reached. In that year the government, facing a food crisis and convinced that private wholesale trade was defeating procurement, used the licensing machinery to take over the wholesale trade in wheat entirely, excluding private wholesalers from the market so that procurement would flow to the state alone. The experiment collapsed within a year under the weight of evasion, administrative incapacity and a poor harvest handled badly, and private trade was readmitted in 1974. The episode matters for this article because nothing in the Act changed before, during or after it: the most radical act of economic nationalisation in independent India's food economy, and its reversal, were both accomplished by order, and Parliament's role in each was confined to debating what the executive had already done. The marketing system into which these controls fitted, the regulated mandis and the state marketing statutes, is treated in the series article on the [APMC Acts and agricultural marketing](/2014/03/15/apmc-act-agricultural-marketing-india/), and the two regimes should be read together: the marketing Acts governed where a farmer could sell, and this Act governed what anyone could do with the produce afterwards.

Food was the centre of the system, but the old Section 2(a) list made the Act a general industrial control statute as well, and its orders reached deep into the industrial economy. Cement was produced, priced and allocated under a Cement Control Order for a generation, with distribution channelled through state nominees, and was freed in stages, partially in 1982 and fully by 1989, as capacity outgrew scarcity. Iron and steel were governed by the Iron and Steel (Control) Order, 1956, under which production, pricing and distribution of steel were administered for decades, with the administered price and allocation machinery dismantled only in the early 1990s as part of the general liberalisation. Paper including newsprint, coal, automobile components and tyres all spent periods inside the declared list, subject to orders of varying intensity. The significance of this industrial career for the statute's interpretation is that the order power was never, even in intention, a food emergency power alone: it was the legal chassis of the permit-and-allocation economy, and when liberalisation stripped the industrial goods away, what remained, foodstuffs, drugs, fertiliser, petroleum, jute and seeds, was the residue that politics would not release. The Schedule of seven entries is therefore not a considered list of what a modern state should control. It is the list of what successive decades of decontrol left behind.

## The food zones and the long geography of control

The movement clause wrote geography into Indian economic life. From the middle 1960s the country was divided into food zones, groups of states or single states treated as self-contained units for rice and wheat, and the movement of grain across a zonal boundary on private account was prohibited, so that a surplus district could watch grain prices fall at harvest while a deficit city a few hundred miles away paid scarcity prices for grain that moved, if it moved at all, on government account through the Food Corporation of India. The rationale was procurement arithmetic: if grain could flow freely to the highest bidder, the state could not assemble its levy and purchase quotas at the prices it had fixed, and the public distribution system would be starved of stock. The cost was borne by farmers in the surplus zones, whose produce was confined to a glutted local market, and by the principle of a common Indian market, which the Constitution's freedom of trade provisions were read, in cases such as K. Ramanathan, as permitting the state to override for essential commodities. The zonal system was loosened in stages from the late 1970s, state by state becoming its own zone and then the zones themselves fading as buffer stocks grew and procurement spread, and inter-state movement of grain on private account became lawful in the ordinary way long before the 2002 removal order tidied the position. The episode left a residue in the statute's politics: the memory of zones is why farmer organisations read any movement restriction, however temporary, as the return of a system under which the countryside subsidised the city, and why governments imposing a localised movement ban should expect, and receive, an argument about 1965 rather than about the week in question.


### Why is sugar given its own machinery inside Section 3?

Because sugar was the commodity in which the state was simultaneously the largest buyer, the price-setter and the referee of an industry of politically organised mills and cane growers, and the general provisions of Section 3(3) were not fine enough for that triple role.

Sections 3(3C), 3(3D) and 3(3E) therefore write the sugar settlement into the Act itself, in terms unusually specific for this statute. The price payable to a producer for levy sugar is calculated with regard to the minimum cane price fixed by the Centre, the manufacturing cost of sugar, the duty or tax paid or payable, and the securing of a reasonable return on the capital employed, with different prices permitted for different areas, factories and kinds of sugar. The Centre may direct that no producer, importer or exporter sell or remove sugar from bonded factory godowns except in accordance with its direction, and it may direct producers and recognised dealers on production, stocks, storage, sale, grading, packing, marking, weighment, disposal, delivery and distribution. When the Supreme Court examined this machinery in the Sitaram Sugar case, it was these written factors that gave the Court something to test the government's price against.

## The Act versus the orders

The table below is the findable artifact of this article, and it states the article's thesis in a form a trader can use. Its lesson is that the Act answers questions about power and the orders answer questions about conduct, and that confusing the two documents is the standard error in writing about this statute.

| Question | What the Act itself provides | What a control order can do | What a trader must therefore check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Licensing | Section 3(2)(a) and (d) empower orders to regulate production, storage, transport and distribution by licences and permits | Impose a licensing requirement on dealers in a named commodity, fix its conditions, fees and security, and provide for suspension or cancellation | The licensing order in force for that commodity in that state, and whether a removal order has freed the commodity from licensing |
| Price | Section 3(2)(c) empowers orders to control the price at which a commodity may be bought or sold; Sections 3(3) to 3(3C) fix only what the state pays on a compulsory sale | Fix maximum wholesale and retail prices, ceiling prices for drugs, and the levy price payable for sugar and foodgrains | The price control order for the commodity, including any Drugs (Prices Control) Order, and the date from which its figures run |
| Stock limits | Section 3(2)(d) empowers orders to regulate storage; Section 3(2)(e) empowers orders to prohibit withholding from sale | Fix the maximum quantity a wholesaler, retailer, miller or importer may hold, by commodity and sometimes by city size, and require stock declarations | The stock limit order in force, its quantity for the trader's class, and whether it has expired or been extended |
| Movement | Section 3(2)(d) empowers orders to regulate transport and distribution | Restrict or prohibit the movement of a commodity out of a district, state or zone, or require movement permits | The movement position for the specific route, since restrictions are often local and temporary |
| Requisition | Section 3(2)(f) empowers orders to require stock to be sold to the government or its nominees, with the price worked out under Sections 3(3) to 3(3C) | Impose a levy of a stated percentage of production or stock, and direct delivery to the Food Corporation of India or a state agency | The levy order for the season, the percentage it fixes, and the procurement price it adopts |
| Information | Section 3(2)(h) and (i) empower orders to collect statistics and to require books, accounts and records to be kept and produced | Require periodic returns of stocks, purchases and sales, maintenance of registers, display of price and stock lists, and issue of cash memoranda | The returns calendar and register formats under the applicable licensing or control order, since breach of these clauses is the most common prosecution trigger |

A trader who has read only the table's first column knows the constitution of the system and nothing about his obligations. A trader who has read only the second column knows the menu of possible controls and still nothing about his obligations, because every entry is a power and not a rule. Only the third column is law in the operative sense, and it is different in every state, for every commodity, and liable to change by notification. This is why the honest answer to a client's question about what the Essential Commodities Act requires is always a request for the Gazette and the state orders, and why general information of the kind this article gives, however carefully verified, is no substitute for advice on a live consignment with its own commodity, district and date.

## The contraction: how the controlled economy was wound down by order

The unwinding of the control regime after 1991 is the mirror image of its construction, and it was executed with the same instrument. Because the controls lived in orders, they could be dismantled by orders, and no repeal of the Act was ever required or seriously proposed by any government that held office during liberalisation. Industrial licensing under the Act withered as industrial policy changed and commodity declarations were withdrawn. The 2002 removal order freed the principal foodstuffs from licensing, stock limits and movement restrictions and fenced the states out of reimposing them without central concurrence. The 2006 amendment rebuilt the Schedule. Fertiliser, the second great administered commodity, kept its Fertiliser (Control) Order, 1985 under Section 3, with movement, price and quality controls moderated rather than abolished, and pharmaceuticals kept and still keep price ceilings through the Drugs (Prices Control) Order, 2013, administered by the National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority, which the Economic Survey 2019-20 examined and found, in a finding discussed below, to have raised the prices of regulated formulations relative to comparable unregulated ones. Decontrol under this Act is therefore never a single event. It is a long administrative retreat, commodity by commodity and clause by clause, which can halt or reverse at any point without any legislature voting on the reversal.

### How are the prices of medicines controlled under this Act?

Through the same Section 3 power, wearing a different order. Drugs are the first entry in the Schedule, and the Central Government has used clause (c) of Section 3(2), the price clause, to issue successive Drugs (Prices Control) Orders, of which the 2013 Order is the operative framework for ceiling prices on essential medicines.

The 2013 Order fixes ceiling prices for formulations of the medicines on the National List of Essential Medicines, calculated from market price data rather than from the manufacturer's costs, a change of method from the cost-based orders that preceded it, and the National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority enforces the ceilings and may recover overcharged amounts with interest. The arrangement is a useful control case for the whole Act, because it shows the order power doing something almost nobody campaigns against, capping the price of essential medicines, with the same legal machinery that caps the stock of onions. It also shows the power's characteristic weakness in a respectable cause: the Economic Survey 2019-20 found that the 2013 Order's regulation had been followed by higher prices for regulated formulations than for comparable unregulated ones, a finding the pharmaceutical debate continues to contest, and a reminder that an order can miss its target in a medicine market as reliably as in a vegetable market.

The retreat also never became a rout, because the political economy of food prices did not change with the economic reforms. A government may believe, as a matter of doctrine, that stock limits are bad economics, and still reach for them in the month when onion prices dominate the news, which is exactly the recurring pattern the brief for this article identifies: the statute functions as a standing emergency power whose use is episodic and whose existence is permanent. Pulses were brought under stock limits in the middle of the last decade when tur prices rose sharply, with raids on warehouses in Maharashtra and other states and large quantities of imported and domestic pulses seized or ordered to be sold. Onions produced the textbook sequence in 2019: after heavy rain damaged the kharif crop and retail prices climbed, the Central Government on 29 September 2019 imposed stock limits of one hundred quintals on retail traders and five hundred quintals on wholesale traders across the country, figures the Minister of State for Consumer Affairs, Food and Public Distribution, Danve Raosaheb Dadarao, later gave in a written answer in the Rajya Sabha, and the government paired the limits with an export prohibition imposed under the foreign trade law and the release of buffer stocks. Each element of that response, the limit, the buffer, the trade measure, sits in a different legal instrument, and only the first is this Act, a distribution of tools that the table above is designed to make legible.

## The ration system and the other standing orders

Alongside the episodic orders that make the news, the Act carries a set of standing orders that run whole systems year in and year out, and these, rather than the stock limit on onions, are its largest continuing footprint on Indian life. The largest is the public distribution system itself. The Public Distribution System (Control) Order, 2001, made under Section 3, set the obligations of state governments, fair price shop dealers and cardholders for the distribution of commodities through the ration network, and after the National Food Security Act, 2013 converted the ration entitlement into a legal right for the covered population, the Targeted Public Distribution System (Control) Order, 2015 was made under Section 3 of this Act to govern the identification of beneficiaries, the issue of ration cards, the allocation and movement of foodgrains, the licensing and monitoring of fair price shops and the grievance machinery for the entitled households. The consequence deserves emphasis: the largest welfare distribution system in the country, on which the food security of hundreds of millions of people depends, runs day to day on orders made under the same Section 3 that imposes a stock limit, and any proposal to repeal the Act must first answer for where that machinery would live.

The fuel orders form the second standing system. The Kerosene (Restriction on Use and Fixation of Ceiling Price) Order, 1993 restricted the use of subsidised kerosene and fixed its ceiling price, and the Liquefied Petroleum Gas (Regulation of Supply and Distribution) Order, 2000 governs the supply and distribution of LPG, the unauthorised possession and diversion of cylinders, and the obligations of distributors, in each case under the petroleum entry of the Schedule and Section 3. The fertiliser regime, the Fertiliser (Control) Order, 1985, regulates the manufacture, import, pricing, movement and quality of fertilisers and the licensing of dealers, and works alongside the subsidy system through which the state pays a large part of the price of urea and other fertilisers. The drugs regime, described above, is the fourth. Around these standing systems sits a penumbra of smaller orders, on the packaging of edible oils, on the declaration of stocks by sugar mills, on the display of prices, which traders meet as the texture of ordinary compliance. The picture that emerges is not of an emergency power occasionally switched on, but of a general enabling Act underneath a permanent layer of administered markets, with emergency orders laid over the top of it when prices spike. Both layers are the same legal instrument at different speeds, and any assessment of the Act that counts only the fast layer understates what repeal would disturb by a very large margin.

## The machinery that runs the Act

No new institution is created by the Act, which is another consequence of its design: a statute that only confers power needs no authority of its own, and the power is exercised through the ordinary executive. At the Centre the administering ministry is the Ministry of Consumer Affairs, Food and Public Distribution, which issues central orders and Schedule notifications and coordinates the price monitoring of essential commodities through the states. Within the states, the civil supplies and consumer affairs departments and the district administration carry the system: the Collector is the pivot of the enforcement provisions, receiving reports of seizure, deciding confiscation, and directing interim sale, while inspectors of the civil supplies department, the food and supplies staff and the police exercise the entry, search and seizure powers that orders confer under clause (j) of Section 3(2). Enforcement data, raids conducted, commodities seized, persons prosecuted, are compiled state by state and laid before Parliament in answers to questions, and it is from those answers, rather than from any annual report the Act requires, that the enforcement record must be reconstructed. The ministry also runs the sensory apparatus that tells the system when to move: the prices of a basket of essential commodities, foodgrains, pulses, edible oils, onions, potatoes, sugar and others, are collected from retail centres across the country and monitored in the ministry, and it is the behaviour of those price series, far more than any stock data, that triggers the sequence of inter-ministerial consultation, buffer release, trade measures and, where the government judges it necessary, a stock limit order under this Act. The order is the last instrument in that sequence more often than the first, a fact that complicates the popular picture of the Act as a hair-trigger device: the machinery around it is deliberative, data-fed and slow right up to the moment the order issues, whereupon it becomes very fast indeed. The Act requires no report, no review and no accounting of its own use, which is itself one of the standing criticisms of the design.

Alongside the Act stands a companion statute that supplies the one power the Act withholds, preventive detention. The Prevention of Blackmarketing and Maintenance of Supplies of Essential Commodities Act, 1980 empowers detention of persons acting in a manner prejudicial to the maintenance of supplies of essential commodities, and the Ministry of Consumer Affairs has confirmed in Parliament that state and central governments continue to exercise powers under that Act in hoarding and blackmarketing cases. The pairing is instructive. The 1955 Act attacks the commodity, through seizure, confiscation and prosecution for breach of an order; the 1980 Act attacks the person, through detention without trial on a preventive standard. A complete account of the legal regime of scarcity in India needs both, and the existence of the detention statute is the strongest evidence that the political system has never regarded the order power, wide as it is, as sufficient by itself.


## One power, many regimes: how the states vary the Act

Because Entry 33 is a Concurrent List entry and the Act is a central statute with state legislatures competent in the same field, Parliament's Act has itself been amended by state legislatures for their own territories, with the President's assent where the Constitution requires it for provisions repugnant to the central scheme, and the result is that the Act a trader faces is not textually identical across India. The state amendments printed to the central Act show the pattern. Bihar, by Bihar Act 9 of 1978, substituted its own Sections 6A and 6C, authorised officers not below the rank of Sub-Divisional Magistrate to discharge the Collector's confiscation functions, gave the Collector the powers of a civil court for summoning witnesses and compelling documents in confiscation inquiries, and declared those inquiries to be judicial proceedings. Maharashtra substituted its own Section 6A in similar terms. Uttar Pradesh amended Section 11 so that cognizance of offences in that state is taken on the report of, or under the authority of, the District Magistrate or an officer empowered by the state government, rather than on the wider wording of the central section. Punjab and Haryana inserted a Section 7A making forfeiture of packaging and conveyances mandatory, rather than discretionary, for offences relating to foodstuffs, and Rajasthan inserted the same provision with a judicial discretion to refrain for recorded reasons.

These variations are not antiquarian. They decide, in a live case, whether a confiscation inquiry is conducted by a district Collector or a sub-divisional officer, whether a court must forfeit the truck or may spare it, and whose written report can set a prosecution in motion, and they are exactly the kind of question on which a defence is won. They also carry the article's federal argument one step further. The Act is often described as centralising, and in the location of the order power it is; but its enforcement text is a negotiated federal document, recast state by state over the decades as state legislatures adjusted the machinery to their own administrative habits, under a Constitution that permitted the adjustments and a Centre that assented to them. The statute book that results, a central Act with divergent state texts of its central enforcement sections, is unusual enough that the first task in any contested case is to establish which text of Section 6A governs the seizure, a question that has only one answer per state and is regularly overlooked by writers who quote the central text as if it were universal.

## Enforcement: seizure, confiscation and punishment

The enforcement of the Act runs on two tracks that do not wait for each other, and understanding that they are independent is the single most important practical point in the whole statute. The first track is confiscation, an administrative proceeding against the goods. Where an essential commodity is seized in pursuance of a Section 3 order, a report of the seizure must be made without unreasonable delay to the Collector of the district, and Section 6A empowers the Collector, whether or not a prosecution is instituted for the contravention, to direct the commodity to be produced before him and, if satisfied that there has been a contravention of the order, to order confiscation of the commodity, of any package, covering or receptacle in which it is found, and of any animal, vehicle, vessel or other conveyance used in carrying it. Two provisos soften the edges: foodgrains or edible oilseeds seized from a producer, where he produced them himself, may not be confiscated, and the owner of a vehicle or other conveyance used for the carriage of goods or passengers for hire may be given the option to pay a fine in lieu of confiscation, capped at the market price at the date of seizure of the commodity sought to be carried. Section 6B requires a show-cause notice in writing, with the grounds stated and an opportunity to make a representation and to be heard, before a confiscation order is made. Section 6C gives an appeal, within one month, to a judicial authority appointed by the State Government, which may confirm, modify or annul the order. Section 6D provides that confiscation shall not prevent the infliction of any punishment to which the person is liable, which is the textual foundation of the two-track structure. Section 6E completes it by barring every other court, tribunal or authority from making orders about the possession, delivery, disposal, release or distribution of the seized commodity while the Collector or the appellate authority holds the matter. The bar is about the goods, not about legality: the High Courts entertain writ petitions against confiscation orders, examining whether the show-cause discipline was observed, whether the satisfaction rested on a contravention the order actually covers, and whether the Collector applied the statutory provisos, and confiscation orders are quashed on those grounds without the bar being treated as an obstacle, since what the writ court reviews is the Collector's decision rather than intermeddling with the custody of the commodity. A trader's remedies therefore run in two channels at once, the Section 6C appeal to the judicial authority for the merits of confiscation, and constitutional review for its legality, and the choice between them, and the interim protection to seek in each, is the first strategic question in any seizure case of value.

Perishables cannot wait for appeals, so Section 6A(2) lets the Collector order a seized commodity that is subject to speedy and natural decay, or whose early sale is otherwise expedient in the public interest, to be sold at the controlled price if one is fixed, or by public auction if none is, or through fair price shops where a retail price has been fixed. Section 6A(3) then controls the proceeds: they are paid to the owner or the person from whom the commodity was seized where no confiscation is ultimately ordered, where the appellate authority so requires, or where the person concerned is acquitted in a prosecution for the same contravention. Section 6C(2) adds that where confiscation is annulled or the person is acquitted and the commodity cannot be returned, he is to be paid its price as if it had been sold to the government, with reasonable interest from the day of seizure, the price being determined under the compulsory-sale provisions of Section 3. The Supreme Court read that payment provision generously in N. Nagendra Rao and Co. v. State of Andhra Pradesh (AIR 1994 SC 2663), holding that the words "for any reason" in Section 6C(2) bear a broad meaning, and the decision is also the authority for the accountability of the seizing state where goods are lost or deteriorate through the neglect of the officials who seized them: the state that takes a trader's stock into its custody takes the risk of its own carelessness with it.

### What happens to seized stock while confiscation proceedings are pending?

It does not sit in a godown waiting for the lawyers, unless the Collector lets it. Under Section 6A(2) the Collector may order perishable or urgently needed stock to be sold at once, at the controlled price, by auction, or through fair price shops, and the money then takes the commodity's place in the proceedings.

The sale proceeds, after deduction of the expenses of the sale, are held to the outcome and paid over to the owner or the person from whom the commodity was seized if no confiscation is ultimately ordered, if the appellate authority so requires, or if the prosecution for the same contravention ends in acquittal. Under Section 6E no court, tribunal or other authority can order the release, possession or distribution of the seized goods in the meantime, so the Collector's interim decision is, for practical purposes, the last word on the goods themselves, and the fight thereafter is about money. Traders experience this as the sharpest edge of the Act: the stock, which is the business, is converted into a fund within days, on an administrative satisfaction, while the merits are argued over months.

The second track is prosecution. The enforcement record that this machinery produces is uneven in a way both sides of the argument use. Raids and seizures are numerous in a crisis month, because the machinery is built for visible action; completed prosecutions are slow, because a criminal trial on a documentary offence competes for court time with everything else; and convictions are few relative to seizures, the gap the Economic Survey 2019-20 pointed to when it spoke of raids with an abysmally low conviction rate enabling rent-seeking and harassment. The confiscation track carries much of the real burden of the system, since it concludes, and it is also the track with the thinner procedural record. Parliament learns of the pattern only through answers to questions, in which ministries report seizures, cases registered and persons proceeded against state by state, and the figures move with the politics of food prices rather than with any secular trend in trading behaviour, rising in spike years and falling in calm ones. An honest reader of that record concludes neither that the machinery is a dead letter nor that it deters as designed: it is best understood as a system whose coercive effect is concentrated in its first fortnight, in the seizure and the godown visit, and whose long tail of proceedings serves mainly to discipline the next season's paperwork.

Section 7 punishes contravention of a Section 3 order on a two-tier scale that reveals what the drafter thought dangerous. Contravention of an order made with reference to clause (h) or clause (i) of Section 3(2), the information and records clauses, is punishable with imprisonment up to one year and fine. Contravention of any other order is punishable with imprisonment for a term that shall not be less than three months and may extend to seven years, and with fine, though the court may, for adequate and special reasons recorded in the judgment, go below the three-month floor, and Section 7(2B) declares that the fact that the offence caused no substantial harm to the general public or to any individual is itself an adequate and special reason. For a second or subsequent conviction under the principal tier, Section 7(2A) raises the floor to six months, again with a reasons-based escape. Section 7(1)(b) and (c) add forfeiture of the property in respect of which the order was contravened, and, if the court so orders, of the packaging and the conveyance used to carry it, a judicial forfeiture that runs alongside the Collector's administrative confiscation of the same things. Section 7(3) adds a distinctive commercial punishment: on a repeat conviction the court shall direct that the person not carry on business in that essential commodity for a period of not less than six months. Section 7A, inserted in 1984, lets the government recover amounts payable under an order, or deposits due to funds created by an order, as arrears of land revenue with simple interest at fifteen per cent per annum. Section 8 deems an attempt or an abetment to be the contravention itself. Section 9 punishes a false statement made in compliance with an order, or a false entry in books an order requires, with imprisonment up to five years, or fine, or both. Section 11 restricts cognizance to a written report by a public servant, a person aggrieved, or a recognised consumer association. Section 12A provides for summary trial of contraventions in the foodstuffs and drugs fields and of notified special orders, with imprisonment in a summary trial capped at one year.

Three procedural provisions tilt the field toward the prosecution and should be known by anyone advising a trader. Section 10C requires the court to presume the existence of the culpable mental state the offence requires, leaving the accused to prove its absence, though only to the standard the section itself sets out with its own definition of proof. Section 14 places the burden of proving that he holds the lawful authority, permit or licence an order demands squarely on the person prosecuted for acting without one. And Section 13 requires the court to presume that an order purporting to be made and signed by an authority under the Act was so made. None of these provisions is unique to this Act, presumptions of this kind are the standard equipment of economic legislation of the period, but together they mean that the contest in an Essential Commodities prosecution is rarely about whether the state can prove its framework and almost always about whether the order covered the act, whether the accused held the document, and whether the seizure and confiscation were regular.

Section 10 deals with the corporate form in which most commodity trade is actually carried on. Where the person contravening an order is a company, every person who at the time was in charge of and responsible to the company for the conduct of its business is deemed guilty along with the company, subject to the defence that the contravention took place without his knowledge or that he exercised all due diligence to prevent it; and where the offence was committed with the consent or connivance of, or is attributable to neglect by, a director, manager, secretary or other officer, that officer is also deemed guilty. Company in this section includes a firm and other associations of individuals, and director in relation to a firm means a partner. Section 10B adds a reputational sanction available against a convicted company: the court may direct publication, at the company's expense, of its name and place of business, the nature of the contravention and the fact of conviction.

An enforcement action under the Act therefore has a characteristic anatomy, and it is worth following a single consignment through it to see the two tracks and the procedural tilts working together. An inspector acting under an order's clause (j) powers enters a godown, finds stock above the limit fixed by the state stock order, and seizes the commodity, the registers and the vehicle standing loaded outside. The seizure is reported to the Collector, who issues a show-cause notice under Section 6B and, on the material before him, decides confiscation under Section 6A; if the stock is perishable or the market is short, he may already have sold it under Section 6A(2) and be holding the proceeds. The trader's appeal lies to the judicial authority under Section 6C, and no civil court or criminal court can be moved for the goods themselves because Section 6E bars the way. Separately, on a written report under Section 11, a prosecution is launched under Section 7; the trader must prove his licence under Section 14, the court presumes his mental state under Section 10C and the authenticity of the order under Section 13, and on conviction he faces imprisonment in the three-month to seven-year band, forfeiture of the same stock the Collector has been dealing with, and, on a second conviction, exclusion from the trade. Every stage of that sequence is set by the sections analysed above, none of it depends on any further legislation, and the whole of it can be set in motion by the discovery of an excess quantity in a warehouse, which is why the stock limit order, a one-page notification, is the most consequential document in Indian food law.

Around the enforcement machinery the Act builds a protective ring for its own officers, which completes the picture of a statute designed to be used without hesitation. Section 15 provides that no suit, prosecution or other legal proceeding lies against any person for anything done or intended to be done in good faith in pursuance of a Section 3 order, and that no suit lies against the government for damage caused by anything done in good faith under such an order. Section 15A, inserted in 1976, adds that no court shall take cognizance of an offence alleged against a public servant for acts done while acting or purporting to act under an order, except with the previous sanction of the Central or State Government as the case may be. Section 12B restrains the civil courts from granting an injunction or other relief against a government or public officer for acts under the Act until notice of the application has been given to that government or officer. Read together with the presumptions of Sections 10C, 13 and 14, the ring has a coherent purpose: the inspector who seizes a godown at midnight in a price crisis is not to be met, the following week, with a damages suit, a private prosecution and an injunction application, each capable of freezing the operation. The price of that protection is borne by the trader whose stock was wrongly seized, whose remedies are the appeal, the writ and the claim to the proceeds or the price, and not an action against the officer; the Nagendra Rao principle, that the state answers for loss caused by its officials' negligence in custody, is the main judicial counterweight the courts have built against the ring, and it is significant that they built it through the payment provisions rather than by piercing the good faith protection itself.

The classification of the offences has its own history, which the brief for this article compresses and which the record makes more interesting. Section 10A, inserted in 1967, provides that every offence punishable under the Act shall be cognizable. The words "and bailable" that once accompanied that provision were omitted in 1974, and the Essential Commodities (Special Provisions) Act, 1981 (Act 18 of 1981) then inserted "and non-bailable" and built a temporary enforcement superstructure of special courts and summary trials, which operated, as the Supreme Court recorded in State of Maharashtra v. Lalit Somdatta Nagpal, from 1 September 1982 for a period extended in stages to fifteen years. When that period ran out in 1997, with a short-lived ordinance in 1998 failing to revive the scheme, the special courts ceased and the parent text stood restored, leaving offences cognizable under Section 10A and their bailable or non-bailable character to be worked out from the punishments Section 7 prescribes under the general classification of the Code of Criminal Procedure, so that the principal tier, carrying three to seven years, is in practice a non-bailable class while the records tier is not. The special provisions episode is a miniature of the statute's whole career: a crisis measure, repeatedly extended, that expired not because Parliament repealed it but because the political will to renew it ran out a decade and a half later.

## What the courts have made of the power

The courts have reviewed this statute for seventy years and have, with consistency, declined to narrow it, while building a small set of real disciplines around its edges. The foundation was laid under the predecessor Act. In Harishankar Bagla v. State of Madhya Pradesh (AIR 1954 SC 465), the Supreme Court upheld the order-making scheme of the Essential Supplies (Temporary Powers) Act, 1946 against the charge of excessive delegation, holding that the legislature had laid down the policy and left only the working out of detail to the executive. In State of Rajasthan v. Nath Mal and Mitha Mal (AIR 1954 SC 307), the Court upheld price fixation under the same predecessor scheme against a delegation challenge, on the footing that the power to fix prices was guided by the statute's declared objects. Both decisions were inherited by the 1955 Act along with its text, and their combined effect was to settle, before the Act was a year old, that neither the width of Section 3 nor the delegation of price fixation within it was constitutionally vulnerable. The Act's later placement in the Ninth Schedule, at Entry 126, put Section 3 beyond challenge on fundamental rights grounds altogether through Article 31B, a fact the Supreme Court noted in Prag Ice.

The one early decision that cut the other way is equally instructive, because it shows where the courts were willing to intervene. In Dwarka Prasad Laxmi Narain v. State of Uttar Pradesh (AIR 1954 SC 224), coal dealers challenged the Uttar Pradesh Coal Control Order, 1953, made under the predecessor Act of 1946. The Supreme Court upheld the licensing scheme itself as a reasonable restriction on the trade, but struck down the clause that empowered the licensing authority to grant exemptions from the order's operation, because that discretion was unguided and uncontrolled and so arbitrary in its potential working. The distinction drawn there runs through the whole law of this statute: the courts will not second-guess the decision to control, the price chosen, or the quantity fixed, but they retain, and occasionally exercise, a jurisdiction over the manner of the power's exercise, unguided discretion, irrelevant material, and procedural default. Control orders have fallen on those grounds in the High Courts throughout the Act's life, usually in cases too small to make the law reports' front rank, and the steady trickle of such decisions is the practical content of judicial review in this field.

Prag Ice and Oil Mills v. Union of India, decided on 21 February 1978 by a seven-judge bench, is the leading authority on price control under the Act. Millers and dealers challenged the Mustard Oil (Price Control) Order, 1977, which had capped the retail price of mustard oil at Rs 10 per kilogram. The Court upheld the order. Price fixation, it held, is legislative in character even when based on objective criteria found in a report or other material, so no rule of natural justice is breached by fixing a price without hearing those affected; the test of validity is the nexus between the order and the purposes of Section 3(1), judged by reasonableness in light of probable consequences; and a reasonable rate of return, while relevant, is not the condition of validity, the consumer's interest in the availability of an essential commodity at a fair price ranking in priority over every other consideration. Shri Sitaram Sugar Company Limited v. Union of India, decided on 13 March 1990 by a five-judge bench and reported at (1990) 3 SCC 223, added the complementary discipline from the other side. Examining the levy sugar prices fixed under Section 3(3C), the Court repeated that price fixation is legislative action to which natural justice does not apply, but held that the action must be inspired by reason: the government cannot fix an arbitrary price, cannot act on extraneous considerations, and must apply its mind to the factors the sub-section itself specifies, cane price, manufacturing cost, duty and a reasonable return on capital, with the court's function exhausted once a rational basis for the conclusion is found. Between the two decisions the judicial settlement on Section 3 is complete: the power is as wide as the executive says it is, the process owed is none, but the satisfaction must be real, the material must be relevant, and a price that ignores the statute's own factors can be struck down.

The confiscation jurisprudence completes the settlement. Shambhu Dayal Agarwala v. State of West Bengal, decided on 3 May 1990, states the two-track doctrine in terms that have been quoted ever since: the Act envisages two independent proceedings, confiscation under Section 6A and prosecution leading to punishment under Section 7, and the Collector's power to sell seized stock in the public interest under Section 6A(2) exists precisely so that supply is not disrupted while proceedings run their course. Read with Section 6D and Section 6E, the position is that an acquittal does not retrospectively invalidate a confiscation, though it entitles the owner to the proceeds under Section 6A(3), and a confiscation does not depend on the prosecution being launched at all. What remains genuinely open in the case law is narrower than the text suggests: whether the satisfaction behind a particular order was formed on relevant material, whether a state order exceeded its delegation in a specific respect, and whether the Collector's confiscation procedure complied with the show-cause discipline of Section 6B, on which confiscation orders continue to be quashed with some regularity by the High Courts. The pattern is one the series has met in other statutes: the framework is unassailable, the individual exercise is not.

Challenges under the freedom of trade run on a parallel track. Licensing and movement orders restrict the fundamental right to practise a trade or carry on a business, and the courts test them as reasonable restrictions in the public interest, a test the state wins in the generality of cases because the Act's purposes, supply, equitable distribution and fair prices, are accepted as legitimate and the licensing of dealers in scarce commodities as a proportionate instrument, while losing the occasional case, in the Dwarka Prasad line, where the instrument chosen is arbitrary in design. The freedom of inter-state trade has been argued against movement bans with similar results, K. Ramanathan being the governing instance: the constitutional value of a common market yields, for essential commodities, to a demonstrated supply purpose. The cumulative doctrine is deferential but not vacant, and its practical effect over seventy years has been to move the real argument about this statute out of the courts and into the legislature and the press, which is where, on the evidence of 2006 and 2020, it has repeatedly been settled and unsettled.

One conceptual gap runs through the litigation and deserves separate notice, because it explains many acquittals. The Act nowhere defines hoarding. The word that dominates public discussion of the statute has no statutory content at all: there is no offence of hoarding, there is only the contravention of an order, and storage becomes criminal only when an order fixes a limit and the holding crosses it, or when an order prohibits withholding from sale and a refusal to sell is proved. A trader holding a very large stock in a district with no stock limit in force commits no offence under the Act however the newspapers describe him, and a trader holding a modest stock one quintal above a notified limit commits the principal offence however innocent his purpose. Prosecutions framed in the language of hoarding therefore collapse when the order is not produced, does not cover the commodity, or had expired, and the gap between the moral vocabulary of enforcement and the documentary vocabulary of the charge sheet is a standing feature of the case law. It is also, from the legislator's point of view, a deliberate choice rather than an oversight: a defined offence of hoarding would require the statute to say how much storage is legitimate, which is exactly the judgment the design reserves to the executive, commodity by commodity and week by week.

## The amendment trail in brief

The Act's own text has been amended on a rhythm that mirrors the country's food politics, and the trail is worth reading as a record of when the state felt the instrument to be too weak. The confiscation code came first: Sections 6A to 6D were inserted by the Essential Commodities (Amendment) Act, 1966 (Act 25 of 1966), in the middle of the food crisis of the middle 1960s, because seizure without a workable confiscation and disposal procedure had left enforcement pointing at prosecutions that took years while the goods rotted. The Amendment Act of 1967 (Act 36 of 1967) added Section 10A on cognizability and made a series of strengthening changes across the Act. The Amendment Act of 1974 (Act 30 of 1974) recast Section 7 in the form the penalties substantially retain, raised the principal punishment band to its three-month to seven-year shape, deleted the words "and bailable" from Section 10A, and added Section 10C's presumption of culpable mental state and the summary trial refinements of Section 12A, a package enacted in the year of the railway strike and acute inflation, when the political demand was for a visibly harder statute. The Amendment Act of 1976 (Act 92 of 1976), passed during the Emergency, strengthened the confiscation chapter again, inserting Section 6E's bar of other courts' jurisdiction and Section 15A's sanction requirement for prosecuting public servants acting under orders. The special provisions episode of 1981 to 1997, described in the enforcement section, was formally an amendment carried in a separate temporary Act, and its expiry restored the parent text without Parliament having to repeal anything. The Amendment Act of 1984 (Act 34 of 1984) added Section 7A's recovery machinery. The Amendment Act of 1986 (Act 73 of 1986) widened Section 11 so that a person aggrieved and a recognised consumer association could set the criminal law in motion, the one amendment in the trail driven from the consumer side rather than the enforcement side. Then the direction reversed: the Amendment Act of 2006 rebuilt the Schedule, and the Amendment Act of 2020 attempted the trigger discipline. Read whole, the trail has a clear moral. For the Act's first three decades every amendment sharpened the teeth; for the last three, every amendment has been about the list of commodities or the conditions of the power's exercise, and not one Parliament since 1981 has voted to make the penalties heavier, which is as close as legislative history comes to a verdict that the enforcement design of 1974 to 1976 was the high-water mark of the punitive approach.

## The 2020 amendment: the triggered power that lasted seventeen months

In June 2020 the government of the day attempted the most interesting redesign in the Act's history, and its fate is the analytical heart of any modern account of the statute. The Essential Commodities (Amendment) Ordinance, 2020 was promulgated on 5 June 2020 as one limb of a three-part agricultural marketing package, and Parliament enacted it as the Essential Commodities (Amendment) Act, 2020 (Act 22 of 2020), which received assent on 26 September 2020 and was deemed to have come into force on the ordinance date. The new statutes of the package, and the trade area freedom at their centre, are treated in the series article on the [farm laws of 2020 explained](/2014/04/01/farm-laws-2020-explained/); this article is concerned with what the amendment did to the 1955 Act, which was surgical. It inserted a new sub-section (1A) into Section 3 and left every other provision untouched. The amendment Bill was passed by Parliament in the monsoon session of 2020 alongside its two companions, and of the three measures it was, at the time of passage, the least controversial in itself: the argument in the Houses was overwhelmingly about the trade and contract farming statutes, and the commodities amendment travelled in their slipstream, a fact that foreshadowed its end.

Sub-section (1A) provided, first, that the supply of such foodstuffs, including cereals, pulses, potato, onions, edible oilseeds and oils, as the Central Government might specify by notification, could be regulated only under extraordinary circumstances, which might include war, famine, extraordinary price rise and natural calamity of a grave nature. It provided, second, that any action on imposing a stock limit should be based on price rise, and that an order regulating the stock limit of any agricultural produce could be issued only if there was a hundred per cent increase in the retail price of horticultural produce, or a fifty per cent increase in the retail price of non-perishable agricultural foodstuffs, over the price prevailing immediately preceding twelve months or the average retail price of the last five years, whichever was lower. It provided, third, by proviso, that a stock limit order should not apply to a processor or a value chain participant, a defined term covering the chain from production in the field to final consumption, including processing, packaging, storage, transport and distribution where value is added at each stage, if that person's stock did not exceed the overall ceiling of installed processing capacity, or the demand for export in the case of an exporter. And it provided, fourth, that nothing in the sub-section touched orders relating to the Public Distribution System or the Targeted Public Distribution System made under this Act or any other law. In substance, for the named foodstuffs, the standing power became a triggered power: dormant in ordinary times, available on defined emergencies, quantified where quantification mattered most, and fenced away from the processing and export investment the government wished to encourage, while the ration system, the use of the Act that touches the poorest consumers, was expressly preserved outside the new discipline.

The design of the thresholds repaid attention at the time and repays it still, because it shows a legislature trying to write the lessons of the critique into law. The two thresholds distinguish the two ways a food price misbehaves. Perishable horticultural produce, onion and potato above all, is violently seasonal: its price can rise by half between harvests in a normal year without any hoarding at all, so the trigger for it was set at a doubling, a movement that ordinary seasonality cannot explain and that therefore signals a genuine emergency. Non-perishable produce, cereals, pulses and oilseeds, stores well and moves in smoother price paths, so a fifty per cent rise in such a market is already an exceptional event and the trigger was set lower. The baseline rule, the lower of the price a year earlier and the average of five years, was designed against the ratchet, so that a limit imposed in a spike year could not be justified the following year by reference to the spike year's own prices. And the processor carve-out addressed the Gulati objection in terms: the stock a dal mill or an oilseed crusher must hold to run its plant through the year is not hoarding, and the amendment said so in the Act, up to the ceiling of installed capacity, rather than leaving the distinction to the discretion of an inspector. Whatever the fate of the package it rode in, sub-section (1A) remains the most carefully engineered provision ever inserted into this statute, and its text will be the starting draft of any future reform that returns to the triggered design.

The amendment's merits were argued then and are argued on both sides, and the stance charter requires both at full strength. For the amendment, its authors in the Ministry of Consumer Affairs and the reform economists who had demanded it for years said that it converted an arbitrary power into a rule: a trader could finally calculate, from published prices, whether a stock limit was legally possible at all, a processor could build a plant knowing its working stock was protected by the capacity ceiling, and the doubling and fifty per cent thresholds were set high enough that only a genuine spike, and not the seasonal firmness of a vegetable market, could trip them. Against it, farmer organisations and opposition parties said that the thresholds and carve-outs so narrowed the power that hoarding in a fast-moving shortage would be lawful until prices had already doubled, that the value chain exemption swallowed the rule for precisely the large aggregators whose stocks move prices, and that the amendment was being carried on the back of two companion statutes they distrusted for independent reasons. Both arguments are serious, and the second's last limb, the bundling objection, turned out to be the one that decided the amendment's fate.

The three statutes of the 2020 package provoked a year of farmer protest on the borders of Delhi, and in November 2021 the government announced their withdrawal. Parliament enacted the Farm Laws Repeal Act, 2021 (Act 40 of 2021), dated 30 November 2021, whose Section 2 repealed the three 2020 Acts and whose Section 3 did the consequential work for this statute in six words of operative text: in Section 3 of the Essential Commodities Act, 1955, sub-section (1A) shall be omitted. The series article on the [farm laws repeal Act of 2021](/2014/04/15/farm-laws-repeal-act-2021-explained/) treats the repeal as a legislative event; for the 1955 Act its effect was restoration, not reform. The pre-2020 position returned entire: the standing power over cereals, pulses, potato, onions, edible oilseeds and oils is again governed by Section 3(1) alone, a stock limit may again be imposed on the government's opinion of necessity or expediency without any price threshold being crossed, and the processor and exporter carve-outs are gone. The restoration was not theoretical. In the years after the repeal the untriggered power was used again in the ordinary way and on the ordinary standard: stock limits were imposed on wheat in June 2023, the first general use of the power against the largest foodgrain in a considerable period, and limits on tur and urad followed in the pulses market, in each case on the government's opinion of necessity under Section 3(1), with no threshold of price rise required to be shown and no processor ceiling interposed by the Act. Whatever view is taken of those measures on their merits, they demonstrate the legal point this section exists to make: the repeal did not merely end an amendment, it changed the standard by which every subsequent order is judged, from a quantified trigger a trader could verify to an opinion he can only challenge at the margins the courts have marked out.

The one experiment in the Act's history with converting discretion into a trigger lasted from 5 June 2020 to 30 November 2021, seventeen months, and it was not defeated on its merits in any legislature. It was repealed because it had been enacted inside a package whose other contents had become politically unsustainable, which is the cost of bundling that the brief for this article flags and that any future attempt at the same reform will have to price in.

## The argument about the Act, stated fairly

The liberalisation critique of the Act, in its strongest form, is not that governments are wicked but that the instrument misfires against its own purpose. Its most authoritative statement is by the government's own economists. The Economic Survey 2019-20, in a chapter titled "Undermining Markets: When Government Intervention Hurts More Than It Helps", described the Act as an anachronistic survival from an India worried about famines and shortages, and found on the price record that the imposition of stock limits on dal in the third quarter of 2006, on sugar in the first quarter of 2009 and on onions in September 2019 had not reduced the volatility of retail and wholesale prices and in the Survey's account had spiked it; that frequent and unpredictable imposition of blanket stock limits distorts the incentives for creating storage infrastructure, for moving up the agricultural value chain and for developing a national market; that enforcement raids ran with an abysmally low conviction rate; and that the Act in operation seemed to enable rent-seeking and harassment, leading the Survey to conclude that there was clear evidence for jettisoning it. Ashok Gulati, the agricultural economist and former chairman of the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices, has made the companion argument in print over many years: that a farmer's produce is perishable and seasonal, that smoothing supply between harvests is precisely the service storage performs, and that a law which treats the stored surplus as presumptively criminal taxes the one investment, in warehouses, cold chains and processing, that would make prices steadier. On this view the Act does not merely fail to cure scarcity economics; it manufactures a part of it, by ensuring that private capital will not build the buffer that public buffer stocks are then created, at fiscal cost, to replace.

The food security defence, also in its strongest form, begins by denying that the comparison is between control and a frictionless market. The market for a staple in a shortage is thin, local and panic-prone; a wholesaler who withholds stock in a rising market is not performing the textbook service of intertemporal arbitrage for the consumer who must eat this week, and the onion episodes the Survey studied involved crops damaged by rain and genuine physical shortage, in which the distributional question, who eats while supply recovers, cannot be answered by price alone without political consequences no elected government will accept. The Ministry of Consumer Affairs' consistent position in Parliament, given in written answers such as the Rajya Sabha answer of November 2019 on the onion limits, is that the Act is invoked episodically and for defined commodities when prices signal distress, that states as well as the Centre need the power because distribution is administered locally, and that the alternative to a standing power is emergency legislation in the middle of a crisis, which is slower, blunter and less accountable than an order laid before Parliament. The defence also points to the parts of the Act nobody proposes to repeal: the price ceilings on essential medicines under the Drugs (Prices Control) Order, the fertiliser control framework, and the compulsory purchase machinery that fills the public distribution system, all of which run on Section 3, so that abolishing the Act to end onion stock limits would simultaneously abolish the legal basis of drug price control and of levy procurement, a trade the critics' own proposals usually avoid by retreating to amendment. And it notes the behaviour of governments themselves as evidence: every government since 1991, of every economic persuasion, has kept the Act and used it, which on the defence's reading is not inertia but revealed judgment by the only actors who bear the electoral cost of a price spike.

The verdict this article reaches, on the record set out above, is that the defence wins on existence and the critique wins on design. A state that runs a public distribution system for hundreds of millions of people, caps the price of essential medicines, and answers electorally for the price of onions cannot rationally surrender the power to act on commodity markets in a shortage, and the Survey's proposal to jettison the Act was never adopted even by the government that published it, for that reason. But the critique is right that the power as drafted is wider than the justification: a standing, untriggered discretion to criminalise storage by notification, exercised episodically after prices have already risen, applied to commodities whose storage the country needs to encourage, and enforced with low conviction rates, is a design that predictably deters investment while delivering little price relief. The 2020 amendment was the legislature's own recognition of exactly that distinction, an attempt to keep the power and discipline its exercise with triggers, thresholds and carve-outs, and its repeal for reasons unconnected with its merits is the clearest evidence that the obstacle to reforming this Act has never been analytical. The middle position is available, was enacted once, and remains available to any government willing to legislate it on its own, unbundled.

## Assessment: what the Act does well, what it does badly, and how to read it

Judged as a piece of legislative engineering, the Act's strengths are the strengths of its design philosophy. It is complete: there is no commodity emergency within its Schedule that the executive cannot lawfully meet within days, and no gap between the mischief and the instrument has ever been the reason a crisis was mishandled. It is administratively honest: it puts enforcement where enforcement capacity exists, in the district, under the Collector, with a confiscation track that can return goods to the market instead of warehousing them as case property. And its compulsory purchase provisions, unusually, write the state's duty to pay, and the factors by which the price is to be worked out, into the Act itself, which gave the courts in Sitaram Sugar something solid to enforce. Its weaknesses are the same philosophy viewed from the other side. Its accountability is retrospective and political rather than legal and prior. Its criminal law is wholly derivative, so the citizen's exposure depends on instruments that are hard to find, scattered across central and state Gazettes, licensing orders and their amendments, a defect the table above exists to mitigate and cannot cure. And its breadth has repeatedly invited the use of scarcity powers for price politics, in which a stock limit is imposed to be seen to act, against the evidence of the Survey that the act of imposition does not move the price.

If the Act is to be reformed rather than merely retained or repealed, the record assembled in this article points to the shape the reform would have to take, and it is neither of the two positions usually argued in public. Repeal without replacement is not a serious option while the ration system, the drug ceilings and the fertiliser regime stand on Section 3. Untriggered retention is not a serious option either, on the Survey's evidence about what untriggered use does to storage investment and therefore to prices. The available middle is the one Parliament enacted in 2020 and withdrew in 2021: statutory conditions on the exercise of the power for the commodities where storage matters, thresholds stated in the Act rather than in the order, carve-outs for processing and export stated in the Act rather than conceded afterwards, and, what 2020 lacked and a successor should add, a duty to maintain a single public register of orders in force, so that the third column of this article's table can be answered from one source instead of from every Gazette in the country. None of that requires a new statute. It requires an amending Act of perhaps a dozen clauses, taken on its own, debated on its own merits, and not carried inside a package whose fate it does not control.

For the three readers this pillar serves, the payloads are these. The trader and the adviser should take from the article the discipline of the third column of the table: never advise from the Act, advise from the orders, for the commodity, the state and the date in question, and treat the Section 6A confiscation track, not the prosecution, as the client's immediate exposure when a seizure happens, because it moves first and decides the goods. The researcher should take the delegation analysis: the Act is the extreme case in the series of a statute whose content lives in subordinate instruments, and its history, including the 2002 removal order and the 2020 triggered experiment, is the best available Indian evidence on what delegated economic power does over time. The aspirant should fix the examinable skeleton: Act 10 of 1955, in force from 1 April 1955; Entry 33 of the Concurrent List as substituted by the Third Amendment of 1954 after Article 369 ran out; Section 2A and the seven-entry Schedule as recast in 2006; Section 3 as the order power, with clause (f) as the levy power and Sections 3(3B) and 3(3C) as its price machinery; Sections 6A to 6E as the confiscation code; Section 7 as the penalty code with its three-month to seven-year principal tier; and the 2020 amendment and its 2021 repeal as the standing illustration of reform by bundling and its risks. Readers assembling the provision map into a deck for a seminar or a revision session can [open the slides in the ReportMedic presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) and work through the section structure visually, which suits a statute whose architecture, rather than whose text, is the thing to be learned.

A comparative glance confirms that India is unusual in degree rather than in kind. Most states keep a standing emergency economic power on the shelf: the closest functional relative of this Act is the Defense Production Act of 1950 in the United States, a Korean War statute that confers standing powers on the President to direct priorities and allocations in industrial production, which has been reauthorised across seven decades and reached for in emergencies far removed from war. The Indian peculiarity is the field of operation. Where the American instrument addresses industrial capacity in a rich economy, the Indian instrument addresses food in an economy where food prices decide elections and where the state is itself the largest buyer and distributor of grain, so the power is exercised oftener, against smaller people, and with consequences that arrive at the ration shop rather than the factory gate. The comparison cuts both ways in the argument: it rebuts the claim that a standing power is itself an aberration, and it sharpens the claim that a power used this routinely, against this constituency, needs the discipline of stated conditions more, not less, than one kept for rare industrial emergencies.

Within the argument of this series, the Act holds a particular place. The series has traced statute after statute whose operative content sits in rules made under them, commencement notifications, schedules filled by the executive, Levy and control orders, and has treated that pattern as the standard condition of Indian legislation. This Act is the extreme case that proves the pattern is a spectrum with an end point: a statute in which the migration of content from the enacted text to the executive instrument is not partial but total, so that the enacted text survives as pure form, a Schedule, a power, an enforcement tail, while the whole substance of the law, what may be sold, by whom, in what quantity, at what price, is elsewhere. A reader who understands why the Indian state built one such statute, at a moment of genuine scarcity and with a constitutional amendment to hold it, understands something about every other statute in the series whose delegation is less complete: the direction of travel is the same, and only the distance differs.

Aspirants routinely lose marks in this area by merging four distinct instruments, and the distinctions are worth stating once, cleanly. The Essential Commodities Act is the order power: it regulates commodities in the Schedule through executive orders and punishes breach of those orders. The Prevention of Blackmarketing and Maintenance of Supplies of Essential Commodities Act, 1980 is a detention statute: it authorises preventive detention of persons prejudicing the maintenance of supplies, and it creates no orders and fixes no limits. The National Food Security Act, 2013 is an entitlement statute: it creates legal rights to receive foodgrains for the covered population, and its delivery machinery borrows this Act's Section 3 through the Targeted Public Distribution System (Control) Order, 2015, without making the 1955 Act an entitlement law. The state agricultural marketing statutes, the APMC Acts, regulate the market yards and the first sale of farm produce, a different junction of the same chain. An answer that keeps those four lanes separate, and can say which instrument does which work, is already ahead of most of the field.

The closing verdict on the Act itself can be short, because the article has been building it throughout. The Essential Commodities Act 1955 is not really a law about commodities. It is a law about who decides, and its answer, given in 1955 and never since revised, is that the executive decides, by order, at the speed of a Gazette notification, within purposes stated so widely that they exclude almost nothing, under a Parliament that laid the foundation, widened the constitutional entry to hold it, and then withdrew to the laying of orders it does not vote on. Every generation of Indian economic argument, the controlled economy's builders, the liberalisers who wound it down by order rather than by repeal, the economists of the Survey who asked for the Act to be jettisoned, the reformers of 2020 who tried to fence it with triggers, and the repeal of 2021 that restored it entire, has been an argument conducted inside that single answer. Until a legislature decides that the answer should change, the Act will remain what it has always been: a heading in the statute book, under which the real law of India's commodity markets continues to be written by other hands.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the Essential Commodities Act 1955 allow the government to do?

It allows the Central Government, and state governments acting under its delegation, to control the trade in any commodity listed in the Schedule to the Act by issuing orders. Under Section 3 an order can license dealers and producers, fix prices, limit the quantity of stock a trader may hold, restrict or prohibit the movement of a commodity, require stock to be sold to the government, require records and returns, and authorise entry, search and seizure. The Act itself imposes almost no rule directly: it confers the power, and the obligations on traders come from the orders made under it, which can be issued, changed or withdrawn by Gazette notification.

### Q: What law governed essential commodities before the Essential Commodities Act 1955?

Control of essential commodities began under the Defence of India Rules during the Second World War and was carried into peacetime by the Essential Supplies (Temporary Powers) Act, 1946, which contained substantially the same scheme: a list of essential commodities, a central power to make orders regulating or prohibiting production, supply, distribution and trade, licensing, and overriding effect for orders. The 1946 Act rested after 1950 on Article 369 of the Constitution, a temporary provision that let Parliament legislate on foodstuffs and allied commodities for five years. When that period ran out in January 1955, an Ordinance kept the regime alive until the permanent Act of 1955 replaced it, saving the existing orders.

### Q: Which constitutional entry gives Parliament power over foodstuffs?

Entry 33 of the Concurrent List in the Seventh Schedule, as substituted by the Constitution (Third Amendment) Act, 1954. The substituted entry covers trade and commerce in, and the production, supply and distribution of, the products of industries declared by Parliament to be under Union control, together with imported goods of the same kind, and additionally foodstuffs including edible oilseeds and oils, cattle fodder including oilcakes and other concentrates, raw cotton and cotton seed, and raw jute. Before the amendment, Parliament's competence over foodstuffs rested on the temporary Article 369, which expired in January 1955; the amendment made the competence permanent weeks before the Essential Commodities Act was enacted.

### Q: Which commodities are on the essential commodities list?

The Schedule to the Act contains seven entries: drugs, within the meaning of the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940; fertiliser, whether inorganic, organic or mixed; foodstuffs, including edible oilseeds and oils; hank yarn made wholly from cotton; petroleum and petroleum products; raw jute and jute textiles; and seeds, covering seeds of food-crops and of fruits and vegetables, seeds of cattle fodder, and jute seeds. The list took this form under the Essential Commodities (Amendment) Act, 2006, which replaced the original statutory definition, with its eleven classes and a wide residuary declaration power, by a Schedule that the Central Government can amend by notification under Section 2A.

### Q: How does a commodity get added to or removed from the Schedule?

By notification of the Central Government under Section 2A of the Act. The government must be satisfied that the change is necessary in the public interest, must record its reasons in the notification published in the Official Gazette, and must act in consultation with the state governments. A notification may declare a commodity essential for a limited period not exceeding six months, which the government may extend for recorded reasons in the public interest, a device used for temporary additions such as masks and hand sanitisers in March 2020. Every such notification must be laid before both Houses of Parliament. Parliament itself can also amend the Schedule by an amending Act, as it did in 2006.

### Q: What is a control order and who issues it?

A control order is an order made under Section 3 of the Act, notified in the Official Gazette, that lays down the operative rules for an essential commodity: who may deal in it, under what licence, at what price, in what quantities of stock, and subject to what records and inspections. Control orders are issued by the Central Government, and, very extensively, by state governments and their officers under the delegation of power made by Section 5, which is why most licensing and stock orders are state instruments. Familiar examples include the Sugar (Control) Order, 1966, the Fertiliser (Control) Order, 1985 and the Drugs (Prices Control) Orders. Breach of a control order, not breach of the Act directly, is what the penalties punish.

### Q: Does the Essential Commodities Act itself fix the price of any commodity?

No. The Act contains no price for anything. Section 3(2)(c) empowers the government to control by order the price at which an essential commodity may be bought or sold, and every actual price, from the mustard oil ceiling upheld in Prag Ice and Oil Mills v. Union of India to the ceiling prices of scheduled medicines under the Drugs (Prices Control) Order, 2013, is set by an order and can be revised by another order. The only prices the Act itself helps to compute are the sums the government must pay when it compulsorily buys stock under a Section 3(2)(f) order, for which Sections 3(3) to 3(3C) supply formulas based on controlled prices, market rates and statutory factors.

### Q: Can the government impose stock limits on traders?

Yes. A stock limit is imposed by an order under Section 3, resting principally on clause (d) of Section 3(2), which empowers orders regulating storage of an essential commodity, supported where needed by clause (e), which empowers orders prohibiting the withholding of a commodity from sale. A stock limit order fixes the maximum quantity that specified classes of trader, typically wholesalers, retailers, millers and importers, may hold at one time, and holding beyond the limit is a contravention punishable under Section 7 and exposes the excess stock to seizure and confiscation. On 29 September 2019, for example, the Central Government imposed nationwide onion stock limits of one hundred quintals for retail traders and five hundred quintals for wholesale traders.

### Q: Can the government compel a trader to sell stock to it under the Act?

Yes, through an order made with reference to clause (f) of Section 3(2). Such an order can require any person holding stock, or engaged in the production of or trade in an essential commodity, to sell the whole or a specified part of the quantity held, produced or received to the Central Government, a State Government, an officer or agent of a government, a government corporation, or other persons the order specifies. This is the levy power behind procurement of foodgrains and sugar. The seller is not expropriated without payment: Section 3(3) requires the agreed or controlled or market price, Section 3(3B) substitutes the approved procurement price for foodgrains, edible oilseeds and edible oils, and Section 3(3C) supplies the formula for sugar.

### Q: What must the government show before it makes a control order?

Section 3(1) requires the government to be of the opinion that the order is necessary or expedient for maintaining or increasing supplies of an essential commodity, or for securing its equitable distribution and availability at fair prices, or for securing the commodity for defence or military operations. The opinion is the government's, and courts do not retake the decision, but the satisfaction must be genuine and formed on relevant material. In Shri Sitaram Sugar Company Limited v. Union of India the Supreme Court held that price fixation under the Act, though legislative in character and attracting no duty to hear affected parties, cannot be arbitrary or rest on extraneous considerations, and must apply the factors the statute specifies, so an order ignoring its own statutory criteria can be struck down.

### Q: Does the Essential Commodities Act override state laws?

In its field of operation, yes. Section 6 provides that any order made under Section 3 shall have effect notwithstanding anything inconsistent with it in any enactment other than the Act itself, or in any instrument having effect under such an enactment, so a Section 3 order prevails over an inconsistent state law or state instrument. The Act also frames the states' own action: state governments issue most control orders only because Section 5 delegates the central power to them, the Centre may act directly at any time, and since the removal order of February 2002 a state needs the Centre's prior concurrence to reimpose licensing, stock or movement controls on the principal foodstuffs. State legislatures retain Concurrent List competence under Entry 33, but inconsistent state law yields to the central scheme.

### Q: Can the Central Government delegate its order-making power to state governments?

Yes. Section 5 empowers the Central Government, by notified order, to direct that the power to make orders or issue notifications under Section 3 be exercisable also by a State Government or by officers subordinate to it, or by central officers, in relation to specified matters and subject to specified conditions. The delegation is cumulative: the Centre keeps its own power and may act directly, as it did with the nationwide onion stock limits of September 2019. Most of the operative law under the Act, including foodgrain dealers' licensing orders and state stock orders, is made by states under Section 5 delegations, and the Supreme Court held in K. Ramanathan v. State of Tamil Nadu that a delegation carries the general power of Section 3(1), including the power to prohibit movement.

### Q: Can stock be confiscated under the Act without a criminal conviction?

Yes. Confiscation under Section 6A is an administrative proceeding before the Collector that runs independently of prosecution. The section says expressly that the Collector may proceed whether or not a prosecution is instituted for the contravention, Section 6D provides that confiscation does not prevent punishment, and the Supreme Court held in Shambhu Dayal Agarwala v. State of West Bengal that the Act envisages two independent proceedings, confiscation under Section 6A and prosecution under Section 7. Before confiscating, the Collector must issue a show-cause notice under Section 6B with the grounds stated and a hearing, and an appeal lies under Section 6C. If the owner is later acquitted, he becomes entitled to the sale proceeds or the price of the goods, but the confiscation itself needed no conviction to be valid.

### Q: What penalties does the Essential Commodities Act carry?

Section 7 punishes contravention of a Section 3 order on two tiers. Breach of an order made under the information and records clauses, clauses (h) and (i) of Section 3(2), carries imprisonment up to one year and fine. Breach of any other order carries imprisonment of not less than three months and up to seven years, and fine, with a judicial discretion to go below the floor for recorded reasons. A repeat conviction raises the floor to six months, and the court must also bar the offender from business in that commodity for at least six months. The property involved is forfeited, with packaging and conveyances forfeitable at the court's direction, false statements under an order carry up to five years under Section 9, and confiscation of the goods themselves proceeds separately under Section 6A.

### Q: Are offences under the Essential Commodities Act bailable?

The Act makes every offence punishable under it cognizable, under Section 10A, but it does not contain a single bail rule of its own in its permanent text, so bail follows the punishment the offence carries under the general classification of the Code of Criminal Procedure. Between 1982 and 1997 the Essential Commodities (Special Provisions) Act, 1981 temporarily wrote "and non-bailable" into Section 10A and created special courts and summary trials; when that period expired the parent text stood restored. The practical result is divided: contraventions in the principal tier of Section 7, punishable with three to seven years, are treated as non-bailable, while offences under the information and records tier, punishable with up to one year, are bailable. The offence, not the Act in the abstract, decides the question.

### Q: When is a company, and when are its directors, liable for an offence under the Act?

Section 10 governs offences by companies, a term that includes firms and other associations of individuals. Where the contravener is a company, the company itself is liable, and so is every person who at the time of the contravention was in charge of and responsible to the company for the conduct of its business. That person escapes liability if he proves the contravention happened without his knowledge or that he exercised all due diligence to prevent it. Directors, managers, secretaries and other officers are separately liable where the offence was committed with their consent or connivance, or is attributable to their neglect. Section 10B adds that a court convicting a company may order its name, place of business and the fact of conviction to be published at the company's expense.

### Q: Why was the Essential Commodities Act amended in 2020?

The Essential Commodities (Amendment) Act, 2020 was enacted to discipline the use of the order power over agricultural foodstuffs, on the stated reasoning that the standing threat of stock limits and controls, inherited from a scarcity era, deterred private investment in storage, processing and agricultural marketing and penalised the trade that moves produce from farm to consumer. It did not repeal any part of Section 3. It inserted Section 3(1A), under which specified foodstuffs, cereals, pulses, potato, onions, edible oilseeds and oils, could be regulated only in extraordinary circumstances such as war, famine, extraordinary price rise or a grave natural calamity, stock limits could be imposed only after defined price rises, and processors and exporters were protected within capacity and export demand limits.

### Q: How did the price trigger in the 2020 amendment work?

Section 3(1A)(b), inserted by the 2020 amendment, made price rise the only lawful basis for a stock limit order on agricultural produce and quantified the rise. An order could be issued only if the retail price of horticultural produce had increased by one hundred per cent, or the retail price of non-perishable agricultural foodstuffs by fifty per cent, over the price prevailing immediately preceding twelve months or the average retail price of the last five years, whichever was lower. The doubling threshold for perishables such as onion and potato and the lower threshold for storable produce such as cereals and pulses were designed so that ordinary seasonal firmness could not trip the power, and only an exceptional spike could. The provision was omitted when the amendment was repealed in 2021.

### Q: Did the repeal of the farm laws restore the Essential Commodities Act to its earlier form?

Yes, completely. The Farm Laws Repeal Act, 2021 (Act 40 of 2021), dated 30 November 2021, repealed the Essential Commodities (Amendment) Act, 2020 along with the other two farm statutes, and Section 3 of the Repeal Act omitted sub-section (1A) from Section 3 of the 1955 Act. With the omission, the extraordinary-circumstances condition, the hundred per cent and fifty per cent price triggers, and the processor and exporter protections all disappeared, and the regulation of cereals, pulses, potato, onions, edible oilseeds and oils returned to Section 3(1) alone, under which a stock limit may be imposed whenever the government is of the opinion that it is necessary or expedient, without any threshold being crossed. One exception within the exception had never changed: orders for the Public Distribution System were outside the 2020 discipline throughout.

### Q: Why do economists argue that stock limits can deepen a shortage instead of relieving it?

The argument is about incentives across seasons. Agricultural produce is harvested in a short window and consumed all year, so someone must store the surplus, and storage is an investment in godowns, cold chains and working capital that is made only if holding stock is lawful and predictable. A stock limit imposed without warning when prices rise forces immediate sales into a falling local market, penalises the traders who stored most, and teaches every trader to store less next season, which deepens the next shortage. The Economic Survey 2019-20 made the point on evidence, finding that stock limits on dal in 2006, sugar in 2009 and onions in 2019 had not reduced price volatility, and Ashok Gulati, former chairman of the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices, has argued the same case in print: the country taxes the storage it needs.
