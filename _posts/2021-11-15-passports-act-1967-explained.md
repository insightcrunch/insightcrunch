The Passports Act 1967 is the rare statute whose existence is itself a court judgment. Before Parliament enacted it, an Indian citizen who wished to travel abroad depended on a document that the executive issued or withheld as a matter of administrative grace, under no statute, guided by no published criteria, and answerable to no procedure that a court could test. The Supreme Court held in 1967 that the right to travel abroad forms part of the personal liberty protected by Article 21 of the Constitution, that personal liberty cannot be taken away except according to procedure established by law, and that executive discretion exercised without a law behind it also offended the equality guarantee of Article 14. Parliament responded within weeks. The Act that followed, the Passports Act 1967, Act 15 of 1967, which came into force on 24 June 1967, states in its own text the grounds on which a passport may be refused, the grounds on which it may be impounded or revoked after issue, the reasons that must be recorded and furnished, and the appeal that lies against an adverse order. Eleven years after enactment, an impounding order made under the Act produced Maneka Gandhi v Union of India, the seven judge decision of 1978 that rebuilt Indian administrative law around a single requirement: a procedure that deprives a person of life or personal liberty must be right, just and fair, and not arbitrary, fanciful or oppressive. A statute about travel documents is therefore the origin point of the general standard of fairness against which administrative action in India is measured, and an applicant refused at a passport office counter and a detenu challenging a detention order invoke, at root, the same doctrinal line.

The One Test for this pillar is practical as well as doctrinal. A reader who finishes the article should know why the statute exists at all, should be able to state the grounds on which a passport may be refused under Section 6 and impounded under Section 10, should understand the position of the applicant against whom a criminal case is pending, which is the situation that affects the largest number of real applicants, and should grasp what Maneka Gandhi decided and what it did not decide. The article states the law from the bare Act wherever the Act speaks, identifies the points on which the Act is silent and subordinate legislation or executive instruction does the work, and argues the one genuinely contested design choice in the statute, the exception to the duty to furnish reasons, at full strength on both sides before reaching a verdict.

## Why the Passports Act 1967 exists: a statute enacted because the executive lost a case

The prehistory matters because it explains the architecture. In the first two decades after Independence, the issue of passports in India rested on executive practice. The Foreign Office tradition that independent India inherited treated a passport as a document of identity and a request addressed to foreign governments for safe passage, issued by the sovereign authority as a facility rather than as an entitlement. An applicant whose papers were refused had no statutory ground to demand, no reasons he was entitled to receive, and no appellate forum. The refusal was, in form, the withholding of a favour.

Satwant Singh Sawhney was a businessman whose passport had been impounded and who was refused a fresh document, and his challenge reached a Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court, which decided the case on 10 April 1967 in Satwant Singh Sawhney v D. Ramarathnam, Assistant Passport Officer. The majority, speaking through Chief Justice Subba Rao, held that the expression personal liberty in Article 21 takes in the right of locomotion and the right to travel abroad, that under Article 21 no person can be deprived of the right to go abroad except according to procedure established by law, and that since the State had made no law regulating or prohibiting the exercise of that right, the refusal of a passport violated Article 21. The majority added a second and independent holding: the discretion vested in the executive in the matter of issuing or refusing passports was unguided and unchannelled, and its exercise was therefore plainly violative of Article 14. Justice Hidayatullah dissented on the reach of personal liberty, taking the view that travel abroad stood on a different footing. The majority view governed, and it created a legislative emergency of a specific kind: the defect the Court identified was not a badly drafted statute but the absence of a statute altogether.

Parliament cured the defect by enacting the Passports Act 1967, and the sequence deserves emphasis because it reverses the usual order of things. Ordinarily a statute comes first and litigation tests it afterwards. Here the constitutional holding came first and specified, in negative terms, exactly what the statute had to supply: a procedure established by law, with discretion channelled by stated grounds so that its exercise could survive Article 14. Read the Act with that origin in mind and its design choices become legible. The refusal grounds in Section 6 are exhaustive, and the section says so in terms, because an open ended discretion was the vice the Court condemned. The impounding grounds in Section 10 are enumerated for the same reason. The duty in Section 10 to record reasons in writing and to furnish a copy on demand is the procedural half of the same answer. The appeal in Section 11 completes it. Every operative provision of the Act is a reply to a sentence in Satwant Singh.

The doctrinal setting of the decision also matters for what followed. The Article 21 jurisprudence of the nineteen fifties, associated with A. K. Gopalan v State of Madras, had treated the fundamental rights as separate compartments, so that a law depriving a person of personal liberty was tested only against Article 21 and not against the freedoms in Article 19 or the equality guarantee in Article 14. The personal liberty tradition in Indian law, which runs through the preventive detention line of cases and is mapped in [the guide to preventive detention law in India](/2010/06/01/preventive-detention-law-india-guide/), was built inside that compartment. Satwant Singh itself applied Article 14 to passport discretion, which was an early crack in the wall. The wall came down in stages over the following decade, and the passport statute stood at both ends of the process: the absence of a passport law exposed the compartment problem in 1967, and an order under the passport law destroyed the compartment approach altogether in 1978.

## What the Act governs, and what it does not

Section 3 states the departure rule in a single sentence: no person shall depart from, or attempt to depart from, India unless he holds a valid passport or travel document for that purpose. Three features of the sentence repay attention. It governs departure, not entry; the requirement of a passport for entering India was the subject of a different and older statute, dealt with in a later section of this article. It binds every person, citizen and non citizen alike, although the grounds of refusal and the scheme of issue are built around citizens. And it makes the document, rather than any permission recorded elsewhere, the operative condition of lawful departure, which is why control of the document, through refusal, impounding, revocation and suspension, is control of the right itself.

Section 4 sets out the classes of documents. Passports are of three classes: ordinary passport, official passport and diplomatic passport. Travel documents comprise an emergency certificate authorising a person to enter India, a certificate of identity issued for purposes specified by rules, and such other certificates or documents as may be prescribed. The distinctions are functional. An ordinary passport is the citizen's general travel document. An official passport is issued to persons travelling on government business who do not hold diplomatic status. A diplomatic passport is issued to persons whose functions attract diplomatic treatment. The emergency certificate runs in the opposite direction from every other document in the section: it exists to bring a person into India, typically an Indian national abroad who has lost a passport or whose document has ceased to be valid and who requires a one way instrument to return. The certificate of identity serves persons who are not in a position to obtain a passport of the ordinary kind, including stateless persons and others whose nationality position requires a travel document that does not assert Indian citizenship in the ordinary way.

Section 20 deserves mention at the threshold because it qualifies the citizenship frame. Notwithstanding anything in the Act, the Central Government may issue a passport or travel document to a person who is not a citizen of India if it is of opinion that issue is necessary in the public interest. The provision is narrow and exceptional, and its existence shows that the Act's subject is the regulation of departure documents as such, with citizenship operating as the ordinary condition of entitlement rather than as a jurisdictional boundary. The converse provision is Section 6, under which not being a citizen of India is the first stated ground of refusal of an ordinary application. Between the two provisions the scheme is coherent: citizenship is the rule, a stated public interest exception exists, and an applicant who is refused on the citizenship ground has been refused on a ground the statute itself supplies, which is the whole point of the post 1967 order.

## Application, inquiry and the orders the authority may pass

Section 5 governs applications. An application for the issue of a passport or travel document, for an endorsement, or for a fresh document in place of one that is lost, damaged or exhausted, is made to the passport authority, the officer or authority appointed under the rules for the region in which the applicant resides or is present. The Central Government is itself a passport authority for the purposes of the Act, and in practice the system runs through regional passport offices, with the Chief Passport Officer at the head of the administrative structure and designated as the appellate authority under the rules. The application is made in the prescribed form with the prescribed fee, and the authority is empowered to make such inquiry as it considers necessary before passing an order. That inquiry power is the statutory hook on which the verification machinery, including police verification, hangs, and a later section examines what that machinery can and cannot lawfully decide.

On an application, Section 5 empowers the authority to pass one of a structured set of orders: to issue the passport or travel document, to make the endorsement sought, to refuse either, or to direct the applicant to furnish further information or documents. The structure matters because the appeal provision in Section 11 is keyed to it: an appeal lies against an order refusing an endorsement or refusing issue, described in the appeal section by reference to the clauses of Section 5 under which such orders are passed. A refusal under the Act is therefore not an informal withholding. It is an order, passed under a named provision, on one or more of the exhaustive grounds in Section 6, and it carries an appellate remedy. The distance between that position and the pre 1967 practice of unreasoned executive withholding is the distance Satwant Singh required the law to travel.

### What happens after a passport application is filed?

The passport authority makes such inquiry as it considers necessary, which in practice includes verification of identity, citizenship and antecedents through the police, and then passes an order under Section 5 granting the passport or endorsement, seeking further material, or refusing. A refusal must rest on a Section 6 ground and is appealable under Section 11.

Duration is dealt with by Section 7, which provides that a passport or travel document continues in force for the period prescribed by rules, unless revoked earlier. The Act deliberately leaves the number to subordinate legislation, and the Passports Rules prescribe a validity of ten years for an ordinary passport issued to an adult, with shorter validity for minors. The drafting choice is sound and worth noticing: validity periods are administrative parameters that change with document security, international practice and the age profile of holders, and a statute that hard coded them would require amendment for every adjustment. What the statute fixes is the legal consequence: expiry is not revocation, a document that has run its period simply ceases to be valid, and the holder's route is a fresh application, dealt with in the questions at the end of this article under the renewal and reissue distinction.

Section 8 permits extension of the period of a passport in the circumstances the rules prescribe, and Section 9 leaves the conditions and forms of passports and travel documents to rules. Section 19 empowers the Central Government to notify that a passport shall not be valid for travel to a specified country, a provision that has carried the endorsement practice by which passports were marked as not valid for travel to particular states with which India had no relations or a restricted relationship. The endorsement system belongs to the same family: a passport could be endorsed for particular countries, and Section 6 contains a separate and narrower set of grounds for refusing an endorsement for a specific country, to which the article turns next.

## Refusal under Section 6: grounds that are exhaustive

Section 6 is the provision an applicant meets first and the provision that does the heaviest constitutional work in the Act. It opens, in both of its operative subsections, with the formula that the passport authority shall refuse on one or more of the following grounds, and on no other ground. That closing phrase is the statutory answer to Satwant Singh. A refusal that rests on a consideration outside the list, on an undisclosed policy, on the adverse opinion of an officer untethered to a stated ground, or on a ground borrowed from a different statute, is a refusal the Act does not authorise. The list is the law, and the authority's task is to bring the case within a clause of the list or to grant the document.

Section 6 divides its work between two decisions. Subsection (1) governs the refusal of an endorsement for visiting a particular foreign country, and states four grounds: that the applicant may, or is likely to, engage in that country in activities prejudicial to the sovereignty and integrity of India; that the presence of the applicant in that country may, or is likely to, be detrimental to the security of India; that the presence of the applicant in that country may, or is likely to, prejudice the friendly relations of India with that or any other country; and that in the opinion of the Central Government the presence of the applicant in that country is not in the public interest. The endorsement grounds are all outward looking and country specific. They exist because a state may have no objection to a citizen travelling generally while having a grounded objection to travel to one destination, and the subsection lets the authority calibrate the restriction to the destination rather than to the person.

Subsection (2) governs refusal to issue a passport or travel document itself, and states nine grounds. The first is that the applicant is not a citizen of India. Citizenship is therefore not a background assumption of the scheme but its first operative test, and the evidentiary questions it generates, which documents prove citizenship and how disputed claims are handled, belong to the citizenship code as a whole, set out in [the complete guide to citizenship law in India](/2021/09/01/citizenship-law-india-complete-guide/). The second ground is that the applicant may, or is likely to, engage outside India in activities prejudicial to the sovereignty and integrity of India. The third is that the departure of the applicant from India may, or is likely to, be detrimental to the security of India. The fourth is that the presence of the applicant outside India may, or is likely to, prejudice the friendly relations of India with any foreign country. These three grounds, with the public interest ground that closes the list, are the predictive and evaluative grounds: they ask the authority to assess what an applicant may do abroad, and they are the grounds on which the fairness discipline developed by the courts bears most heavily, because a prediction stated without material is indistinguishable from the unguided discretion the Act was enacted to displace.

The fifth ground, clause (e), is of a different character. It is historical and verifiable: that the applicant has, at any time during the period of five years immediately preceding the date of the application, been convicted by a court in India of an offence involving moral turpitude and sentenced in respect of it to imprisonment for not less than two years. Every element is a condition. The conviction must be by a court in India. The offence must involve moral turpitude, a concept the courts have treated as turning on the nature of the act rather than on the label of the charge, so that offences of dishonesty, fraud and grave sexual wrongdoing fall within it while technical or regulatory offences ordinarily do not. The sentence must be of imprisonment for two years or more; a conviction with a lesser sentence, or with a fine alone, does not engage the clause however discreditable the conduct. And the conviction must fall inside the five year window counted back from the application date, so the ground is self extinguishing with time: a conviction that bars an application in one year ceases to bar it when the window has passed. The precision is deliberate, and it contrasts instructively with the predictive grounds. Where Parliament intended a factual bar, it wrote one with edges.

The sixth ground, clause (f), is that proceedings in respect of an offence alleged to have been committed by the applicant are pending before a criminal court in India. The seventh, clause (g), is that a warrant or summons for the appearance, or a warrant for the arrest, of the applicant has been issued by a court, or that an order prohibiting the departure of the applicant from India has been made by a court. The eighth, clause (h), is that the applicant has been repatriated and has not reimbursed the expenditure incurred in connection with the repatriation, a small ground with a clear fiscal logic: a citizen brought home at public expense who has not repaid the cost cannot demand a fresh document to repeat the journey at potential further cost. The ninth, clause (i), is that in the opinion of the Central Government the issue of a passport or travel document to the applicant will not be in the public interest. Clause (i) is the residuary ground and the one that requires the most careful handling in practice, because a public interest opinion untethered to material would recreate inside the statute the very discretion the statute exists to channel. The courts have accordingly treated the opinion as reviewable: it must be formed on material, for reasons connected with the purposes of the Act, and it must survive the fairness standard that Maneka Gandhi attached to every exercise of power under the Act.

### Can the authority refuse an endorsement for one country only?

Yes. Section 6 in its first subsection states four grounds on which an endorsement for visiting a particular foreign country must be refused, covering prejudicial activities in that country, detriment to the security of India, prejudice to friendly relations, and the public interest opinion of the Central Government. Endorsement refusal restricts a destination without refusing the passport itself.

Two structural features of Section 6 complete the picture. The authority shall refuse where a ground is made out: the section is framed as a duty, not a permission, which means the evaluative work happens at the stage of deciding whether the ground exists, not at the stage of deciding what to do about it. And the grounds operate at the date of application on the material then available, which is why the state of a criminal proceeding, the currency of a warrant, and the age of a conviction are each capable of changing the answer between one application and the next. An applicant refused on a ground that has since ceased to exist, because a proceeding has ended in acquittal or discharge, because a warrant has been recalled, or because the five year window has closed, is not met by a permanent bar. The Act contains no lifetime exclusion anywhere, and that absence is one of its considered liberal features.

## The pending criminal case: Section 6(2)(f) and the 1993 exemption

No provision of the Act touches more lives than clause (f) of Section 6(2). Criminal proceedings in India are numerous, they are frequently slow, and a large proportion of the persons against whom they are pending are employees, students, patients, traders and family members with ordinary and urgent reasons to travel. Read alone, the clause is stark: where proceedings in respect of an offence alleged to have been committed by the applicant are pending before a criminal court in India, the passport authority shall refuse to issue a passport. If the clause operated without qualification, every undertrial in the country, including persons against whom proceedings would eventually fail, would be confined within India for the duration, which in contested cases can run to many years. The statute does not operate without qualification, and the qualification is the single most useful thing an applicant in this position can learn.

The qualification comes from Section 22 of the Act, which empowers the Central Government, where it is of opinion that exemption is necessary in the public interest, to exempt any person or class of persons from the operation of provisions of the Act. Acting under that power, the Central Government issued Notification GSR 570(E) dated 25 August 1993, published by the Ministry of External Affairs and superseding an earlier notification of 1976. The notification exempts citizens of India against whom proceedings in respect of an alleged offence are pending before a criminal court in India, and who produce an order from the court concerned permitting them to depart from India, from the operation of clause (f) of Section 6(2), subject to conditions. The architecture is exact. The statutory ground is not deleted; it is disapplied, for a defined class, on a defined condition. The condition is an order of the very court before which the proceedings are pending. The criminal court, which controls the applicant's attendance at trial and is best placed to judge the risk of flight against the needs of the prosecution, becomes the gatekeeper, and the passport authority issues the document on the strength of the court's order rather than on its own assessment of the case.

The conditions attached to the exemption govern the validity of the document issued under it, and they form a ladder that applicants and courts repeatedly misunderstand. Where the court order specifies the period for which the passport is to be issued, the passport is issued for that period. Where the order gives permission to travel abroad for a period exceeding one year without specifying the validity of the passport, the passport is issued for the period of travel specified. Where the order gives permission to travel for a period of less than one year without specifying validity, the passport is issued for one year. And where the order specifies no period at all, neither for the issue of the passport nor for travel abroad, the passport is issued for one year. The default position under the notification is therefore a one year document, and an applicant who requires a longer validity must obtain a court order that says so. A line of High Court decisions has worked through the consequences: a passport authority that issues a one year document on a silent court order acts in accordance with the notification and not arbitrarily; an applicant has no entitlement to a ten year document while proceedings are pending; and a passport issued for one year can be renewed for a further year where the applicant has not travelled abroad during the currency of the document and the court's order remains in force. An office memorandum of the Ministry of External Affairs issued in October 2019 restated that the notification has statutory force and must be applied strictly, which ended a period in which some offices treated the court order route as discretionary guidance.

What counts as proceedings pending is the threshold question, and the courts have drawn the line at the court door. Clause (f) speaks of proceedings pending before a criminal court. A first information report registered by the police, an investigation in progress, and even a charge sheet filed but not yet taken on the file of a court with cognizance taken, stand at different points on the road to that position, and High Courts have repeatedly held that adverse police reports founded on a non cognizable report that no magistrate has ordered to be investigated, or on an investigation that has not matured into proceedings before a court, do not by themselves attract clause (f). The distinction matters administratively because police verification reports frequently record every mention of an applicant in police records, while the statute asks a narrower question about the state of proceedings in a court. Where proceedings are pending before a court, the route is the court order. Where they are not, clause (f) is not engaged at all, and a refusal that cites it rests on a ground the facts do not supply.

The pending case position also interacts with the documents of an existing holder. Section 10 contains a parallel ground, clause (e) of subsection (3), under which a passport may be impounded where proceedings against the holder are pending before a criminal court. The 1993 notification addresses the issue stage, and holders facing impounding on the pendency ground rely on the same court centred logic, together with the hearing and reasons discipline of Section 10 and the fairness doctrine. The practical advice embedded in the scheme, and it is guidance about the law rather than advice on any individual case, is uniform: the criminal court's order is the key document at every stage, it should be sought before the passport application is pressed, and its wording, in particular whether it specifies a period, determines the validity the passport office will grant.

Read down the appeal column and the design of the remedies becomes visible. Refusal and impounding orders of the passport authority are appealable as a class. Orders of the Central Government are not, and the residue of cases, which are disproportionately the security and public interest cases, is left to judicial review under the Constitution. Read down the reasons column and the dividing line of the whole Act appears: at the application stage the adverse order travels through the appeal structure of Section 5 and Section 11, while after issue the statute adds an express duty to record reasons and to furnish them on demand, qualified by a single exception that a later section of this article argues at full strength on both sides.

## Variation, impounding and revocation under Section 10

Section 10 governs the life of the document after issue, and its powers are graduated. Under subsection (1), the passport authority may vary or cancel the endorsements on a passport or travel document, or vary its conditions, and may require the holder to deliver up the document for that purpose. Under subsection (3), the authority may impound the document, cause it to be impounded, or revoke it, on eight grounds. Under subsection (4), the authority may revoke a document on the application of the holder, the consensual exit used where a holder surrenders a document, for example on acquiring a different status. Impounding and revocation are distinct acts with distinct consequences: an impounded passport is retained by the authority and the holder cannot travel on it, but the document subsists and can be restored if the order is reversed or the ground falls away; a revoked passport is cancelled as an instrument, and the holder's route back is a fresh application tested against Section 6 at that date. Suspension under Section 10A, examined separately, is a third and interim act.

The eight grounds of subsection (3) repay the same clause by clause attention given to Section 6, because a holder facing an order is entitled to know which clause is invoked and whether its elements are present. Clause (a) applies where the authority is satisfied that the holder is in wrongful possession of the document, the case of a person holding a passport issued to another, or holding a document after the basis of issue has been displaced. Clause (b) applies where the passport was obtained by the suppression of material information or on the basis of wrong information provided by the holder or by another person on the holder's behalf, and a proviso added in 1993 extends the power to any other passport the holder afterwards obtains, closing the route of answering impounding with a second application built on the same defective foundation. Clause (c) is the security and public interest ground: the authority deems impounding or revocation necessary in the interests of the sovereignty and integrity of India, the security of India, friendly relations of India with any foreign country, or in the interests of the general public. This is the clause under which Maneka Gandhi's passport was impounded, and it is the clause whose exercise the fairness doctrine disciplines most closely. Clause (d) applies where the holder has, after issue, been convicted by a court in India of an offence involving moral turpitude and sentenced to imprisonment for not less than two years, mirroring the conviction ground at the application stage without its five year window, because the relevant fact is that the conviction postdates the grant. Clause (e) applies where proceedings in respect of an alleged offence by the holder are pending before a criminal court in India. Clause (f) applies where a condition of the document has been contravened. Clause (g) applies where the holder has failed to comply with a notice under subsection (1) requiring delivery up of the document. Clause (h) applies where a warrant or summons for appearance, or a warrant for arrest, has been issued against the holder, or a court has made an order prohibiting the holder's departure from India, and the authority is satisfied that the warrant or order exists.

Subsection (6) extends the powers upward: the authority to whom a passport authority is subordinate may itself impound or revoke on any ground available to the passport authority. Subsections (7) and (8) add a judicial route: a court convicting the holder of an offence under the Act or the rules may also revoke the document, an appellate court or a High Court in revision may do the same, and a revocation founded on a conviction falls away if the conviction is set aside. Subsection (9) imposes the holder's corresponding duty: on revocation, the holder must surrender the document without delay to the authority specified. The presence of a court ordered revocation route, with automatic revival on the setting aside of the conviction, underlines the scheme's character. The document follows the legal position of the holder, and each power over it is attached to a stated contingency rather than to a general supervisory discretion.

### What is the difference between impounding, revocation and suspension?

Impounding retains the document with the authority, so the holder cannot travel on it but it can be restored if the order is reversed. Revocation cancels the document as an instrument, leaving a fresh application as the route back. Suspension under Section 10A is an interim invalidation pending Section 10 proceedings on the security and public interest ground.

## Suspension under Section 10A: the interim power

Section 10A was inserted by the Passports (Amendment) Act 2002, Act 17 of 2002, deemed to have come into force on 23 October 2001, together with Section 10B which validated the administrative intimations by which departures had previously been restrained. The provenance explains the provision. Before it, the executive restrained departures in urgent cases by intimating immigration authorities at airports, a practice without a clear statutory home, and Section 10B converted those intimations into orders under the new section for a transitional period. Section 10A supplies the statutory home. Where the Central Government or a designated officer is satisfied that a passport or travel document is likely to be impounded or revoked under clause (c) of Section 10(3), the sovereignty, security, relations and general public ground, and that action is necessary in the public interest, the Government or the officer may suspend the document with immediate effect, or pass another appropriate order rendering it invalid, for a period not exceeding four weeks. The period may be extended, by order and for reasons recorded in writing, until the proceedings under Section 10 are concluded. Every holder against whom such an order is passed must be given an opportunity of being heard within a period of not later than eight weeks from the date of the order, whereupon the Central Government may modify or revoke the suspension. The designated officer must communicate the order immediately to the authorities at airports and other points of embarkation and to the passport authority, and those authorities must give effect to it at once.

The design is a study in interim power disciplined by time limits. The trigger is confined to a single clause of Section 10, the clause (c) ground, so suspension is unavailable as a shortcut around the other grounds, each of which must travel the ordinary route of a Section 10 order. The initial duration is capped at four weeks. Extension requires reasons in writing and is tethered to the conclusion of the substantive proceedings, so an extension cannot become a substitute for deciding them. And the hearing guarantee is written into the section with its own deadline, eight weeks, which converts the Maneka Gandhi discipline from a judicially implied requirement into an express statutory term for the one power in the Act that operates with immediate effect before any hearing can occur. A suspension that is never followed by proceedings, or a hearing that is never offered, fails the section on its own terms.

## Reasons, the hearing, and the record

Section 10 in subsection (5) imposes the duty on which the whole post issue scheme turns. Where the passport authority varies or cancels endorsements or varies conditions under subsection (1), or impounds or revokes under subsection (3), it shall record in writing a brief statement of the reasons for the order and furnish a copy of the statement to the holder on demand, unless the authority is of the opinion that furnishing the copy will not be in the interests of the sovereignty and integrity of India, the security of India, friendly relations of India with any foreign country or in the interests of the general public. The duty has three components, each doing distinct work. Recording is mandatory in every case, including the cases in which furnishing is withheld, so a reasoned record always exists inside the file even where the holder cannot see it. Furnishing is on demand, which places a small but real burden of initiative on the holder, and the demand should be made, because the statement of reasons is the foundation document of any appeal: Section 11 requires an appeal petition to be accompanied by a copy of the statement of reasons where such a copy has been furnished. And the exception is framed as an opinion the authority must form, on one of four stated interests, which makes the withholding itself a reviewable act rather than a silence the law does not reach.

The hearing requirement is not written into Section 10 in terms, and its presence in the law is the work of Maneka Gandhi. The Supreme Court read the rule of natural justice into the impounding power: the holder must ordinarily be given an opportunity of being heard before, or where urgency requires immediate action then promptly after, the order is made. The reasoning drew strength from the structure of the section itself. A power whose exercise requires recorded reasons, whose orders are appealable with the reasons as the foundation of the appeal, and whose consequences fall on a fundamental right, is a quasi judicial power in character, and quasi judicial power attracts the hearing rule without express words. Later practice has institutionalised the position through show cause notices preceding impounding and revocation orders, so that the ordinary sequence is notice, representation, order with recorded reasons, and appeal. An order that skips the sequence carries the defect on its face.

The paper record of a passport dispute is unusually determinative of its outcome, because the appeal and any later judicial review are both conducted on the order, the recorded reasons and the material the authority had before it. A holder who keeps the application file complete, the court order in a pending case matter current, and the reasons statement obtained on demand is in a materially different position from one who reconstructs the record afterwards, and applicants who maintain their papers in ordered digital form, for which [the ReportMedic office file viewer for reading Excel, Word and PowerPoint documents](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) is one practical aid among the ordinary tools of document management, avoid the commonest unforced error in this territory, which is litigating from an incomplete file. That practical observation is general information about how the procedure works, and it is not advice on any individual application or appeal.

## Maneka Gandhi: the passport order that rebuilt administrative law

On 4 July 1977 the Regional Passport Officer at Delhi made an order impounding the passport of Maneka Gandhi under clause (c) of Section 10(3), in the interests of the general public. The order gave no further reasons. When reasons were sought, the Government of India replied that it had been decided, in the interest of the general public, not to furnish a copy of the statement of reasons. The holder challenged the order and the provision. A bench of seven judges of the Supreme Court heard the case and delivered judgment on 25 January 1978 in Maneka Gandhi v Union of India. The bench comprised Chief Justice M. H. Beg and Justices P. N. Bhagwati, Y. V. Chandrachud, V. R. Krishna Iyer, N. L. Untwalia, S. Murtaza Fazal Ali and P. S. Kailasam, and Justice Bhagwati wrote the principal opinion. What the Court did with a passport impounding order changed the meaning of Article 21 for every subject that Article touches.

The holdings must be stated with precision, because the case is cited for more than it decided and for less. The first holding concerns the content of procedure. The expression procedure established by law in Article 21 does not mean any enacted procedure, however arbitrary. The procedure contemplated by Article 21 must be right, just and fair, and not arbitrary, fanciful or oppressive; otherwise it would be no procedure at all in the constitutional sense. The second holding concerns the relationship between the rights. Articles 14, 19 and 21 are not mutually exclusive silos. A law depriving a person of personal liberty must satisfy not only Article 21 but also the requirement of non arbitrariness in Article 14 and, where a freedom in Article 19 is engaged, the restrictions discipline of that Article. The golden triangle, as later writing named the conjunction, was assembled in this case from the foundations laid by R. C. Cooper v Union of India in 1970, where an eleven judge bench had broken the compartment approach in the property and banking nationalisation context. The third holding concerns the provision itself. Section 10(3)(c) was upheld. The Court accepted that the clause, read with the procedural safeguards of the section and with the principles of natural justice read into it, was not arbitrary, and it recorded the statement made on behalf of the Government that the impounding had been ordered in the interests of the general public and that the reasons, though withheld from the holder, would be disclosed to a court if the order were challenged. The fourth holding concerns the order and its procedure. The power to impound is quasi judicial in character, the rule of audi alteram partem applies to its exercise, and the holder must ordinarily be heard; on the facts, and in the setting of the Government's statement to the Court, the impounding order was allowed to stand. The decision is therefore a study in a particular judicial technique: the statute was saved by the procedure read into it, and the individual's order survived, while the doctrine announced transformed the ground for every later case.

The significance of the case for passport law specifically is easily overstated and easily missed. Maneka Gandhi did not hold that a passport cannot be impounded on security or public interest grounds, did not strike down clause (c), and did not give a holder a merits veto over an adverse decision. What it did was procedural and structural. It established that the fairness requirements of natural justice govern the exercise of the power, that the authority's satisfaction must be formed on material and reached fairly, and that an order under the Act is amenable to review for arbitrariness under Article 14 in addition to the statute's own appeal. The merits of a security assessment remain with the authority; the fairness of the route by which the assessment is reached and applied belongs to the courts.

The significance of the case beyond passport law is the larger story, and it is the series thesis of this pillar in its strongest instance. The interrelationship holding dissolved the compartment doctrine across the fundamental rights chapter. The fairness holding converted Article 21 from a shield against lawless executive action into a standard by which enacted procedures themselves are judged, and from that root grew the vast later expansion of Article 21 into livelihood, health, environment, education and privacy. When the nine judge bench in Justice K. S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v Union of India recognised privacy as a fundamental right in 2017, it worked within the architecture Maneka Gandhi had built, and the personal liberty and proportionality analysis in [the article on the Puttaswamy privacy and Aadhaar judgment](/2019/05/15/puttaswamy-privacy-aadhaar-judgment/) traces that descent. The administrative fairness doctrine that the case generalised, the requirement that state power over the individual be exercised through a procedure that is fair and on reasons that can be tested, is the working standard across the courts and tribunals mapped in [the guide to the judicial system laws of India](/2021/04/01/judicial-system-laws-india-guide/). A statute about travel documents produced the doctrine because the case happened to present, in a small and clean form, the largest question in Indian public law: whether the Constitution is satisfied by the existence of a law, or demands in addition that the law and its administration be fair. The Court chose fairness, and Indian administrative law has lived on that choice since.

### Must the holder be heard before a passport is impounded?

Ordinarily, yes. Maneka Gandhi read the rule of natural justice into the power in Section 10, holding the power quasi judicial in character and the holder entitled to an opportunity of being heard. Where urgency requires immediate action, as under Section 10A, the hearing follows within the period the provision prescribes rather than preceding the order.

## Appeals under Section 11, and the remedies beyond appeal

Section 11 provides the appeal. A person aggrieved by an order of the passport authority refusing an endorsement or refusing the issue of a passport or travel document under Section 5, by an order under the proviso to Section 7, or by an order varying endorsements or impounding or revoking under subsections (1), (3) or (6) of Section 10, may appeal to the appellate authority, the authority to whom the passport authority is subordinate, designated under the rules, a function discharged by the Chief Passport Officer. The appeal must be preferred within the period prescribed by the Passports Rules, which is thirty days, computed in accordance with the Limitation Act 1963. An appeal filed late may be admitted if the appellant satisfies the appellate authority that there was sufficient cause for the delay. The petition must be in writing and accompanied by a copy of the statement of reasons for the order appealed against, where such a copy has been furnished, and by the prescribed fee. The appellate authority must follow the prescribed procedure, must not dispose of an appeal without giving the appellant a reasonable opportunity of representing the case, and its order confirming, modifying or reversing the order appealed against is final within the statutory scheme.

The exclusions define the remedy as sharply as the grant. The governing proviso states that no appeal shall lie against any order made by the Central Government. The exclusion is unqualified in its terms, and it covers the orders that matter most in the hardest cases, because impounding on the security and general public ground is frequently effected at the level of the Central Government or its designated officers, and refusal founded on the public interest opinion in Section 6 is an opinion of the Central Government by the clause's own terms. The consequence is a two track remedial structure. Orders of the regional passport authority travel upward through a departmental appeal in which the reasons can be canvassed on their merits. Orders of the Central Government, and the opinions on which they rest, are examinable only by a constitutional court on judicial review, where the questions are the legality of the ground, the fairness of the procedure, the existence of material, and the proportionality of the measure, rather than a rehearing of the merits. The distinction is not a defect discovered by litigants; it is the structure Parliament wrote, and any account of the Act that describes the appeal as a general remedy against passport decisions misdescribes it.

Beyond the statute, the constitutional remedies frame everything. An applicant or holder may invoke the writ jurisdiction of a High Court under Article 226, or of the Supreme Court under Article 32, against a refusal, an impounding, a revocation, a suspension, or a failure to decide. The writ courts have been the forum in which most of the working law of the Act has been made: the reach of clause (f) and the status of the 1993 notification, the treatment of adverse police reports founded on matters that are not proceedings before a court, the requirement of a show cause notice before impounding, the impermissibility of refusing renewal on grounds outside Section 6, and the insistence that a passport not be withheld as a lever in disputes, whether matrimonial, commercial or investigative, that the Act does not make relevant. Judicial review in this field is not an appeal on facts, and the courts repeat the limit as faithfully as they enforce the discipline: the authority decides, on material, fairly, within the grounds the statute states, and the court polices those conditions.

## Offences and penalties under Section 12

Section 12 supplies the criminal enforcement of the scheme, and its structure tracks the duties the earlier sections create. Subsection (1) makes five acts punishable with imprisonment extending to two years, or fine extending to five thousand rupees, or both: contravening Section 3 by departing or attempting to depart without a valid passport or travel document; knowingly furnishing false information or suppressing material information with a view to obtaining a passport or travel document, or altering or attempting to alter the entries in a document without lawful authority; failing to produce a passport or travel document for inspection when called upon by the prescribed authority; knowingly using a document issued to another person; and knowingly allowing another person to use a document issued to oneself. Subsection (1A), inserted by the amendment of 1993, addresses the non citizen who applies for or obtains a passport by suppressing information about nationality, or who holds a forged passport or travel document, and prescribes a markedly heavier sentence: imprisonment of not less than one year and extending to five years, with fine of not less than ten thousand rupees and extending to fifty thousand rupees. Subsection (2) punishes abetment of an offence under the section with the punishment of the offence abetted, where the act abetted is committed. Subsection (3) is the residuary penalty: contravention of a condition of a document, or of any provision of the Act or the rules for which no punishment is provided elsewhere, carries imprisonment extending to three months, or fine extending to five hundred rupees, or both.

Sections 13 to 17 complete the enforcement frame. Section 13 empowers arrest without warrant by designated officers in defined circumstances, and Section 14 confers powers of search and seizure. Section 15 requires the previous sanction of the Central Government before a prosecution is instituted for an offence under the Act, a filter that reserves the criminal route for cases the Government has examined, and Section 16 protects action taken in good faith. Section 17 declares that passports and travel documents issued under the Act are the property of the Central Government, a provision that explains the legal character of impounding and surrender: the holder has a right to the document's use while it is validly held, and no proprietary claim against its recall under the Act. The penalty figures of 1967 and 1993 have not been revised with inflation, and the fines in subsection (1) and subsection (3) are trivial in money terms beside the real consequences of conviction, which are the sentence of imprisonment the court may impose and the statutory consequences that follow a conviction into later applications under the conviction grounds of Sections 6 and 10. The non citizen offence in subsection (1A) is the exception that proves the calibration: where Parliament addressed document fraud aimed at the nationality foundation of the scheme, it prescribed a mandatory minimum and a fine two orders larger.

## Police verification: what it can and cannot decide

Police verification is the stage of the process applicants experience most directly, and it is the stage about which the largest volume of inaccurate writing circulates. Its legal basis is modest. Section 5 empowers the passport authority to make such inquiry as it considers necessary before passing an order, and the verification of identity, address, citizenship and antecedents is conducted under that power through the police of the districts in which the applicant resides or has resided. The report that returns is material before the authority. It is not an order, and it decides nothing by itself. The decision remains the authority's, and it must be a decision on a Section 6 ground.

Three consequences follow, and each is established in the case law of the High Courts. An adverse report must be connected to a statutory ground before it can support a refusal. A report recording that a criminal case is pending supports clause (f) only if proceedings are pending before a criminal court, and the court order route then governs. A report recording a non cognizable complaint, a neighbour dispute, a matrimonial conflict or an unverified allegation supplies no clause of Section 6, and a refusal built on it is a refusal without a ground. The authority must apply its own mind: a refusal that reproduces a police recommendation without independent satisfaction on a stated ground repeats the error Satwant Singh condemned, of discretion exercised without the channel of the statute. And the verification process has a defined subject matter. It verifies the facts the application asserts and the antecedents the statute makes relevant. It is not a general character inquiry, not a forum for the settlement of private disputes, and not a mechanism by which agencies with no statutory role in the Act can hold a document in suspense. Look Out Circulars, the instruments by which departures are in practice stopped at airports at the instance of investigating agencies and other authorities, illustrate the boundary. They are not issued under the Passports Act at all; they rest on executive instructions of the Ministry of Home Affairs, they operate at the immigration check, and the courts have subjected them to the fairness discipline of the constitutional cases, requiring a basis in law, a connection with the purposes for which they are issued, and review at intervals, while keeping them analytically separate from refusal, impounding and suspension under the Act.

## The other passport statute: entry, the 1920 Act, and the repeal of 2025

For a century, Indian law carried two passport statutes with similar names and opposite directions of travel, and confusing them is the commonest error in writing on this subject. The Passport (Entry into India) Act 1920, Act 34 of 1920, was enacted to take power to require passports of persons entering India, in the setting of the control of movement that followed the First World War. It governed entry. The Passports Act 1967 governs the issue of passports and travel documents to citizens and other persons and regulates departure from India; its own Section 25 performed the tidying of renaming the older statute, changing the short title of the Indian Passport Act 1920 to the Passport (Entry into India) Act 1920 so that the two instruments could be told apart. The distinction is not antiquarian. A provision about the documents a foreigner must hold on arrival, and a provision about the document a citizen must hold on departure, serve different state interests, are administered within different statutory schemes, and are amended and repealed on different occasions.

The occasion arrived with the Immigration and Foreigners Act 2025, Act 13 of 2025, which commenced on 1 September 2025 and consolidated the law governing foreigners and immigration. Section 36 of that Act repealed four statutes: the Passport (Entry into India) Act 1920, the Registration of Foreigners Act 1939, the Foreigners Act 1946 and the Immigration (Carriers' Liability) Act 2000, with savings for acts done under them so far as consistent with the new Act. The history and scheme of the repealed foreigners legislation is set out in [the article on the Foreigners Act 1946](/2021/11/01/foreigners-act-1946-india-explained/), at the point where the entry statute's repeal belongs. The Passports Act 1967 is not in the repeal list. It was not repealed, it was not amended by the 2025 Act, and it remains in force as the governing statute for the issue of passports to Indian citizens and for departure from India. The two statutes that shared a name for over half a century were separated by the 2025 reform: the entry statute was absorbed into a consolidated immigration code, and the departure statute, which is a fundamental rights statute in origin and effect, was left standing on its own foundations.

### Does the Passports Act govern entry into India as well as departure?

No. The Act governs the issue of passports and travel documents and regulates departure from India under Section 3. Entry was governed by the Passport (Entry into India) Act 1920, a separate statute of the colonial period, which the Immigration and Foreigners Act 2025 repealed; the Passports Act 1967 was not repealed and remains in force.

## The reasons exception, argued both ways

The exception in Section 10(5), under which the authority may withhold the statement of reasons where furnishing it would not be in the interests of the sovereignty and integrity of India, the security of India, friendly relations with any foreign country or the interests of the general public, is the one design choice in the Act on which a serious argument runs in both directions, and the stance charter of this series requires each side at full strength.

The case for the exception, as the Government of India put it in substance in Maneka Gandhi and as defenders of the provision maintain, is that a defined class of passport decisions rests on material whose disclosure defeats the decision itself. Intelligence about a traveller's intended contacts, the assessment of a foreign government shared in confidence, the existence of an investigation whose subject would be alerted by a reasoned order: in each case a duty to hand the reasons to the holder is a duty to hand the material to the person the material concerns. The exception is, on this view, confined rather than general. It is attached to four stated interests and to no others. It does not dispense with recording reasons, so the discipline of forming them operates in every case, and the recorded reasons exist for the appellate authority and for a court. And the Maneka Gandhi settlement itself depended on the exception being workable: the Court upheld the impounding framework in a case in which reasons were withheld from the holder, on the Government's statement that they would be placed before a court on challenge. A rule requiring disclosure to the holder in every case would not produce more fairness, the argument concludes; it would produce decisions taken on less material, or grounds drafted in formulas so empty that disclosure would convey nothing.

The case against the exception, as counsel for holders have argued in the line of cases following Maneka Gandhi, is that reasons are not a courtesy but the working part of the remedial structure. Section 11 builds the appeal on the statement of reasons: the petition must be accompanied by a copy where one has been furnished, and an appellant denied the statement must appeal against an order whose basis is unknown, framing grounds in the dark while the appellate authority reads the file the appellant cannot see. The exception therefore operates, in the cases where it is invoked, as a bar on the remedy rather than merely on disclosure, and those cases are the security and public interest cases in which the holder most needs a remedy. The interests that trigger the exception are stated at a width that invites routine use: the interests of the general public is a phrase without edges, and an authority minded to withhold reasons will rarely lack a formula that fits it. The safeguard offered in reply, that reasons are recorded and can be shown to a court, substitutes judicial inspection in chambers for the adversarial testing that reasons ordinarily receive, and a holder cannot point a court to the flaw in a reasoning process the holder has never seen.

The verdict the record supports is a qualified defence of the exception with the burden placed where the courts have in practice placed it. The exception is defensible for the class of material, intelligence and foreign confidence material in the main, whose disclosure genuinely defeats the decision, and the alternative of universal disclosure to the holder is not a position any security apparatus could operate. But the exception holds only under three conditions, each of which the courts have enforced and each of which is a condition of its legitimacy rather than a gloss upon it. The opinion to withhold must be formed genuinely, on the facts of the individual case, and recorded as an opinion, not recited as a formula. The recorded reasons must exist and must be capable of being placed before the appellate authority and a reviewing court, as the Government undertook in Maneka Gandhi itself. And the withholding must go to the statement furnished to the holder, never to the existence of reasons as such, so that an order defended on the basis that no reasons need exist anywhere fails at the threshold. An exception administered on those terms is a confidentiality rule. An exception administered without them is the unguided discretion of the pre 1967 practice returning through a side door, and the whole history of the statute is an argument against permitting that return.

## The amendment record: 1978, 1993 and 2002

The Act has been amended on three occasions of substance, and each amendment repaired a weakness that practice had exposed. The Passports (Amendment) Act 1978, Act 31 of 1978, worked through the machinery provisions, refining definitions and the endorsement scheme in the light of the first decade of administration. The Passports (Amendment) Act 1993, Act 35 of 1993, was the substantial revision. It inserted subsection (1A) into Section 12, creating the aggravated offence for a non citizen who obtains a passport by suppressing nationality or holds a forged document, with a mandatory minimum sentence. It added the proviso to clause (b) of Section 10(3), extending impounding and revocation to any other passport obtained by a holder whose document was impounded for suppression or wrong information. It omitted Section 18, a spent provision tying issue to the old emigration legislation. And it adjusted the endorsement and fee machinery that the growth of air travel and of applications from the Gulf migration had strained. The amendment of 1993 is also the year of the exemption notification for pending criminal cases, and the two belong together as a pair: in the same period, Parliament sharpened the consequences of document fraud while the executive softened, by the court order route, the treatment of the honest applicant facing trial.

The Passports (Amendment) Act 2002, Act 17 of 2002, inserted Sections 10A and 10B, the suspension power and the validation of departure restraining intimations, and has been examined in its own section. The pattern across the three amendments is consistent and instructive. None of them touched the refusal grounds in Section 6, the impounding grounds in Section 10(3), or the reasons duty in Section 10(5). The substantive liberties architecture of 1967, the exhaustive grounds and the recorded reasons, has stood unamended for the life of the Act, while the amendments worked at the edges: enforcement against fraud, interim power in security cases, and administrative machinery. A legislature that believed the grounds too narrow or the reasons duty too strict has had repeated occasions to say so and has not. The stability of the core is itself evidence about the settlement the Act embodies.

## Five errors in circulation, and what the Act actually says

Writing about this statute repeats a small stock of errors with great confidence, and correcting them is a service in itself, because each error sends a real applicant in a wrong direction.

The first error is that a pending criminal case is an absolute bar to a passport. It is not. Section 6(2)(f) states the ground, and the notification GSR 570(E) of 25 August 1993 disapplies it for the citizen who produces an order of the court seized of the case permitting departure, with the validity of the document fixed by the ladder the notification prescribes. Pages that state the ground without the notification describe a law that has not operated in that bare form since 1993.

The second error is that the Passports Act was repealed in the immigration reform of 2025. It was not. The Immigration and Foreigners Act 2025 repealed the Passport (Entry into India) Act 1920 and the three foreigners statutes. The Passports Act 1967 is absent from the repeal schedule and remains in force. The error is generated by the shared word passport in the two titles, which is why the article has kept the two statutes apart at every point.

The third error is that the authority must always give reasons to the holder. The duty in Section 10(5) is real but qualified on its face: reasons must always be recorded, and must be furnished on demand unless the authority forms the opinion that furnishing them would harm one of the four stated interests. An account that omits the exception misdescribes the provision, and an account that treats the exception as swallowing the duty misdescribes it in the opposite direction.

The fourth error is that an adverse police verification report decides the application. It does not. The report is material gathered under the inquiry power in Section 5. The decision is the passport authority's order, it must rest on a Section 6 ground, and a report that records matters amounting to no ground, an uninvestigated complaint, a private dispute, a non cognizable report no magistrate has acted on, cannot support a lawful refusal.

The fifth error is that a Look Out Circular is an order under the Passports Act. It is not. The Act's instruments for stopping a departure are refusal before issue, impounding, revocation and suspension after issue, each on stated grounds with stated remedies. The Look Out Circular is an executive instrument administered at immigration points under instructions of the Ministry of Home Affairs, outside the Act's scheme, and conflating the two misdirects the remedy: a circular is challenged as an executive action in the writ court, not appealed under Section 11.

## After Maneka Gandhi: the doctrine at work in passport cases

The fairness doctrine announced in 1978 did not remain an abstraction recited in examinations. It became the operating law of the passport office, through a line of decisions of the High Courts and the Supreme Court that applied its elements, material, hearing, reasons and reviewability, to the recurring fact situations of passport administration. The show cause notice before impounding or revocation, issued as standard practice, is Maneka Gandhi in administrative form: the holder is told the clause invoked and the substance of the ground, is invited to answer, and receives an order that records reasons. Orders that inverted the sequence, impounding first and explaining afterwards without urgency to justify it, were quashed in case after case, and the accumulation of those decisions is why the sequence is no longer controversial in practice.

The doctrine also policed the boundaries of the grounds. Courts set aside refusals and impoundings founded on the pendency of proceedings that had ended, on warrants that had been recalled, on matrimonial and family disputes in which a passport was being withheld as pressure in litigation the Act does not serve, and on reports of agencies whose concerns, however legitimate in their own field, matched no clause of Section 6 or Section 10. In each class of case the reasoning is the same and it is traceable to the twin sources of this article: the grounds are exhaustive, so a consideration outside them cannot be smuggled in under the public interest clause without material connecting it to the Act's purposes; and the procedure must be fair, so a decision reached without hearing the person affected, or without material the person could answer, fails the standard Maneka Gandhi set. Proportionality entered the same stream. A measure that restrains travel must answer the ground invoked and not exceed it in duration or scope, so that impounding deployed where a narrower response fits the ground, or continued after the ground has run its course, invites correction on review. The doctrinal line from Satwant Singh through Maneka Gandhi to the proportionality analysis of the privacy cases is unbroken, and passport administration is one of the fields in which every link of it can be watched operating on ordinary facts.

## Three applications worked through

The scheme is best tested against the situations that recur, and the three that follow are composites of the standard cases, stated to show which provision does the work at each step.

Take first the applicant against whom a prosecution is pending. He applies for an ordinary passport to take up employment abroad. Police verification reports the pending case accurately. The passport authority cannot grant the document on the bare application, because Section 6(2)(f) is engaged, and cannot refuse it outright as a final answer, because the 1993 notification has reordered the position. The correct course is the court order route: the applicant applies to the criminal court for permission to depart, the court, judging the stage of the trial, the applicant's roots and the prosecution's position, grants permission, specifying a period or not as it thinks fit, and the passport authority then issues the document with the validity the notification's ladder assigns to that form of order. If the court refuses permission, the passport is not issued, and the applicant's remedy is against the court's refusal in the criminal proceeding itself, not against the passport authority, which has applied the only scheme open to it. If the authority, instead of following the route, refuses on clause (f) without reference to the notification, the refusal is vulnerable on review, because it treats as absolute a ground the executive has itself qualified for a quarter of a century and more.

Take second the holder whose passport is impounded on the general public ground. She receives a show cause notice citing clause (c) of Section 10(3) in general terms. She is entitled to answer it, to a hearing in the sense the case law gives that term, to an order that records reasons, and to demand the statement of reasons under Section 10(5). If the statement is furnished, she appeals under Section 11 if the order is of the passport authority, carrying the reasons into the appeal as its foundation. If the statement is withheld under the exception, her route is the writ court, where the authority must justify both the ground and the withholding, and must be ready to place the recorded reasons before the court as the Government undertook in Maneka Gandhi. If the impounding was effected by an order of the Central Government, no appeal lies at all, and the writ route is the only one, which is why the identity of the ordering authority is the first thing a holder's adviser establishes. At every step the questions are the statutory ones: which clause, what material, what reasons, which forum.

Take third the applicant with an old conviction. He was convicted of an offence involving moral turpitude and sentenced to three years imprisonment, and he applies six years after the conviction. Clause (e) of Section 6(2) does not bar him: the conviction falls outside the period of five years immediately preceding the application, and the ground is spent by its own terms. The authority cannot revive it under the public interest clause without fresh material connecting his travel to a purpose of the Act, and a refusal that simply recites the conviction misapplies the section. Change one fact and the answer changes with it: had he applied three years after the conviction, clause (e) would have barred issue as a matter of duty, and no discretion in the authority could have granted the document. The contrast between the two versions of the same life is the cleanest demonstration in the Act of what an exhaustive ground with defined elements does: it binds the authority against the applicant while the elements last, and it binds the authority in the applicant's favour the moment they lapse.

## What the statute settles, and what it leaves open

The Act settles the constitutional question that produced it. Departure from India is regulated by a law, the discretion to refuse or recall the document that departure requires is channelled by exhaustive grounds, reasons must be recorded, an appeal lies against the authority's orders, and the fairness of the whole is guaranteed by a doctrine that the Act's own litigation history generated. For the applicant, the settlement is worth stating plainly: no passport decision in India is lawfully taken on an unstated ground, by an unrecorded process, without a remedy. The distance from the practice of 1966 is the measure of the statute.

What the Act leaves open is equally definite. It leaves the merits of security and public interest assessment to the executive, subject to fairness review rather than rehearing. It leaves validity periods, forms, fees and process to rules, which is why the working detail of applications changes more often than the law does. It leaves the hardest remedial gap where Parliament placed it, in the exclusion of Central Government orders from the appeal, so that the gravest cases have the thinnest statutory remedy and depend on the writ courts. And it leaves the reasons exception dependent on administrative good faith disciplined by judicial inspection, a dependence the courts accepted in 1978 with the Government's undertaking on the record, and which every later holder denied reasons tests again. A narrow statute produced the doctrine of general application by which Indian administration is judged. The doctrine, in return, is what keeps the narrow statute honest.

## The constitutional lineage behind the two decisions

The two passport decisions bookend a thirty year argument about the structure of fundamental rights, and the argument is worth following because the Act sits inside it at both ends. A. K. Gopalan v State of Madras, decided in 1950 on a challenge to preventive detention, established the compartment doctrine: each fundamental right was a self contained code, a law depriving a person of personal liberty fell to be tested only against Article 21, and the expression procedure established by law meant a procedure prescribed by enacted law, without a fairness content. Within that architecture, the executive's passport practice of the nineteen fifties and sixties was almost unassailable in principle, because there was no enacted procedure at all and Article 21, on the Gopalan reading, had nothing to say to executive action that no law authorised and no law forbade.

Kharak Singh v State of Uttar Pradesh, decided in 1964 on police surveillance, contained the seed of the alternative. The majority struck down domiciliary visits while upholding the rest of the surveillance regulation, and Justice Subba Rao's dissent treated personal liberty as a compendious expression covering the residue of freedoms, including movement, that the other rights did not exhaust. Three years later, as Chief Justice, he wrote the majority in Satwant Singh on the logic the dissent had prepared: personal liberty in Article 21 takes in locomotion and travel abroad, and a deprivation without law fails. The comparative material the Court drew on pointed the same way. In Kent v Dulles, decided by the Supreme Court of the United States in 1958, the right to travel was described as part of the liberty of the citizen, and passport denial on unlegislated grounds was rejected. The Indian majority's twin holdings, Article 21 for the absence of law and Article 14 for the unguided discretion, were constructed to leave Parliament a clear path: enact a law, channel the discretion, and the constitutional objection is answered. Parliament took the path within the year, first by Ordinance and then by the Act, whose Section 27 repealed the Passports Ordinance 1967 and saved actions taken under it, deeming the Act to have commenced on 5 May 1967.

R. C. Cooper v Union of India in 1970 then removed the doctrinal obstacle to the second act of the story. The eleven judge bench, in the bank nationalisation case, held that the fundamental rights interpenetrate, that a law's validity is tested by its direct effect on rights rather than by the compartment its subject matter suggests, and the Gopalan exclusivity was displaced. When Maneka Gandhi reached the Court in 1978, the interrelationship holding was therefore available to be generalised: Articles 14, 19 and 21 read together, a liberty depriving procedure required to be just, fair and reasonable, and natural justice read into a silent statute. The passport cases are not illustrations of that larger story. They are two of its load bearing events, and the statute they produced and then interpreted is the thread that ties the story together.

## Institutions: who runs the Act

The Act distributes its functions across a small set of actors, and confusing their roles is a recurring source of misdirected applications and appeals. The Central Government stands at the apex in three capacities: it is itself within the definition of passport authority for the purposes of the Act, it forms the opinions on which the public interest grounds in Sections 6 and 10 depend, and it holds the exemption power in Section 22, the rule making power in Section 24, and the sanction power for prosecutions in Section 15. Section 21 empowers it to delegate its functions under the Act to subordinate officers and authorities, and the working system is built on that delegation. Passport authorities appointed under the rules run the regional offices at which applications are received, inquiries ordered and orders passed. The appellate authority designated under the rules, the Chief Passport Officer, hears the Section 11 appeals and heads the organisation administratively within the Ministry of External Affairs, whose consular and passport division carries the policy charge of the system. Indian missions and posts abroad discharge the corresponding functions for citizens outside India, issuing and replacing documents, granting emergency certificates for return, and acting on impounding and revocation where the holder is abroad.

Two features of the institutional design deserve notice. The police have no statutory office under the Act at all. Verification is an inquiry the passport authority causes to be made, and the reporting officer is a witness to facts, not a decision maker; the institutional habit of treating an adverse report as a decision inverts the Act. And the courts appear inside the scheme in defined roles rather than as external reviewers alone: the criminal court whose order unlocks the pending case exemption, the convicting court that may revoke under Section 10, the appellate and revisional courts that may do the same, and the writ courts above the whole structure. The Act is, in institutional terms, a conversation between an administrative authority and the courts at almost every operative point, which is a further consequence of its origin in a judgment.

## Endorsements, country validity and the emigration overlay

The endorsement system belongs to the Act's original world, in which a passport was issued for specified countries and travel beyond the endorsed list required a further endorsement. Section 5 provides for applications for endorsement, Section 6 in its first subsection states the grounds for refusing one, Section 10 in its first subsection permits the authority to vary or cancel endorsements, and Section 19 empowers the Central Government to notify countries for travel to which a passport shall not be valid. The country specific instruments were used in the periods when India's external relations required them: passports carried endorsements excluding travel to states with which India had no diplomatic relations or maintained a policy of non recognition, the best remembered being the endorsements of the apartheid period and the years before the establishment of full relations with Israel in 1992. The movement of practice since has been toward the passport valid for all countries save those notified, but the statutory machinery survives intact, and it matters in principle even where it sleeps in practice, because it demonstrates the Act's preferred technique: restriction calibrated to the destination and the ground, in place of restriction of the person at large.

A separate overlay that applicants meet in practice is not part of this Act at all, and separating it prevents a common confusion. The emigration check regime, under which certain citizens travelling to certain countries for employment require emigration clearance, rests on the Emigration Act 1983 and is administered through the Protector of Emigrants under the Protector General of Emigrants. The passport records the holder's category for that regime by an endorsement practice, emigration check required or not required, assigned under the rules by criteria centred on educational qualification. A refusal of emigration clearance is not a refusal of a passport, an endorsement question under the Emigration Act is not a Section 6 ground, and a passport authority that treated the emigration overlay as a ground for withholding the document itself would be acting outside the Act. The two regimes share a document and a traveller. They do not share a statute, a decision maker or a remedy.

## Renewal, reissue and the special applications

The vocabulary of the counter distinguishes renewal from reissue, and the distinction is statutory in origin even though its working detail sits in the rules. Section 5 covers applications for a fresh passport or travel document in place of one that has expired, been lost, been damaged, or been exhausted of pages, as well as applications on a change in the holder's particulars. In the usage of the system, renewal refers to the grant of a new document on the expiry of the old one's validity, and reissue to the grant of a replacement for a subsisting document that is lost, damaged, exhausted or overtaken by a change of name, appearance or other recorded particular. The legal character of both is the same, and the courts have treated an application for renewal as an application to be decided under the Act at the date it is made: the grounds in Section 6 are applied to the applicant's position at that date, so that proceedings begun since the original issue, or a conviction recorded in the interval, are as relevant to renewal as to a first issue, and conversely a ground spent by time does not survive into the renewal because it once existed.

Special categories of application follow the same statute with adjusted machinery. A minor's passport is issued with a shorter validity under the rules, running for five years or until the holder attains majority, whichever is earlier, and applications for minors engage the consent framework the rules prescribe for the parents or guardian. A lost or damaged document is replaced on proof of the loss and the circumstances, and the replacement carries the consequence that the lost document stands cancelled in the system, so that its later presentation is an offence risk under Section 12 rather than a convenience. An applicant whose name or other particulars have changed seeks reissue on the strength of the legal record of the change. Expedited processing schemes, of which the tatkaal scheme is the standing example, are executive arrangements layered on the Act: they alter the sequence and speed of verification, which may follow issue instead of preceding it, and they neither add a ground to Section 6 nor subtract one, so that an expedited application refused must still be refused on a statutory ground, and a document issued under expedition remains liable to impounding if verification afterwards establishes a Section 10 ground. The Act's silence about speed is total, and it is one more instance of the division of labour the statute observes between law and administration.

## The study map: propositions an examiner tests

For the student and the aspirant, this statute is examined as constitutional law wearing a statutory disguise, and the propositions that recur are a fixed set worth isolating. The first is the Satwant Singh pair: travel abroad is within personal liberty under Article 21, and passport discretion without a statute offended both Article 21 and Article 14. The second is the legislative response: the Act of 1967, Act 15 of 1967, in force from 24 June 1967, with exhaustive grounds in Section 6 and Section 10 as the direct answer to the Article 14 limb. The third is the Maneka Gandhi triad: procedure under Article 21 must be right, just and fair and not arbitrary, fanciful or oppressive; Articles 14, 19 and 21 interrelate; and natural justice is read into Section 10, whose clause (c) the Court upheld on the Government's undertaking regarding disclosure to a court. The fourth is the remedial map: appeal under Section 11 to the appellate authority, no appeal against an order of the Central Government, reasons recorded always and furnished on demand subject to the Section 10(5) exception, and suspension under Section 10A confined to the clause (c) ground with a four week initial cap and a hearing within eight weeks. The fifth is the pending case mechanism: Section 6(2)(f), the exemption notification GSR 570(E) of 25 August 1993 under Section 22, the court order as the key, and the one year default validity. An answer that can deploy those five sets of propositions accurately, with the section numbers attached to the right proposition and the two judgments attached to the right holdings, covers the examined ground of the statute.

The errors examiners reward least are the errors this article has flagged in the general writing: treating Maneka Gandhi as having struck down the impounding power, when it upheld the provision and restructured its procedure; treating the pending case ground as unqualified, when the 1993 notification has governed it for three decades; treating the 2025 immigration consolidation as having repealed this Act, when the repeal schedule names the entry statute of 1920 and leaves the Act of 1967 standing; and treating a passport as a right to a document rather than as the practical condition of exercising the right to travel, a distinction on which Satwant Singh itself turns, since the fundamental right is the liberty to go abroad and the document is the statutory machinery through which that liberty is regulated.

## The predictive grounds examined: sovereignty, security and friendly relations

The predictive grounds, clauses (b), (c) and (d) of Section 6(2) with their parallels in the endorsement subsection and in Section 10(3)(c), are the provisions on which the Act's constitutional settlement is most severely tested, because they authorise the state to act on an assessment of what a person may do rather than on what a person has done. Their language is uniform and deliberate: the applicant may, or is likely to, engage in the relevant conduct. The standard is therefore probability assessed on material, not proof of an offence, and the authority is not required to wait for the conduct to occur. At the same time, the whole history of the Act stands against treating the predictive formula as a formality. A likelihood asserted without material that a reviewing court can examine is the unguided discretion of the old practice dressed in statutory words, and the courts have accordingly required three things of a predictive refusal or impounding: material in existence at the date of the order, a rational connection between the material and the likelihood asserted, and a measure proportioned to the risk identified, so that a restriction tied to one destination is not inflated into a total refusal where the ground, properly read, reaches only the destination.

The three grounds differ in their object, and the differences are operative. The sovereignty and integrity ground addresses activities aimed at the territorial and constitutional unity of India, the paradigm being organised activity abroad directed against that unity. The security ground addresses detriment to the security of India, a concept the courts have kept distinct from ordinary law and order, so that a record of common crime, without more, does not translate into a security ground, while activity bearing on defence, espionage or organised violence does. The friendly relations ground addresses prejudice to India's relations with a foreign country, and it is the ground most dependent on the executive's assessment, since the state of relations and the sensitivity of a foreign government are matters on which courts accord the widest margin; even here, the margin is a margin of assessment, not an exemption from material, and the ground must be the relations of India that are prejudiced, not the convenience of an agency. The public interest grounds that close both lists, in Section 6 the opinion of the Central Government that issue will not be in the public interest and in Section 10(3)(c) the interests of the general public, function as residuary clauses for the case that fits the Act's purposes without fitting the named heads, and the case law treats them as controlled by the named heads that precede them: a public interest objection must be of the same family, security, integrity, relations and the integrity of the document system itself, and not a repository for considerations, from tax disputes to private litigation advantage, that other statutes exist to pursue through their own machinery.

The endorsement grounds of Section 6(1) apply the same trio at the level of a single country, and their existence disciplines the use of the wider grounds. Where the material supports an objection to travel to one state, the calibrated response the Act contemplates is refusal or variation of the endorsement for that state, and a total refusal of the document requires material that supports the wider measure. That calibration principle, drawn from the structure of the section rather than from any single sentence, is the proportionality discipline of the privacy era operating inside a statute of 1967, and it is the point at which the predictive grounds are most often misapplied in practice and most often corrected on review.

## The holder abroad: protection, repatriation and return

The Act is usually read from the applicant's side of the counter, but its scheme also runs outward, to the citizen already abroad whose document fails, is lost, is impounded, or whose return the state must arrange. The emergency certificate in Section 4 is the instrument of return: a one way travel document issued by an Indian mission to a national who cannot travel on an ordinary passport, so that the citizen is never, by reason of a document failure alone, stranded without a lawful means of coming home. The missions that issue it act within the same statutory scheme as the regional offices, which is why an impounding decision can be given effect against a holder abroad and why a document reported lost in one jurisdiction stands cancelled in the system everywhere.

Repatriation is the harder case, and clause (h) of Section 6(2) and its logic repay a final look. A citizen in distress abroad, without funds, in detention, or in a conflict zone, may be brought home at public expense under arrangements the state makes as a protective function. The Act attaches a single, precise consequence to that protection where the expenditure is not reimbursed: the unreimbursed cost is a ground for refusing a fresh passport. The ground is sometimes criticised as penalising poverty, and the criticism deserves its answer. The provision does not punish the fact of repatriation; it addresses repetition, and it is extinguished by reimbursement, so that its practical operation is as a debt recovery condition attached to a further grant rather than as a character judgment. Read alongside the emergency certificate, the scheme is coherent in both directions: the state will always bring its citizen home, on a document issued for the purpose, and it will ask the citizen who was brought home at its expense to settle the cost before the ordinary document of departure is placed in his hands again.

The comparative position throws the Indian settlement into relief. In the United Kingdom, passports are issued under the royal prerogative, without a governing statute of the Indian kind, and the grounds of refusal rest on published executive policy, a position closer to the Indian practice before 1967 than to the Indian law after it. In the United States, the Secretary of State's passport power rests on statute, and Kent v Dulles confined its exercise to the authority Congress had actually granted. India, by the accident of Satwant Singh, arrived at the most juridified of the three positions: a statute with exhaustive grounds, recorded reasons and an appeal, glossed by a fairness doctrine of constitutional rank. The comparison is not a league table. It is a reminder that the Indian arrangement was not the natural shape of passport administration anywhere, but the product of a court insisting, in 1967, that a law exist, and of a Parliament that answered with a statute built to the Court's specification.

## The duty to decide, and the cost of silence

The Act contains no provision deeming a passport granted when an authority delays, and no statutory timetable for the disposal of an application. The gap has been filled by the courts on fairness principles. An application is a claim upon a statutory function connected to a fundamental right, and indefinite inaction is a refusal in substance without the discipline of a refusal in form: no ground is stated, no order exists to appeal, and the applicant is worse off than a person refused outright. High Courts entertaining writ petitions against passport delay have therefore directed disposal within fixed periods, treated unexplained inaction as arbitrary, and in the pending case line of decisions have structured the sequence itself, requiring the authority to inform the applicant within a stated time if a court order is needed and to dispose of the application within a further stated time once the order is produced. The principle unifying those directions is Maneka Gandhi applied to silence: a procedure that never produces a decision is not right, just and fair, whatever the merits of the decision would have been.

The same logic disciplines the two pressure points where delay concentrates. The first is verification. An inquiry under Section 5 must be an inquiry, conducted and concluded; a file parked awaiting a report that no one pursues is not an inquiry in progress but a decision deferred, and courts have directed authorities to decide on the material available where verification is not completed within a reasonable course despite the applicant's cooperation. The second is the court order route in pending cases. The applicant's side of that route, obtaining the order, lies in the criminal court's list and timetable, and the passport authority's obligation is to make the requirement known promptly and specifically, identifying the clause engaged and the document required, rather than allowing the applicant to learn of the requirement by attrition. None of this appears in the text of Section 5. All of it is the working content the fairness doctrine has given to the section's spare language, and an account of the Act that stopped at its text would misdescribe the law an applicant actually meets.

## The document as property, identity and evidence

Section 17 declares that every passport and travel document issued under the Act is the property of the Central Government. The declaration has practical consequences that are frequently misunderstood. The holder's interest in the document is a statutory entitlement to its use while it is validly held, not ownership; the document cannot be pledged, retained by an employer or agent as security, or withheld by a private party as leverage, and practices of that kind, which surface in the context of overseas employment and recruitment, have no sanction in the Act and engage its offences where a document is used by another. When the authority impounds or the holder surrenders a document, no property is taken, which is one reason the constitutional analysis of impounding has always run through Article 21 rather than through property guarantees.

Beyond the Act, the passport functions across Indian law as a premier instrument of identity and as evidence of the facts it records. It is accepted throughout the administrative system as proof of identity, of date of birth as recorded, and of address, and it is issued on the footing that the holder is a citizen of India, the ordinary applicant having established citizenship to the authority's satisfaction as the first condition of grant. Two limits on that evidentiary role are worth fixing. A passport is strong evidence of the citizenship it presupposes, but the authoritative determination of a disputed claim to citizenship belongs to the machinery of the Citizenship Act 1955, and the passport authority's satisfaction at the point of issue is an administrative satisfaction, liable to correction if the foundation fails, which is precisely the territory of the suppression ground in Section 10(3)(b) where the foundation was false from the start. And the document certifies the holder to foreign states; it confers no rights against them, a distinction the definition of a passport in the older case law, a document of identity and a request for safe passage and protection, captures exactly.

## The subordinate layer: rules, forms and fees

Section 24 empowers the Central Government to make rules to carry out the purposes of the Act, and the Passports Rules made under it carry the operative detail of the system: the authorities by whom functions are discharged, the forms of application, the documents to accompany them, the fees payable, the period within which appeals are preferred, the procedure of the appellate authority, the conditions and forms of the documents themselves, and the validity periods that Section 7 leaves to prescription. Rules made under the section must be laid before each House of Parliament. The division between the Act and the rules is the division this article has observed throughout: everything that determines whether a citizen may be refused the means of exercising a fundamental right is in the Act, in exhaustive terms, while everything that determines the paperwork by which the right is administered is in the rules, where it can be adjusted without reopening the settlement of 1967. A change in the fee, the form or the validity period is an administrative event. A change in the grounds would be a constitutional one, and the history of the amendments shows Parliament behaving as if it understood the difference.

## At the border: the departure check and the interface with other laws

Section 3 is enforced at the immigration counter, and the mechanics of that enforcement locate the Act within the wider border system. The departing traveller presents the document to the immigration authority at the port of departure; the check verifies that the document is valid, that it is the traveller's own, and that no order of impounding, revocation or suspension, and no restraint operating through the immigration system, stands against departure. Carriers bear their own responsibilities under the immigration legislation for the passengers they carry, and the practical consequence of a document failure is felt at check in before it is felt at the counter. Section 23 of the Act provides that its provisions are in addition to, and not in derogation of, the enactments it names and other laws in force, which is the statutory expression of a larger truth: the passport is a necessary condition of lawful departure, not a sufficient passport in the colloquial sense of a guarantee. A visa is the destination state's permission and belongs to that state's law. An emigration clearance, where required, belongs to the Emigration Act. A court order restraining departure binds independently of the document. The holder of a perfectly valid passport may still be unable lawfully to travel, and the holder whose passport is refused has lost the foundation on which every other permission would have rested. Keeping those layers separate, as the Act's own structure does, is the precondition of directing any grievance to the authority that actually caused it.

## From the passport counter to proportionality: the doctrinal line completed

The series to which this pillar belongs has repeatedly found a narrow statute generating a doctrine of general application, and the passport statute is the instance against which the others are measured. The line runs in a clean sequence. In 1967 a missing statute taught that liberty cannot be conditioned on executive grace. In 1978 an impounding order taught that enacted procedure must itself be fair, and that the rights interrelate. In the decades following, the fairness standard generalised across administrative law: licensing, blacklisting, admissions, service discipline and every other field in which the state acts upon the individual absorbed the requirements of material, hearing, reasons and review that the passport cases had articulated in a small compass. When the constitutional courts developed the structured proportionality test in the privacy and Aadhaar litigation, asking whether a measure pursues a legitimate aim, is suitable and necessary for it, and balances its burden against its object, they were refining a discipline whose first Indian statement in the liberty context was the demand that a procedure be right, just and fair and not arbitrary, fanciful or oppressive. Passport adjudication continues to supply the discipline with its most ordinary and most instructive facts, because the interests are easy to state and the burdens easy to see: a person kept from a job, a treatment, a family or a trial abroad by a document decision feels the whole weight of the state in a single sheet of paper, and the law's answer, that the decision must rest on a stated ground, reached fairly, for recorded reasons, with a remedy attached, is the answer Indian public law gives in every field. That is the statute's final significance, and it is why an article about refusal grounds and impounding clauses is, in the end, an article about the standard by which government in India is judged.

## The minor powers: variation, delivery up and surrender

Below impounding and revocation sits a tier of smaller powers that the authority uses far more often, and Section 10 arranges them with the same care. Under subsection (1), the authority may, for reasons it records in the manner the section prescribes, vary or cancel the endorsements on a document or vary its conditions, and for that purpose require the holder to deliver up the document. Variation is the instrument for changes that fall short of taking the document away: the removal of a country endorsement that policy no longer requires, the correction of a condition, the narrowing or widening of the document's permitted use as the holder's position changes. The delivery up notice is the procedural hinge of the tier, and the Act makes it do double duty, because a failure to comply with the notice is itself a ground of impounding under clause (g) of subsection (3). The design is proportionate in form: the holder who delivers the document for variation keeps the document, varied; the holder who ignores the notice converts a minor administrative act into a ground for the major one, by the holder's own choice. Revocation on the holder's own application under subsection (4) completes the tier at its consensual end, the route used where a holder acquires a status inconsistent with the document held, takes the citizenship of another country, which Indian law does not permit to coexist with Indian citizenship, or simply wishes to close the document's life in an orderly way. Even surrender is documented, because a document that is the property of the Central Government under Section 17 ends its life by return to its owner, not by destruction at the holder's hand.

## Suppression, second passports and the integrity of the system

Clause (b) of Section 10(3), the suppression ground, carries more litigation than its single sentence suggests, because it is where the Act's administrative scheme meets the criminal scheme of Section 12 and where the facts are most often disputed. The elements are three: information that is material, suppression or falsity in its provision, and a connection between the information and the grant. Materiality is judged against the grounds themselves: information bearing on identity, citizenship, antecedents within Section 6, or the existence of another passport is material by that test, while an error in a detail that could not have affected any ground is not the suppression the clause addresses, and courts have set aside orders that treated trivial or inadvertent inaccuracies as if they were fraud on the system. Knowledge matters on the criminal side, where Section 12(1)(b) punishes only the person who knowingly furnishes false information or suppresses material information with a view to obtaining the document, while the administrative power in Section 10(3)(b) is directed at the document's foundation and can operate where the grant was in fact built on wrong information, whoever supplied it. The 1993 proviso extending the power to any other passport the holder obtains prevents the obvious evasion, and the case law on duplicate and second passports, obtained in different names, spellings or jurisdictions within the system, is the working out of that proviso. The system interest at stake is not pedantic. A passport system in which one person can hold two live identities is a system whose document foreign states will cease to trust, and the trust of foreign states in the document is, in the end, what the document is for.

## A Union subject: the federal position

The Act is a Union statute in the strictest sense, and the federal position explains features of its administration that otherwise puzzle. Entry 19 of the Union List in the Seventh Schedule assigns to Parliament the subject of admission into, and emigration and expulsion from, India, together with passports and visas. No state legislature can enact a passport law, no state government can issue a travel document under its own authority, and the document is uniform across the country in form, grounds and remedies. The states appear in the system in one capacity only, as the machinery of verification: the district police who report on an applicant act in aid of a Union function, on a reference from a Union authority, under a Union statute. That arrangement, in which the Centre decides and the states inquire, inverts the pattern of much Indian administration and accounts for a characteristic friction of the system, since the applicant experiences the process through a local police station while the law that governs the experience is made in Delhi and tested in the constitutional courts. It also accounts for the uniformity of the case law's direction of travel: because the grounds and the remedies are national, a principle settled by one High Court on the reach of clause (f) or the discipline of Section 10 is available to applicants everywhere, and the passport system is one of the few areas of everyday administration in which a single national standard, stated by the Supreme Court in two judgments eleven years apart, governs the counter practice of every office in the country.

## The definitions that carry the scheme

Section 2 collects the definitions on which the operative provisions depend, and three of them do structural work. A passport is defined as a passport issued under the Act, and a travel document likewise, which ties the departure rule in Section 3 to documents of the Act's own creation and distinguishes them from foreign passports and from informal papers. The passport authority is defined by reference to appointment under the rules, which is what permits the system to be staffed by designated officers across the country and in missions abroad while remaining a single statutory authority for the purposes of orders and appeals. And the definitions of the document classes in Section 4, read with the rule making power, allow the forms themselves to evolve, from the booklet of 1967 to the machine readable document of the present system, without amendment of the Act. The definitional architecture is unglamorous and exact, and it is part of why a statute drafted in a few weeks in 1967 has administered a system grown beyond anything its drafters processed, without its operative sections requiring reconstruction.

The extent clause completes the frame. The Act extends to the whole of India, and its practical operation follows the citizen abroad through the missions, so that the system it creates has no territorial gap in which a holder is outside the statute's protection or outside its discipline. A holder in a foreign country is served by the same Act, subject to the same grounds, and entitled to the same reasons and remedies, administered through the post that serves the place of residence. Uniformity of extent matches the uniformity of the document itself, and both follow from the Act's character as the single national code of departure.

The citizen abroad also illustrates the Act's treatment of status change. A holder who voluntarily acquires the citizenship of another country ceases to be an Indian citizen by the operation of the Citizenship Act, and the Indian passport in his hands loses its foundation at the same moment. Continued use of the document after that change is not a technical irregularity; it engages the suppression ground and the offences provisions, because the document asserts a citizenship the holder no longer possesses, and the surrender route under Section 10 is the lawful close of the document's life. The symmetry is characteristic of the statute: the document follows status, at issue, during its currency, and at its end, and every legal event in the holder's life that the Act cares about is an event that changes, confirms or terminates that status.

A closing observation on method belongs to the pillar itself. Every position stated in this article that carries a section number, a date or a penalty has been taken from the text of the Act, from the text of the judgments, or from the text of the 1993 notification and the 2025 repeal provision, and where the working position rests on rules or executive instruction rather than on the Act, the article has said so at the point of statement. In a field where the secondary writing repeats the absolute bar error, the repeal error and the unqualified reasons error from one another in a chain, that discipline is not pedantry. It is the difference between an account of the law and an account of the writing about the law, and the Act, which exists because a court insisted on the distinction between power and the appearance of power, deserves the first kind.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Why was the Passports Act 1967 enacted?

Because the Supreme Court held in Satwant Singh Sawhney v D. Ramarathnam, decided on 10 April 1967, that the right to travel abroad forms part of the personal liberty protected by Article 21, that it cannot be deprived except according to procedure established by law, and that the executive's unguided discretion to issue or refuse passports also violated Article 14. No statute governed passports at that point. Parliament enacted the Passports Act 1967, Act 15 of 1967, in force from 24 June 1967, to supply the missing law, with exhaustive grounds of refusal and impounding as its answer to the judgment.

### Q: What passports and travel documents does the Act provide for?

Section 4 provides three classes of passport: ordinary passport, official passport for persons travelling on government business without diplomatic status, and diplomatic passport. Travel documents under the section are an emergency certificate authorising a person to enter India, used chiefly for the one way return of a national whose ordinary document is unavailable, a certificate of identity issued for purposes specified by rules, serving persons who cannot obtain an ordinary passport, and such other certificates or documents as may be prescribed. Section 20 additionally permits the Central Government to issue a document to a person who is not a citizen of India where issue is necessary in the public interest.

### Q: On what grounds can a passport be refused?

Only on the grounds in Section 6, which states them exhaustively. Under Section 6(2) the authority shall refuse where the applicant is not a citizen of India; may engage abroad in activities prejudicial to sovereignty and integrity; whose departure may be detrimental to security; whose presence abroad may prejudice friendly relations; who has been convicted within the preceding five years of an offence involving moral turpitude with a sentence of two years or more; against whom criminal proceedings are pending before a court; against whom a warrant, summons or departure prohibition is outstanding; who has not reimbursed repatriation expenditure; or whose issue the Central Government considers contrary to the public interest. Section 6(1) states four narrower grounds for refusing an endorsement for a particular country.

### Q: Can a passport be impounded or revoked?

Yes, under Section 10(3), on eight grounds: the holder is in wrongful possession of the document; it was obtained by suppression of material information or on wrong information; impounding is necessary in the interests of sovereignty and integrity, security, friendly relations or the general public; the holder has after issue been convicted of an offence involving moral turpitude with a sentence of two years or more; proceedings against the holder are pending before a criminal court; a condition of the document has been contravened; the holder has failed to deliver up the document on notice; or a warrant, summons or departure prohibition stands against the holder. Section 10A adds an interim suspension power for the security and public interest ground.

### Q: What did the Maneka Gandhi judgment decide?

In Maneka Gandhi v Union of India, decided on 25 January 1978 by a bench of seven judges on a challenge to an impounding order under Section 10(3)(c), the Supreme Court held that a procedure depriving a person of personal liberty under Article 21 must be right, just and fair and not arbitrary, fanciful or oppressive; that Articles 14, 19 and 21 are not mutually exclusive and must be read together; and that the rule of natural justice, including a hearing for the holder, is to be read into the impounding power. The Court upheld Section 10(3)(c) itself and allowed the impounding on the facts to stand, on the Government's undertaking that reasons would be placed before a court on challenge.

### Q: Can a person with a pending criminal case get a passport?

Yes, through the court order route. Section 6(2)(f) makes pending criminal proceedings a ground of refusal, but Notification GSR 570(E) dated 25 August 1993, issued under the exemption power in Section 22, exempts a citizen against whom proceedings are pending from that ground where the citizen produces an order of the court seized of the case permitting departure from India. The passport is then issued with the validity the notification prescribes. A pending case is therefore not an absolute bar, but the criminal court's permission is the key document, and without it the statutory ground operates and refusal follows.

### Q: What appeal lies against a passport decision?

Section 11 gives an appeal to the appellate authority, the authority to whom the passport authority is subordinate, designated under the rules and discharged by the Chief Passport Officer. The appeal lies against orders refusing issue or endorsement under Section 5 and against orders varying endorsements or impounding or revoking under Section 10, and must be filed within the period prescribed by the Passports Rules, thirty days, with power in the appellate authority to admit a late appeal for sufficient cause. The petition must be accompanied by the statement of reasons where one has been furnished. No appeal lies against an order made by the Central Government; the remedy there is judicial review.

### Q: Which passport related statute was repealed in 2025?

The Passport (Entry into India) Act 1920, Act 34 of 1920, which governed the requirement of a passport for persons entering India. Section 36 of the Immigration and Foreigners Act 2025, Act 13 of 2025, in force from 1 September 2025, repealed it together with the Registration of Foreigners Act 1939, the Foreigners Act 1946 and the Immigration (Carriers' Liability) Act 2000. The Passports Act 1967, which governs the issue of passports to Indian citizens and departure from India, is not in the repeal schedule, was not amended by the 2025 Act, and remains in force. The two similarly named statutes were thereby separated.

### Q: What does Section 3 of the Passports Act require before a person departs from India?

Section 3 provides that no person shall depart from, or attempt to depart from, India unless the person holds a valid passport or travel document for that purpose. The requirement binds citizens and non citizens alike, it governs departure rather than entry, and it makes the document itself the operative condition of lawful departure, which is why refusal before issue and impounding, revocation or suspension after issue each control the right to travel in practice. Contravention of Section 3 is an offence under Section 12(1)(a), punishable with imprisonment extending to two years, or fine extending to five thousand rupees, or both.

### Q: Who is the passport authority and how is an application under Section 5 decided?

The passport authority is the officer or authority appointed under the Passports Rules for the region, acting within a system headed administratively by the Chief Passport Officer in the Ministry of External Affairs, with the Central Government itself a passport authority under the Act and Indian missions discharging the functions abroad. On an application under Section 5 the authority may make such inquiry as it considers necessary, which is the statutory basis of verification, and then passes an order issuing the passport or travel document, making the endorsement sought, calling for further material, or refusing. A refusal must rest on a ground in Section 6 and is appealable under Section 11.

### Q: How long is an ordinary passport valid and how do renewal and reissue differ?

Section 7 leaves validity to the Passports Rules, which prescribe ten years for an ordinary passport issued to an adult and a shorter period for minors, five years or until the holder attains majority, whichever is earlier. In the usage of the system, renewal is the grant of a fresh document when the earlier document's validity expires, while reissue is the replacement of a subsisting document that is lost, damaged, exhausted of pages or overtaken by a change in the holder's particulars. Both are decided under the Act at the date of application, so the grounds in Section 6 are applied to the applicant's position at that date.

### Q: What does police verification examine in a passport application?

Police verification is the inquiry the passport authority causes to be made under Section 5 into the facts the application asserts: identity, address and residence, citizenship, and antecedents relevant to the grounds in Section 6, including whether criminal proceedings are pending against the applicant before a court. The report that results is material before the authority, not a decision. It can support a refusal only where what it records amounts to a statutory ground, so an uninvestigated complaint or a private dispute recorded in a report supplies no ground, and the authority must apply its own mind and pass its own order under Section 5.

### Q: How does the 1993 notification fix the validity of a passport issued during a pending criminal case?

Notification GSR 570(E) of 25 August 1993 prescribes a ladder. Where the court order specifies the period for which the passport is to be issued, the passport issues for that period. Where the order permits travel abroad for a period exceeding one year without specifying the passport's validity, the passport issues for the period of travel specified. Where the order permits travel for less than one year without specifying validity, and where the order specifies no period at all, the passport issues for one year. A one year passport may be renewed for a further year where the holder has not travelled abroad on it and the court order remains in force.

### Q: What reasons must the passport authority record and furnish when it impounds a passport?

Section 10(5) requires the authority, when it varies endorsements or conditions or impounds or revokes a passport, to record in writing a brief statement of the reasons for the order in every case, and to furnish a copy of the statement to the holder on demand. The single qualification is that furnishing may be withheld where the authority is of the opinion that doing so will not be in the interests of the sovereignty and integrity of India, the security of India, friendly relations with any foreign country or the interests of the general public. Recording is never dispensed with, and the statement, where furnished, is the foundation document of the appeal under Section 11.

### Q: What does Section 10A add to the power to impound a passport?

Section 10A, inserted by the Passports (Amendment) Act 2002 with effect from 23 October 2001, adds an interim suspension power. Where the Central Government or a designated officer is satisfied that a passport is likely to be impounded or revoked under Section 10(3)(c), the sovereignty, security, relations and general public ground, and that action is necessary in the public interest, the document may be suspended with immediate effect, or otherwise rendered invalid, for a period not exceeding four weeks, extendable for recorded reasons until the Section 10 proceedings conclude. The holder must be given an opportunity of being heard within eight weeks of the order, and the suspension is confined to that single ground.

### Q: When does the conviction ground in Section 6(2)(e) bar the issue of a passport?

The ground bars issue only where every element coincides: the applicant has been convicted by a court in India, the offence is one involving moral turpitude, the sentence imposed is imprisonment for not less than two years, and the conviction falls within the period of five years immediately preceding the date of application. A conviction followed by a fine or a shorter sentence does not engage the clause, however discreditable the conduct, and a qualifying conviction older than the five year window has spent its force by the clause's own terms. The parallel provision in Section 10(3)(d) applies the same conviction elements to a holder convicted after the passport was issued.

### Q: What offences does Section 12 of the Passports Act create and what punishments does it prescribe?

Section 12(1) punishes departure without a valid document, knowingly furnishing false information or suppressing material information to obtain a document, unauthorised alteration of entries, failure to produce a document for inspection, and knowingly using or allowing the use of another's document, with imprisonment extending to two years, or fine extending to five thousand rupees, or both. Section 12(1A) punishes a non citizen who obtains a passport by suppressing nationality or holds a forged document with imprisonment of one to five years and fine of ten thousand to fifty thousand rupees. Section 12(3) punishes other contraventions with imprisonment extending to three months, or fine extending to five hundred rupees, or both.

### Q: What remedy remains where Section 11 bars an appeal against an order of the Central Government?

Judicial review under the Constitution. The proviso to Section 11 states that no appeal shall lie against any order made by the Central Government, a category that covers the public interest opinion underlying refusals under Section 6 and impounding effected at the Centre's level. The holder or applicant may instead invoke the writ jurisdiction of a High Court under Article 226 or of the Supreme Court under Article 32, where the court examines whether a statutory ground existed, whether material supported it, whether the procedure was fair in the Maneka Gandhi sense, and whether the measure was proportionate, without rehearing the merits as an appellate body would.

### Q: What is an endorsement on a passport and when can it be refused or varied?

An endorsement is the authority's entry on a passport permitting or recording travel to a particular country, a survival of the scheme under which passports were issued for specified destinations. Section 6(1) states the grounds on which an endorsement for a foreign country must be refused: likely prejudicial activities in that country, detriment to the security of India, prejudice to friendly relations, and the Central Government's opinion that the applicant's presence there is not in the public interest. Under Section 10(1) the authority may vary or cancel endorsements after issue, requiring the holder to deliver up the document for the purpose, and Section 19 empowers notification that a passport is not valid for travel to a specified country.

### Q: What is a Look Out Circular and is it issued under the Passports Act?

A Look Out Circular is an executive instrument by which the departure of a named person is restrained at immigration points, issued at the instance of investigating agencies, courts and other authorities under instructions of the Ministry of Home Affairs. It is not issued under the Passports Act, forms no part of the Act's scheme of refusal, impounding, revocation and suspension, and is not appealable under Section 11. Its legal discipline comes from the constitutional cases: the courts require a basis in law, a connection with the purposes for which circulars are issued, and periodic review, and a person restrained by a circular challenges it in the writ jurisdiction rather than through the passport authorities.
