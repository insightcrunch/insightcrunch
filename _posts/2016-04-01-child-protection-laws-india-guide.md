Ask a room of Indian lawyers a simple question, who is a child, and the room will not give a single answer, because Indian law does not contain one. The child protection laws in India are not a code. They are an accumulation of statutes passed across nine decades, each written against a different harm, and each fixing the boundary of childhood where its own harm required the line to be drawn. A person of seventeen is a child for the statute on sexual offences and for the statute on juvenile justice, an adolescent for the statute on labour, outside the education right altogether, a minor for some purposes of the trafficking statute and an adult for others, and, if female and married, the subject of one of the sharpest conflicts the Supreme Court has had to resolve in this field. None of these answers is an error. Each is the correct application of a different Act to the same human being.

That is the claim this article exists to establish, and it can be stated in one sentence: there is no child in Indian law, only children as defined statute by statute, and nearly every practical confusion in this field, in police stations, hospitals, schools and courtrooms, begins when a definition carried in one Act is silently imported into another. The teacher who assumes the education statute governs a disclosure of abuse, the employer who assumes the labour statute's adolescent is an adult for all purposes, the magistrate's clerk who applies the marriage statute's age to a sexual offence, each has made the same mistake, which is to suppose that the word child names a fixed legal category. It does not. It names a conclusion that must be reached separately under every statute in the room, and the first professional skill in this field is asking which statute is speaking before asking what it says.

## The shape of the field

Child protection law in India is a field assembled from statutes that were never designed as a set. Its core is a group of Acts each addressed to a distinct injury. The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015 (Act 2 of 2016) governs two populations at once, children alleged to have broken the law and children found to be in need of care and protection, and it builds the institutional machinery, Child Welfare Committees, Juvenile Justice Boards, child care institutions, that much of the rest of the field leans on. The Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012 (Act 32 of 2012) is a special criminal statute addressed to sexual assault, sexual harassment and pornography involving children, with its own offences, its own procedure and its own reporting duty. The Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006 (Act 6 of 2007) is addressed to a social practice rather than a category of offender, and it renders a child marriage voidable, punishes those who solemnise, promote or permit it, and appoints prohibition officers to intervene before the event. The Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, the statute of 1986 as substantially recast by the Amendment Act of 2016 (Act 35 of 2016), polices the boundary between childhood and work. The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 (Act 35 of 2009) confers an enforceable entitlement to schooling. Around this core sits a second ring: the Commissions for Protection of Child Rights Act, 2005 (Act 4 of 2006), which creates the national and state commissions that monitor the whole field; the Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956 (Act 104 of 1956), addressed to trafficking and sexual exploitation for commercial purposes; the Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques (Prohibition of Sex Selection) Act, 1994 (Act 57 of 1994), addressed to sex selection before birth; and the older general and personal law statutes on majority, guardianship and adoption that decide who may act for a child and when a child may act alone.

### How many different ages of childhood does Indian law use?

Seven statutes do the definitional work on which the rest of the field depends. The juvenile justice statute and the sexual offences statute fix eighteen. The child marriage statute splits the line by sex. The labour statute works at fourteen and eighteen, the education statute at six to fourteen, the Majority Act at eighteen, and the trafficking statute at sixteen.

The juvenile justice statute defines a child as a person who has not completed eighteen years. The sexual offences statute uses the same line, a person below eighteen. The child marriage statute splits the answer by sex, a male below twenty-one and a female below eighteen. The labour statute defines a child as a person below fourteen and creates a second category, the adolescent, between fourteen and eighteen. The education statute confers its right on children of six to fourteen. The Indian Majority Act, 1875 (Act 9 of 1875) fixes majority at eighteen, with an exception at twenty-one. The trafficking statute grades its protection through the bands of child below sixteen and minor between sixteen and eighteen. Every other reference to childhood in Indian law borrows, expressly or by assumption, from one of these.

The connective point, and the one a single-statute reading never shows, is that these definitions were not coordinated when they were enacted and have never since been reconciled by any general provision. Parliament has legislated definition by definition, harm by harm. When the juvenile justice statute was replaced in 2015, its eighteen-year line was carried forward from the Act of 2000 without any attempt to touch the marriage statute's twenty-one or the labour statute's fourteen. When the labour statute was amended in 2016, the amendment deliberately linked its child definition to the education statute's upper limit of fourteen, creating one deliberate alignment inside a field that otherwise aligns by accident. The result is a body of law that is dense, active and in places genuinely protective, but which answers its most basic question five different ways.

## The constitutional ground on which the statutes stand

The statutes operate inside a constitutional text that speaks about children in several distinct registers, and the registers matter because they carry different legal force. Some provisions are enforceable rights. Some are directives that bind the conscience of the state without being enforceable in court. One is an enabling power that removes a constitutional obstacle to protective legislation. Reading the field without separating these registers produces the common error of treating every constitutional mention of children as a right a child can sue on, which the text does not support.

The enabling provision is clause (3) of Article 15. Article 15 prohibits discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex and place of birth, and clause (3) provides that nothing in the article prevents the state from making any special provision for women and children. That clause is the constitutional doorway through which most protective legislation for children walks: a statute that treats children differently from adults, or girls differently within a protective scheme, is not struck down for inequality because the Constitution expressly authorises the special provision. Article 23 prohibits traffic in human beings and forced labour, and it is enforceable against private persons as well as the state, which makes it one of the few fundamental rights that reaches the trafficker and the employer directly rather than through a statute alone. Article 24 is narrower and absolute within its field: no child below the age of fourteen years shall be employed to work in any factory or mine or engaged in any other hazardous employment. The fourteen in Article 24 is one source of the labour statute's fourteen, and its limitation to factories, mines and hazardous employment is the reason the wider prohibition on child work had to be built by statute rather than read out of the Constitution.

The only fundamental right addressed to children as a class in their own right is Article 21A, inserted by the Constitution (Eighty-sixth Amendment) Act, 2002, which obliges the state to provide free and compulsory education to all children of the age of six to fourteen years in such manner as the state may by law determine. The closing words matter: the right is real and enforceable, but its content is supplied by the statute Parliament enacted to give it effect, the education statute of 2009, whose scheme and limits are treated in the statute's own article on [the Right to Education Act 2009 guide](/2015/01/01/right-to-education-act-2009-guide/), at the point where the education right is conferred on the six to fourteen band and on no one outside it. Article 21A also explains the education statute's otherwise puzzling shape. The right ends at fourteen not because Parliament thought education unnecessary at fifteen, but because the constitutional text it implements fixes the band, and a statute conferring the Article 21A right cannot widen the band the article itself drew.

The directive principles carry the rest of the constitutional programme for children. Clause (e) of Article 39 requires the state to direct its policy towards securing that the health and strength of workers and the tender age of children are not abused and that citizens are not forced by economic necessity to enter avocations unsuited to their age or strength. Clause (f) requires that children are given opportunities and facilities to develop in a healthy manner and in conditions of freedom and dignity, and that childhood and youth are protected against exploitation and against moral and material abandonment. Article 45, as recast by the same Eighty-sixth Amendment that inserted Article 21A, directs the state to endeavour to provide early childhood care and education for all children below the age of six years, which is the constitutional home of the anganwadi and pre-school end of the field, deliberately placed in the non-enforceable part of the text. Article 51A, clause (k), completes the structure from the other side, making it a fundamental duty of a parent or guardian to provide opportunities for education to a child or ward between six and fourteen. The preamble of the juvenile justice statute of 2015 itself recites this constitutional ground, invoking Article 15(3), Articles 39(e) and 39(f), Article 45 and Article 47 as the provisions under which the state acts for children, which is a reminder that Parliament understands the field as the working out of these texts rather than as a freestanding invention.

Two consequences follow from this architecture. The first is that constitutional litigation about children usually proceeds by borrowing: because the child-specific enforceable rights are few, courts have repeatedly read the general right to life and personal liberty in Article 21 to carry content for children, including conditions inside institutions, protection from exploitation and the procedural dignity of a child in the justice system. The second is that the directive principles do real work in interpretation even though they cannot be sued on directly. When a court chooses between two readings of a child statute, the reading that serves Article 39(f) has a constitutional wind behind it, and the statutes are drafted, and defended when challenged, in that vocabulary.

## Seven statutes, five ages

The definitional divergence is the spine of this article, and it deserves to be taken statute by statute before it is taken as a whole, because each age was chosen for a reason internal to its statute and the reasons do not add up to a policy. The juvenile justice statute of 2015 defines a child, in Section 2(12), as a person who has not completed eighteen years of age, and defines a child in conflict with law by reference to the same line, measured on the date the offence was committed. The eighteen was inherited from the Act of 2000, which had itself unified a fractured position under the Act of 1986, under which a juvenile was a boy below sixteen or a girl below eighteen. The sexual offences statute of 2012 defines a child, in Section 2(1)(d), as any person below the age of eighteen years, and the choice was conscious: the statute was drafted against the Convention on the Rights of the Child, whose first article fixes childhood at eighteen, and against the background of an older penal code in which the age of consent had sat far lower and offences against children were fragmented across provisions written for adults. The child marriage statute of 2006 defines a child, in Section 2(a), as a person who, if a male, has not completed twenty-one years of age and, if a female, has not completed eighteen years of age. That split is not a protective judgment at all in its origin; it is the marriageable ages of the general law, twenty-one for a bridegroom and eighteen for a bride as fixed by the amendment of 1978 to the earlier restraint statute, carried into a prohibition Act that simply adopted the ages at which marriage was permitted and defined childhood as everything below them.

The labour statute works from the opposite end. As recast in 2016, the Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act defines a child, in Section 2(ii), as a person who has not completed fourteen years, or such age as the education statute specifies, whichever is more, and an adolescent as a person who has completed fourteen but not eighteen years. The architecture is a graded permission rather than a single line: a child below fourteen may not be employed in any occupation or process, subject to the contested exceptions for helping in a family enterprise and for work as an artist; an adolescent may work, but not in the hazardous occupations and processes listed in the statute's Schedule. The education statute confers its right on a child of six to fourteen, defined in Section 2(c) of the Act of 2009, and the fit with the labour statute is the one deliberate piece of coordination in the field: the years in which a child must be in school are the years in which a child may not be at work, and the 2016 labour amendment wrote that linkage into its own definition. The Indian Majority Act, 1875 stands behind all of these as the general law of legal capacity: every person domiciled in India attains majority on completing eighteen years, except that where a guardian of the person or property has been appointed by a court, or the property is under a Court of Wards, majority arrives only at twenty-one. Majority is not the same question as childhood in the protective statutes, but it decides when protective supervision ends and contractual and proprietary independence begins, and its twenty-one-year exception is a survival of the same older assumption that produced the marriage statute's male age.

The trafficking statute grades rather than divides. The Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956 defines a child as a person who has not completed sixteen years and a minor as a person who has completed sixteen but not eighteen years, and a major as a person who has completed eighteen, and those three bands drive its penalty structure and its rescue and institutional provisions, as the statute's own treatment in this series records. A person of seventeen taken into a brothel is therefore a minor rather than a child under the trafficking statute, fully a child under the sexual offences statute, and the difference is not pedantry, because the band determines which offence range applies and which protective institution receives the rescued person.

### What age does the trafficking law use for a child?

Under the Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956, a child is a person who has not completed the age of sixteen years. A minor is a person who has completed sixteen but not eighteen years, and a major is a person who has completed eighteen years. The sexual offences statute, by contrast, protects every person below eighteen.

The trafficking statute is the outlier in the table, and its line deserves a direct statement. Those bands were designed for a penalty ladder and a rescue system built in the middle of the twentieth century, and they survive unrevised alongside a sexual offences statute, enacted in 2012, that protects every person below eighteen without gradation. The practical consequence is that the same rescued seventeen-year-old is described by two statutes in two different words, and any report, charge sheet or committee order that does not say which statute it is speaking under invites error.

The survival of the sixteen-year line illustrates a general feature of the field: definitions are amended only when their own statute is reopened, and statutes are reopened one at a time. Proposals to rebuild the trafficking law, including the comprehensive anti-trafficking bills introduced in Parliament in 2018 and again in 2021, carried proposals to align the definition of a child at eighteen and to drop the minor and major distinctions, and neither bill was enacted, so the older bands remain the law. A reader who wants the general proposition should hold it in this form: in Indian law the age of a child is a property of the statute being applied, never of the person alone, and the trafficking statute is the standing proof.

### How is a person's age determined when documents conflict?

Age is proved in a fixed order under Section 94 of the juvenile justice statute. First comes the date of birth certificate from a school or the matriculation certificate, then a birth certificate issued by a corporation, municipal authority or panchayat, and only in the absence of both, an ossification test or other medical evidence of age.

Because so much turns on which side of a line a person falls, the field needs a law of age determination, and the juvenile justice statute supplies the one that is used across it. The Supreme Court had laid down a materially similar hierarchy in Jaya Mala v. Home Secretary, Government of Jammu and Kashmir (1982), while warning that medical estimation carries a margin of error of about two years on either side, and the benefit of that margin in practice goes to the person whose childhood is in question. Documentary proof first, medical evidence last, and doubt resolved in favour of childhood: that sequence is the field's working rule wherever a statute's age line decides liberty, liability or protection.

The determination question is not a technicality at the margin of the system. It decides whether an accused person faces a Juvenile Justice Board or a sessions court, whether a rescued person goes to a children's home or is treated as an adult offender, whether a marriage is annulled or subsists, and whether a worker is rescued from a factory or left in it. Age fraud, absent birth registration and school records that do not exist for the children most at risk are recurring features of the record before the committees, which is why the statute insists on an order of proof rather than leaving the question to impression.

## One person, five answers: the seventeen-year-old at the centre of the field

The cleanest way to see the whole structure is to run a single person through it. Take a girl of seventeen who has been married, who has left school, and who is found working in a garment unit. Under the sexual offences statute she is a child, and any sexual act with her is an offence against a child regardless of the marriage, because the statute asks only her age. Under the juvenile justice statute she is a child, and if she is found in need of care and protection she goes before a Child Welfare Committee, not a criminal court of adult jurisdiction. Under the labour statute she is an adolescent, and her work is lawful if the process is not hazardous and the conditions prescribed are met, unlawful if it is. Under the education statute she is nobody: the right to free and compulsory education was conferred on the band from six to fourteen, and at seventeen she stands outside it, so no school can be compelled to admit her under that Act and no parent can be charged with the fundamental duty in respect of her. Under the child marriage statute she is a child, her marriage is a child marriage, voidable at her option, and the adults who solemnised it have committed an offence. Under the majority statute she is a minor with a year to wait. Five statutes, five answers, and every one of them correct, which is precisely why a professional who carries one statute's answer into another statute's question does damage.

### Is a married seventeen-year-old still a child in the eyes of the law?

Yes. Marriage does not emancipate a minor under Indian law, and it confers no majority, no contractual capacity and no exemption from the protective statutes. A married girl of seventeen remains a child under the sexual offences statute and the juvenile justice statute, and her marriage is voidable at her option under the child marriage statute.

Yes, for almost every purpose that matters, and the marriage changes less than families and police officers commonly assume. A married girl of seventeen remains entitled to be produced before a Child Welfare Committee if she is in need of care and protection, and holds a marriage that the child marriage statute makes voidable at her option rather than valid in the ordinary sense. The Supreme Court's reading of the marital exception in the rape law, examined later in this article, proceeded on exactly this footing: the protective statutes speak with one voice about her age, and the exception that contradicted them was the anomaly.

The one answer that changes with marriage is practical rather than definitional. A married minor is frequently treated by the machinery as an adult householder, produced before a magistrate rather than a committee, restored to a marital home rather than a natal one, and recorded in registers that have no column for her position. The gap between the law's answer and the machinery's answer is a recurring theme in the committee and commission record, and it is one reason the definitional question cannot be dismissed as academic. The definitions are the instructions the machinery is supposed to follow, and where the definitions are scattered across seven statutes, the machinery follows whichever one it happens to know.

## How the framework was assembled: accumulation, not design

The sequence in which the field was built explains its shape, because no Parliament ever sat down to design it. The first stratum is the law of status inherited and adapted in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries: the Indian Majority Act of 1875 fixing capacity at eighteen, the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 (Act 8 of 1890) structuring court-supervised guardianship, and the personal law codes that followed, including the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956 (Act 32 of 1956) and the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956 (Act 78 of 1956), which between them govern who is a minor for guardianship and who may be given in adoption within the communities they cover. The Child Marriage Restraint Act, 1929 (Act 19 of 1929), the Sharda Act of the textbooks, belongs to this stratum too: it restrained rather than prohibited, it punished the adult bridegroom and those who performed the ceremony while leaving the marriage itself valid, and its ages, fourteen for a girl and eighteen for a boy at enactment, raised by amendment to fifteen and eighteen in 1949 and to eighteen and twenty-one in 1978, are the direct ancestors of the split definition in the Act of 2006.

The second stratum is the welfare and control legislation of the middle twentieth century, of which the trafficking statute of 1956 is the survivor in this field, enacted to give effect to the international convention on the suppression of traffic in persons signed at New York in 1950, and organised around rescue, protective homes and graded bands of age. The third stratum begins in 1986, a year that produced two of the field's foundations for unrelated reasons: the Juvenile Justice Act, 1986 (Act 53 of 1986), the first national juvenile statute, which unified the patchwork of state children Acts while keeping the sex-split definition of a juvenile, a boy below sixteen and a girl below eighteen; and the Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986 (Act 61 of 1986), which prohibited child work in listed occupations and processes and regulated it elsewhere, building on the constitutional floor of Article 24 and on committee work reaching back decades. The prenatal diagnostic statute of 1994 belongs to the turn that followed, addressed to a technology rather than a workplace or a courtroom: the misuse of diagnostic techniques for sex selection, policed through registration of clinics, prohibition of sex determination and advertisement controls, and connected to child protection by its object, the survival of the girl child before birth.

The fourth stratum is the rights stratum, and it is the one that gives the field its present centre of gravity. India acceded to the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child on 11 December 1992, with a declaration on the article concerning child labour recording that the relevant rights would be implemented progressively in step with national resources. The Convention's definition of a child as every human being below eighteen, and its architecture of survival, development, protection and participation rights, became the standard against which domestic law was measured, and the Juvenile Justice Act of 2000 (Act 56 of 2000) was the first full legislative response: it fixed eighteen uniformly for both sexes, separated the child in conflict with law from the child in need of care and protection as two jurisdictions, and built the committee and board structure the field still uses. The Commissions for Protection of Child Rights Act, 2005 followed, creating a national commission in March 2007 and state commissions charged with examining whether laws, policies and administrative machinery are in consonance with the Constitution and the Convention. The child marriage statute of 2006 replaced restraint with prohibition, making the marriage voidable at the instance of the child party and void in cases of trafficking, enticement and sale. The education statute of 2009 converted Article 21A into an enforceable scheme. The sexual offences statute of 2012 filled the field's most conspicuous gap, the absence of a dedicated sexual offence code for children, and did so at the Convention's age of eighteen. The juvenile justice statute of 2015 re-enacted the Act of 2000 with a harder edge on children of sixteen to eighteen accused of heinous offences, examined in the statute's own articles in this series rather than here. The labour amendment of 2016 completed the stratum by prohibiting the employment of children below fourteen in all occupations and processes, creating the adolescent category, and making the ratification of the two International Labour Organization conventions possible: India ratified the Minimum Age Convention, 1973 (No. 138) and the Worst Forms of Child Labour Convention, 1999 (No. 182) on 13 June 2017, a quarter of a century after acceding to the Convention on the Rights of the Child.

### When did India accept the Convention on the Rights of the Child?

India acceded to the Convention on the Rights of the Child on 11 December 1992. The accession carried a declaration on the Convention's article concerning economic exploitation, recording that requirements including minimum ages for employment would be implemented progressively, in step with national conditions and available resources, rather than immediately.

The accession came within three years of the Convention's adoption by the United Nations General Assembly. That declaration is the honest summary of India's posture across the whole field: the eighteen-year standard was accepted as the destination in 1992, and the statutes have been travelling towards it at different speeds ever since, the juvenile and sexual offences statutes arriving first and the labour and marriage statutes still carrying older lines.

Read as a sequence, the strata leave visible seams. Each statute carries the age its own decade thought right: sixteen in the welfare stratum's trafficking bands, fourteen in the constitutional and labour stratum, eighteen in the rights stratum, twenty-one surviving in the marriage statute from the status stratum's view of when a man may marry. The field is therefore best understood as an archaeological section rather than a building. Its inconsistencies are dated, and dating them is the beginning of judging which are defensible.

## Where the statutes collide, and how the courts have answered

A field built by accumulation will generate cases in which two statutes, each correctly applied, point in different directions, and the jurisprudence of this field is largely the record of those collisions. Three lines of decision matter at the level of the whole field. The first concerns which age governs when two statutes claim the same person. The courts' working answer is that the special and later protective statute governs its own subject, and that a person's status is therefore always relative to the proceeding: juvenility under the juvenile justice statute is determined by age on the date of the offence, a position the Supreme Court settled in Pratap Singh v. State of Jharkhand (2005), and no finding of majority or minority under a personal law or the Majority Act displaces the definition in the statute under which the proceeding is brought. The juvenile justice statute reinforces the point structurally by providing, in Section 1(4), that its provisions apply to all matters concerning children in need of care and protection and children in conflict with law notwithstanding anything contained in any other law, a non obstante clause that tells the reader, in terms, that its definition travels with its proceedings.

The second line concerns the collision between the criminal law's marital exception and the protective statutes' eighteen. Exception 2 to Section 375 of the Indian Penal Code excluded sexual intercourse by a man with his own wife from rape where the wife was not under fifteen, a line drawn in an era when the age of consent itself was lower, and left standing while the age of consent rose to eighteen in 2013 and while the sexual offences statute of 2012 fixed eighteen without any marital exception. In Independent Thought v. Union of India (2017) 10 SCC 800, the Supreme Court read the exception down so that it applies only where the wife is not under eighteen, harmonising the penal code with the child marriage statute, the juvenile justice statute, the sexual offences statute and the domestic violence statute, all of which treat a girl below eighteen as a child entitled to protection. The Court was explicit that it was not striking the exception down and not addressing marital rape of an adult wife; the decision is a definitional judgment, a refusal to let one statute's survival contradict the considered age line of the whole protective field, and it is the strongest judicial endorsement available of the proposition that eighteen is the field's gravitational centre even though it is not its universal rule.

The third line concerns institutions. In Exploitation of Children in Orphanages in the State of Tamil Nadu v. Union of India (2014) 2 SCC 193, the Supreme Court treated the commissions statute, the education statute and the sexual offences statute, read with the juvenile justice statute then in force, as a single implementation scheme, and directed attention to whether the machinery those Acts promised, commissions, committees, registered institutions, was actually in place. The pattern of that decision generalises: courts asked to protect children in institutions have repeatedly reached across the statute set, because the child in an institution is simultaneously a child in need of care and protection under one Act, a potential victim under a second, a rights-holder under a third and a subject of the commission's monitoring under a fourth. The older decision in M.C. Mehta v. State of Tamil Nadu (1996), on children in the match and fireworks industries of Sivakasi, did the same work for the labour end of the field, combining Article 24, the labour statute and directions on compensation, rehabilitation and inspection into a single operative order. A reader who wants the deep treatment of the sexual offences statute's own offences and procedure should go to [the POCSO Act 2012 explained](/2016/04/15/pocso-act-2012-explained/), which owns that statute's detail; the point for the map is that the courts do not respect the boundaries between these Acts when a child's case crosses them, even though the definitions oblige everyone else to do so.

## The machinery that the statutes share

For all their definitional divergence, the statutes converge on a shared machinery, and knowing the machinery is what turns the map into something usable. The pivotal institution is the Child Welfare Committee, constituted under the juvenile justice statute for every district: a bench of five, including a chairperson and at least one woman and one expert on matters concerning children, exercising the powers of a Metropolitan Magistrate or Judicial Magistrate of the first class, and holding final authority over the care, protection, treatment and rehabilitation of children in need of care and protection. Its sibling, the Juvenile Justice Board, three members including a judicial officer and two social workers, handles the child in conflict with law. The division between the two bodies is the field's master distinction, and confusing them is not a venial error, because a child produced before the wrong body has been placed in the wrong legal universe: the committee's universe is welfare and restoration, the board's is inquiry and disposition. The statute's own scheme, including the preliminary assessment by which a child of sixteen to eighteen accused of a heinous offence may be transferred for trial as an adult, is treated in full in [the Juvenile Justice Act 2015 explained](/2016/05/15/juvenile-justice-act-2015-explained/), and is deliberately not re-argued here.

Around the committees and boards sits the administrative skeleton: District Child Protection Units, Special Juvenile Police Units in every district, child care institutions that must be registered under the juvenile justice statute on pain of penalty, and the Childline service on the number 1098, the emergency outreach service for children in distress whose existence the juvenile statute itself recognises. Above the district machinery stand the commissions created by the Act of 2005: the National Commission for Protection of Child Rights and the State Commissions, bodies with the powers of a civil court for inquiry, charged with examining safeguards under any law for the protection of child rights, reviewing the factors that inhibit children affected by violence, trafficking, abuse and exploitation, inspecting institutions, and monitoring the implementation of the sexual offences statute, a duty Section 44 of that statute assigns to them expressly. The enforcement architecture those commissions operate is examined in [the NCPCR and the child rights commissions](/2016/08/01/ncpcr-child-rights-commission-india/), which owns their powers and their limits. The child marriage statute adds its own officer to the district picture, the Child Marriage Prohibition Officer, whose preventive and reporting functions belong to [the Prohibition of Child Marriage Act 2006](/2016/06/15/prohibition-child-marriage-act-2006/) and its treatment in this series.

The machinery has a documentary life that outsiders underestimate. Committee orders, inspection reports, case files, school records and medical reports circulate among the committee, the police unit, the institution and the commission in ordinary office formats, and the officer who must read a district return or a school record has no use for a summary when the figures matter. A reader working through such material in spreadsheet, document or presentation form can [open and read Excel, Word and PowerPoint files in the browser with the ReportMedic office file viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) rather than relying on whatever software a borrowed machine happens to carry, which is a mundane point with real consequences for the portability of case papers between the offices this field runs on.

## The duties the field lays on adults

The definitional map has a practical edge, because the duties the statutes impose on adults are triggered by the definitions. The heaviest of these is the reporting duty in the sexual offences statute. Section 19 of the Act of 2012 obliges any person, expressly including a person in charge of an institution, who has apprehension that an offence under the statute is likely to be committed, or knowledge that such an offence has been committed, to report it to the Special Juvenile Police Unit or the local police, and Section 21 punishes a failure to report, with a heavier penalty for a person in charge of a company or institution who fails to report an offence by a subordinate under his control. The duty is deliberately indifferent to professional confidence: it reaches the doctor who treats the child, the teacher who is told, the hospital administrator, the hostel warden and the parent who learns from another child, and it is not answered by reporting up an internal chain instead of to the police. This is the provision that converts the definition of a child into an obligation for adults who never think of themselves as part of the child protection system, and the boundary that matters to them is the statute's own: the duty is engaged by a person below eighteen, whatever any other statute says about that person's status.

Schools sit at the crossing point of several statutes at once, and their position shows the map at its most concrete. A school must respect the education statute's scheme for the children it admits between six and fourteen, including the prohibition on physical punishment and mental harassment in Section 17 of that Act. A school is a workplace under the labour statute, which governs any young person it employs in any capacity. A school is an institution whose head carries the reporting duty described above, and whose staff are among the aggravated categories in the sexual offences statute when an offence is committed by a person in a position of trust. And a school is routinely the first holder of the documents on which age is determined elsewhere, the admission register and the birth certificate from school records that stand at the head of the Section 94 hierarchy, which gives the school's record-keeping a legal significance far beyond administration. The general position described in this article is legal information about a scattered field, not advice on any individual case, and anyone facing a live question about a particular child should take advice on that case, because the applicable definition, the forum and the reporting clock all turn on facts that a general account cannot carry.

## One age for every statute? The reform argument tested

The obvious reform proposal follows directly from the map: fix eighteen everywhere, repeal the outliers, and let the word child mean one thing. The proposal deserves to be tested at full strength on both sides, because it is half right, and identifying which half is the useful work.

The case for uniformity is coherence, and it is made by serious holders. The Committee on the Rights of the Child, reviewing India's reports, has repeatedly pressed for harmonisation of the definition of the child across domestic legislation. Enakshi Ganguly and Bharti Ali of the HAQ Centre for Child Rights in New Delhi have argued across two decades of documentation that the patchwork of ages is not a design but an accretion, and that it produces concrete denials of protection, of which the marital exception read down in Independent Thought was only the most visible: a girl counted as a child for protection but as a wife for consent, a boy counted as a child for offences against him but as a worker for his exploitation. On this view the divergences that survive are historical residue, and the burden of proof should lie on anyone who would keep a line below eighteen in a protective statute.

The case against collapsing every age into one is that the statutes are not all doing the same thing, and it too has serious holders. A protective statute and a permissive statute use age differently: the sexual offences statute uses eighteen to mark the boundary of a wrong, while the labour statute uses its ages to mark the boundary of a permission, what work a person may lawfully do, and a single line cannot do both jobs without damage in one direction or the other. Set the labour line at eighteen and all work below eighteen becomes unlawful, including the light and family work that poor households depend on and that the International Labour Organization's own minimum age convention permits states to grade; set it at fourteen and the permission has been bought by surrendering protection. When Parliament amended the labour statute in 2016, the then Minister of State for Labour and Employment, Bandaru Dattatreya, defended the graded structure and the family enterprise exception in exactly these terms, as a recognition of the socio-economic reality of family work; Kailash Satyarthi, the Nobel laureate and founder of the Bachpan Bachao Andolan, answered that the exception would perpetuate caste-based occupations and swallow the prohibition for the children most at risk. That exchange is the uniform-age debate in miniature: the question is never whether eighteen is the right age for childhood, but whether a permission-grading statute and a protection statute can share one number without one of them ceasing to do its work.

The marriage age is where the uniformity argument is hardest to answer, because the divergence there is not between a permission and a protection but between the sexes. A statute that fixes twenty-one for a male and eighteen for a female is not grading a harm; it is recording an assumption, inherited from the restraint statute of 1929 as amended in 1978, that a husband should be older than a wife and that male maturity for marriage arrives later. The Task Force on the age of marriage for women, chaired by Jaya Jaitly and reporting in 2020, recommended raising the female age to twenty-one on grounds of health, nutrition and equality of the marriage line itself; Mary John of the Centre for Women's Development Studies, among others, opposed the increase, arguing that it would criminalise the marriages of the poor without touching the poverty, health access and schooling failures that produce early marriage, and that a higher line unenforced against the families it governs becomes one more offence to charge the vulnerable with. The amendment bill that followed that debate is owned by the child marriage article in this series and is not pursued here. For the map, the conclusion is narrower: of all the divergent ages in the table, the male marriage age is the one that no protective rationale explains, and it survives because the marriage statute adopts the general marriageable ages rather than setting a protective line of its own.

The verdict the evidence supports is therefore a discriminating one. Uniformity at eighteen is right for the protective statutes, and the field has largely arrived there: the juvenile, sexual offences and commissions statutes already agree, the trafficking statute's sixteen is an unreformed survival rather than a position anyone defends on principle, and Independent Thought shows the courts pulling a penal survival into line when it contradicts the protective consensus. Divergence is defensible only where a statute is grading a permission rather than marking a wrong, which is the labour statute's ground, and even there the grading is honestly debatable, as the family enterprise argument shows. The reform worth wanting is not one age for every statute. It is one age for every protective purpose, graded ages only where a permission is genuinely being graded, and the deletion of divergences, like the marriage statute's, that serve no purpose anyone will state in public.

## How to navigate the field

A reader facing a live situation does not need the whole map at once; the reader needs the right statute first, and the map's practical yield is a short discipline. Identify the harm before the person: sexual harm goes to the sexual offences statute and its reporting duty, work to the labour statute and its two categories, marriage to the prohibition statute and its officers, schooling to the education statute within its band, and a child without care, or accused of an offence, to the juvenile justice statute and its committee or board. Then read the age in that statute and no other. Then ask the second question, which the worked example teaches: whether the same person is simultaneously inside another statute for a different purpose, because the seventeen-year-old worker who is also a wife and also a victim is not three cases but one, and the machinery handles her best when each statute is applied to its own question and none is allowed to answer for the others. The labour definition itself, including the adolescent category and the hazardous list, is carried by [the child labour amendment explained](/2016/07/01/child-labour-act-2016-amendment/) at the point where the definition is applied to work, and the reader who needs that ground should take it from there rather than from this summary.

For the aspirant, the field is examined as a set of fixed points, and the fixed points are exactly the ones this article has isolated: the constitutional provisions, Articles 15(3), 21A, 23, 24, 39(e), 39(f), 45 and 51A(k); the definitional table, with the marriage statute's split and the trafficking statute's sixteen as the traps; the ratification dates, 1992 for the Convention and 2017 for the two labour conventions; and Independent Thought as the judgment that ties the field together. A candidate who can reproduce the table and say why each row sits where it does understands the field better than one who has memorised any single Act in depth, because the examiners' recurring move in this area is to test the seams between statutes rather than the centre of any one of them.

The series thesis has been that a field cannot be understood statute by statute. In most fields the connective problem is institutional, the same bodies recurring under different names. Here it is definitional, and it is invisible from inside any single Act, because every Act is internally consistent and the inconsistency exists only between them. That is why the missing definition matters more than any gap in coverage: India does not lack statutes protecting children, it lacks a shared answer to the question of who a child is, and until the protective statutes finish converging on the answer the Convention gave in 1989 and India accepted in 1992, the first task in every real case will remain the one this article has rehearsed. Ask which statute is speaking. Then ask what it says.

## The two central statutes at headline level

The juvenile justice statute is the field's load-bearing wall, and its architecture explains why so many other statutes lean on it. The Act of 2015, which received assent on 31 December 2015 and came into force on 15 January 2016, repealed and replaced the Act of 2000 (Act 56 of 2000), and it works through a deliberate duality. One jurisdiction concerns the child in conflict with law, defined as a child alleged or found to have committed an offence and not completed eighteen on the date of the offence, and routed to a Juvenile Justice Board. The other concerns the child in need of care and protection, a category Section 2(14) builds from situations rather than offences: a child found without a home or settled place of abode, a child found begging or living on the street, a child residing with a person who has injured, exploited or abused the child or who is unfit to exercise control, a child who is mentally ill or addicted and without support, a child abused, tortured or exploited for sexual abuse or illegal acts, a child vulnerable to induction into drug abuse or trafficking, a child who is a victim of an armed conflict, civil unrest or natural calamity, and a child at imminent risk of marriage whose parents or guardians are likely to solemnise it. The breadth of that list is the point: the care jurisdiction is the field's intake valve, and a child who enters the system through any other statute, rescued from work, recovered from trafficking, stopped on the point of marriage, is very often handed to the committee the juvenile statute creates, because it is the only body with general authority over a child's custody, care and restoration.

The sexual offences statute is built on the opposite principle, narrow subject, complete code. Before 2012, sexual offences against children were prosecuted through the general penal code, whose provisions on rape, outraging modesty and unnatural offences were written for adult victims, carried no child-specific procedure, and left male children largely outside the law of rape altogether. The Act of 2012, which received assent on 19 June 2012 and came into force on 14 November 2012, created graded offences of penetrative sexual assault, aggravated penetrative sexual assault, sexual assault, aggravated sexual assault, sexual harassment and the use of a child for pornographic purposes; it made the offences gender neutral as to the child; it built a child-specific procedure for recording statements, medical examination, evidence and trial in special courts; and it wrapped the process in the reporting duty and identity protections described earlier in this article. Its one definitional commitment, a child is a person below eighteen, is therefore the load-bearing fact of the whole statute, and the amendment of 2019 that raised its punishments, owned by the amendment article in this series, changed penalties without touching the definition, which is why the definition can be stated once here and relied on across the cluster.

## The personal law layer: guardianship, custody and adoption

Beneath the protective statutes sits an older layer that decides not what may be done to a child but who may act for one, and its ages do not match the protective layer either. The Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956 defines a minor as a person who has not completed eighteen years, and arranges natural guardianship in a sequence that begins with the father and after him the mother, with the welfare of the minor as the governing consideration the courts have steadily enlarged. The Guardians and Wards Act, 1890, the older and religion-neutral statute, works with the Majority Act's framework and empowers district courts to appoint guardians of the person and property of a minor, and where such a guardian is appointed, the ward's majority for property purposes is deferred to twenty-one, which is the survival already noticed in the table. Muslim personal law fixes majority for most purposes by the same general statute, while classical rules on guardianship of property and on the mother's custody, hizanat, in the early years operate alongside it. The result is that a person may be a major for the protective statutes at eighteen, a minor for a court-supervised property arrangement until twenty-one, and subject to a custody rule that turns on the child's own age in years that appear in no central statute at all.

Adoption shows the two layers interacting most sharply. Within the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956, adoption is a personal law institution with its own conditions, including that the child given must ordinarily be below fifteen unless a custom permits otherwise, an age that appears nowhere else in the field. The juvenile justice statute built a second, secular route beside it: adoption of orphaned, abandoned and surrendered children through the Central Adoption Resource Authority and the child welfare machinery, open regardless of religion, a route the Supreme Court had anticipated in Lakshmi Kant Pandey v. Union of India (1984), where guidelines for inter-country adoption were laid down precisely because no general adoption statute existed. The adoption framework and its authority are owned by the adoption article later in this cluster and are mapped here only to make the structural point: even the creation of a parent-child relationship runs on two tracks with two different age logics, one drawn from personal law and one from the welfare statute, and a practitioner must know which track a given child is on before any age question in it can be answered.

## The statute at the edge of the field: sex selection before birth

The prenatal diagnostic statute tests the field's boundary, because the person it protects has not been born. The Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques (Prohibition of Sex Selection) Act, 1994, originally the Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques (Regulation and Prevention of Misuse) Act, was enacted against the misuse of ultrasound and allied techniques to determine the sex of a foetus and the selective abortion of female foetuses that followed, and it was renamed and widened in 2003 to reach pre-conception techniques as well. Its machinery is regulatory rather than penal in design: registration of genetic counselling centres, laboratories and clinics, a prohibition on determining or communicating the sex of a foetus, a prohibition on advertisement of sex selection, written consent and record-keeping requirements, and appropriate authorities with powers of search and seizure. Its place in a child protection map is sometimes questioned, and the answer is that the field's subject is not the born child as such but the child as the law values her, and a statute whose object is that girls should survive to be born is the field's outermost ring. The statute also demonstrates the map's lesson in reverse: it contains no definition of a child at all, because its subject precedes every definition, and it protects by regulating the adults and institutions around a person who has, as yet, no legal age of any kind.

## Overlaps and gaps: what happens between the statutes

A map is most useful where it shows the spaces between its features, and the child protection field has four gaps that recur in the case law, the commission record and the examination papers alike. The first is the education gap between fourteen and eighteen. The constitutional right and the education statute stop at fourteen; the labour statute permits regulated non-hazardous work from fourteen; the protective statutes continue to eighteen. A person of fifteen is therefore a child whom the state must protect, whom an employer may lawfully engage in permitted work, and whom no authority is obliged to educate. Successive policy documents have proposed extending the education right upwards, and the National Education Policy of 2020 frames education as a continuum from early childhood through secondary school, but the enforceable right in Article 21A and the Act of 2009 remains where the Eighty-sixth Amendment put it, and closing the gap would require constitutional or legislative change rather than interpretation, because a court cannot widen a band the constitutional text itself has drawn.

The second gap is the overlap, rather than the space, between the two central statutes where the accused is also a child. A sexual offence committed by a person of sixteen against a person of fifteen engages the sexual offences statute as the definition of the wrong and the juvenile justice statute as the law of the accused, and the case proceeds before a Juvenile Justice Board under the protective definition both statutes share. The shared eighteen is what makes the overlap workable; had the two statutes drawn their lines differently, the same act would have fallen between two jurisdictions, which is the practical argument for definitional convergence among protective statutes stated in its strongest form. The strain shows instead at the consent boundary inside the sexual offences statute: because the statute fixes eighteen without exception, consensual adolescent relationships are prosecuted as aggravated offences, a pattern the Madras High Court remarked on in cases including Vijayalakshmi v. State (2021), where the court observed that adolescents in relationships were being swept into a statute designed for predatory abuse, and which the Law Commission of India addressed in its Report No. 283 of 2023 on the age of consent under the statute, recommending that the age of eighteen be retained while courts be given guided discretion in sentencing in cases involving children of sixteen to eighteen where the relationship carried tacit approval rather than exploitation. The Commission's position is itself a map judgment: the definition stays uniform, and the flexibility is placed in sentencing rather than in the age line, which is the opposite choice from the labour statute's graded permission.

The third gap is federal. Child protection is administered by states and districts inside central statutes, and the statutes assume machinery, committees in every district, boards, units, registered institutions, that the record shows to be unevenly built. The Supreme Court's decision in Sampurna Behura v. Union of India (2018), on the implementation of the juvenile justice statute, ran through the machinery item by item, vacancies in the commissions and committees, the constitution of boards, the registration of institutions, the state of the juvenile justice fund, and issued directions because the statutory scheme existed on paper in districts where its bodies had never been properly staffed. The commissions created by the Act of 2005 have themselves reported the same pattern in their reviews: the law's promises are uniform across India, while the institutions meant to keep them vary with the administrative energy of the state, so that the practical content of a child's protection depends on geography in a way the definitions never mention.

The fourth gap is the one the prenatal statute and the majority statute mark at the two ends of childhood. At one end, the person not yet born is protected only obliquely, through the regulation of diagnostic practice and the abortion law's own conditions, and acquires the field's full protection at birth. At the other end, protection stops at eighteen with a completeness that the lived reality of care leavers contradicts, and the juvenile justice statute acknowledges the contradiction in a small way through its aftercare provision, supporting persons who have completed eighteen but not twenty-one and who leave institutional care to join the mainstream of society. Aftercare is the field quietly admitting that its own central line is an administrative necessity rather than a description of independence, and the admission is worth noticing because it is the one place the statutes themselves record the cost of drawing any line at all.

## The record of the commissions: the field watched as a whole

One institution in the field is charged with seeing it whole, and its record is the closest thing the field has to a self-portrait. The National Commission for Protection of Child Rights, set up in March 2007 under the Act of 2005, is required to ensure that laws, policies, programmes and administrative mechanisms conform to the child rights perspective in the Constitution and the Convention, and for that purpose it reviews safeguards under any law, inquires into violations, examines the factors that inhibit the enjoyment of rights by children affected by violence, trafficking, maltreatment and exploitation, inspects institutions where children are detained or reside, and reports to government on the working of the safeguards with recommendations. Its monitoring of the sexual offences statute under Section 44 of that Act and of the education statute gives it a statutory seat inside three of the core statutes at once, which no other body holds. The State Commissions replicate the function at the level where the machinery actually runs.

The commissions' significance for the definitional question is that their jurisdiction is defined by child rights rather than by any single statute's age. A complaint to a commission about a person of seventeen in a workplace, or a person of fifteen out of school, does not fail because the education statute or the labour statute has placed that person outside its own band; the commission takes the field as its territory and the Convention's eighteen as its reference point, and its inquiries habitually proceed by asking which of the statutes in the room has failed to do its part. That is why the commissions belong in a map of definitions and not only in an account of institutions: they are the one actor in the system whose working definition of a child is the field's destination rather than any statute's compromise, and the distance between their reference point and the operative definitions is a working measure of how far the convergence described in this article still has to travel.

## The moving line: a short history of the age of consent

One age in the field has moved so often that its history is the clearest proof that these lines are choices rather than facts of nature. When the Indian Penal Code was enacted in 1860, the age below which intercourse was rape stood at ten, and the marital exception protected a husband whose wife was above that age. The death of Phulmoni Dasi, a girl of about eleven, from injuries inflicted by her adult husband, who could not be convicted of rape because his wife was above the excepted age, produced the Age of Consent Act, 1891, which raised the protected age to twelve. The line rose in stages across the following half century, to fourteen in 1925 and to sixteen by the amendment of 1940, with the marital line held one year lower at fifteen, and sixteen is where the Penal Code's threshold stood when the Republic began and where it remained for over sixty years. The sexual offences statute of 2012 then fixed eighteen for its own offences, the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2013 raised the Penal Code's age of consent to eighteen in the following year, over the recommendation of the Justice Verma Committee, which had favoured retaining sixteen, and the marital exception's fifteen survived untouched until Independent Thought read it up to eighteen in 2017. In a century and a half the line moved from ten to eighteen by five distinct legislative or judicial steps, each defended in its day as the natural boundary of childhood. The lesson for the map is not that the present line is wrong; it is that every line in the table has a biography, and the biographies, not any theory of childhood, explain why the lines differ.

## Article 21 and the children the specific provisions do not reach

Because the child-specific enforceable rights are confined to Articles 21A, 23 and 24, the courts built much of the field's working law out of the general guarantee of life and personal liberty, and the decisions form a recognisable line. In Sheela Barse v. Union of India (1986), the Supreme Court took up the condition of children in jails and protective homes and treated the institutional treatment of children as an Article 21 question, pressing states to separate children from adult prisoners and to give the juvenile statutes real institutions. In Vishal Jeet v. Union of India (1990), on child prostitution, the Court directed preventive and rehabilitative measures and treated the sexual exploitation of children as a violation of Articles 21 and 23 together, drawing the trafficking statute and the Constitution into one frame. In Gaurav Jain v. Union of India (1997), concerning the children of women in prostitution, the Court addressed segregation, admission to schools and hostels, and the right of those children to equality of opportunity and dignity, refusing to let the circumstances of a child's birth determine the child's entitlements. In Bachpan Bachao Andolan v. Union of India (2011), arising from children trafficked into circus work, the Court directed the enforcement of the labour and juvenile statutes against the trafficking of children for labour and laid the obligation on the state to identify, rescue and rehabilitate, reading the statutory scheme through Articles 21, 23 and 24. The pattern across these decisions is constant: where a statute's definition or machinery runs out, Article 21 is asked to carry the child the rest of the way, and the Court's willingness to do so is the reason the field functions as a field at all rather than as seven disconnected codes. It is also a reason for caution in reading the map: the definitions decide jurisdiction, forum and duty, but the rights a court will enforce for a child are not exhausted by the statute under which the child arrives.

## The one deliberate alignment: school at fourteen, work from fourteen

Inside a field assembled by accident, the relationship between the education statute and the labour statute stands out because it was designed. The Constitution (Eighty-sixth Amendment) Act, 2002 simultaneously inserted Article 21A for the six to fourteen band, recast Article 45 towards early childhood care below six, and added the parental duty in Article 51A(k) for the same band, so the constitutional scheme itself imagines childhood in two compartments: care and pre-schooling below six, guaranteed schooling from six to fourteen. When the labour statute was amended in 2016, Parliament wrote the school band into the work statute: the definition of a child in the labour Act is a person below fourteen or such age as the education statute specifies for the right to education, whichever is more, so if the education right is ever extended upwards, the prohibition on employment rises with it automatically. That drafting device, a definition by reference to another statute, is the only place in the field where one statute's age is made to follow another's, and its presence proves the negative of the general position: Parliament aligns definitions when it turns its mind to the alignment, and the rest of the table differs because no one turned their mind to it.

The 2016 amendment also shows how a permission-grading statute argues about itself. Its restructuring replaced a listed set of prohibited occupations and processes for children with a general prohibition below fourteen, created the adolescent category with a prohibition confined to hazardous work, recast the Schedule of hazardous occupations and processes under three heads, made offences cognizable and compoundable only in limited circumstances, raised the penalties on employers substantially, and placed rehabilitation in a statutory frame through the child and adolescent labour rehabilitation fund. The Standing Committee examining the bill and parliamentarians in debate pressed the two points that have followed the amendment since: the exception permitting a child to help in a family or family enterprise after school hours or during vacations, and the narrowing of the hazardous list. The government's defence, that family work is a social and economic reality the law should regulate rather than pretend away, and the critics' answer, that the exception is drawn around precisely the home-based and caste-ordered work in which child labour hides, were rehearsed earlier in this article in the uniformity debate; the amendment article in this series carries the full record. For the map, what matters is the shape: the labour statute is the field's one openly graded instrument, and its grading, fourteen for prohibition and eighteen for hazard, is the considered position of a Parliament that in the very same decade fixed eighteen without gradation for sexual protection. The difference between the two statutes is therefore not confusion. It is two judgments about two different things, and the definitional table is the record of both.

## The marriage statute inside the field

The child marriage statute repays a closer headline reading than its size suggests, because its design choices explain its definition. The Act of 2006, in force from 1 November 2007, replaced the restraint model of 1929 with prohibition, but it stopped short of the step its title implies: a child marriage is not void. Under Section 3 it is voidable at the option of the contracting party who was a child, on a petition filed before that party completes two years after attaining majority, and under Section 12 it is void only in the aggravated cases, where the minor was taken or enticed out of a lawful guardian's keeping, compelled by force or induced by deceit, or sold for the purpose of marriage and afterwards trafficked or used for immoral purposes. The voidable design was a deliberate protection of the girl within the marriage: a marriage that is void from the start can strip her of maintenance, residence and the legitimacy of her children, while a marriage voidable at her option lets her exit with the statute's provisions for maintenance, custody and the legitimacy of children born of the marriage intact, and Section 6 declares such children legitimate for all purposes notwithstanding annulment. The state amendment by Karnataka, which rewrote Section 3 in its application to that state so that a child marriage is void, shows the alternative design operating inside the same Act, and the argument between the two designs, exit at the child's option with protections, against nullity as a deterrent signal, is a live one in the marriage article's territory.

The punishment scheme completes the picture and explains the definition's odd shape. The Act punishes the adult male who contracts a child marriage, those who perform, conduct, direct or abet it, and parents or guardians who promote it, permit it or negligently fail to prevent it, with rigorous imprisonment extending to two years and fine extending to one lakh rupees, while providing that no woman shall be punishable with imprisonment under the parental head. The offence provisions are aimed at everyone except the child, which is why the definition of a child in this statute had to track the marriageable ages rather than any protective theory: the statute needs to know whose marriage is prohibited, and it answers with the ages the general law already used for capacity to marry, twenty-one and eighteen. Seen from inside its own design, the split definition is coherent. Seen from the field, it remains the table's hardest row to defend, because its coherence is borrowed from a capacity rule that has nothing to do with the harm the statute exists to prevent.

## Why eighteen won: the gravitational pull of a single number

The convergence of the protective statutes on eighteen was not inevitable, and explaining it is part of understanding the divergences that remain. Eighteen arrived with several independent reinforcements. It is the age of majority in the general law since 1875, so it was the standing legal boundary of childhood long before any protective statute adopted it. It became the age of the franchise when the Constitution (Sixty-first Amendment) Act, 1988 lowered the voting age from twenty-one to eighteen, fixing eighteen as the Republic's age of civic adulthood. And it is the age in the first article of the Convention on the Rights of the Child, which defines a child as every human being below eighteen unless majority is attained earlier under the applicable law, a definition India accepted in 1992 without seeking to use the exception. A number that is simultaneously the age of legal capacity, the age of the vote and the age in the governing international instrument acquires a momentum that no rival line can match, and each new protective statute has adopted it for that reason rather than from any fresh inquiry into maturity. The Juvenile Justice Act of 2000 used it to end the sex-split line of 1986. The commissions statute of 2005 built its mandate on child rights as the Convention frames them. The sexual offences statute of 2012 took it as given.

The counterfactual is worth stating, because it shows what the surviving divergences are. If eighteen is the destination, the lines below it need specific defences, and they divide exactly as this article's verdict divided them. Fourteen in the labour and education statutes is defended as a grading of permission and as the limit of the schooling the state has undertaken to guarantee, and reasonable holders dispute both defences. Sixteen in the trafficking statute is defended by nobody and survives because the statute has not been successfully reopened. Twenty-one for a male in the marriage statute is defended as a marriage policy rather than a child policy, which is a confession that the row does not belong to the protective table at all. The convergence story also has a limit that its advocates rarely state: eighteen is a legal convention, not a developmental finding, and the statutes that converge on it converge on a compromise between the claims of protection and the claims of autonomy that every legal system strikes somewhere in late adolescence. India's argument has never really been about where the line sits. It has been about whether the line, once chosen for protection, will be honoured across the statutes that protection uses.

## The words themselves: from delinquent to child in conflict with law

The vocabulary of the field carries its history as plainly as its ages do. The earliest statutes spoke of the delinquent and the neglected child, the language of the reformatory. The Act of 1986 spoke of the juvenile and the neglected juvenile. The Act of 2000 renamed the offender-side category the juvenile in conflict with law, a phrase borrowed from the international instruments, and the Act of 2015 completed the movement by making the subject a child throughout: a child in conflict with law, a child in need of care and protection. Each renaming was argued for on the same ground, that the label attached to a child by the first document in the file shapes the treatment the system gives, and each was resisted in debate as cosmetic. The renamings are not the subject of this article, but they belong on the map for one reason: they show Parliament repeatedly legislating on the premise that the categories are constructed, chosen and revisable, which is the same premise the definitional table teaches. A system that has renamed its central category three times in thirty years cannot plausibly claim that its ages are natural facts immune from revision, and the history of the words is the standing answer to any defence of a divergent age that rests on nothing more than its age.

## The health system inside the field

Doctors and hospitals meet the field at three points, and each is governed by a different statute's definition. The first is the reporting duty already described, which falls on medical practitioners with the same force as on any other person and overrides the instincts of professional confidentiality. The second is the medical examination of a child victim under Section 27 of the sexual offences statute, which must be conducted by a registered medical practitioner in a government hospital or a hospital run by a local authority, and in the case of a girl child by a woman doctor, with the consent of the parent or guardian or other person competent to consent, and in the presence of a parent or a person in whom the child has trust. The third is the general duty inserted into the criminal procedure code in 2013, Section 357C, under which all hospitals, public or private, must immediately provide first aid or medical treatment free of cost to victims of the specified sexual offences and inform the police of the incident. A hospital that asks which definition applies before treating has misunderstood the structure: the treatment duty is unconditional, the examination follows the sexual offences statute's eighteen, and the report follows the same line. The medical profession's own guidance on the examination of survivors, issued through the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare's medico-legal protocols, works inside the same framework, and the abolition of the two-finger test in the jurisprudence, condemned in Lillu v. State of Haryana (2013) as violating the dignity and privacy of the survivor, belongs to the same settlement: the health system's role is care and evidence in that order, under definitions it does not choose.

## Trafficking across three statutes

Trafficking is the field's hardest subject to map because three bodies of law claim it at once, and a rescued child passes through all three. The trafficking statute of 1956 is the oldest claimant, organised around prostitution and commercial sexual exploitation, with its graded bands of child, minor and major and its machinery of special police officers, rescue, protective homes and corrective institutions. The general penal law is the second claimant: the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2013 substituted Section 370 of the Penal Code with a comprehensive trafficking offence covering recruitment, transportation, harbouring, transfer or receipt of persons by threat, force, coercion, abduction, fraud, deception, abuse of power or inducement, for the purpose of exploitation, with exploitation defined to include prostitution and other sexual exploitation, forced labour and slavery-like practices, and with the consent of the victim rendered immaterial; the corresponding provision in the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 sits in Section 143. The juvenile justice statute is the third claimant, and its chapter of offences against children includes cruelty to a child, employing a child for begging, administering intoxicants, exploiting a child employee, and, in Section 81, the sale and procurement of children for any purpose, an offence that operates independently of the other two statutes and is triable within the child protection system itself. The practical consequence is that a single rescue can generate a prosecution under the penal code, proceedings under the trafficking statute for the premises and the keepers, and a care and protection case before the committee for the child, each under its own definition and its own forum. The failure of the comprehensive anti-trafficking bills of 2018 and 2021, which would have gathered this subject into one statute with one definition, is the reason the threefold structure persists, and it is the clearest available demonstration of the cost the field pays for legislating harm by harm: the trafficker is one person, the child is one person, and the law that meets them arrives in three volumes.

## One field, many administrations: the state layer

The central statutes are only half the field, because almost everything they promise is delivered, or not delivered, by states and districts. The subjects are distributed across the Seventh Schedule in fragments: education sits in the Concurrent List after the Forty-second Amendment, public order and police in the State List, welfare of labour and social security in the Concurrent List, and children as such appear in no entry, which is why the central statutes rest on combinations of entries and why administration is unavoidably shared. States legislate at the edges in ways that matter. Goa's children's legislation of 2003, the Goa Children's Act (Act 18 of 2003), was for years the country's only comprehensive state children's statute, defining a child at eighteen and creating children's courts before the national sexual offences statute existed, and it is regularly cited as the proof that the gap POCSO filled in 2012 was a gap of the Centre's making and a state's initiative. Karnataka's amendment of the child marriage statute, making child marriages void in that state, has already been noticed. State commissions for protection of child rights exist on paper in every state under the Act of 2005, with a record of constitution, staffing and activity that the national commission's own reviews describe in uneven terms.

The state layer also holds the field's data, and the data discipline of this article should be stated plainly, because the field attracts figures the way a magnet attracts filings. Registered crime against children is counted by the National Crime Records Bureau in its annual Crime in India reports, and child labour was counted by the Census of 2011 in categories that do not match the labour statute's definitions, so that every headline number in this field is the product of a definition, and quoting it without its definition repeats the article's central error in statistical form. The durable statements are structural: registered offences against children have risen across the period in which the reporting duty and the special statute have operated, a rise the official reading attributes substantially to reporting itself; census counts of working children fell between 2001 and 2011 while remaining in the millions; and no official series measures the quantity the map is about, the number of children whom the statutes' divergent definitions place in the wrong column. A map that invented such a figure would be committing the offence it exists to correct, so the figure is not given, because it does not exist in a form that can be honestly stated.

## The first twenty-four hours: the police and the production of a child

The field's definitions do their most consequential work in the first day of a case, and the scheme deserves a connected statement. A child found in circumstances engaging the care jurisdiction, begging, lost, rescued from work or from a brothel, recovered from a railway platform or a hospital, is taken charge of by the police or the District Child Protection Unit and must be produced before the Child Welfare Committee, under Section 31 of the juvenile justice statute, within twenty-four hours excluding the time of journey, with a report of the circumstances. The Special Juvenile Police Unit, designated in every district and at police station level through child welfare police officers, is the police formation built for exactly this interface, and its existence is the system's admission that the ordinary police response, arrest, lock-up, production before a magistrate, is the wrong response to a child who has committed no offence. The committee then inquires and disposes: restoration to family with or without supervision, placement in a children's home or fit institution, foster care, sponsorship, or a declaration that the child is legally free for adoption. A child alleged to have committed an offence travels the parallel track to the Juvenile Justice Board, with bail as the working rule under the statute rather than the exception, and with the prohibition on joint trial with an adult and on disqualification attaching to a finding against the child. The two tracks share the first twenty-four hours and the definition of the child, and diverge on everything else, which is why the intake decision, which track, whose definition, which body, is the single most consequential act in the system and the one least visible in the appellate record. Errors at intake, the victim produced as an accused, the accused lodged in an institution meant for the unprotected, are the errors the commissions' inspection reports return to most often, and they are definitional errors before they are anything else: someone at a desk applied the wrong statute's answer to the person in front of them.

The missing child is the case in which the intake machinery is tested most often, and it has acquired a dedicated layer of its own. A missing child report sets the police machinery in motion under the juvenile statute's framework, and the Ministry of Women and Child Development operates the TrackChild portal as a national database for missing and recovered children, with the Khoya Paya portal opened to citizen reporting of sightings, so that a child recovered in one state can be matched against a report filed in another. The Supreme Court's directions in the Bachpan Bachao Andolan litigation on missing children pressed states on standard operating procedures, prompt registration of cases and the staffing of the juvenile police units, because the record showed reports treated as station diary entries rather than as the opening of a care and protection case. The definitional point recurs in its plainest form: a missing person of sixteen is a child for the search, a potential victim for the trafficking provisions and a runaway in the language of the station register, and which of those descriptions governs the first day's work decides whether the child is searched for as a person to be protected or noted as a person who has left.

## Three readers at the same door

The map's discipline can be tested on the three readers the brief for this article names. A teacher is told by a pupil of fourteen that her stepfather touches her, and that she is to be married in the vacation. The teacher's obligations run under three statutes at once and none of them is the education statute: the disclosure engages the reporting duty under the sexual offences statute, owed to the police and not discharged by informing the head of the institution alone; the threatened marriage engages the child marriage statute, whose prohibition officer and the police can act before the event, and the girl is a child in need of care and protection whom the committee can protect; and her age for every one of these purposes is fourteen-going-on-fifteen under definitions that treat her as a child throughout, while her schooling right under the education statute is the one entitlement in the room that has just expired by age. A doctor in a casualty department receives a boy of sixteen with injuries from a factory machine. The treatment duty is immediate and free where the injury discloses a sexual offence, and in all cases the medico-legal record the doctor creates is the document on which three later inquiries will depend: the labour statute's, because an adolescent in a hazardous process is an offence by the employer; the juvenile statute's, because the boy may be a child in need of care and protection; and the compensation machinery that follows a workplace injury. A police officer registering a case involving a girl of seventeen recovered from a brothel must write three files with three vocabularies: minor under the trafficking statute for the premises offences, child under the sexual offences statute for the assault offences, child in need of care and protection for the committee, and the files will be read by three different authorities. The reader who has followed the map this far will see that none of these professionals needs to know more law than their own statute. Each needs to know that their statute is not the only one in the room, and which door the child also has to be taken through. That is the whole practical content of the proposition that there is no single definition of a child in Indian law.

## What the field does not contain

A map should mark its empty spaces, and the child protection field has several that its statute list conceals. There is no general statute against the abuse of children in the home outside the sexual offences statute and the cruelty provision of the juvenile justice statute, which reaches a person having the actual charge of or control over a child who assaults, abandons, abuses, exposes or wilfully neglects the child in a manner likely to cause unnecessary mental or physical suffering, and corporal punishment is prohibited by name only in the school, through Section 17 of the education statute, leaving the home to the general law of assault and the cruelty provision's threshold. There is no statute addressed to the adolescent as such: the person between fourteen and eighteen is protected as a child by the protective statutes, permitted as a worker by the labour statute, and holding an enforceable right to schooling under neither, and the specific vulnerabilities of that band, early marriage, hazardous work in the informal economy, recruitment into crime, are governed by instruments written for younger children or for adults. There is no consolidated children Act of the kind the Goa statute of 2003 sketched at state level, and the two attempts to consolidate the trafficking subject failed in Parliament, leaving consolidation as the field's perennial recommendation and perennial casualty. And there is no Indian statute that defines its terms by reference to the field as a whole: each Act's definition clause opens with the words unless the context otherwise requires and closes its doors behind it, so that the unity of the subject exists in the committee room, the commission's report and the Constitution's directives, but not in any enacted text. The empty spaces matter for the same reason the divergent ages matter. A reader who knows only the statute list will overestimate the field's coherence in both directions, supposing a single definition where there are five, and supposing coverage where the statutes, read together, leave a band of adolescents and a set of harms to the general law and to Article 21.

## The commissions' test, restated as the article's test

The standard by which this field asks to be judged is written into the commissions statute itself: that all laws, policies, programmes and administrative mechanisms be in consonance with the child rights perspective in the Constitution and the Convention. Measured against that standard, the definitional record is a picture of a destination accepted and a journey unfinished. The protective core has converged on eighteen. The constitutional text still carries a fourteen in Article 24 and a six to fourteen band in Article 21A that no court can move. The labour statute grades its permissions between fourteen and eighteen on a rationale that its defenders state openly and its critics answer in kind. The marriage statute borrows a capacity rule that splits childhood by sex. The trafficking statute grades protection at sixteen because a Parliament has not yet replaced it. Each of these positions is intelligible in the statute that holds it, and the field is indefensible only when the positions are mistaken for one another. That mistake, in a charge sheet, a hospital register, a school file, a committee order, is the practical harm this article exists to prevent, and it is prevented by the cheapest discipline in the whole of Indian law: before answering any question about a child, ask which statute is asking it.

## The international layer beyond the two ratifications

The Convention and the two labour conventions are the field's headline obligations, but the international layer is thicker, and its instruments explain several domestic features that otherwise look arbitrary. India ratified the two Optional Protocols to the Convention, on the involvement of children in armed conflict and on the sale of children, child prostitution and child pornography, in 2005, and the sale protocol's catalogue of offences is one of the templates behind the juvenile statute's offence of sale and procurement of children and behind the sexual offences statute's pornography provisions. India ratified the Hague Convention on Protection of Children and Co-operation in Respect of Inter-country Adoption of 1993 in 2003, and the Central Adoption Resource Authority's position as the central authority under that Convention is the reason the secular adoption route is built as a regulated channel with a designated authority rather than as a private arrangement. India ratified the United Nations Convention against Transnational Organized Crime and its Protocol to Prevent, Suppress and Punish Trafficking in Persons, Especially Women and Children in 2011, and the Protocol's definition of trafficking, built on act, means and purpose with the means element dispensed with for a child, is the definition the substituted Section 370 of the Penal Code tracks. The South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation Convention on Preventing and Combating Trafficking in Women and Children for Prostitution, signed in 2002, supplies the regional layer for the same subject. The point for the map is the direction of travel these instruments record. Every one of them works at eighteen or treats childhood as the protected category without a lower protective line, so each ratification tightens the gravitational pull described earlier, and each leaves the domestic outliers, sixteen in the trafficking statute and the marriage split, more exposed than before. International law has not forced the convergence; the statutes could lawfully have kept their lines. It has instead made the convergence the path of least resistance for every drafting exercise since 1992, which is how soft obligations harden into a definitional consensus without any single decision ever being taken.

## The load-bearing statute is not single-aged either

It sharpens the map to notice that even the juvenile justice statute, the field's standard-bearer for eighteen, works internally with more than one age. Eighteen is its jurisdictional line, but the statute fixes sixteen as the age below which the transfer question cannot arise, since the preliminary assessment machinery applies only to a child who has completed sixteen; it fixes the age of the child for adoption purposes by its own provisions and regulations; it runs aftercare to twenty-one for the young person leaving institutional care; and its offence provisions grade several wrongs by the age and position of the adult offender rather than of the child. None of this is inconsistency. It is the ordinary condition of a working statute, which needs a jurisdictional line and then a set of working thresholds inside it. The observation matters because it disarms the crudest version of the uniformity argument, the version that imagines a well-drafted field would contain exactly one number. No such field exists anywhere. The English Children Act 1989, often cited as the model of a consolidated children's statute, fixes childhood at eighteen and then grades its own provisions with thresholds for leaving care, for accommodation duties and for the ages at which a child's own wishes acquire procedural weight. The demand that survives scrutiny is the narrower one this article has defended: one line for protection, thresholds admitted where they grade a permission or a procedure, and no line retained merely because it is old.

## The education boundary in court

The education statute's band has been tested at both ends, and the two leading decisions fix its meaning for the map. In Society for Unaided Private Schools of Rajasthan v. Union of India (2012), the Supreme Court upheld the Act's central scheme, including the obligation on unaided non-minority schools to admit children of the six to fourteen band from disadvantaged groups and weaker sections to the extent of twenty-five per cent of the entry class, reading the statute as the working out of Article 21A. In Pramati Educational and Cultural Trust v. Union of India (2014), a Constitution Bench exempted minority institutions, aided and unaided, from the Act, on the ground that applying it to them would abrogate the right of minorities to establish and administer educational institutions under Article 30(1). Between them the decisions hold the band firm and mark its constitutional edges: within six to fourteen the right is enforceable and its burdens can be distributed across the school system, and outside the band, and inside the minority institution, the statute does not run. For the definitional map the pair completes the education row of the table with the only gloss it needs: the six to fourteen line is not an administrative choice the executive could revise, but the working boundary of a constitutional right as the Court has construed both the right and its limits.

## Charging across the overlap: the sexual offences statute and the general penal law

Where the protective statute and the general penal law cover the same act, the field has a priority rule, and it belongs on the map because it decides real charge sheets. Section 42 of the sexual offences statute provides that where an act or omission constitutes an offence punishable under that statute and also under specified provisions of the penal code, the offender shall be liable to the punishment that is greater in degree, and Section 42A, inserted by the amendment of 2019, declares that the statute's provisions are in addition to and not in derogation of any other law, and prevail to the extent of any inconsistency. The corresponding offences in the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, which replaced the Penal Code, sit alongside the special statute in the same way. The practical meaning is that the special statute's definition controls the prosecution's framing for a victim below eighteen: the charge is laid under the statute built for the child, with its special court, its procedure and its presumptions, and the general law supplies a floor rather than a rival. This is the charging expression of the principle the courts stated in the juvenility cases: within its own subject, the special protective statute is the governing text, and its definition of a child is not diluted by the fact that another code, applied to another question, might describe the same person differently.

## The informal economy: where the labour definitions meet the household

The labour rows of the table do their hardest work in the informal economy, and one episode fixes the point. Under the original Act of 1986, which prohibited child employment only in listed occupations and processes, the executive widened the list by notification, and on 10 October 2006 domestic work and employment in dhabas, restaurants, hotels and the hospitality sector were added to the prohibited list for children, a change that brought the statute into the household for the first time and generated the period's defining argument about enforcement inside private homes. The 2016 amendment absorbed that history into a general prohibition below fourteen, but the structural problem the notification exposed remains: the children the labour statute most needs to reach work where inspectors do not ordinarily go, inside homes as domestic help, in family enterprises the amendment expressly accommodates, and in supply chains whose formal employer is several contracts away. The adolescent category sits at the centre of this difficulty, because the adolescent in non-hazardous informal work is lawful, invisible and uncounted all at once, and the distance between the table's clean line at fourteen and the worksite's reality is the distance the rehabilitation fund, the district task forces and the commissions' inspections exist to close. A definitional map that did not record this would mistake the law's clarity for the field's condition; the lines are clear, and it is their reach that is contested.

## How to read a definition clause: the method under the map

The discipline this article has practised has a name in interpretation, and stating it converts the map into a transferable skill. A statutory definition is not a dictionary entry. It is an instruction that operates only inside its own Act, which is why definition clauses open with the words in this Act, unless the context otherwise requires, and why the Supreme Court has repeatedly held that a definition given in one statute cannot be borrowed to construe another statute dealing with a different subject. The General Clauses Act, 1897 supplies general definitions for central legislation, and it contains no definition of a child at all, a silence that is itself instructive: the general interpretation statute of the Republic leaves childhood to be defined harm by harm, exactly as the field has done. The method follows. Identify the proceeding. Take the definition from the statute that governs the proceeding. Resist the importation of a neighbouring definition however familiar it is. Check whether a constitutional provision fixes the band, as Articles 21A and 24 do, because where the Constitution has drawn the line no statute may redraw it. And where two protective statutes both apply, apply each to its own question, as the courts did in Independent Thought and in the orphanages case, rather than electing one and silencing the other. That method, and not any memorised table, is what the aspirant, the teacher, the doctor and the police officer in this article's worked examples actually needed, and it is the sense in which the missing definition is not a defect to be lamented but a structure to be learned.

## The youngest lines of all: criminal responsibility at seven and twelve

The table in this article lists the ages at which the protective statutes stop treating a person as a child, but the penal law also fixes the ages at which a person starts being treatable as an offender, and they are the oldest lines in the field. Section 82 of the Indian Penal Code, carried into Section 20 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, provides that nothing is an offence which is done by a child under seven years of age, an absolute immunity that no evidence of maturity can displace. Section 83 of the Code, Section 21 of the Sanhita, extends a qualified immunity to a child above seven and under twelve who has not attained sufficient maturity of understanding to judge the nature and consequences of the conduct. These lines descend from the English common law doctrine of doli incapax, they have survived every recodification since 1860 unchanged, and they complete the field's true picture: Indian law does not merely fail to agree on when childhood ends; it also fixes the beginning of responsibility at points that no other statute uses, so that a child of eight is a full child for every protective purpose and simultaneously a person whose criminal responsibility must be individually proved. The juvenile justice statute then takes every person below eighteen who is alleged to have offended out of the ordinary courts altogether, whatever Sections 82 and 83 say, which produces the final layering: responsibility begins at seven or twelve under the general law, jurisdiction belongs to the Board until eighteen under the special law, and punishment in the adult sense is excluded below eighteen by the special law's own scheme. Three statutes, three different questions, three different numbers, each answering correctly the question it was asked.

## Capacity in daily life: the contract the minor cannot make

The Majority Act's eighteen does its work in the most ordinary transactions, and one old decision states the rule the whole commercial world still runs on. In Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose (1903), the Privy Council held that an agreement by a minor is void, not merely voidable, because the Indian Contract Act, 1872 premises contractual competence on majority, so that a mortgage executed by a minor could not be enforced against him and money advanced on it could not be recovered as a loan. The consequence runs through daily life: the bank account operated under guardianship, the property that cannot be conveyed, the employment and apprenticeship arrangements that an adult must countersign in substance if not in form, the marriage that personal law permits while the prohibition statute penalises. Capacity is the quiet half of the field's subject. Protection decides what the world may not do to a child; capacity decides what a child may not yet do in the world, and the two halves share the majority line at eighteen while sharing nothing else, which is one more reason the number eighteen feels like the field's centre even to lawyers who could not name the statutes that converge on it. When the person in question is under a court-appointed guardian, the line moves to twenty-one for the property under supervision, and the conveyancer who assumes eighteen without asking about guardianship makes the practitioner's version of the mistake this article began with: applying a correct definition to a case it does not govern.

## Protection and autonomy: the tension the definitions conceal

Underneath the definitional argument runs a second argument that the ages encode without stating, between the child as an object of protection and the child as a holder of rights whose own view counts for more as the years accumulate. The Convention holds both ideas at once: its article on the best interests of the child and its article on the child's right to be heard sit beside the doctrine of evolving capacities in its fifth article, under which parental and state direction is to be exercised consistently with the capacities the child is developing. The juvenile justice statute writes the same settlement into Section 3, which lays down the general principles to be followed in administering the Act, among them the principle of best interest, the principle of participation, under which every child has a right to be heard and to participate in all processes and decisions affecting the child's interests, the principle of safety, the principle of positive measures, the principle of a fresh start, under which a child in conflict with law is entitled to have the past erased from the record, and the principle of non-stigmatising semantics, which is the statutory form of the renaming history traced earlier in this article. The definitions serve the protective half of the settlement; the principles serve the autonomy half; and the statutes work because the two halves are kept in different provisions, the age lines deciding jurisdiction and the principles deciding how jurisdiction is exercised once it attaches.

The tension becomes visible wherever a protective definition is used to override an older child's own choice, and the case law on adolescents in relationships, on girls who leave home to marry, and on children who refuse restoration to their families is largely the record of committees and courts negotiating it. A girl of seventeen who has left home with a partner presents the system with a child in need of care and protection whose stated wish is the opposite of protection as her family defines it; the committee must apply the definition, hear the child under the participation principle, and decide a restoration that the definition alone cannot settle. Nothing in the definitional table resolves such a case, and nothing should: the table's job is to say whose law applies, and the principles' job is to say how it is applied to a person whose claim to decide grows with every year the table counts. Holding the two apart is the last refinement of the article's method, and the point at which the map hands the reader back to the statutes themselves, which is where this series intends its maps to end.

## The school as the field's crossroads

No institution stands at the intersection of more of these statutes than a school, and the school's position gathers the whole map into one building. For its pupils of six to fourteen the school is the site of a constitutional right, bound by the education statute to admit without screening or capitation, to teach through a prescribed curriculum with qualified teachers, to observe the norms on infrastructure and pupil-teacher ratio, and to inflict no physical punishment or mental harassment, with the grievance machinery of the education statute and the monitoring of the child rights commissions under Sections 31 and 32 of that Act standing behind the parent who complains. For a pupil of any age the school is a place whose staff carry the reporting duty of the sexual offences statute and whose management falls within that statute's aggravated categories where an offence is committed by a person in a position of trust or authority over the child. For the adolescent on its rolls the school is also the holder of the date of birth record that will later stand first in the hierarchy of age proof, and its admission register is consulted by boards, committees and courts in proceedings the school will never hear of. And at its gate the school meets the labour statute, in the child withdrawn for work, the seasonal migration that empties classrooms, and the employer's claim that the adolescent sweeps the premises lawfully after hours. A head teacher who understands only the education statute understands perhaps half the law that operates inside the school fence, and the frequency with which school-level errors appear in commission reports, the disclosure reported internally and never to the police, the child marriage known and never reported, the age recorded from a parent's statement and never corrected, is the field's everyday proof that the definitional map is a working instrument and not a classroom diagram.

## The scheme beneath the statutes

Statutes create the rights and the bodies; a centrally sponsored scheme pays for much of the machinery, and the map is incomplete without it. The Integrated Child Protection Scheme, launched in 2009 and continued in restructured form as Mission Vatsalya, is the funding and administrative channel through which the Centre supports the state and district machinery the juvenile justice statute mandates: the District Child Protection Units that anchor the system in each district, the State Child Protection Society above them, the Childline service, the child care institutions' standards and the sponsorship, foster care and aftercare programmes that give the committee's orders somewhere to land. The scheme's significance for the definitional question is indirect but real. Because the scheme funds the machinery of the juvenile justice statute, that statute's institutions and its eighteen-year definition become the default infrastructure for children who arrive under other statutes as well, so that the rescued worker, the trafficked minor and the child stopped from marriage are all processed, in practice, through the one system that has buildings, staff and a budget line. Administrative gravity thus reinforces legal gravity: the statute with the machinery becomes the field's centre, and its definition becomes the working definition of every desk that has to fill a form before it can consult a commentary.

## Restoration, foster care and the money that follows the child

The committee's orders are not exhausted by placing a child in an institution, and the statute's preference runs the other way: restoration to the family is the first object, and the alternatives are arranged as a descent from the family outwards, through kinship care, foster care by an unrelated family found fit, sponsorship that supports the child inside the child's own family or community, and institutional care as the last resort rather than the default. Foster care and sponsorship are the provisions through which the field's money reaches a child without removing the child, and the Juvenile Justice Fund, for which Section 105 of the Act of 2015 provides, is the statutory receptacle for voluntary contributions applied to the welfare and rehabilitation of children under the Act. The bearing of all this on the definitional map is the bearing it has everywhere in this article: every one of these dispositions is available only for a child as the juvenile statute defines the word, so that the young person of nineteen in an institution, however unfinished the work of care, stands outside the committee's authority except through the narrow aftercare window to twenty-one. The line at eighteen is thus not only jurisdictional but fiscal and administrative; it decides whose restoration the state will fund, whose foster placement it will supervise and whose file it will close, and the closing of files at majority, recorded in inspection reports as a routine discharge, is the point at which the field's central definition is felt by the person it defines as the withdrawal of the state itself.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which laws protect children in India?

Protection is spread across a group of statutes rather than gathered in one code. The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015 governs children in need of care and protection and children in conflict with law. The Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012 covers sexual offences. The Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006 covers child marriage, the Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act covers work, and the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 covers schooling. Around them sit the Commissions for Protection of Child Rights Act, 2005, the Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956 and the law on prenatal diagnostic techniques, with the Constitution above them all.

### Q: Who is a child under Indian law?

There is no single answer, because Indian law contains no general definition of a child and each statute fixes its own age. The juvenile justice and sexual offences statutes use eighteen. The child marriage statute uses twenty-one for a male and eighteen for a female. The labour statute uses fourteen for a child and eighteen for the upper limit of an adolescent. The education statute covers six to fourteen, the Majority Act fixes eighteen, and the trafficking statute grades protection at sixteen and eighteen. The correct answer is always the definition in the statute governing the question being asked, and importing a definition from a neighbouring statute is the field's most common practical error.

### Q: Why does the definition of child differ between statutes?

Because each statute was written against a different harm and at a different time. A protective statute marking the boundary of a wrong has reason to draw the line high, which is why the sexual offences statute sits at eighteen. A statute grading a permission, such as the labour statute deciding what work a person may do, has reason to grade its ages rather than use one line. Some lines are inherited rather than chosen: the marriage statute adopted the general marriageable ages, and the trafficking statute keeps bands drawn in the middle of the twentieth century. The divergences are therefore partly principled and partly historical, and they persist because statutes are amended one at a time.

### Q: What does the Constitution say about children?

The Constitution speaks in several registers. Article 15(3) permits special provision for children. Article 23 prohibits trafficking and forced labour, and Article 24 prohibits the employment of a child below fourteen in factories, mines and hazardous work. Article 21A guarantees free and compulsory education to children of six to fourteen. In the directive principles, Article 39(e) protects the tender age of children from abuse, Article 39(f) requires that childhood be protected against exploitation and moral and material abandonment, and Article 45 directs early childhood care and education below six. Article 51A(k) makes it a parent's or guardian's duty to provide education opportunities between six and fourteen.

### Q: Which international obligations shape Indian child law?

The governing instrument is the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child, which India accepted in 1992, and whose definition of a child as a person below eighteen has pulled the protective statutes towards that line ever since. India ratified its two Optional Protocols in 2005. In labour law, India ratified the International Labour Organization's Minimum Age Convention (No. 138) and Worst Forms of Child Labour Convention (No. 182) in 2017, after the labour statute was amended in 2016. The Hague Convention on inter-country adoption, ratified in 2003, shapes the adoption framework, and the Palermo Protocol on trafficking, ratified in 2011, shapes the penal law of trafficking.

### Q: Who enforces child protection law in India?

Enforcement is shared across institutions rather than lodged in one body. Child Welfare Committees in every district decide the care and protection of children, and Juvenile Justice Boards handle children in conflict with law. The police act through Special Juvenile Police Units and child welfare police officers, and District Child Protection Units anchor the administration. The National Commission for Protection of Child Rights and the State Commissions monitor the whole field and the working of particular statutes. Labour inspectors enforce the labour statute, Child Marriage Prohibition Officers act under the marriage statute, and schools, hospitals and the Childline service on 1098 form the front line where cases first surface.

### Q: What should you do if you know a child is being harmed?

Report it. Where the harm is a sexual offence, Section 19 of the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012 makes reporting a legal duty for any person who knows an offence has been committed or apprehends that one is likely, and the report goes to the Special Juvenile Police Unit or the local police; Section 21 punishes a failure to report. A child in distress can be reached through the Childline service on 1098, and a child in need of care and protection can be brought before the Child Welfare Committee of the district. Reporting internally, to an employer or an institution's head alone, does not discharge the statutory duty.

### Q: Which child protection duties apply to a school?

A school stands under several statutes at once. Under the education statute it must admit children of six to fourteen without screening and must not inflict physical punishment or mental harassment. Its staff carry the reporting duty of the sexual offences statute, and its management falls within that statute's aggravated categories where an offence is committed by a person in a position of trust. The school also holds the date of birth records that stand first in the legal hierarchy for proving age elsewhere. Child rights commissions monitor the education statute's working, and a school that knows of a threatened child marriage among its pupils has the same reporting channels as any other citizen.

### Q: At what age does a person stop being a child under the Juvenile Justice Act 2015?

At eighteen. Section 2(12) of the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015 defines a child as a person who has not completed eighteen years of age, and a child in conflict with law is measured by the same line on the date the offence was committed, so a person who offends at seventeen and is apprehended later is still dealt with under the Act. The eighteen line was inherited from the Act of 2000, which had unified the older split under which a juvenile was a boy below sixteen or a girl below eighteen. Within the Act, sixteen matters for the transfer assessment and twenty-one is the limit of aftercare.

### Q: What is the age of majority in India and which statute fixes it?

Eighteen, fixed by the Indian Majority Act, 1875. Section 3 of that Act provides that every person domiciled in India attains majority on completing eighteen years. Majority is the age of legal capacity: the age at which a person may contract, hold and convey property, and act without a guardian. It is a different question from the protective definitions, and the two should not be confused. A person of nineteen is a major of full capacity who is nevertheless a child for the purpose of the child marriage statute if male, and a person of seventeen is a minor for capacity who is fully protected as a child by the protective statutes.

### Q: Can a person be a child under one Indian law and an adult under another at the same time?

Yes, and the position is routine rather than paradoxical, because legal status in this field is relative to the statute and the proceeding. A man of nineteen is an adult of full capacity under the Majority Act and for the franchise, and simultaneously a child under the child marriage statute, whose marriage is prohibited and punishable. A person of seventeen is a child for the sexual offences and juvenile justice statutes, an adolescent who may do non-hazardous work under the labour statute, and outside the education right altogether. No court resolves the overlap by choosing one status for all purposes; each statute is applied to its own question about the same person.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court decide about sexual relations with a wife below eighteen?

In Independent Thought v. Union of India (2017) 10 SCC 800, the Supreme Court read down Exception 2 to Section 375 of the Indian Penal Code, which had exempted intercourse by a man with his own wife where the wife was not under fifteen. The Court held that the exception must be read so that it applies only where the wife is not under eighteen, harmonising the penal code with the child marriage statute, the juvenile justice statute, the sexual offences statute and the domestic violence statute, which all treat a girl below eighteen as a child. The Court did not strike the exception down and expressly did not address marital rape of an adult wife.

### Q: Why does the right to education stop at fourteen when other child laws protect to eighteen?

Because the right is constitutional in origin and the Constitution itself draws the band. Article 21A, inserted by the Eighty-sixth Amendment in 2002, guarantees free and compulsory education to children of six to fourteen, and the education statute of 2009 implements that article in its own terms. A statute giving effect to the article cannot widen the band the article fixed, and extending the enforceable right to eighteen would require constitutional or legislative change. The result is a genuine gap: a person of fifteen is protected as a child by the protective statutes and may lawfully do regulated work, but holds no enforceable right to schooling, a gap policy documents have proposed closing without yet changing the text.

### Q: What is the difference between a Child Welfare Committee and a Juvenile Justice Board?

The two bodies divide the juvenile justice statute's two jurisdictions. A Child Welfare Committee, a five-member body for each district sitting as a bench with magisterial powers, deals with children in need of care and protection: children found begging, abused, abandoned, exploited or at risk, and it orders restoration, placement, foster care and related measures. A Juvenile Justice Board, headed by a judicial magistrate with two social worker members, deals with children in conflict with law, those alleged or found to have committed offences. Producing a child before the wrong body places the child in the wrong legal universe, welfare in one case and inquiry into an offence in the other.

### Q: Did the juvenile justice law always treat everyone below eighteen as a child?

No. The Juvenile Justice Act, 1986 defined a juvenile as a boy who had not attained sixteen years or a girl who had not attained eighteen, carrying a sex split into the first national statute. The Act of 2000 unified the line at eighteen for both sexes, in step with the Convention on the Rights of the Child, and separated the child in conflict with law from the child in need of care and protection. The Act of 2015 kept eighteen as the definition of a child while adding the preliminary assessment by which a child of sixteen to eighteen accused of a heinous offence may be tried as an adult, a change in treatment rather than in the definition itself.

### Q: Why did India ratify the ILO child labour conventions only in 2017?

Because ratification required the domestic law to be brought closer to the conventions first. The Minimum Age Convention (No. 138) requires a declared minimum age for admission to employment, and the Worst Forms of Child Labour Convention (No. 182) requires the elimination of hazardous and exploitative work for everyone below eighteen. India's labour statute, as it stood before 2016, prohibited child work only in listed occupations and processes, which sat uneasily with both instruments. The Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2016 prohibited employment of children below fourteen in all occupations and of adolescents in hazardous work, and India deposited its ratifications of both conventions on 13 June 2017, twenty-five years after accepting the Convention on the Rights of the Child.

### Q: What does the law on prenatal diagnostic techniques have to do with child protection?

It is the field's outermost ring. The Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques (Prohibition of Sex Selection) Act, 1994, renamed and widened in 2003, prohibits the determination and communication of the sex of a foetus and regulates the clinics and laboratories that perform diagnostic procedures, in order to prevent sex selection and the selective abortion of female foetuses. Its object is the survival of the girl child before birth, which is why it belongs in a child protection map even though it contains no definition of a child: the person it protects has no legal age at all, and the statute reaches her by regulating the adults and institutions around her.

### Q: Why can majority begin at twenty-one for some persons and eighteen for others?

Because the Majority Act carries an exception for court-supervised guardianship. The general rule in Section 3 of the Indian Majority Act, 1875 is majority at eighteen. But where a guardian of a minor's person or property has been appointed by a court, or the property is under the superintendence of a Court of Wards, majority is deferred until twenty-one. The exception is a survival from the era in which twenty-one was the ordinary age of majority, preserved for the class of minors whose property a court was already administering, on the reasoning that supervision should not end while the court's own arrangement is incomplete. It affects capacity over the supervised property, not the person's status under the protective statutes.

### Q: Would a single definition of a child at eighteen work across all Indian laws?

For the protective statutes, substantially yes, and the field has largely converged there. For every statute, no, because not all statutes use age for the same purpose. A protection statute marks the boundary of a wrong, while the labour statute grades a permission, and forcing one number to do both jobs would either prohibit all work below eighteen, including the family and light work poor households depend on, or else lower protection to fit a work rule. The defensible reform is narrower: eighteen wherever a statute protects, graded ages only where a permission is genuinely being graded, and the removal of divergences, such as the marriage statute's split by sex, that no protective rationale supports.

### Q: Is an unborn child protected by Indian law?

Obliquely, and through the regulation of others rather than through rights of its own. The prenatal diagnostic statute prohibits sex determination and sex selection, protecting the female foetus by policing clinics and practitioners. The abortion law, the Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act, 1971, fixes the conditions on which a pregnancy may be terminated, and its limits operate as the boundary within which the unborn child's interest is weighed. The general law recognises limited interests of a child in the womb for some property purposes, treating a child later born alive as already in existence for the child's own benefit. But the protective statutes of the child protection field begin at birth, and no statute defines an unborn child as a child for their purposes.
