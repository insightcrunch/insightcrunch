The reproduction schemas are a pair of numerical models built by Karl Marx in the second volume of Capital to answer a question that sounds too simple to need asking: what has to happen, sector by sector and purchase by purchase, for an entire capitalist economy to finish one year and begin the next with its machinery intact, its workers fed, and its owners supplied? Marx divided all production into two great departments, one turning out the means of production and one turning out the means of consumption, and then showed that the two departments can only keep each other going if their outputs stand in definite proportions. The models were a demonstration, not a description. They proved that balanced reproduction is possible under stated proportions, and by doing so they defined the precise way in which it can fail, which is through the proportions being missed.

That abstract-sounding exercise carried heavy freight in the arguments of Marx's successors, because the schemas became a battlefield. One school of readers took them to prove that capitalism could expand indefinitely by its own internal demand, another took the same arithmetic to prove that expansion was impossible without markets outside the capitalist world, and a third defended the arithmetic against both. The same models also turned out to be the direct ancestor of input-output analysis, a technique national statistical offices build into their accounts, and of the two-sector growth models that later planned industrialisation in large economies. A glossary article on the schemas therefore has to do three things at once: state the machinery exactly, keep the rival readings separate, and keep a firm grip on what a consistency demonstration can and cannot establish.

## The reproduction schemas in the second volume of Capital

The schemas occupy the third and final part of the second volume, where Marx turns from the circulation of an individual capital to the reproduction and circulation of the aggregate social capital, meaning the sum of all individual capitals considered as one interlocking whole. The shift of level is the whole point. For a single firm, replacing worn-out machinery and rehiring labour looks like a private matter of buying and selling. For society as a whole, every purchase is simultaneously somebody else's sale, every output is somebody else's input or meal, and the question becomes whether the total product, divided as it is among replacement, wages, consumption, and accumulation, comes out in the physical shapes and values that the next round of production requires. A society can produce too much of one thing and too little of another in aggregate even when every individual transaction was willingly made, and the schemas are the instrument Marx built to state that possibility exactly.

The second volume itself is a posthumous construction, and a reader meeting the schemas should know what kind of text they are reading. Marx died leaving the manuscripts for the second and third volumes in varying states of completion, and Friedrich Engels edited the second volume for publication in 1885, assembling its third part from drafts written across more than a decade. The editorial history matters for method, and it is treated in the series article on the [marx engels authorship question](/2013/06/01/marx-engels-authorship-question/), while the architecture of the first volume, on which everything here depends, is mapped in the [capital volume one complete guide](/2010/06/01/capital-volume-one-complete-guide/). What matters for present purposes is that the schemas are working drafts of a powerful mind rather than a polished doctrine: Marx worries at difficulties in the open, tries numerical arrangements, discards some, and leaves at least one problem, the replacement of fixed capital, visibly half-solved. The unfinished surface is not a defect to apologise for. It is evidence that the models were tools for thinking, built to be used.

### What are Marx's reproduction schemas?

Two-sector models in the second volume of Capital that divide the whole economy into producers of means of production and producers of consumption goods, and set out the proportions between the two that must hold if the economy is to reproduce itself, at the same scale or while accumulating.

The definition rewards slow unpacking, because every word in it is load-bearing. Two sectors: not two industries among thousands, but two exhaustive classes into which every product falls according to its destination, either entering further production as an instrument or material, or entering individual consumption. Models: the schemas are arrangements of figures chosen by Marx to satisfy conditions he specifies, in the way a geometry construction satisfies a theorem. The proportions between them: the argument is never about totals alone, since a larger product badly divided between the departments reproduces worse than a smaller product rightly divided. Reproduce itself: the verb is deliberate, borrowed from biology, and it frames the economy as a system that must replace its own conditions of existence each cycle, machines, materials, labour power, and the consumption of its owners included. At an unchanged scale or in growth: the two cases, simple and expanded reproduction, receive a section each below, because the balance condition differs between them and the difference is where most of the later argument lives.

### How is the closing part of the second volume organised?

It opens with an introduction on the aggregate social capital, passes through a critical chapter on earlier treatments of reproduction by the physiocrats and by Adam Smith, and then devotes one long chapter to simple reproduction and a final chapter to accumulation and reproduction on an expanded scale.

The organisation is itself an argument. By placing his own models after a long critical examination of the physiocratic Tableau and of Smith's doctrine that the whole price of the product resolves into revenues, Marx signals that the schemas exist to correct a specific inherited error: the habit of dissolving the replacement of constant capital out of the picture. Smith's analysis, on Marx's reading, made the portion of the product that replaces used-up means of production vanish analytically, and with it vanished the entire problem of inter-sectoral proportion. The critical chapter is therefore not a history lesson bolted onto the front of the theory. It explains why a model was needed at all, and why the model's first service is bookkeeping discipline: nothing in the total product may be wished away, and every portion must find its destination. Readers who skip the polemic often find the schemas arid. Readers who take it on board see the schemas as an answer to a muddle that had stood for a century.

There is a further quiet point in the placement. The second volume's first two parts follow one capital through its metamorphoses and turnover; the third part asks what those metamorphoses look like when every capital's purchases are another's sales. The schemas are thus the volume's summit rather than an appendix, the place where the analysis of circulation becomes an analysis of the economy as a system. Everything in the third volume about the distribution of surplus value among profits, interest, and rent presupposes that the aggregate product has been successfully reproduced, so the schemas underwrite more of the overall project than their forbidding arithmetic suggests.

## Two departments and three components

Department One comprises every line of production whose output re-enters production: coal and iron, machine tools and the machines that make them, raw cotton and yarn, railway track, factory buildings, and the raw and auxiliary materials consumed in making anything at all. Department Two comprises every line whose output is consumed by persons: bread and clothing, furniture and housing services, entertainments, and medicines. The classification follows the use of the product, not the technique of its manufacture, so a single industry can straddle the line. A bakery sells loaves to households, which places that output in the second department, while a colliery sells coal both to steelworks, where it belongs to the first department's flow, and to households for their grates, where the same physical coal counts as a consumption good. What decides is the destination of each portion of output, a principle that later national accountants would recognise as classification by use.

Each department's annual product is then analysed into three components of value, a decomposition carried over from the first volume and only summarised here, since its full treatment belongs to that volume's guide, already linked above. Constant capital is the value of the means of production used up during the year, transferred to the product as machines wear and materials are consumed. Variable capital is the value laid out in wages, which returns expanded because labour power creates more value than its own upkeep costs. Surplus value is that excess, the source of profit, rent, interest, and the capitalists' own consumption. Every figure in the schemas is a sum of these three elements, and the movements between departments are exchanges between particular elements, which is why the analysis can be so exact: the schemas track not just how much each department produces, but which slice of its product must travel to which destination.

### What does each department hand over to the other?

The first department supplies the second with replacement machinery and materials, and receives back the consumption goods its workers and owners live on; the second department supplies those consumption goods and receives the equipment without which its own production would stop at the year's end.

The reciprocal dependence is easy to state and hard to keep fully in view, which is why the schemas exist. Neither department is self-sufficient. The first cannot eat its own lathes; the second cannot weave with good intentions. But the dependence is not symmetrical in one crucial respect: the first department also replaces its own means of production internally, through exchanges among its own firms, and adds any net investment there too, while the second department's internal traffic consists of its workers and owners buying its output. Of the exchanges the schemas trace, only one stream crosses between the departments in each direction, and the equality of those crossing streams is the balance condition in each case. Everything else, the great internal churn within each department, cancels out of the final reckoning, though Marx works through it in detail to show that the cancellation is legitimate.

Marx further divides the second department into two sub-departments, one producing necessities and one producing luxuries, and the subdivision is not decoration. Workers spend their wages in the necessities branch, while the luxury branch lives from the spending of capitalists, so the circulation between the sub-departments has its own balance to strike: luxury workers are paid wages that they spend on necessities, and the necessities branch must release to the luxury branch's owners a corresponding slice of revenue, or the luxury workers cannot eat and the luxury owners cannot sell. The subdivision lets the schemas register something easily forgotten, that the composition of consumption matters and not merely its total. An economy can hit the aggregate proportion between departments and still misfire inside the second, producing yachts where bread was required by the wage bill already paid out.

### Why does classification by destination matter so much?

Because the same object performs different economic functions in different hands, and reproduction fails or succeeds at the level of functions. A tonne of coal burned under a boiler is productive consumption; a tonne burned in a parlour is individual consumption; confusing the two destroys any account of replacement.

The point generalises beyond coal. Steel embodied in a lathe is a means of production; steel embodied in a motor car sold to a household is, for the schemas' purposes, a finished consumption good, and the steel industry's output must be split accordingly when the departments' accounts are drawn. Critics sometimes present this as pedantry, and in one sense it is: the schemas are pedantic, deliberately, because the error they were built to hunt was an accounting error, Smith's vanishing constant capital. But the pedantry carries a thesis. It says that the economy's ability to continue is a property of the material composition of the total product, not just its value total, and that a society whose factories turn out the wrong mix in the right total value will discover the mistake physically, in idle machines for want of materials or in unsold goods for want of buyers whose incomes were spent elsewhere. Value proportions and material proportions must hold together, and the schemas are the earliest rigorous statement of that dual requirement in the literature of economics.

## Simple reproduction and the first balance condition

Simple reproduction is the case in which nothing accumulates. Every capitalist consumes the whole of the surplus value as revenue, no net investment occurs, and production restarts each year on exactly the scale it reached the year before. Marx is explicit that this is an abstraction at odds with the restless expansion of real capitalism, and he adopts it for the same reason a physicist begins with a frictionless plane: the balance requirements of reproduction are already intricate when the scale is constant, and they can be isolated cleanly only before accumulation complicates every exchange. Simple reproduction is the baseline against which the expanded case is then defined, and most of the confusion in the later literature comes from readers skipping the baseline and arguing directly about growth.

State the first balance condition in words, naming its components. Within Department One, the portion of the year's product equal to its constant capital is replaced inside the department itself, as producers of machinery and materials supply one another. Within Department Two, the portion equal to its variable capital plus its surplus value is realised inside the department, as its workers and owners purchase its consumption goods. One stream remains, and it must balance exactly: the variable capital plus the surplus value of the first department, which together represent the incomes of its workers and capitalists and must be spent on consumption goods, must equal the constant capital of the second department, which represents the value of the means of production that department has used up and must replace in kind. When that equality holds, the first department's income earners buy consumption goods worth precisely what the second department needs to spend on replacement equipment, and the exchange between the departments clears.

The equality deserves a slow paraphrase, because it is the hinge of the entire construction. Workers and capitalists of the machine-making department end the year holding money incomes, wages and surplus, and holding no product they can consume, since their department makes nothing edible or wearable. Capitalists of the consumption-goods department end the year holding saleable goods but having worn out machinery and used up materials whose value they must restore before production can restart. Each side holds exactly what the other lacks. If the first department's wage-and-surplus fund is larger than the second department's replacement requirement, then money seeking consumption goods bids against a smaller physical stock than it expects, while a portion of the first department's output finds no buyer at all. If the fund is smaller, consumption goods pile up unsold while worn equipment goes unreplaced. Only at the equality do the two departments' needs mesh tooth for tooth, which is what Marx means by calling the condition the basis of simple reproduction.

### What has to balance when nothing accumulates?

The consumption fund of the first department, its wages plus its surplus, must equal the replacement requirement of the second department, its used-up constant capital; the two sums are exchanged against each other, and every other flow settles inside its own department's boundaries.

The elegance of the result should not disguise its strangeness. The condition links magnitudes that no single actor controls and no market signal directly reports. No ministry announces the second department's aggregate replacement bill; no statistic tells the first department's employers what their combined wage bill ought to be. In the model's world the equality is simply assumed to hold so that its consequences can be traced. In any real economy the equality is an outcome that decentralised decisions grope towards or fall short of, which is why the schemas, built as a proof of possibility, simultaneously serve as a map of failure modes. Marx draws attention to exactly this double character: the conditions of normal reproduction, he observes, turn into as many conditions of abnormal reproduction, possibilities of crisis, since equilibrium in a self-organising system is itself an accident. That sentence, buried in a chapter of arithmetic, is one of the most quoted in the whole second volume, and it earns its fame.

The worked example Marx gives for the simple case uses round thousands, and the two conditions table below reproduces it so the reader can verify the arithmetic mentally. Department One produces means of production worth six thousand, of which four thousand replaces its own used-up constant capital, one thousand is wages, and one thousand is surplus. Department Two produces consumption goods worth three thousand, of which two thousand replaces its constant capital, five hundred is wages, and five hundred is surplus. The crossing exchange is then two thousand against two thousand: the second department buys two thousand of means of production, and the first department's workers and capitalists buy two thousand of consumption goods. Inside the first department, four thousand of equipment changes hands among its own firms. Inside the second, one thousand of consumption goods is bought by its own workers and owners. Every portion of the nine thousand of total product has found its destination, and the year can begin again identically.

Three features of the example repay attention. First, the total product of the first department is exactly twice that of the second, a proportion fixed by the assumed conditions rather than by nature; change the assumed scale of either department and the balance breaks until the other adjusts. Second, the exchange across the boundary, two thousand, is much smaller than the traffic inside the first department, four thousand, which is why aggregate statistics that record only inter-industry flows can badly mislead. Third, the arrangement has no slack in it. Marx chose numbers that satisfy the condition exactly, and any reader who perturbs one figure, raising the second department's constant capital to twenty-one hundred while leaving everything else, will watch the machine jam: a hundred of means of production goes unbought, a hundred of consumption goods unsold, and the disequilibrium then propagates into the following year's starting positions. The schemas' fragility under perturbation is not a blemish on them. It is their message about the system they model.

### Where does the money for the exchanges come from?

From the capitalist class itself, in the model's closed circuit: employers advance wages in money before the product is sold, owners spend revenue ahead of its return, and the money thrown into circulation by these advances flows back as purchases replenish the funds it left.

The money question might seem pedantic, yet Marx devotes long stretches to it because it conceals a fallacy he wants to kill. If workers are paid money wages and capitalists receive money profits, and the whole product must be bought with money, where does the money to buy the surplus portion come from? The schemas' answer is that the question confuses a stock with a flow. The money that buys the product of a given year is the same money that paid that year's wages and advanced that year's costs, circulating repeatedly; the capitalist class advances the money for its own consumption out of its funds and recovers it when workers spend their wages, and the portion of the product embodying surplus value is purchased with money that the class itself has put into circulation through its advances and its spending. A special sub-case completes the account: the producer of gold, the money material, sits inside the first department and injects new money without needing to buy in return, which lets the model explain how the money stock itself can grow. Hoards, reserves set aside against the uneven timing of sales and purchases, do the smoothing work, and Marx's treatment of them is the seed of much later writing on liquidity. None of this is the article's main road, but a glossary that omitted it would leave readers defenceless against the oldest undergraduate objection to the schemas, and the objection has a clean answer.

## Expanded reproduction and the second balance condition

Expanded reproduction relaxes the baseline's central assumption. Capitalists now consume only part of the surplus value and convert the rest into additional capital, buying more means of production and hiring more labour power, so that production restarts on a larger scale. This is accumulation, the normal state of the system Marx is analysing, and the schemas' second case asks what proportions allow it to proceed smoothly. The answer begins from the same identity as before and then departs from it: for accumulation to occur, the first department must produce more means of production than are needed merely to replace what both departments used up, and that material surplus of equipment and materials is the physical precondition of growth. An economy cannot invest what it has not produced, and the schemas make the truism quantitative.

State the second balance condition in words. Department One's variable capital, together with its surplus value, must exceed the constant capital of the second department. The excess is not a disequilibrium; it is the room for accumulation. The first department's income fund, swollen by the requirement to maintain additional workers and by the portion of surplus its owners set aside, must find in the second department a correspondingly enlarged demand for equipment, and the second department in turn must enlarge its constant capital out of its own surplus if it is to employ the additional consumption-goods capacity that growing employment in the first department will call for. Accumulation in the two departments is therefore not independent. The first department's decision to expand dictates, through the balance condition, a required expansion in the second, and the departments must grow in step or the crossing exchange fails to clear.

The sequence of moves in Marx's worked example shows the interlocking clearly. His starting figures for the expanded case differ from the simple case in the second department, which begins smaller relative to the first: means of production worth six thousand in the first department, consumption goods worth three thousand in the second, but with the second department's constant capital at fifteen hundred rather than two thousand. The first department's wages plus surplus then total two thousand, exceeding the second's replacement need by five hundred, and that excess is the margin available for growth. The first department's capitalists capitalise half their surplus, five hundred, dividing it between four hundred of additional constant capital and one hundred of additional variable capital in the proportion their technology dictates, and consume the remaining five hundred. Their department now needs consumption goods for one thousand of original wages, one hundred of new wages, and five hundred of consumption spending: sixteen hundred in all. The second department must therefore raise its constant capital from fifteen hundred to sixteen hundred, financing the extra hundred from its surplus and adding fifty of extra wages in its own technical proportion, which leaves six hundred of its surplus for its owners' consumption. The crossing exchange clears at sixteen hundred, and the following year opens with both departments enlarged, the first at sixty-six hundred of product and the second at thirty-two hundred, in proportions from which the dance can repeat.

### What changes when part of the surplus is accumulated?

The first department's income fund must now overshoot the second department's mere replacement requirement, and the overshoot is the accumulation fund: additional equipment for both departments, additional wages for the extra workers the new equipment sets in motion.

Notice what the example demonstrates and what it pointedly does not. It demonstrates that a path exists along which both departments grow year after year with every market clearing, given the starting proportions and the accumulation decisions assumed. It does not demonstrate that any actual economy finds that path, and Marx's text is studded with the frictions he is abstracting from: the need for money hoards to be built up in advance, since a capitalist must amass the whole sum for a new plant before spending it; the lumpiness of fixed investment, which makes one year's surplus insufficient and forces multi-year saving; the requirement that additional labour power actually be available, which the model secures by assuming a reserve of unemployed workers; and the timing problem that the second department's expansion must anticipate the first's, or run behind it, with unsold goods or shortages in the interval. The schemas assume a choreography and then study its steps. Who or what performs the choreography in a decentralised economy is a question the arithmetic cannot answer, and the later dispute, treated below, is largely an argument about whether the unanswered question voids the whole exercise.

One refinement of the second condition is worth stating because later writers built on it. The accumulation decisions of the two departments are linked asymmetrically. In Marx's example, the first department's capitalists choose their accumulation rate on their own, and the second department's capitalists must then adjust theirs to whatever the balance condition requires, consuming more or less of their surplus as the arithmetic dictates. The consumption-goods sector is thus cast as the follower, its owners' revenue expanding or contracting to fit the machine sector's plans. Several later readers, including the Soviet planner whose model appears in the lineage section below, took the hint and built theories in which the growth of the whole economy is governed from the first department outward. Others objected that the asymmetry is an artefact of the example's construction rather than a law. The schemas themselves merely display the linkage; the article records the dispute without joining it, in keeping with the demonstration not description rule that governs this whole topic.

### Why must the machine-making department move first in the example?

Because accumulation is physically made of its products: no department can install equipment that has not yet been produced, so the surplus of means of production must exist before any department can embody it, and consumption capacity follows the employment that investment creates.

There is also a quieter assumption to surface, the assumption about labour supply. Expanded reproduction requires more workers each year, drawn in the model from the reserve army of the unemployed that the first volume's accumulation chapter generates through mechanisation. The schemas take that reserve as given and do not model its replenishment, a gap Luxemburg and others pressed on. Similarly, the schemas hold the rate of surplus value and the technical proportions within each department fixed across the years they trace, so all growth is extensive, more of the same, rather than driven by changing methods. Marx knew both simplifications perfectly well and flagged the second himself, noting that accumulation in reality proceeds alongside a rising composition of capital, which would complicate the proportions period by period. The fixed-coefficient world of the schemas is a laboratory, and the article's later section on the strongest objection returns to what laboratories can prove.

## The two conditions table

Everything so far can be compressed into a single instrument. The two conditions table sets the two cases side by side, department by department, with the balance condition for each stated in words and the consequences of failure stated beside it. The figures in the third column are Marx's own illustrative thousands, included so that a reader can check the equalities with pencil and paper; they are examples chosen to satisfy the conditions, not measurements of any economy that ever existed. Read across each row: what the department makes, what it must fetch from its partner, the requirement that has to hold, and the specific jam that follows when it does not.

| Case and department | What it produces | What it must obtain from the other | Balance condition in words | What happens when the condition fails |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Simple reproduction, Department I | Means of production worth 6,000 (4,000 constant capital, 1,000 variable capital, 1,000 surplus value) | Consumption goods for its workers and capitalists, paid for from its wages and surplus | Its variable capital plus surplus value (2,000) must equal Department II's constant capital (2,000) | Part of its product goes unsold while consumption goods are bid up beyond their value |
| Simple reproduction, Department II | Consumption goods worth 3,000 (2,000 constant capital, 500 variable capital, 500 surplus value) | Replacement machinery and materials worth its constant capital | Its constant capital (2,000) must be exactly covered by Department I's spending on consumption | Worn equipment goes unreplaced and next year's output shrinks for want of means |
| Expanded reproduction, Department I | Means of production totalling 6,000, composed of 4,000 constant capital, 1,000 variable capital and 1,000 surplus value | Consumption goods for original and additional workers, plus its owners' reduced consumption fund | The same fund, at 2,000, must exceed Department II's constant capital of 1,500 by the accumulation margin | Without the excess there is no material fund for growth, and accumulation stalls at replacement |
| Expanded reproduction, Department II | Consumption goods to the value of 3,000 (1,500 constant capital, 750 variable capital, 750 surplus value) | Additional machinery and materials as its constant capital rises to match Department I's spending (1,600) | It must enlarge its constant capital out of surplus until its demand equals Department I's consumption spending | If it will not or cannot expand, Department I's extra output lacks a buyer and the growth path breaks |

The table is an instrument for readers, and its classroom use is straightforward: change any single figure and trace the damage. Raise the second department's constant capital in the simple case by one hundred and follow the hundred of unsold equipment into the next period's shrunken replacement fund; lower the first department's accumulation in the expanded case and watch the second department's planned expansion lose its market. A few minutes of such perturbation teaches the schemas' central lesson better than pages of commentary, because the lesson is tactile: balance is a razor's edge in the model precisely because the model has stripped away every buffer, buffer stocks, credit, foreign trade, and price flexibility, that in real economies absorbs the shocks the table transmits instantly. Marx's system balances the way a pencil balances on its point, demonstrably possible and practically precarious, and he would not have wanted the demonstration read any other way.

## The demonstration not description rule

The article's governing discipline can now be named, and it should be applied to every later section without exception. The demonstration not description rule holds that the schemas show balanced reproduction to be possible under stated proportions, which is a claim about possibility, and that reading them as a portrait of any actual economy, or as a guarantee of either stability or collapse, mistakes the logical character of the exercise. Marx built a construction, the way a geometer constructs a figure to prove that a configuration can exist. The construction settles the question it addresses, whether the interlocking exchanges of a reproducing capitalism are coherent at all, and it settles no question beyond that one without further argument.

The rule cuts in both directions, which is what makes it a rule rather than a slogan. Against one party of readers, it blocks the inference from the schemas' smooth functioning to the conclusion that real capitalism is self-stabilising: a model that assumes the proportions cannot testify that markets achieve them. Against the opposite party, it blocks the inference from some alleged gap in the arithmetic to the conclusion that capitalism must break down for want of buyers: a model whose exchanges clear by construction cannot be refuted by pointing at the construction and demanding to know who clears them, since the clearing was the hypothesis under examination. Both inferences were in fact drawn, at length and with passion, by writers discussed in the dispute section below, and the rule is the sieve that separates what those writers showed from what they merely asserted.

### What exactly does a consistency demonstration establish?

It establishes that a described arrangement contains no internal contradiction, that the parts can fit as specified; it refutes claims that the arrangement is impossible, and it identifies the exact conditions on which the possibility depends, nothing more and nothing less.

The philosophy of science lurking inside that answer is worth one paragraph, because the schemas are a unusually pure case of a widely used instrument. Existence proofs by construction appear across economics and engineering: a bridge design demonstrates that a span can carry a load by exhibiting a structure that carries it on paper, and the demonstration is valuable even though no paper bridge carries a lorry. Its value lies in relocating the argument. Before the construction, opponents could rest on the claim that the thing could not be done at all; after it, they must argue the narrower and harder case that the conditions it requires are never met in practice. The schemas performed exactly that relocation in the debates of the eighteen nineties. Critics of Marx had argued that capitalist reproduction was arithmetically incoherent, since nobody could say who buys the surplus product; the schemas exhibited the purchases. From then on, the serious argument had to be about whether the required proportions arise spontaneously, a much better argument, and one that continues in the literature on coordination and effective demand. That is the schemas' permanent achievement, and it survives every concession made in the objection section below.

### Why can both sides of a dispute quote the same schemas?

Because a construction underdetermines the conclusions drawn from it: the schemas fix what proportions growth requires but not whether markets find them, so a reader who trusts market adjustment reads harmony into the gap and a reader who distrusts it reads breakdown into the same gap.

Hold that answer in mind as a vaccine against a common rhetorical trick in this literature. When a writer announces that the reproduction schemas prove some large thesis, the correct response is to ask which additional premise, beyond the arithmetic, is carrying the weight. In the harmony readings, the added premise is usually that competition reliably forces capitals into the proportions the schemas require. In the breakdown readings, the added premise is usually that some magnitude, the consumption of the masses or the availability of external markets, is fixed in a way the schemas themselves do not assume. Neither premise is disreputable; both are arguable; but both are additions, and the history of the dispute is the history of those additions being mistaken for deductions. The sections that follow therefore present each reading with its addition made explicit. That is the neutrality this topic demands: not a pretence that the readings are equal in merit, which is a judgement readers can form, but an accuracy about where arithmetic ends and advocacy begins.

## One model, opposite conclusions

The schemas had barely been published before they were conscripted. The second volume appeared in 1885, and within a decade its arithmetic was the chosen weapon of three separate controversies: whether capitalism could develop in Russia at all, whether capitalist production depends on consumption by third parties outside the system, and whether the system's accumulation must eventually break against the limits of its own markets. Each controversy produced a reading of the schemas that claimed the arithmetic as its proof, and the readings contradict one another. That a single construction could arm all sides is not a scandal in the text. It is the demonstration not description rule operating in history, and the controversies reward study precisely because each party's extra premise is so visible once the pattern is pointed out. The full treatment of the underconsumption side of the quarrel belongs to the series article on [overproduction and underconsumption](/2013/11/01/overproduction-and-underconsumption/); this section keeps to the schemas themselves and the hands that wielded them.

The first conscription was Russian. The Narodnik economists argued that capitalism in Russia was doomed to stunted artificiality, because the impoverishment of the peasantry destroyed the home market: if the mass of producers are ruined by capitalist development, who will buy its products? Their leading theorist rested the case on the realisation problem, the supposed impossibility of selling that portion of the product which embodies surplus value within a society of capitalists and workers alone. The young Lenin answered with the schemas directly, in an essay of 1893 on the so-called market question and then in his study of the development of capitalism in Russia. His use of the models was exact and limited: he showed that the schemas exhibit the surplus being realised through the departments' exchanges, with the market for means of production growing faster than the market for consumption goods, and concluded that capitalist development creates its own market as it proceeds, the impoverishment argument resting on a bookkeeping error about where demand originates. Note the discipline of the claim. Lenin did not argue that the schemas prove Russian capitalism would flourish in practice, only that one specific impossibility claim against it collapsed. It is the cleanest early example of a consistency demonstration doing its proper work.

### How did Lenin turn the schemas against the Narodniks?

By exhibiting, in the schemas' own figures, the realisation his opponents declared impossible: Department One's demand for equipment grows independently of final consumption, so a home market for capitalist production arises from production itself rather than from prosperous consumers.

The second conscription ran the argument to the opposite extreme. Mikhail Tugan-Baranovsky, a Ukrainian economist writing on the history of English crises, took the schemas to show that capitalist production is in principle independent of consumption altogether. If the proportions are maintained, he argued, production can expand by producing means of production for the production of further means of production, machines to make machines to make machines, with personal consumption shrinking to a minor appendage of the process; society could in the limit resemble a single vast automatic factory whose output nobody consumes. Crises, on his account, therefore arise not from any insufficiency of consumption but from disproportionality, from investment flooding into one branch while another starves, a conclusion he drew from the English cycle's pattern of boom-time overbuilding in the heavy industries. His reading had real force against crude underconsumptionism, and it permanently installed disproportionality in the theory of crisis. Its extra premise, though, is visible on inspection: that the proportions can be treated as maintainable in abstraction from the consumption that ultimately employs most labour and validates most investment. Readers who find the premise heroic will find the conclusion unwarranted; readers who accept it must still explain why a system indifferent to consumption repeatedly breaks down in company with unsold consumption goods.

### What did Tugan-Baranovsky think the schemas had shown?

That balanced expansion does not require growing final consumption, since demand for means of production can sustain round after round of growth; crises then come from investment being distributed in wrong proportions among branches, not from any failure of consumers to buy.

The third conscription is the most famous, and its full biography belongs to the series profile of [rosa luxemburg intellectual biography](/2014/08/01/rosa-luxemburg-intellectual-biography/); here the argument alone is stated. In The Accumulation of Capital of 1913, Rosa Luxemburg subjected the expanded reproduction schemas to a relentless cross-examination and declared them unable to close. Her question was the realisation question turned up to full volume: granting every proportion, granting the money circuit, who actually buys the growing portion of the product that embodies the surplus destined for accumulation? Workers buy only what their wages cover; capitalists buy consumption goods and equipment, but the equipment purchases presuppose the very expansion whose product is in question; somewhere, she argued, the chain of purchases must rest on buyers outside the closed world of capitalists and workers, on peasants, artisans, and colonial peoples whose non-capitalist milieus capitalism penetrates, markets, and destroys. Imperialism, in her theory, is the competitive struggle among capitalist states for the shrinking remainder of that non-capitalist world, and accumulation's arithmetic is its engine. The schemas, for Luxemburg, thus prove by their failure to close that expanded reproduction requires an external market: the exact contrary of the conclusion Tugan-Baranovsky had drawn from the identical figures.

### Why did Luxemburg judge the expanded schemas incomplete?

Because she could not find, inside a society divided only into capitalists and workers, the purchasers for the accumulating surplus: each buyer's spending seemed to presuppose the sale it was meant to explain, so she located the missing demand in non-capitalist strata and territories.

The replies came swiftly and are part of the record any honest glossary must keep. Otto Bauer, answering in the Austrian party's theoretical journal in the same year, constructed expanded schemas of his own, more elaborate than Marx's, with population growth and rising productivity worked in, and ran them forward over successive years to exhibit accumulation proceeding without any external buyer; the construction was a direct counter-demonstration, and within the terms of a consistency dispute it was the logically correct move. Nikolai Bukharin, writing a full-length critique a decade later, pressed the methodological charge: Luxemburg, he argued, had confused the question of the material form of the surplus product with the question of its money realisation, and had treated as a gap in the circuit what was only the circuit's normal operation viewed with suspicion. Anton Pannekoek and others defended her intuition that the formal closure left the real problem untouched. The dispute was never formally settled, because it is not purely formal: Bauer showed the arithmetic closes, Luxemburg's defenders replied that arithmetic was never the issue, and both claims are true relative to their premises, which is the same-model-opposite-conclusions pattern in its most instructive form.

A coda completes the pattern, and it is the strangest turn of all. Henryk Grossmann, in a large work of 1929 on the accumulation and breakdown law, took Bauer's own expanded reproduction figures, which had been built to refute Luxemburg, and simply extended them further into the future, year upon year, until the arithmetical requirements of accumulation, at the assumed rate of surplus value and pace of mechanisation, demanded more surplus than the system generated, at which point the scheme choked and breakdown ensued by arithmetic necessity. Bauer's shield became Grossmann's sword with no alteration except the length of the run. Nothing in the episode discredits any participant; Bauer showed a path exists for the years he traced, Grossmann showed that one particular path, extended, eventually fails its own conditions, and neither result touches the question of which path, if any, an actual economy follows. Readers who grasp why both constructions are valid learn more about formal models from this quarrel than from any treatise on method.

It remains to record what the dispute was for. None of these writers was playing with toy economies for amusement. The Russian argument concerned the strategy of a revolutionary movement in a peasant empire. Tugan-Baranovsky's harmony of proportions fed a revisionist confidence that capitalism had outgrown its catastrophes. Luxemburg's external market underwrote a theory of imperialism and of capitalism's historical limits, written on the eve of a world war that seemed to many to confirm it. Grossmann's breakdown arithmetic was composed as the world economy slid towards 1929. The schemas mattered because their interpreters believed, each in a different direction, that the fate of a civilisation could be read off a table of figures. The demonstration not description rule is the modern reader's protection against repeating that belief in any direction, while still taking the figures, and the arguers, seriously.

## Disproportionality and the road to crisis theory

Between the dispute's extremes sits the reading that has aged most quietly and perhaps most durably: the schemas as a theory of how crises become possible. On this reading, the models' service is negative and precise. They specify the proportions that smooth reproduction requires, and thereby specify a failure mode that needs no external shock and no insufficiency of aggregate demand: the proportions themselves can be missed, because nothing in a decentralised economy guarantees them in advance. Capital is committed to particular branches, in particular material forms, on the basis of expected prices, and the required proportions are between physical outputs of heterogeneous goods. A boom that overbuilds the equipment industries relative to consumption capacity, or that starves them, stores up a correction in which some capitals cannot sell and others cannot buy, however sound the totals appear. The series' general treatment of [marxist crisis theory guide](/2013/10/01/marxist-crisis-theory-guide/) develops this family of explanations alongside its rivals; the schemas' contribution is to give disproportion an exact address.

The mechanism deserves a concrete pass. Suppose investment enthusiasm concentrates on expanding the first department, encouraged by cheap credit and rising orders for machinery. For a time the expansion validates itself: equipment makers order from equipment makers, employment rises, wage spending lifts the second department too, and every signal confirms the boom. But the new capacity in the first department eventually comes on stream as a flow of finished machines seeking buyers whose own expansion plans were calibrated to the boom's pace, while the second department, having grown more slowly, cannot absorb means of production at the rate the first department can now supply them. The crossing condition fails from the first department's side: part of its product finds no purchaser, prices in the heavy industries sag, orders collapse, and the same interdependence that multiplied the boom multiplies the contraction, as equipment makers cancel the orders that were other equipment makers' markets. Nothing in this story requires consumers to be too poor or capitalists too greedy; it requires only that decentralised investment, committed in lumpy and irreversible forms, can miss proportions that are knowable only after the fact. The schemas contribute the proportions. Experience contributes the missing.

### How can an economy that is able to balance still break down?

Because the balance conditions are proportions among outputs decided in advance by thousands of separate capitals, in material forms that cannot be costlessly reshaped; the conditions describe what must coincide, while nothing in the mechanism forces the coincidence to occur.

It is worth being exact about the standing of this crisis reading among the schemas' uses, because it illustrates the article's rule from a third angle. The disproportionality reading does not claim the schemas prove crises happen; it claims they prove a specific channel exists through which crises can happen, a possibility result about breakdown rather than a possibility result about smooth growth. Both are legitimate constructions from the same instrument, and they are not rivals: the models exhibit the conditions of smooth reproduction and, by exhibiting them, exhibit the space of departures from them. A reader who has internalised this double service has understood the schemas better than most of their famous interpreters, several of whom grasped one face of the instrument and denied the other. The remaining sections of this article turn from what the schemas were made to say to the two questions a glossary must still answer: whether the instrument can bear any of this weight at all, and what became of it in the hands of economists who had never heard of the dispute.

## The strongest objection: a monument to formalism

Fairness to this topic requires stating the hostile case at its full strength, without softening, because the schemas attract a contempt that milder summaries never answer. The objection runs as follows. Here is a model with two sectors, when real economies have thousands; with fixed technical coefficients, when real production substitutes, economises, and innovates continuously; with no money worthy of the name, no credit system, no banking, no speculation, no financial assets whose prices can detach from any underlying flow; with no uncertainty, no expectations, no errors except the ones the examiner inserts by hand; with all exchange at values, bracketing the price formation through which any actual allocation is decided; with labour power available in whatever quantity the arithmetic requires; and with a single annual rhythm in which every capital conveniently turns over together. Such a model, the objection concludes, can establish nothing whatsoever about an actual capitalist economy, and the century of controversy conducted over its figures, the reputations built and broken on its interpretation, the revolutionary strategies hung upon its equalities, amount to a monument to formalism: the scholastic veneration of an accounting frame mistaken for a theory of the world. On this view, the schemas belong to the history of doctrine the way phlogiston belongs to chemistry, as an exhibit of ingenuity wasted inside a dead end.

The objection's factual premises are nearly all correct, and the article concedes them in a block rather than piecemeal. The coefficients are fixed. Money is a veil in the models except where Marx worries at its edges. Credit, the actual nervous system of capitalist investment, is absent by design, since the second volume abstracts from it throughout. Expectations play no role; no capitalist in the schemas ever misjudges anything, because misjudgement is not representable in the frame. The labour supply is elastic at exactly the points where reality is stiffest. Any reader who finishes the schemas believing they have seen an economy has been misled, and any teacher who presents them as one commits the descriptive fallacy this article's rule exists to prevent. The concession is total as far as description goes. The dispute is about what follows from it.

What follows, the answer runs, is far less than the objection claims, because it misclassifies the instrument. A consistency demonstration is not a failed description; it is an argument of a different logical type, aimed at a different target. Its target is an impossibility claim. When critics asserted that the surplus product could not possibly be sold within a capitalist society, or that accumulation was arithmetically self-contradictory, those assertions were universal and modal: they said no arrangement could work. A single working arrangement, however simplified, destroys a universal negative of that kind, the way one black swan destroys a law about swans. The schemas destroyed several such laws, and the destruction redirected the entire debate onto empirical and institutional ground: not whether reproduction can cohere, but whether the institutions of credit, competition, and state policy make it cohere or fail to. That redirection is a genuine intellectual event, and it is irreversible; nobody can now responsibly argue the old impossibilities, and the credit belongs to the toy model. Toys that eliminate false necessities are not monuments to formalism. They are how fields clear their ground.

### What is a consistency proof worth against the formalism charge?

Exactly what it costs the impossibility claims it kills: it cannot describe, predict, or stabilise anything, but it removes counterfeit necessities from the argument, and it specifies the conditions whose real-world satisfaction becomes the proper subject of inquiry.

The honest answer must also mark where the defence stops, because it does stop, well short of the uses the famous interpreters made. The schemas cannot bear the weight of the substantive conclusions drawn from them by any side. They do not show that capitalism is stable, since they assume the proportions stability requires. They do not show it must collapse, since the collapses derived from them always smuggle in a premise, a fixed rate of accumulation, a closed world market, a frozen technique, that the schemas do not contain. They do not even show that disproportionality causes actual crises, only that it is a coherent candidate mechanism whose presence in any given crisis is an empirical question. Henryk Grossmann's breakdown, Luxemburg's external market, Tugan-Baranovsky's self-sustaining machine production: each is an argument built on the schemas plus something else, and the something else is where each stands or falls. A century of argument over the models is therefore not a monument to formalism, but it is a monument to the difficulty of remembering what a model is. The schemas themselves, read with the rule in hand, remain the cleanest classroom exhibit in economics of the difference between showing that something can happen and showing that it will.

## The ancestor in the text: Quesnay's table and Smith's vanishing capital

Marx did not present the schemas as a creation from nothing, and the critical chapter he placed before them names their ancestor with unusual warmth. In 1758 Francois Quesnay, physician at the court of Louis XV and founder of the physiocratic school, published his Tableau economique, a zigzag diagram tracing how the annual product of a nation circulates among three classes: the productive class of farmers, the proprietary class of landlords, and the sterile class of artisans and merchants. The Tableau was the first attempt in European thought to depict the economy as a reproductive circuit, a flow that must return to its starting point replenished if the nation is to stand still, let alone grow. Marx, who despised much of the physiocratic doctrine built on the diagram, called the attempt itself an extremely brilliant conception, and his own third part is in one sense the Tableau rebuilt: the classes replaced by departments defined by the destination of their product, the zigzag replaced by exact exchange conditions, and the agricultural monopoly on productivity discarded.

### Why did Marx praise a royal physician's diagram so highly?

Because Quesnay had seen, a century before anyone else, that an economy is a circuit of reproduction whose flows must return in the right material shapes, and had tried to draw the whole circuit at once; Marx's schemas take over the ambition and correct the anatomy.

The contrast case in the critical chapter is Adam Smith, and Marx's complaint against him is the complaint the schemas exist to answer. Smith taught that the price of every commodity resolves entirely into wages, profit, and rent, the three revenues. Elegant as the doctrine sounds, it dissolves the constant capital: the value of the raw materials and worn machinery embodied in the product disappears from the account, as if society could consume its seed corn and its looms along with its bread. Marx calls this Smith's dogma, and shows at wearisome length that it cannot be true, since a nation's product must replace its used-up means of production in kind before any revenue can be consumed at all; a society that ate its replacement fund would not reproduce, whatever its revenues. The dogma's persistence matters because it is the precise error that makes reproduction invisible. If the whole product is revenue, there is no inter-departmental problem, no proportion to maintain, nothing for a schema to state. Restore the constant capital, and the problem springs into view at once, which is why Marx's polemic occupies so much space: he is clearing the ground of a conceptual habit that had hidden his subject for a hundred years.

The historical excursion is not antiquarianism. It fixes the schemas' intellectual coordinates between two failures of vision: the physiocrats, who saw the circuit but misread its anatomy, and the classical school after Smith, which perfected the anatomy of the individual commodity while losing the circuit entirely. Later economists would repeat Smith's dogma in modern dress whenever aggregate income accounting was allowed to eclipse the material composition of output, and would rediscover the circuit whenever war, blockade, or planning forced the material question back onto the table. The schemas' author had positioned his construction to be rediscovered in exactly those moments, a fate the lineage section below documents. For the glossary reader, the critical chapter's practical yield is a test to apply to any economic aggregate: ask what portion of it replaces used-up means of production, and distrust any framework in which the answer cannot be stated.

## The exchanges in slow motion

The balance condition is a single equality, but the traffic that satisfies it is a choreography of distinct exchanges, and walking through them slowly dispels the suspicion that the equality is a conjuring trick. Take the simple case with Marx's figures. Three great exchanges complete the circuit. First, inside Department Two, one thousand of consumption goods never leaves the department at all: its workers spend five hundred of wages on its products, and its capitalists spend five hundred of surplus on its products, and the money laid out returns to the department's sellers as it circulates. Second, inside Department One, four thousand of means of production changes hands among the department's own firms, as machine builders buy steel, steelmakers buy coal, and collieries buy timber and pumps, each replacing the constant capital it consumed; this internal traffic is the largest single flow in the economy and the one most easily forgotten, since it appears in no consumption statistic. Third, across the boundary, the two thousand exchange: Department Two's capitalists, who must replace two thousand of used-up means of production, buy that value from Department One, and the money they pay becomes the fund from which Department One's workers draw their wages in the next round and its capitalists their consumption, spent in turn on Department Two's goods. When the third exchange has run its course, each department holds the material shape it needs to restart: equipment and materials in place, workers fed and rehired, owners supplied.

The money movements inside that choreography run in counterpoint to the goods, and Marx tracks them with a bookkeeper's patience because the counterpoint answers the question of who finances the circuit. Wages are advanced by capitalists before the product exists; the money returns when workers buy consumption goods, largely from the other department's sellers, who use it to buy equipment, which returns it to the first department's sellers, whose wage advances begin the round again. No new money is required for the circuit to repeat at the same scale, beyond the hoards that individual capitals hold against the mismatch between their selling and buying dates. The circuit metaphor is exact: the same money performs every exchange in turn, and its velocity, not its quantity, is what the scale of reproduction demands. Readers troubled by the apparent magic of the surplus being bought should locate the answer here: the money that buys the surplus portion of the product is advanced by the capitalist class in the course of the circuit itself, as wages, as equipment purchases, as revenue spending, and it returns to the class through the same circuit, which is why the question who buys the surplus has a bookkeeping answer even for readers who remain unsatisfied by it at the level of economic substance.

The subdivision of the second department adds a fourth, smaller choreography that the main account keeps folded. Necessities and luxuries exchange with each other under their own constraint: the workers of the luxury branch earn wages they cannot spend on luxuries, since wages buy necessities, so the luxury branch's wage bill must be matched by a flow of necessities from the other branch, paid for out of the luxury branch's takings from capitalist spending. The capitalists of the necessities branch, in return, spend part of their surplus on luxuries, and the two flows must coincide. Marx works the case because it demonstrates that the balance problem is fractal: every subdivision of production reproduces the same formal requirement at a smaller scale, and an economy can satisfy the grand proportion between the departments while violating the proportion within the second, choking on caviar while bread runs short. The lesson for readers of modern input-output tables, which subdivide the economy into hundreds of branches, is direct: the schemas' two departments are a microscope setting, not a claim about how many sectors an economy has, and the formal problem is identical at every magnification.

## Fixed capital: the difficulty Marx left in the open

Among the schemas' moving parts, one component refuses to move smoothly, and Marx's handling of it is the best internal evidence that the models are honest constructions rather than rigged demonstrations. Fixed capital, machinery, buildings, and plant, transfers its value to the product gradually, a fraction each year across its working life, but is replaced in kind all at once, at death. In any single year, therefore, one group of capitalists is accumulating a money depreciation fund, selling product that embodies worn-out value without yet buying any replacement equipment, while another group, whose machines have just expired, is spending an accumulated fund on replacement in bulk. For simple reproduction to proceed, the year's total of depreciation charges across the economy must exactly equal the year's total spending on replacement in kind. There is no mechanism in the model that enforces the coincidence; it must be assumed, and Marx states plainly that this requirement, introduced into a system of production driven by no plan, is a standing source of disturbance, one of the most potent potentialities of crisis his analysis uncovers.

### What problem in the schemas did Marx leave visibly unsolved?

The replacement of fixed capital: value is recovered gradually through depreciation charges while machines are replaced in a lump, so smooth reproduction needs the year's depreciation fund to match the year's replacement spending, a coincidence the model must assume rather than produce.

Sit with that admission for a moment, because it dispatches two lazy verdicts at once. Against the verdict that the schemas are apologetics for capitalist harmony, the fixed capital passage shows their author deliberately exhibiting a point at which his own construction requires luck. Against the verdict that they are a breakdown proof, the same passage shows him treating the difficulty as a potentiality of crisis, one possibility among others, rather than as a theorem of necessary collapse. Later writers in several camps have returned to the depreciation problem, some building crisis theories on the bunching of replacement investment, others dissolving it with credit, which lets a firm replace ahead of its fund or lend its fund to firms whose machines have expired, at the price of tying the circuit to the health of the banking system. The model's silence about credit, noted in the objection section, matters most exactly here. Marx left the seam visible, and the seam has been a productive site of argument ever since, which is what honest models do: they tell you where they would tear.

## Successive years: watching the expanded path unfold

A single year of expanded reproduction shows a step; the construction's character appears only when the steps are chained. Follow Marx's figures forward. The economy opens in the first year with the first department at six thousand of product and the second at three thousand, accumulates as the earlier section described, and opens its second year enlarged: the first department now works with forty-four hundred of constant capital and eleven hundred of variable, the second with sixteen hundred of constant and eight hundred of variable. If both sets of capitalists repeat their behaviour, the first department's owners again capitalising a similar share of a larger surplus, the machine runs forward, each year's product exceeding its predecessor's, and the proportions between the departments shift as it goes, the first department's share of the total rising during the phase in which accumulation is weighted towards equipment and settling as the second department's induced expansion catches up. The path is not a smooth proportional swelling of the whole economy. It is a sequence of corrections, in which the leading department creates the demand that pulls the follower into line period by period.

Two properties of the path deserve emphasis, because later builders of growth models prized exactly them. The first is that growth is not uniform across the economy even when everything goes right: the department producing means of production must, during any acceleration, grow faster than the department producing consumption goods, since it supplies both departments' additional equipment as well as its own. Any plan or market process that tried to expand all branches at one rate would violate the crossing condition during the transition. The second is that the path is path-dependent in a strict sense: each year's proportions are inherited from the preceding year's decisions, and a starting imbalance cannot be wished away, only worked through, since the capital stock that exists is the capital stock that must be employed or scrapped. Marx's second numerical illustration of expanded reproduction, which begins from different proportions than the first, exists precisely to make this point: he varies the starting position to show the mechanism adjusting, the second department compelled at first to accumulate at a rate far above the first's until the proportions between them reach the relation the ongoing accumulation requires.

The money side of accumulation adds a wrinkle that the figures conceal and the text confronts. Before capitalists can invest an enlarged sum, someone must have accumulated idle money: investment is lumpy, a new plant costs a fortune entire, and the surplus arrives in a trickle. In the interval, the accumulating capitalist withdraws money from circulation by hoarding rather than spending, which looks, from the standpoint of sellers, like a failure of demand, and the model must explain why the withdrawal does not jam the exchanges of the current year. Marx's answer distributes the process across the class: at any moment some capitals are hoarding towards an investment while others, their funds complete, are disbursing, and the gold producer's new output tops up the money stock, so that hoarding and dishoarding offset one another closely enough for the circuit to run, though the offset is another of the model's assumed coincidences rather than a guaranteed one. Observe the pattern repeating: at every joint, the construction exhibits what must coincide and declines to promise that it will. That is the schemas' honesty, and their permanent invitation to theories of money and credit to continue the work at exactly the joints the arithmetic leaves exposed.

Readers of the later growth literature will recognise the skeleton under the skin. The requirement that the capital-goods sector outgrow the consumption-goods sector during acceleration, the dependence of the whole path on the share of investment allocated to expanding the machine base, the very idea that an economy's growth rate is set inside its structure of production rather than by the volume of saving alone: these are the working parts of the two-sector growth models that appear in the lineage section, and they are all present in embryo in Marx's chained arithmetical years. The embryo is not the adult, and the article's rule forbids pretending otherwise, but the genealogy is written in the figures themselves for anyone who runs them forward.

## The assumptions under the microscope

A glossary owes its readers an inventory of what the models leave out, since the exclusions define the territory on which the schemas' conclusions hold. There are six principal simplifications, each deliberate, each removable at a price. First, society consists of capitalists and wage workers only: no peasants, no independent artisans, no state, no landlords drawing rent as a separate flow. Second, all exchange takes place at values, with the transformation of values into prices of production postponed, so the schemas track value magnitudes and material shapes while bracketing the price system that any real allocation must pass through. Third, foreign trade is excluded; the economy is a closed world, which is precisely the feature Luxemburg attacked and precisely the feature that makes the closure question well-posed. Fourth, money is metallic and its value constant, and credit is absent, so no purchase can anticipate a fund not yet accumulated except through the hoards the text describes. Fifth, technique is frozen within each period: the proportions between constant and variable capital in each department are given, and accumulation adds more of the existing methods rather than different ones. Sixth, each capital turns over once in the year, so the annual product and the annual flow of exchanges can be treated as one movement.

### Why do the schemas shut out foreign trade?

To pose the hardest version of the question. If reproduction can be shown to close in a world with no outside, then external markets are a convenience rather than a necessity of the system; admitting trade from the start would let the model evade the very difficulty it was built to test.

Each simplification has a known price, and stating the prices converts the objection section's blanket charge into a set of answerable questions. Admit foreign trade and the proportions become conditions on a trading system rather than a single economy, with deficits doing the adjusting the departments cannot. Admit credit and the timing coincidences the model must assume, the matching of hoards, of depreciation funds, of anticipatory expansion in the second department, can be financed into existence, at the cost of adding bank solvency to the list of things that can fail. Let technique change and the proportions must be recomputed each period, with mechanisation shifting demand towards the first department in a pattern Marx sketches elsewhere but does not build into the schemas. Let prices diverge from values and a second, monetary set of proportions must hold alongside the material set, doubling the conditions of balance. The schemas survive each complication as a limiting case, the way frictionless motion survives in mechanics: not as the world, but as the reference configuration from which the world's departures are measured. Whether that status suffices is the judgement the reader has been equipped to make; the article's duty is to ensure the judgement is made knowingly.

## From the schemas to input-output analysis and growth theory

The most consequential fact about the reproduction schemas is one their disputants mostly ignored: the construction escaped. While Marxist economists argued over what the models proved about capitalism's fate, a parallel line of economists took the models' form, the economy as a table of interlocking sectoral flows whose proportions must balance, and built from it some of the twentieth century's standard instruments. The lineage is documented, not conjectured. The originator of input-output analysis engaged with Marx's work directly, the multisectoral growth theorists worked in the same formal idiom, and one two-sector growth model drawn from the schemas' logic was installed at the heart of a great state's industrialisation plans. Stating the lineage factually, without any implication that it validates the rest of Marxist economics, is a neutrality requirement of this article: descent is a historical relation, not a certificate of truth, and plenty of fruitful techniques have disreputable or contested ancestors.

Wassily Leontief, born in Russia and educated in Petrograd and Berlin, published as his doctoral work in 1928 an essay on the economy as a circular process, in which the line of descent from Quesnay's Tableau through Marx's reproduction schemas to a modern statistical system of inter-industry flows is drawn explicitly. Leontief's move was to take the schemas' bookkeeping ambition literally and empirically: where Marx divided production into two departments and asserted their exchanges, Leontief proposed to measure the actual exchanges among dozens of industries, recording how much steel the vehicle industry buys, how much coal the steel industry buys, and so on through the whole table, until the economy appears as a grid of deliveries from every sector to every other. Emigrating to the United States, he constructed the first input-output table for the American economy from the statistics of 1919 and 1929, published in 1936, and followed it with the full apparatus of the method: technical coefficients stating each industry's recipe of inputs, and a matrix inversion that computes the total output, direct and indirect, required to deliver any specified bill of final goods. The inversion answers, for a many-sector economy, the very question the schemas posed for two sectors: what must every part produce so that all parts can be supplied?

### Did Marx invent input-output analysis?

No. He built its recognised ancestor: a two-department accounting of inter-sectoral flows with balance conditions. Leontief's invention was to make the accounting empirical and many-sectored, estimating the coefficients from statistics and solving the system for an actual economy's requirements.

The distinction in that answer matters to this article's standards, because the lineage claim is often inflated in the retelling. Marx did not estimate coefficients, did not invert matrices, did not imagine the statistical offices that would compile the tables, and would not have recognised the use to which the tables are chiefly put, which is national accounting and policy simulation rather than the critique of political economy. What he supplied was the form of the question and its first rigorous solution in miniature, the demonstration that an economy's consistency can be stated as a set of proportion conditions among sectors. Leontief himself acknowledged the ancestry in print, and historians of economics treat the descent as settled. Input-output tables are compiled as part of the national accounts of a great many countries, the American benchmark tables by the Bureau of Economic Analysis among them, and supply-and-use tables in the same spirit are standard equipment in the statistical systems coordinated internationally. The schemas' great-grandchildren are filing cabinets of official numbers, a destiny none of the disputants foresaw.

Growth theory took the other branch of the inheritance. In 1937 John von Neumann presented a model of an expanding economy in which all goods are produced by linear processes, all income is reinvested, and the economy grows at a uniform maximal rate set by its technology, with the rate of interest equal to the rate of growth; the model is a many-sector generalisation of the expanded reproduction idea, growth as a balanced proportion path, and its proof technique founded a literature. Earlier and more directly descended, the Soviet economist Grigori Feldman, working for the state planning commission in 1928, built a two-sector growth model on foundations he drew from the second volume: one sector producing capital goods, one producing consumption goods, with the economy's long-run growth rate governed by the share of investment directed to the capital-goods sector, since that sector's capacity limits all future investment. Feldman's model told planners that shifting investment towards heavy industry lowers consumption in the short run and raises the growth of everything thereafter, the exact trade-off the schemas' successive years display, and it was later acknowledged in the Western literature, notably by Evsey Domar, as an anticipation of postwar growth theory. In the nineteen fifties the Indian statistician Prasanta Mahalanobis built a Feldman-type two-sector model into the frame of India's Second Five Year Plan, carrying the schemas' growth logic into the development planning of a very different economy. The line from the second volume's arithmetic to the planning commissions is one of the strangest and best documented in the history of economic thought.

### How did the schemas' logic reach the planning commissions?

Through Feldman's two-sector model of 1928, which took from the schemas the insight that growth is governed by the allocation of investment to the capital-goods sector, and through its later reuse, in Mahalanobis's version, as the frame of a national five-year plan.

A reader should notice what survived the journey and what was dropped at the border. What survived is the form: sectors, proportions, the capital-goods sector as the governor of growth, consistency as a system of simultaneous requirements. What was dropped is everything that made the schemas Marxist in content: the analysis of value into constant and variable portions, surplus value as the engine and stake of the process, the class structure of the circuit, the whole critical intent. The statistical offices that compile input-output tables are not thereby practising Marxist economics, and the planning models' users included governments of every description. The form travelled because the form is a solution to a problem every complex economy poses to anyone who would describe or steer it, and forms are portable in a way doctrines are not. This is also why the lineage, though genuine, cannot be used as an argument for the truth of the doctrine it escaped from: techniques are vindicated by their uses, doctrines by their claims, and confusing the two ledgers is a version of the descriptive fallacy this article has resisted throughout.

## The schemas in the English-speaking century

The schemas reached English readers late, and their route explains some peculiarities of the Anglophone debate. The second volume circulated in English from early in the century, but the reproduction chapters were long treated as a quarry for specialists, and the continental dispute was known mostly at second hand. The figure who carried the schemas into the centre of English-language Marxist economics was Paul Sweezy, whose Theory of Capitalist Development of 1942 presented the models lucidly, situated them among the crisis theories, and effectively set the syllabus from which two generations of Anglophone students learned them; his presentation, sympathetic to the disproportionality reading and sceptical of Luxemburg's, shaped the default interpretation in English for decades. An English translation of Luxemburg's Accumulation of Capital finally appeared in 1951, introduced by Joan Robinson, who praised the book's intuition while faulting its arithmetic, a double verdict that modelled for English readers how to honour the question and dispute the answer. Michal Kalecki, working in Warsaw and then in exile, had meanwhile rebuilt a theory of the business cycle on departmental foundations of his own, dividing the economy among capital-goods, consumption-goods, and luxury production, and deriving fluctuations from the time structure of investment decisions: a development of the schema-form by a thinker who reached several Keynesian results by an independent road.

The academic mainstream absorbed the form while forgetting the source, as the lineage section describes, and the schemas settled into their double afterlife: as a set text in the Marxist curriculum, examined and glossed, and as an unmarked stratum inside the standard toolkit of economists who could not have named them. For the purposes of a glossary, the interesting consequence is pedagogical. A student who meets input-output tables in a national accounting course and the reproduction schemas in a history of thought seminar is meeting the same intellectual object at two magnifications, and the curriculum rarely tells them so. This article's insistence on the lineage is meant as a repair of that silence, and as a standing invitation to check any claim that a technique sprang from nowhere: in economics, techniques have pedigrees, and the pedigrees cross party lines with cheerful indifference to the doctrines of their breeders.

## One capital or many: the standpoint of the aggregate

The third part's founding gesture, treating the sum of all capitals as a single interlocking whole, looks innocent and is anything but. Individual capitals experience the economy as an environment: prices given, markets external, other firms' decisions as weather. The aggregate standpoint abolishes the environment, since there is nothing outside the whole for it to trade with, and forces every sale to appear simultaneously as a purchase and every revenue as somebody's cost. Many errors in political economy, on Marx's diagnosis, are standpoint errors: propositions true of one capital that fail for the aggregate, or true of the aggregate and meaningless for one capital. An individual capitalist can enrich himself by paying wages below value or by selling above it; the capitalist class cannot, since what one gains in such exchanges another loses. An individual can convert his whole capital into money and exit; the aggregate cannot sell itself to itself. The schemas police this boundary relentlessly, and much of their reputation for austerity comes from the policing.

The aggregate standpoint also changes what counts as an explanation. From the standpoint of one capital, demand is a fact of nature to be discovered by market research. From the aggregate standpoint, demand is generated inside the model: the demand for consumption goods is the wage bill plus capitalist revenue spending, the demand for equipment is the replacement bill plus accumulation, and each of those magnitudes is determined by the capitals' own advances and decisions in the previous round. This internal generation of demand is the deep reason the schemas could answer the realisation question in the form they did, and also the deep reason their answer satisfies nobody completely. The model shows demand arising from the circuit's own motions, but the motions are assumed to proceed at the assumed scale; a general failure of confidence, a collective decision to hoard rather than invest, is not representable in a frame where accumulation rates are parameters set by the examiner. Later theories of effective demand would make exactly that collective hesitation their central object, and would do so, as the Kalecki section below records, partly with tools inherited from the departmental frame.

There is a reciprocal error worth naming, since aggregate reasoning seduces its users as reliably as individual reasoning seduced Smith. What holds for the aggregate does not automatically guide the parts: the balance condition states what the totals must be, and says nothing about which firm should expand or contract to bring them about, nor about the price movements and bankruptcies through which an unplanned economy gropes towards them. Readers who slide from the schemas' totals to prescriptions for individual capitals, or to confidence that the totals enforce themselves, commit the mirror image of the fallacy the schemas were built to correct. The construction disciplines both directions of slide, which is a large part of its continuing value as a teaching instrument: it is a machine for detecting standpoint errors, and its arithmetic is almost incidental to that function.

## Is this just accounting?

A sceptical reader, having followed the construction this far, is entitled to put the deflationary question directly: is any of this more than bookkeeping? The equalities hold because the numbers were chosen to satisfy them; the categories are definitions; the conclusions appear to be unpacked from the premises without residue. Where bookkeeping ends and theory begins is a fair question for every formal construction in economics, and the schemas draw it in an especially sharp form, since their author presents them with so little rhetorical dressing. The answer worth defending has three parts, and each part identifies something the accounting frame does that mere bookkeeping cannot.

First, the frame itself was a discovery. That the annual product must be decomposed so that replacement of used-up means of production appears as a distinct flow, neither revenue nor profit, is not a definition but a correction of the reigning doctrine, as the discussion of Smith's dogma showed. Bookkeeping conventions are theory-laden choices: what a system of accounts refuses to record, its users will reliably forget, and the classical accounts forgot the largest single flow in the economy, the replacement traffic inside the first department. The schemas' first theoretical act was to build accounts in which that flow could not be made to vanish, and the modern national accounts, with their intermediate consumption and capital consumption allowances, ratify the choice every time they are compiled.

Second, the frame generates impossibility results, which bookkeeping alone cannot do. Once the accounts are built, certain combinations are arithmetically excluded: no distribution of a given product can simultaneously replace capital, feed the workforce at the assumed standard, and fund an accumulation larger than the material surplus of equipment allows. The exclusion of Luxemburg's reading, or of its rivals, is arguable, as the dispute section showed, but the excluded region itself is not: the schemas mark out which claims about reproduction contradict arithmetic, and every party to the dispute was forced to argue inside the marked territory. A consistency frame that disciplines a century of controversy is doing theoretical work by any reasonable standard.

Third, the frame isolates variables. By exhibiting reproduction with credit, technique change, and foreign trade switched off, the schemas let a reader see exactly which phenomena each of those factors is responsible for when it is switched back on: credit's role appears as the financing of the coincidences, technique change as the drift of the proportions, trade as the export of imbalance. This is the laboratory function again, and it is the opposite of scholasticism: the point of the stripped model is to make the unstripped world legible piece by piece. The sceptic's question therefore receives a layered answer. Yes, the schemas are accounting, in the sense that their propositions are consistency relations among defined magnitudes. No, they are not just accounting, because the definitions corrected a blinding error, because the relations exclude real claims, and because the simplifications map the joints at which richer theories must attach. A glossary that failed to make this defence would leave its readers armed with the vocabulary and disarmed against the first clever dismissal they meet.

## Terminology: schemas, schemes, and the word reproduction

A precision glossary should pause over its own title words, since the English vocabulary of this topic is a sediment of translation choices. Marx's German term is Reproduktionsschemata, and English editions have rendered it variously as reproduction schemas and reproduction schemes; the scholarly literature has settled mostly on schemas, partly under the influence of the word's use for formal constructions elsewhere, and this article follows that settlement. The choice is not inert. Scheme in ordinary English suggests a plan, even a plot, and has misled generations of students into expecting a policy proposal, whereas schema suggests a diagram or formal pattern, which is the truth of the matter: the models are patterns of circulation exhibited in tables of figures. German compounds in the surrounding vocabulary reward the same care. The erweiterte Reproduktion is reproduction on an expanded scale, the einfache Reproduktion reproduction on the existing scale, and the departments are Abteilungen, a word whose bureaucratic plainness, divisions or sections, captures the classification's administrative character better than the grandeur of sector.

Reproduction itself is the term carrying the most history. Quesnay's generation used it for the annual renewal of the nation's wealth, and the word's biological resonance, the organism replacing its tissues, was already present in the physiocratic imagination of the economy as a body with a circulation, a metaphor Quesnay the physician did nothing to discourage. Marx takes the word over and hardens it: in his usage, reproduction means that production is simultaneously the renewal of its own material conditions and of the social relations embodied in them, so that a capitalist economy, merely by continuing, reproduces capital on one side and wage labour on the other, the worker emerging from each cycle as propertyless as he entered it and the owner as owner. That second sense, the reproduction of relations, is mostly developed outside the schemas' chapters, and a glossary should flag it to prevent a confusion common in the secondary literature, where the schemas are sometimes credited with proving claims about class reproduction that their arithmetic does not touch. Inside the schemas, reproduction is the narrower, material business: the replacement of used-up inputs and the renewal of the product in the right shapes. Keeping the two senses apart is small work, and it prevents large misunderstandings.

Two further terms need pinning, because the literature leans on them. Realisation means the sale of the produced commodities at their values, the conversion of the product back into money, and the realisation problem is the question whether that conversion can be completed for the whole product within the system. Disproportionality, sometimes rendered as disproportion, names the failure of sectoral outputs to stand in the proportions reproduction requires, and it should not be confused with overproduction simpliciter: an economy can overproduce in one department while underproducing in another, and the schemas' teaching is that the pair of failures is a single event seen from two sides. With these terms fixed, the reader is equipped for the disputes in the primary literature, where half the quarrels are sustained by sliding senses of exactly these words.

## Perturb the numbers: two worked disruptions

Understanding in this subject is best verified by breaking the machine twice, deliberately, and watching where the fracture runs. Take first the simple reproduction figures and suppose that, in a single year, the second department's technique changes so that its constant capital rises from two thousand to twenty-two hundred, more materials being consumed per unit of consumption goods, while nothing else alters. The second department now needs twenty-two hundred of means of production, but the first department's spending fund for consumption goods remains two thousand, fixed by its wages and surplus. The exchange across the boundary cannot clear at the old values: two hundred of the second department's requirement goes unfinanced, or, what amounts to the same disequilibrium in monetary dress, the second department's capitalists bid for equipment with money they do not have while two hundred of consumption goods corresponding to the first department's unspent expectations go unsold. The following year opens damaged: the second department, under-replaced, produces less; its reduced output then fails the first department's workers, whose wages chase a shrunken stock of goods. A purely internal technical change in one branch has propagated through the crossing condition into a general contraction, without any failure of total demand in the aggregate sense, since the totals of intended spending are unchanged. What changed is the shape of demand, and the schemas exist to make that distinction impossible to miss.

The second disruption attacks the expanded case. Begin from Marx's expanded figures and suppose the first department's capitalists, in a burst of confidence, capitalise not half but three quarters of their surplus, enlarging their demand for the second department's replacement expansion beyond what the second department's surplus can finance in one step. The crossing condition now requires the second department to raise its constant capital faster than its own accumulation fund permits; it cannot comply in the period given, and part of the first department's enlarged output, built for a partner who cannot yet absorb it, goes unsold. The surplus machines exist physically, which is the bitter part: the material precondition of growth was produced, and the growth still fails, because the complementary condition on the other side of the boundary was a financing and timing condition, not a material one. Notice what each disruption teaches. The first shows balance failing through composition while aggregates hold. The second shows accumulation failing through asynchrony while every physical magnitude is present. A reader who can run both perturbations unaided has the schemas; a reader who cannot has only read about them, and the difference is exactly the difference the classroom use of the two conditions table is designed to close.

Credit enters both stories as the obvious solvent, and its disciplined admission shows the framework's extensibility rather than its weakness. In the first disruption, bank lending to the second department's capitalists could finance the extra two hundred of equipment, converting an immediate jam into a debt to be serviced from later revenues; in the second, credit could bridge the second department's lag, letting it install capacity ahead of its accumulated fund. The schemas do not forbid these rescues; they abstract from them, and the abstraction is what lets a reader see what credit is for in a reproducing economy, namely the socialisation of the coincidences, the pooling of one capital's idle fund against another's premature need. The price of the rescue is equally visible from inside the frame: every bridged coincidence becomes a claim that a later period must honour, and the failure modes migrate from unsold goods to bad debts. Marx's successors in the theory of finance would develop that migration into a crisis theory of its own. The schemas neither contain it nor exclude it; they mark the ground on which it must be built.

## The labour the schemas borrow

Every term in the schemas is supplied elastically except one that the text quietly assumes, and a careful glossary should expose the assumption: labour power. Expanded reproduction requires additional workers each period, in step with the additional variable capital, and simple reproduction requires the existing workforce to reappear at its posts, fed, rested, and reproduced in the demographic sense across generations. The schemas do not model the production of labour power, which takes place in households outside the circuit of capitals; they borrow the workforce from the first volume's account, where accumulation itself, by mechanising production, continually sets free a relative surplus population, a reserve army whose existence holds wages in check and stands ready to staff new investment. Within the second volume the reserve is an offstage guarantee, and several critics have treated the guarantee as a cheat, since the smoothness of the growth path depends on hands being available exactly when the figures call for them.

The criticism deserves a measured reply rather than dismissal. The reserve army mechanism is not a blank cheque drawn on nowhere; it is argued in the first volume from the same accumulation process the schemas trace, with the rising composition of capital expelling workers from mechanising branches even as accumulation absorbs them elsewhere, so that the supply of labour and the demand for it are twin products of one movement. Whether the two products arrive in matching quantities is, once again, a coincidence the combined construction requires rather than proves, and the honest summary is that the schemas borrow a labour supply whose own theory exhibits the same structure as the schemas themselves: a possibility demonstrated, a coincidence assumed. Luxemburg's critique drew strength from pressing on the human material in this way, asking not only who buys the surplus but who staffs the ever-widening circuit, and the demographic premise of endless proletarianisation sat inside her larger claim that the system requires a non-capitalist outside to feed on, in labour as in markets. Presenting her reading fairly means acknowledging that its grip on the labour question was as serious as its grip on the market question, even for readers who judge Bauer's constructions a sufficient reply on both.

Wages raise a parallel point about the value of labour power, which the schemas take as given within each period. If the consumption standard of the working class rises, the second department's output must be recomposed towards more and better necessities, and variable capital grows relative to the product, squeezing the surplus from which accumulation is funded; if it falls, the reverse. The schemas can register such changes only as new starting figures, not as processes, since the wage is a parameter in each year's construction. Nothing follows from this limitation against the schemas' logic, but a reader should know that the models are silent on the distributive struggle that sets their own parameters. The silence is principled in one respect: Marx's procedure throughout the second volume is to take the results of the first volume's analysis as inputs and to study circulation's conditions on that basis. A complete theory would close the loop. The schemas are a component of one, and the demonstration not description rule has a cousin here: component, not whole.

## Kalecki: the departmental road to effective demand

The most creative continuation of the schemas inside economics came from Michal Kalecki, the Polish economist who in the early nineteen thirties constructed a theory of the business cycle and of effective demand independently of, and in some respects ahead of, the Keynesian revolution in Britain. Kalecki's point of departure was the reproduction framework, which he recast with three departments, capital goods, consumption goods for capitalists, and consumption goods for workers, and then set in motion with a single dynamic principle: capitalists' spending decisions, above all investment, are taken in advance of the profits they generate, and profits in any period are determined by the spending capitalists have already committed. His famous summary of the result reverses the lay intuition about saving and earning: workers spend what they earn, while capitalists earn what they spend. The departmental skeleton is the schemas', the circulation of the product among sectors and classes under balance conditions, but the physiology is new, because the driving variable is no longer the proportions alone but the time-lagged decisions that push the economy from one set of proportions to another.

Kalecki's development matters for this glossary for two reasons. It demonstrates that the schemas' frame could generate a living research programme rather than only exegesis, answering the formalism charge in the most convincing way, by fertility. And it shows the direction in which the frame had to be altered to handle the phenomenon its inventor had bracketed: disequilibrium as the normal state. In Kalecki's cycle, investment orders placed in one period raise profits and output in the next, call forth further investment, and eventually raise the capital stock to a point at which the same flow of investment no longer grows profits, whereupon orders fall and the mechanism reverses. The proportions of the schemas appear in his work as the moving economy's momentary configurations rather than as its resting points, and the balance conditions survive as the accounting skeleton that any dynamic path must satisfy period by period, whatever else it does. Later economists who built flow-of-funds accounts and stock-flow-consistent models were, knowingly or not, working the same seam: impose the schemas' discipline that every flow comes from somewhere and goes somewhere, then let behaviour, not arithmetic, decide the path. The lineage that began with Leontief's tables thus has a theoretical twin, and between them they account for most of what the twentieth century found usable in the third part of the second volume.

## Reading the schemas: a practical guide to the chapters

Students approaching the third part in the original need a map of where the weight lies, because the chapters are uneven in kind. The introductory chapter restates the problem, the circulation of the aggregate social capital, and dismantles the notion that society's product can be analysed as a mere sum of individual incomes. The critical chapter on predecessors, Quesnay and Smith above all, is the most readable sustained stretch and the best entry point for a first reading, since its polemical energy carries the conceptual correction painlessly. The chapter on simple reproduction is the core: its early sections work the crossing exchange until the movement of every portion is familiar, its later sections take up the second department's subdivision and the fixed capital difficulty, and its monetary passages, the sections tracing how money returns to its starting points, are the densest in the volume and can be deferred to a second pass without loss of the main line. The chapter on accumulation and expanded reproduction is shorter than its reputation suggests and repays slow reading with the numbers recomputed by hand, both illustrations, since the second illustration's different starting proportions teach more than the first's smoothness.

Two reading habits separate those who master the material from those who merely survive it. The first is to recompute: the schemas are arithmetic, and passive reading of arithmetic produces the illusion of understanding that an examination promptly exposes; a pencil is not optional equipment. The second is to keep a running list of the assumptions in force at each stage, because the text relaxes them one by one, foreign trade remaining excluded longest, and most misreadings in the secondary literature are failures to notice which assumption a given passage is operating under. The standard English text is the Fernbach translation in the Penguin edition of the second volume, which follows the German second edition that Engels prepared; scholarly citation conventionally references the chapters rather than pages, since pagination varies across editions and languages. Readers who find the monetary sections impenetrable should know that commentators have found them so for a century, that Marx himself revised them repeatedly, and that the main results of the chapters stand independent of the hardest passages on the reflux of money.

## Proportions and prices: how adjustment would have to work

The schemas state what must coincide; the question of how an unplanned economy could grope towards the coincidence is answered only in hints scattered through the volumes, and assembling the hints is a legitimate glossary task. The primary mechanism is price. A department producing beyond the proportion finds its goods cheapening relative to the other's, its profit rate sagging, its credit tightening, while the underproducing department enjoys the reverse; capital migrates, slowly and through bankruptcies as often as through smooth reallocation, towards the profitable proportions. Marx's third volume builds the equalisation of profit rates across branches into a systematic account, and the migration that account describes is the same migration the schemas require, driven by the differentials the schemas' imbalance creates. The mechanism is real but it is a mechanism of correction after error, operating through losses, idle capacity, and unemployment, which is why the tradition's crisis theorists could treat the schemas as a crisis theory in embryo without misusing them: the proportions are enforced, if at all, by the damage their violation causes.

Two features of that enforcement deserve emphasis, because they distinguish the Marxian picture from any thermostat model of equilibrium. The first is irreversibility. Capital committed to a branch takes material form, a mill, a furnace, a fleet, and cannot migrate as a sum of value without passing through sale, scrapping, or ruin; the adjustment the schemas require is therefore lumpy and destructive, proceeding in historical time with real waste, not as a smooth reallocation of a plastic substance. The second is that the target moves. While capital migrates towards yesterday's proportions, accumulation and technical change redraw the proportions themselves, so the system chases a configuration that its own chasing alters. Nothing in this picture contradicts the schemas; it is, rather, the picture the schemas' stillness implies once motion is restored to it, and the stillness should be read the way one reads a freeze-frame of a runner, as information about the stride, not as a claim that runners are statues. Teachers assigning the chapters do well to ask students to narrate one full adjustment in words, from imbalance through price signals to scrapped plant, since the narration is where the formalism becomes economics.

## The hoard: accumulation's waiting room

Idle money sits at several points of the schemas doing quiet structural work, and the hoard deserves a section of its own because popular accounts treat hoarding as a pathology while the construction treats it as a necessity. Three distinct hoards appear. The depreciation hoard gathers, year by year, the value transferred from fixed capital, waiting for the machine's death to release it into replacement spending; it is the monetary face of the fixed capital problem, and its existence means that at any moment a considerable sum of money is sterilised in funds awaiting a physical event. The accumulation hoard gathers the surplus of capitalists saving towards an investment whose lump cost exceeds one period's takings; it is the waiting room in which small annual savings assemble themselves into a plant. The reserve hoard, finally, is every capital's buffer against the asynchrony of buying and selling, the float that keeps wages payable in a week when sales have not yet returned their money. Each hoard is rational from its owner's standpoint, and each withdraws money from circulation for a time, which is why the text must work to show the circuit tolerating them.

The toleration mechanism is distribution in time and across persons: while one capital's fund is filling, another's is emptying into expenditure, and the aggregate of hoards can remain roughly constant even as every individual hoard rises and falls. The qualification roughly carries weight, because nothing coordinates the phases; a synchronised rush to accumulate funds, or a synchronised completion of them, would disturb the circuit's money supply independently of any real imbalance in the departments, a monetary source of disproportion layered over the material one. Later monetary theory would make much of this layering. Within the schemas it remains a noted possibility, one more of the construction's exhibited joints, and the glossary records it as such: the hoard is the schemas' internal proof that even a model built to exclude finance cannot finish its story without pools of idle money forming and draining inside the circuit, finance in embryo, present before it is named.

## What the schemas leave out: land, the state, and the household

Three absences structure the models as surely as the departments do, and naming them completes the assumptions inventory begun earlier. Land is the first. The schemas contain no agriculture as a distinct sphere and no rent as a distinct revenue; food and raw materials appear simply as products of the two departments, and the landlord, a central figure in Quesnay's Tableau, has been dismissed from the cast. The dismissal is methodological, rent being treated in the third volume as a distribution of surplus value rather than a source of value, but it has consequences a reader should register: the schemas cannot speak to the food supply's special rigidities, harvest variation, or the price of land, and schemes of reproduction for economies where agriculture dominates require the framework to be reopened rather than merely applied.

The state is the second absence. No taxation skims the flows, no public works add a purchaser outside the departments, no central bank manages the money the circuit uses. Every modern reader instinctively supplies the state, and the instinct should be resisted while the model is being learned, because the state's fiscal circuit, taxing wages and profits and spending on goods and salaries, inserts a third set of exchanges whose balance conditions would need stating in their own right. Later builders in the Kaleckian tradition did exactly that extension, adding government and foreign sectors to the departmental skeleton, and the extensions are well behaved: the skeleton accepts new bones. But they are extensions, and the original's austerity on this point is a fact about the original, not a gap the reader is licensed to paper over silently.

The household is the third and deepest absence, since the circuit of capitals presupposes throughout a sphere it never enters, in which labour power is daily and generationally remade. Workers appear in the schemas at the moment of spending wages; the cooking, nursing, bearing, and raising that stand behind the wage's conversion into renewed labour power occur offstage, unpriced and uncounted. Critics in the feminist tradition have pressed this absence hardest, observing that the schemas reproduce everything except the reproducers, and that an economy's accounts which treat labour power as a flow purchased like coal conceal the unpaid labour on which the flow depends. The observation is correct as a description of the model's boundary, and the model's defenders reply, also correctly, that every construction has a boundary and that this one's is declared in the first volume's definition of labour power's value. The glossary's duty is to mark the boundary visibly, since invisible boundaries are the ones that mislead: the schemas show how the product circulates among capitals, and they neither show nor claim to show how the producers of that product are themselves produced.

## Why two departments, and not three or three hundred

The choice of exactly two departments is sometimes mistaken for primitive simplicity, and a precision glossary should state the reasoning, since it governs what the models can and cannot be adapted to. Marx needs the division to follow the destination of the product because his question is material replacement: what must be produced, in what shapes, for the process to renew itself. Two destinations exhaust the possibilities, since every product either re-enters production or is finally consumed, and so two departments are not a simplification of a longer list but the complete list at the level of the question being asked. Finer divisions answer finer questions. Kalecki's three departments separate capitalist consumption from workers' consumption because his question concerns who spends; input-output practice divides into hundreds of branches because its questions concern the input recipes of particular industries. Each partition is correct relative to its question, and the error lies only in demanding of one partition the answers belonging to another, as when the schemas are reproached for not distinguishing steel from coal, a distinction their question does not need and their method can supply on request by disaggregating Department One.

Aggregation has a price, and it should be quoted honestly. Within Department One, the proportions among machinery, materials, and energy matter for any concrete application, and the two-department balance can hold while the department's interior is skewed, a possibility the fractal character of the balance problem, noted with the luxury subdivision, already implied. The schemas therefore state necessary conditions, not sufficient ones: satisfaction of the crossing condition does not guarantee smooth reproduction, it guarantees only that reproduction is not ruled out at the highest level of aggregation. This is a genuine limitation and also, once stated, a clarification of the entire literature, since several celebrated refutations of the schemas amount to exhibiting an interior imbalance and declaring the frame refuted, when the frame itself predicts that interior imbalances are where trouble begins. The proportion conditions form a hierarchy, and the schemas occupy its summit; descent into the hierarchy is application, not correction.

## Numbers as rhetoric: why the figures are chosen as they are

Marx's figures are round, small, and arranged to make the equalities visible at a glance, and the choice of illustrative numbers in this literature is never innocent. A constructor selecting numbers that satisfy a condition performs the demonstration; a constructor selecting numbers that fail it can exhibit the failure; the reader's obligation is to notice that both performances are available for almost any frame and to ask what the selection proves. Marx's simple reproduction figures prove the crossing condition satisfiable, full stop. His expanded figures prove a growth path exists from the stated start. Bauer's more elaborate figures, constructed against Luxemburg, prove the path survives the addition of population growth and changing proportions. Grossmann's extension of Bauer proves that the same path, driven further under frozen assumptions, eventually violates its own requirements. Four performances, four morals, one family of arithmetics: the numbers are arguments, and their roundness is the tell that they are arguments rather than evidence.

This is why the article's earlier insistence that the table's figures are illustrative, labelled as such, is not a bureaucratic scruple but the central hermeneutic rule of the topic. Empirical magnitudes appear nowhere in the schemas or their classical sequels; when a participant in the dispute needed the real world, the need was met by rhetoric about the real world, not by data, a striking fact about a controversy over the fate of actual economies. The modern reader, armed with input-output tables and national accounts that the disputants lacked, can perform an exercise none of them could: take a published table for an actual economy, aggregate it into two departments, and inspect the proportions, discovering how far the observed structure sits from any smooth-reproduction configuration and how much of the gap is closed by the foreign sector and the state, the very absorbers the model excludes. The exercise does not test the schemas, which make no empirical claim; it tests the reader's understanding of what the absorbers do, and it is recommended to students as the point at which the nineteenth-century construction and the twenty-first century's statistics finally meet.

## Material balances: the schemas at work in the planning office

The schemas' most direct employment was neither academic nor polemical. In the Soviet Union of the nineteen twenties, economists charged with constructing the first accounts of a planned economy reached for the reproduction framework as a working tool. The Central Statistical Administration under Pavel Popov compiled a balance of the national economy for 1923 and 1924, an economy-wide table of production and distribution arranged so that the sources and uses of every major product could be checked against each other, and its compilers acknowledged the methodological ancestry running from Quesnay through the second volume. Out of that exercise grew the method of material balances, the planning technique by which supply and demand for hundreds of key commodities, steel, grain, fuel, freight capacity, were reconciled in physical units before plan targets were fixed. Whatever the later fate of the planning system, the episode belongs in this article because it is the schemas' one great historical application in their own material spirit: proportions first, in physical terms, with money aggregates subordinate. Leontief, who had worked in that statistical milieu before emigrating, carried the ambition into the market-economy form of the input-output table, and the two traditions, the Gosplan balance and the Harvard table, are cousins whose common grandfather is the third part of the second volume.

Wartime mobilisation in the market economies converged on the same instruments from the opposite direction. Production boards allocating steel, shipping, and machine tools among competing military and civilian claims were, in substance, maintaining material balances and solving proportion problems of exactly the schemas' type, usually without knowing the pedigree. The convergence teaches the article's recurring lesson from a fresh side: the proportion problem is not an artefact of Marxist doctrine but a property of any economy examined as a material system, and it becomes visible whenever price signals are suspended, overridden, or simply too slow, in war, in blockade, in rapid industrialisation, in postwar reconstruction. The schemas are the first statement of the problem in its general form, which is why they keep being reinvented by practitioners who have never read them, and why a glossary of Marxist economics is justified in claiming them as one of the tradition's permanent contributions to the general toolkit.

## The neglected volume

Readers often ask why, if the schemas matter this much, the second volume is the least read of the three volumes of Capital, and the question has a documented answer. The first volume offers narrative, history, and the theory of exploitation in prose of literary force; the third offers the recognisable furniture of capitalism, profit, interest, rent, merchants, and banks; the second, arriving posthumously and wearing the dress of circulation bookkeeping, was long treated as the trilogy's corridor, necessary to pass through and impossible to linger in. Engels himself worried in his preface at the volume's difficulty and at the fragmentary state of its manuscripts. The schemas suffered their volume's fate: cited in every history of the dispute, worked through by few, and usually known through the presentations of intermediaries, Luxemburg's assault, Bukharin's reply, Sweezy's syllabus, rather than in the text.

The neglect is doubly unfortunate, and a glossary can say so without proselytising. It is unfortunate for critics of Marx, because the schemas are the strongest evidence against the caricature of his method as prophecy dressed in economics: here the method is construction, accounting discipline, and the open registration of unsolved difficulties, and a critic who has not met this Marx argues against a simpler opponent than the real one. It is unfortunate for sympathisers, because the schemas impose on their admirers the demonstration not description rule, the most effective available antidote to the tradition's own prophetic temptations. The volume's neglect also has a practical scholarly consequence worth recording: because the text is hard and the intermediaries are vivid, the intermediaries' framings hardened into the topic's common knowledge, and several claims examined in this article, that the schemas prove harmony, that they prove breakdown, that they describe an economy, are framings acquired from the dispute's literature rather than from the chapters themselves. Returning to the chapters, pencil in hand, remains the only known cure, which is why the reading guide above is written as it is.

## Inside Department Two: necessities, luxuries, and the wage

The subdivision of the consumption department rewards one more pass, because several live questions hide inside it. The necessities branch sells to everyone: workers of both departments, workers of both its own sub-branches, and capitalists in their capacity as consumers of ordinary goods. The luxury branch sells almost exclusively to capitalists and to the better-paid strata whose revenue derives from surplus value. The two branches therefore face different demand logics. Demand for necessities is anchored in the wage bill and the size of the employed population, magnitudes set by the circuit's own advances; demand for luxuries is anchored in the surplus and in the capitalists' choice between consuming it and accumulating it, the most volatile decision in the whole system. A shift of taste or confidence among owners towards accumulation contracts the luxury branch even in a growing economy, releasing labour and capital that the necessities branch cannot instantly absorb, while a shift towards display expands it at the expense of the investment fund. The schemas register these movements only as changes in starting figures, but the subdivision is the place in the model where the capitalists' consumption decision, elsewhere a parameter, shows its power to disturb the proportions from inside the second department.

The wage's dual character is also visible here and worth stating for students, who often miss it. In the circuit, wages are simultaneously a cost advanced by capital and the principal fund of demand for the second department's necessities. A general wage reduction, advantageous to each employer taken singly, contracts the fund that realises the necessities branch's product, the aggregate standpoint's classic trap. The schemas do not develop a wage theory, and the trap should not be inflated into an underconsumption theorem, which the dispute section showed to be a separate and contestable construction. But the bare structure, wages as both cost and demand, is inscribed in the departments' accounts, and later theories of effective demand would build their central mechanism on exactly that inscription. A glossary performs its office by pointing at the inscription and declining to over-read it.

## Gross product and value added: the double-counting lesson

The schemas' figures permit one arithmetical observation with a long afterlife in national accounting. In the simple reproduction example, the total product of the two departments is nine thousand, yet the new value created during the year is only four thousand, the sum of wages and surplus in both departments, since five thousand of the gross figure merely replaces, and so counts again, value transferred from used-up means of production. Measures of an economy's output must choose between the gross concept, which counts the replacement traffic, and the value-added concept, which nets it out, and the modern product accounts choose value added for most purposes while retaining gross measures for others. The choice is not a technicality. Smith's dogma was precisely the error of treating the gross product as if it were all revenue, all value added, and the schemas were built to exhibit the difference; every modern statistician who distinguishes intermediate consumption from final demand is maintaining Marx's distinction against Smith, usually without knowing its provenance. Students who grasp the nine thousand and the four thousand, and can say exactly what the five thousand between them is, hold the key to the schemas' accounting philosophy and to a permanent puzzle in the interpretation of output statistics, where gross and net measures can move in different directions in the same year and partisans of each can proclaim opposite verdicts on the economy's health.

## The circuit beneath the schemas

The schemas presuppose a micro-structure that the second volume's first part builds, and a short verbal account of it will steady readers who wonder what an individual capital is doing while the departments balance. Each capital passes through a circuit: money is advanced to purchase means of production and labour power; production consumes them and yields a mass of commodities embodying more value than the advance; the commodities are sold and the money returns, enlarged, ready to begin again. The three forms, money capital, productive capital, and commodity capital, succeed one another in time and coexist in space, since at any moment a firm's resources are divided among cash, work in progress, and finished stock awaiting sale. Reproduction at the level of society is the sum of these circuits geared into one another, each firm's sale supplying another's purchase, each firm's purchase financing another's sale, and the schemas are the gearing drawn at the highest level. The connection explains a feature of the models that otherwise puzzles beginners, their indifference to which particular firms populate the departments: the construction concerns the gearing, and the gearing's conditions are the same for any cast of firms whose combined advances match the figures.

The circuit picture also clarifies the schemas' treatment of time, which is easy to underrate. Production and circulation take time, capitals turn over at different speeds, a shipyard's circuit spans years while a baker's spans days, and the second volume's middle part is devoted to the consequences of turnover differences for the annual rate of surplus value and the money advances an economy requires. The schemas simplify that richness into a common annual rhythm, a simplification the assumptions inventory recorded, but the simplification should be read as a normalisation rather than a denial: the turnover analysis stands behind the annual figures as a reminder that the year's flows are the resultant of countless overlapping circuits of different periods, and that the coincidences the schemas require include coincidences among those periods. When later writers in the monetary tradition objected that the models lack real time, the fair reply is that the volume as a whole is saturated with time, turnover, lags, and maturities, and that the schemas abstract from it for stated purposes, as a map abstracts from the terrain's third dimension. The abstraction can still be refused, but it should be refused knowingly, with the surrounding volume in view.

## Sensitivity: what a change in the rate of surplus value does

The schemas' parameters can be varied one at a time to map the construction's sensitivities, and the most instructive variation concerns the rate of surplus value, the ratio of surplus to wages within each department. Raise that rate, by lengthening the working day or cheapening the wage goods that define labour power's value, and several magnitudes move together: the surplus available for accumulation and for capitalist consumption grows relative to the wage fund, the second department's composition shifts towards the luxury branch whose demand the surplus feeds, and the crossing condition's terms all change value, requiring a new configuration for balance. Lower the rate, through shorter hours or dearer necessities, and the movements reverse, with the necessities branch gaining weight as wages claim a larger share of the product. The schemas themselves merely register these variations as fresh starting figures, but registering them exposes a substantive truth about the system they model: distribution and reproduction are not separate topics, since every change in the division of the product between wages and surplus rewrites the proportions the economy must achieve to continue, and thereby alters the terrain on which the next period's distributive struggle occurs.

This interlock is one reason the schemas resist the harmony reading more stubbornly than their smooth arithmetic suggests. A configuration that balances at one rate of surplus value will generally fail to balance at another, so the economy's distributive conflicts are simultaneously fights over the conditions of its own continuation, and a victory by either side that shifts distribution sharply imposes a re-proportioning crisis on the victor. Marx does not draw this moral in the schemas' chapters, being occupied with the construction, but the moral is latent in the construction's logic and later writers in the Kaleckian tradition drew it explicitly, making the distribution of income the hinge between the reproduction accounts and the dynamics of demand. The glossary records the latency without capitalising on it: the schemas provide the form in which distribution and reproduction constrain each other, and the provision is theirs, while the theories built on the form belong to their builders.

## Five misreadings to correct

The secondary literature recycles a stable set of errors about the schemas, and a glossary article earns its keep by correcting them in one place. The first misreading takes the schemas to describe a real economy, usually a stylised Victorian one, and then complains that no economy ever looked like that. The correction is the article's rule: the schemas are a construction with chosen figures, and their author says in terms that simple reproduction is an abstraction and expanded reproduction a statement of conditions. Judging them as reportage mistakes their genre, as criticising a map's projection for not being the terrain.

The second misreading takes the schemas to prove that crisis is impossible, since reproduction balances in them. The correction is that the models assume the balance whose possibility they demonstrate, and the same chapters register the fixed capital difficulty and remark that the conditions of normal reproduction are equally conditions of abnormal reproduction, possibilities of crisis. A construction that exhibits its own failure modes is not a certificate of stability, and attributing that certificate to it requires ignoring the text at its most explicit.

The third misreading inverts the second, taking the schemas to prove that crisis is inevitable, typically by pointing to some magnitude the construction supposedly cannot accommodate. The correction is symmetrical: the exchanges in the models close, demonstrably, in both the simple and the expanded cases, and every historical breakdown derivation from them has been shown, in the dispute section, to rest on an added premise about frozen rates or closed frontiers. Inevitability may be arguable on other grounds; the schemas do not supply it.

The fourth misreading calls the schemas a growth model in the modern sense, a machine for computing an economy's growth rate from its parameters. The correction is that the construction has no behavioural engine: accumulation rates are set by assumption, technical change is excluded within each period, and no mechanism generates the path the figures trace. The schemas stand to growth models as a balance sheet stands to a business plan, a constraint the plan must respect, and their historical influence on genuine growth models, Feldman's and von Neumann's, flowed through builders who added the missing engines themselves.

The fifth misreading asserts that expanded reproduction requires an external market, presenting Luxemburg's conclusion as the schemas' own teaching. The correction is that the requirement is precisely what the dispute was about: Bauer's counter-constructions exhibit internal closure over successive years, Bukharin's critique disputes her reasoning at the level of method, and the question remains a live one about premises, not a delivered verdict of the arithmetic. Readers who meet the claim in textbooks should ask which side of a famous quarrel the textbook has silently joined. A sixth error, too common to omit, confuses the departments with industries or with the primary, secondary, and tertiary sectors of modern statistics; the correction is the destination principle from the terminology section, coal for the grate against coal for the furnace, and it dissolves the confusion at once.

## A balance sheet: what the schemas settle and what they leave open

It is time to draw up the account this article has been keeping in pieces. Settled, first, is the coherence of reproduction: a capitalist economy's interlocking exchanges can close, at constant scale and in growth, and the proportions on which closure depends can be stated exactly. Settled, second, is the refutation of the inherited bookkeeping error that dissolved replacement out of the product, and with it the standing of material composition alongside value totals in any adequate account of an economy. Settled, third, is a method: consistency construction as an instrument for clearing false impossibilities out of a field's path, exhibited here in its classical form. Settled, fourth, is a lineage of technique, into input-output accounting, multisectoral growth theory, material balances, and department-based theories of effective demand, each documented rather than asserted.

Left open, first, is every question of mechanism: how proportions are approached, missed, and restored in an economy of decentralised decisions, and with what costs in idleness and ruin. Left open, second, is money in its developed forms, credit above all, whose inclusion transforms the coincidences the models assume into debts that can fail. Left open, third, is the state, foreign trade, and the household, the three absences, each of which re-enters any application and each of which later builders have reattached with varying success. Left open, fourth, is the empirical question in every particular: which failure mode, disproportion, underconsumption, financial fragility, or external strangulation, accounts for any given historical crisis, a question the schemas equip readers to ask precisely and forbid them to answer cheaply. An instrument that settles the logic and opens the empirics has done the work instruments are for. The schemas, judged by that standard and denied every larger claim, are among the most successful constructions in the history of economics, and their success is entirely compatible with the falsity of any doctrine that has been hung upon them, which is the demonstration not description rule stated one final time.

## The manuscripts behind the chapters

The schemas' unfinished surface has a documentary explanation, and glossary readers who cite the chapters should know its outlines. Marx drafted the material of the second volume repeatedly across two decades, leaving a series of manuscripts of very different dates and states, from the comprehensive draft of the middle eighteen sixties to later reworkings of particular parts composed towards the end of the eighteen seventies. The third part's chapters draw principally on two of these layers, an earlier full treatment and a later, tighter reworking of the reproduction material that Marx prepared when he contemplated publishing the volume himself. Engels, editing after his friend's death, had to interweave the layers, completing transitions, selecting among variants, and occasionally supplying connective argument, and he told the reader in his preface that he had done so while altering the substance as little as the task allowed. Scholars with access to the manuscripts in the complete historical-critical edition have since mapped the joins with precision, and the map matters for one practical reason: some celebrated obscurities of the monetary sections are joins, places where strata of different decades meet, rather than depths of doctrine, and commentary that treats every sentence as equally final misreads the geology of the text.

The geology also explains the schemas' peculiar combination of rigour and openness. A writer who returns to a construction across fifteen years, recomputing its examples and worrying its weakest joint, the fixed capital replacement, is not decorating a doctrine but interrogating an instrument, and the later layer's greater compression suggests a mind still testing whether the conditions can be stated more cleanly. That the interrogation was never finished is a fact about a life, not about the construction's validity, and the distinction generalises across this series' subjects: unfinished business in a manuscript is evidence of a research programme interrupted, and the schemas' open joints, the depreciation coincidence, the hoards' synchronisation, the labour supply, have functioned in the subsequent literature exactly as open joints in a programme should, as the places where successors attached their own work.

## Derived demand: the two kinds of hunger in the system

One distinction the schemas enforce quietly deserves extraction, because it organises much practical reasoning about industrial economies. Demand in the construction is of two kinds with different masters. The demand for means of production is derived: it arises from the departments' own production plans, replacement schedules, and accumulation decisions, and it can therefore grow, for a time, entirely on its own momentum, as Tugan-Baranovsky saw, each investment generating the orders that justify the next. The demand for consumption goods is final in the necessities branch, anchored in employment and wages, and derivative in the luxury branch, anchored in the surplus and its division. Business commentary that treats demand as one magnitude with one mood misses the schemas' elementary lesson, that an economy can suffer a glut in the derived circuit and a shortage in the final circuit simultaneously, or the reverse, and that the two circuits' cycles need not coincide. Industrial recessions characteristically begin in the derived circuit, in cancelled equipment orders, while consumption continues on the momentum of employment not yet destroyed, and recoveries characteristically begin there too, when replacement postponed through the slump can be postponed no longer. The schemas contain no cycle theory, but their two-circuit anatomy is the map on which several cycle theories, Kalecki's included, were drawn, and readers who carry the map will find the territory of industrial fluctuations less confusing than those who navigate by aggregate demand alone.

## The circular flow in the textbook: a diluted descendant

Nearly every beginning student of economics meets a diagram of two boxes, households and firms, with arrows of goods and money circling between them, and is rarely told that the diagram has ancestors. The textbook circular flow is the reproduction idea diluted for pedagogy: the circuit retained, the sectoral anatomy removed, replacement dissolved into a single flow of income, Smith's dogma restored in the very act of introducing the subject. Comparing the diagram with the schemas is the quickest way to see what Marx's construction adds. The textbook flow cannot represent a crisis of proportion, cannot distinguish replacement from income, cannot even state the question who buys the surplus, because its categories were chosen to make the circuit look frictionless. The schemas were chosen to make its frictions statable. Neither instrument is dishonest; they answer different needs, the one to orient a novice in an afternoon, the other to discipline a science. But the comparison arms the student against a common illusion, that the economy's circularity is a triviality everyone always understood. It was understood late, partially, and against resistance, and the schemas are the document in which the understanding became exact.

There is a further stage in the dilution worth noting for the research-minded. The monetary circuit literature of the later twentieth century, working from Keynes's finance motive and from the French and Italian circuit schools, rebuilt the flow of money creation and destruction by banks into a formal circuit and discovered, sometimes with surprise, that its bookkeeping reproduced structures familiar from the second volume: advances, reflux, the financing of the wage bill, hoards as leakages. Intellectual descent in economics is often like this, a spiral in which a later generation formalises what an earlier one sketched, in ignorance or defiance of the sketch. The schemas sit near the spiral's centre. A glossary cannot trace every turn, and should not try; it is enough to establish that the centre exists and that the turns keep passing through it.

## Wear, throughput, and the physical reading

A final perspective on the schemas has gained ground as environmental accounting has matured, and it can be stated without importing any doctrine. Read physically, the schemas are a material balance: tonnes of coal, steel, grain, and timber enter the departments, are transformed, worn out, eaten, and replaced, and the construction's proportions are conditions on those physical flows as much as on values. Marx worked in both registers throughout, insisting that proportions in value and proportions in material form stand or fall jointly, and the material register connects the schemas to an independent tradition of thought about the economy as a throughput system, in which production is the transformation of low-entropy materials and energy into goods and waste. Material flow analysis, as practised by statistical and environmental agencies, tracks an economy's physical metabolism in tables that a reader of the third part will find eerily familiar, sector by sector, input by input. The familiarity is not descent in this case so much as convergence: any honest material accounting of an economy reinvents the balance form, because the form is dictated by conservation, things must come from somewhere and go somewhere. That the same form serves the value analysis of a nineteenth-century critic of capitalism and the physical accounting of twenty-first century environmental statistics is the broadest vindication the construction can claim, and it is a vindication of exactly the modest kind this article has defended throughout: of the instrument, not of any verdict pronounced with it.

## The schemas in Marx's larger plan

The second volume was one instalment of a project its author never completed, and the schemas' place in the plan explains their peculiar abstraction. Marx's design, sketched in the late eighteen fifties, envisaged six books: on capital, on landed property, on wage labour, on the state, on foreign trade, and on the world market and crises. Only the book on capital was substantially drafted, as the three volumes known to us, and within it the analysis proceeds at the level he called capital in general, examining the laws of the mode of production before the modifying influences of landownership, state policy, and international exchange are allowed to complicate them. The schemas' famous exclusions, no rent, no state, no foreign trade, are therefore not oversights but the plan's architecture: each excluded factor had a book of its own in which it would have entered, and the reproduction construction was deliberately built at the level where those factors are still switched off. Critics who reproach the models for lacking what the plan reserved for later volumes are, knowingly or not, reproaching a torso for lacking limbs its sculptor designed and did not live to carve. The reproach may still land, since a torso is what we have, but it should be aimed accurately: at the incompleteness of the project, not at a blunder in the construction.

The plan also clarifies the schemas' relation to the world market book that was never written, in which crises were to receive their full treatment as phenomena of the total system in motion. The second volume's crisis remarks, the conditions of normal reproduction turning into conditions of abnormal reproduction, the fixed capital coincidence, are promissory notes against that unwritten book, and the tradition's subsequent century of crisis theory can be read as a series of attempts to redeem the notes by different hands. Luxemburg reached for the world market volume's subject, the system's outside, and made it the key; the disproportionality theorists stayed inside the second volume's own hints; the breakdown school tried to extract the unwritten conclusion from the written arithmetic. Reading the dispute with the plan in mind lowers its temperature productively: the parties were competing to complete an unfinished architecture, and their constructions differ as completions of an unfinished building differ, constrained by the standing walls and free above them.

## The stationary state and the circular flow: Schumpeter's testimony

Simple reproduction has cousins across the classical tradition, and recognising them prevents a parochial reading. John Stuart Mill's stationary state, the classical economists' long-run resting point in which accumulation ceases and population and capital stand still, shares the schemas' constant scale but not their purpose: Mill's state is a prophecy and eventually a hope, where Marx's simple reproduction is a laboratory abstraction adopted for a chapter and discarded. Joseph Schumpeter, whose own system begins from a stationary circular flow of economic life, explicitly traced the circular flow's pedigree through Quesnay and the second volume, and in his history of analysis treated the reproduction schemas as the great intermediate construction between the Tableau and Leontief, whose Harvard teacher he was. The testimony matters because Schumpeter was no partisan of Marxist conclusions; he is a hostile witness confirming the lineage, and hostile witnesses are the kind historians trust. His circular flow strips the circuit of its class anatomy and its value theory, retaining the form for a theory of development in which the entrepreneur disturbs the flow from within, a reminder that the schemas' form could serve theories whose spirit is the opposite of their author's, and a further instance of the portability of forms that the lineage section recorded.

## The general glut debate behind the schemas

The realisation question that the schemas answer was old before Marx posed it, and its prehistory sharpens what his answer achieved. In the decades after the Napoleonic wars, British and French economists fought over the possibility of a general glut, a simultaneous overproduction of goods in all lines. Ricardo and the followers of Say denied the possibility in principle: production creates its own demand, since every seller is a buyer, and gluts could afflict particular trades only. Malthus and Sismondi insisted that general overproduction was possible and actual, pointing to the distress after the wars, and located the weakness in insufficient consumption by the unproductive classes or the impoverished masses. Marx's judgement on the debate, elaborated in his notebooks on the history of theory, was that both sides argued at the wrong level: the deniers rested on tautologies about barter that money's existence refutes, since sale and purchase can separate in time, while the affirmers reached for remedies, unproductive consumption, public works, that mistook the symptom. The schemas are his constructive contribution to the quarrel, and they reframe it completely. A general glut in their terms is not an excess of everything over demand in the abstract but a failure of proportions and timing among flows that are individually necessary, and the possibility of crisis is established not by the masses' poverty, though Marx believed in that poverty on other grounds, but by the circuit's structure: sale and purchase separated, replacement and revenue entangled, proportions discoverable only through their violation. Students who know the glut debate can watch the schemas absorb and supersede it, which is the most instructive way to read the chapters, as a settlement of accounts with a fifty-year-old argument rather than as a free-standing curiosity.

## Seasons and the annual rhythm

The schemas run on a year, and the year is an agricultural inheritance. Quesnay's Tableau followed the harvest cycle, in which production genuinely is annual, inputs are advanced in spring and the product arrives entire in autumn, and reproduction poses itself as the question whether the harvest restores the advances and feeds the classes until the next. Marx retains the annual frame for an industrial economy whose processes are continuous, and the retention is worth a moment's thought, since it is both a convenience and a distortion he understood. The convenience is accounting clarity: a common period lets every capital's turnover be normalised and the flows added. The distortion is that industrial capital has no natural period, its circuits overlapping like waves of different lengths, and the year's unity is imposed by the statistician, as it is in modern national accounts, whose annual tables inherit the same agricultural skeleton beneath the refineries and server farms. Seasonal rhythms persist inside the industrial year in any case, in construction, in clothing, in the holiday trade of the second department, and a reader alert to the schemas' frame will notice how much of the economy's visible pulsation is the old harvest calendar beating inside the new machinery. The observation is a small one, but it exemplifies the article's method: the construction's conventions are themselves evidence, about the history of the form, and reading them knowingly is part of reading the schemas at all.

## Stocks and flows: the quiet distinction

Beneath the schemas' arithmetic sits a distinction that economics would take another century to formalise, and the glossary should surface it. The schemas track flows, the year's production, purchases, and replacements, but they presuppose stocks, the accumulated mass of machinery, buildings, and materials in existence at the year's start, from which the flows of wear and replacement derive. The constant capital consumed in a year is a flow drawn from a stock; the depreciation fund measures the stock's slow consumption; accumulation is the flow that enlarges the stock. Keeping the two orders of magnitude distinct is elementary and constantly violated in public argument, where debts, deficits, capital, and income are traded as if interchangeable, and the schemas are an early masterclass in the discipline: every figure in them is labelled by its order, a flow per year or a fund awaiting its moment, and no conclusion is drawn by mixing them. Modern statistical practice has built the distinction into its architecture, with balance sheets standing beside the flow accounts and the perpetual inventory method estimating capital stocks from accumulated investment flows, an instrument whose logic, stock equals past flows minus depreciation, is the schemas' fixed capital bookkeeping generalised. A student who internalises the distinction from these chapters possesses a permanent instrument of criticism, applicable far beyond this topic, and examiners know it, which is why the distinction rewards the emphasis this article gives it.

## Reading a slump through the schemas

As a closing exercise in the construction's use, consider how a severe industrial slump of the classic interwar type appears when mapped onto the departments, not as evidence for any theory but as a demonstration of the map's resolving power. The collapse begins characteristically in the first department: orders for machinery and construction fall away, and because that department's internal traffic is the economy's largest flow, its contraction multiplies itself as equipment makers cancel their own orders for steel, components, and plant. Employment in the heavy industries crumbles first and fastest. The second department is initially sheltered by the wage fund of those still employed and by the consumption of owners drawing on accumulated revenue, then succumbs as the first department's unemployment reaches its markets, and its lighter industries follow the heavy ones down with a lag. Replacement investment is postponed across both departments, capitalists choosing to let plant wear without renewing it, which deepens the first department's famine while quietly ageing the capital stock, until the ageing itself, breakdowns, obsolescence, and the sheer exhaustion of postponement, recreates a floor of replacement demand from which recovery can begin. Every element of that narrative corresponds to a term in the schemas: the internal traffic of Department One, the crossing exchange's lag, the depreciation fund's suspension, the reserve of labour swelling as variable capital contracts. The narrative does not prove the schemas, and rival frameworks narrate the same events in their own vocabularies; the exercise shows only that the departmental map resolves the slump's anatomy into intelligible parts without forcing, which is what a good map does, and why this one has been carried by so many travellers into territories its maker never visited.

## The other reproduction: what the first volume's chapter adds

The phrase simple reproduction does double duty in Marx's work, and the two uses illuminate each other once distinguished. In the first volume's chapter of that name, the question is not proportions among departments but the reproduction of the social relation itself: viewed as a continuous process rather than a single act, capitalist production continually recreates its own preconditions, the worker separated from the means of production and compelled to sell labour power again, the capitalist in possession of the accumulated surplus and positioned to advance it again. The worker's wage, spent on subsistence, returns him to the market as empty-handed as before; the capitalist's consumption, however luxurious, is funded by a surplus the process replenishes; even the individual capitalist's exit changes nothing, since his capital persists under new ownership. That chapter's reproduction is sociological, a claim about the self-renewal of a class structure, and it requires no arithmetic. The second volume's reproduction is material, a claim about flows and proportions, and it is nothing but arithmetic. Confusion between the two has generated a small library of cross-purposed commentary, in which the schemas are praised or blamed for demonstrating the renewal of class relations, a thesis they neither state nor support. The glossary's terminology section flagged the distinction; this section's fuller statement exists because the confusion is the single most common error in student work on the topic, and because the two chapters, held apart, are each stronger: the first volume explains why the circuit's participants reappear in their roles, the second what their reappearance must accomplish materially, and together they describe a system that renews both its product and its personnel.

## When banks create the money

The schemas' money is a produced commodity, gold, whose quantity the circuit can expand only through the gold producer's labour, and the modern reader instinctively asks what survives when money is instead created by bank lending, appearing with the loan and vanishing with repayment. The honest answer distinguishes the construction's layers. The material layer is untouched: departments, proportions, replacement, and the crossing conditions are statements about goods and labour, and they bind an economy whatever its monetary constitution, since no banking system can lend into existence a machine that has not been produced. The monetary layer changes profoundly. Where gold money forced the coincidences to be financed from accumulated hoards, credit money lets them be financed from anticipated revenue, which relaxes the timing constraints the schemas labour under, the depreciation mismatch, the accumulation hoard's slow assembly, the second department's lag, at the price of binding the circuit's continuity to the solvency of promises. A credit economy can reproduce through imbalances that would jam the schemas' world, and can therefore also accumulate imbalances to magnitudes the schemas' world could not reach before jamming. Marx's third volume, with its analysis of credit and fictitious capital, begins the theory of that transformed world, and the monetary schools that later rebuilt the circuit with endogenous bank money were completing a programme the three volumes together sketch. The schemas' contribution to the completed programme is the part that cannot change: the material conditions, stated in their purest form, against which any monetary system's performance is finally audited by reality.

## Opening the closed economy: trade enters

The schemas' world has no frontier, and lifting that assumption in imagination shows both the construction's robustness and the precise location of Luxemburg's question. Admit foreign trade and each department gains an external vent and an external source: a surplus of means of production can be exported, a deficit of consumption goods imported, and the crossing condition relaxes into a condition on the economy plus its trade balance, with the difference settled in money or debt. Imbalances that would jam the closed circuit can persist for years in the open one, exported to partners or financed by creditors, which is why the closed assumption was methodologically necessary: it prevents the model from dissolving its problem into the environment. Luxemburg's argument, restated in this frame, is that the vent is not a convenience but a necessity, that the surplus destined for accumulation cannot find buyers at home even in principle. The counter-arguments restated in the same frame are that the vent's usefulness proves nothing about its necessity, as a spare tyre's usefulness does not prove the car cannot run without it, and that the historical expansion of trade among capitalist countries themselves, rather than into non-capitalist milieux, sits awkwardly with the necessity claim. The glossary takes no side; it notes that the exchange clarifies what the dispute was ever about, the difference between a system's conveniences and its conditions, and that the schemas' closed world is the instrument that makes the difference visible. Empirically, the question of how much external demand has mattered to accumulation in particular epochs remains open and investigable, which is where the series article on [the companion study of empirical testing](/2016/08/01/testing-marxist-economics/) takes up the wider problem of putting such claims to evidence.

## The schemas among their neighbours in the cluster

A reader working through this cluster of the series will meet the reproduction schemas again in disguise, and a word of orientation prevents confusion. The transformation problem, treated in its own article on the [transformation problem explained](/2016/04/15/transformation-problem-explained/), asks how the values in which the schemas are kept are converted into the prices of production at which goods actually exchange, and its classic formalisation by Ladislaus von Bortkiewicz was built on a three-department reproduction scheme, the luxury branch promoted to a department of its own producing the money commodity. The schemas thus supply the accounting skeleton for the transformation debate as they do for so much else, and the reader who has mastered the two conditions will find the neighbouring article's tables familiar in form, however different the question. The complete guide to [marxist economics complete guide](/2016/04/01/marxist-economics-complete-guide/) places both constructions in the discipline's wider map. What should be carried across the cluster is the habit this article has enforced: for each construction, state what it demonstrates, what it assumes, and what it leaves open, before asking whether it is true. The schemas are the cluster's training ground for that habit precisely because their demonstrations are so clean and their historical misreadings so instructive.

## Turnover and the mass of money

The schemas assume a common annual rhythm, and the volume's middle part explains what that assumption costs and buys, in a result worth extracting for glossary readers. The money a circuit requires depends on the prices of the commodities it circulates divided by the number of times each unit of money can be reused in the period, its velocity; and the capital a production requires depends on how long it is locked up in the process, the turnover time. Branches differ wildly in both, and the annual product figures in the schemas are therefore resultants of capitals turning over at very different speeds, some several times within the year, others once in several years. Two consequences follow for the reproduction construction. The wage fund advanced at any moment is much smaller than the year's variable capital, since the same money pays wages repeatedly as it returns, which is why the schemas' annual flows dwarf the money stock that services them. And the synchronisation problem is worse than the annual frame suggests, because the coincidences must hold not among annual totals alone but among the staggered maturities of thousands of circuits, a point the monetary tradition in Marxist economics has mined ever since. The schemas abstract from the staggering; the abstraction is legitimate for their question, and this section exists so that the reader knows the question was narrowed deliberately, with the wider problem analysed in the same volume rather than ignored by its author.

## The wage-goods critique: the schemas argued over in a parliament of planners

The two-department logic met its most consequential policy test in the development debates of the nineteen fifties, and the episode deserves record as the schemas' last great public controversy. The Feldman-Mahalanobis strategy, described in the lineage section, directed investment preferentially to the capital-goods sector, accepting restrained consumption growth in the near term as the price of a higher growth path later, and the Second Five Year Plan of India was framed upon it. Its critics, most sharply the Bombay economists C. N. Vakil and P. R. Brahmananda, answered from inside the same departmental logic: the binding constraint on the Indian economy, they argued, was not the supply of machines but the supply of wage goods, the food and necessities that additional employed workers would immediately need to buy, and a plan that expanded employment in heavy industry faster than the output of wage goods would release inflation rather than growth, the crossing condition failing in a form the schemas make instantly legible. The wage-goods critique did not deny the two conditions; it wielded them, insisting that the second department's necessities branch had its own claim to priority that the machine-first strategy violated. The episode is the dispute pattern one final time, the same construction arming opposed policies, with the difference that the arguers were advising a government and the proportions at stake were those of a subcontinent. It also marks the historical moment at which the schemas' logic passed fully into the common property of development economics, detached from any doctrine, a fate this article has traced from several directions and can now let rest.

## Inside Department One: the fractal anatomy again

Department One is a world in itself, and opening it briefly rewards the reader with the construction's fractal secret in full. Within the machine-making department, some firms produce finished equipment for final installation, some produce the machine tools with which equipment is made, some produce the steel and energy both consume, and each layer stands to the others in relations formally identical to the grand relation between the departments: outputs that are other producers' inputs, proportions that must hold among them, replacement flows dwarfing the final delivery. An economy's capacity to expand is set in the deepest layer, in the industry that makes the machines that make the machines, a regress that terminates practically in a handful of bottleneck branches whose proportions govern the whole, as planners under the material balances method discovered when their economy-wide tables kept resolving into a short list of critical commodities. The schemas' two departments conceal this interior deliberately; the concealment is the price of their clarity. But the fractal structure explains an otherwise puzzling feature of industrial life, the way disturbances in obscure upstream branches, bearings, specialty steels, industrial gases, propagate with undiminished force into the final outputs of distant industries, a propagation modern supply-chain analysis maps with input-output tables at the magnification the schemas declined. The two constructions, the two-department schema and the three-hundred-branch table, are the same instrument at different settings, and competence in this subject is largely the ability to move between the settings without losing the thread of the argument.

## Proportions as a way of seeing

The article can close its substantive work by naming the habit of mind the schemas train, since a glossary's final gift should be portable. To think in proportions is to ask of any economic magnitude not only how large it is but what it must match: investment against the output of the equipment industries, employment against the output of wage goods, replacement against the depreciation of the stock, the growth of each part against the growth of the parts that supply and absorb it. Most public economic argument fails the habit, celebrating totals whose composition makes them unachievable, or mourning totals whose composition was the whole story. The schemas were the first rigorous schooling in the habit within economics, administered through arithmetic plain enough for a schoolroom and deep enough to occupy a century of disputants, planners, statisticians, and theorists who agreed on nothing else. Their author's larger doctrines stand or fall elsewhere, on arguments this article has not needed to touch. The schooling survives every verdict on the doctrines, and it is available to any reader willing to do the one thing the topic has demanded from the first page: take up a pencil, choose figures, and check whether the crossings clear.

## The law every child knows: Marx's letter on proportionality

The intellectual core of the schemas exists in miniature in a private letter, and quoting its argument in paraphrase gives readers the construction's soul in a page. Writing to Ludwig Kugelmann in July 1868, as the first volume was finding its readers, Marx insisted that the necessity of distributing society's labour in definite proportions is a law that no form of society can abolish; what changes historically is only the form in which the law asserts itself. Under communal production the proportioning is done consciously, by deliberation; under commodity production it asserts itself blindly, through exchange value, through prices that punish overproduction and reward shortage, and therefore through waste, fluctuation, and crisis. The schemas are that letter expanded into arithmetic. They state the proportion law in its purity, for a society of commodity producers, and their silence about enforcement is the letter's point: the law will be enforced, by adjustment or by damage, and the theory's business is to state what enforcement must achieve. Readers who grasp the letter grasp why the construction's author could hold simultaneously that reproduction balances by necessity in the long run of the law and that it balances by accident in the practice of any year, a conjunction that has confused readers who met only one half of it. The letter also arms the reader against the planning misreading in advance: that proportions must hold does not entail that any bureau can hold them, since the same law operated in the economies Marx analysed without any bureau at all, and the schemas are agnostic, as their author was in the letter, about the comparative merits of the forms through which proportion asserts itself.

## New goods and the fixed catalogue

One limitation of the construction has grown more visible as innovation has become the centre of economic attention, and registering it completes the inventory of what the frame excludes. The schemas presuppose a fixed catalogue of goods: the departments produce the same articles period after period, in changing quantities, and reproduction means the renewal of a known material world. Genuinely new products, which create departments rather than expanding them, enter the frame only as next period's revised catalogue, their birth unmodelled. The exclusion is not a detail. A theory of capitalist dynamics in which the list of goods is immortal will systematically understate the disturbance that innovation causes to the proportions, since a new product redraws demand across every boundary at once, orphaning equipment in the old lines and demanding capacity in lines that did not exist when the period's proportions were set. Schumpeter built his theory of development on exactly this disturbance, taking the circular flow as his baseline precisely in order to show the entrepreneur breaking it, and the sequence of the two constructions in a student's education should mirror the sequence in the literature: first the schemas, for the discipline of the circuit; then the acknowledgement that the circuit's catalogue is itself mortal, and that a complete dynamics would need to model the birth and death of branches alongside their proportions. The acknowledgement costs the schemas nothing within their terms, since a consistency construction for a given catalogue retains its force for every catalogue, but it marks the frontier beyond which the instrument, unaided, does not go.

## Population inside the frame

Bauer's counter-construction against Luxemburg introduced one element Marx's own figures hold frozen, and it deserves extraction because it changes the feel of expanded reproduction. If the workforce grows, through natural increase and the drawing of new strata into wage labour, then part of what looks like accumulation is simply equipping additional hands at the existing standard, and the second department must expand merely to feed a larger population at unchanged consumption per head. Bauer built his scheme on an assumed growth of population and showed the exchanges clearing across successive years under it, which strengthened his formal case: the construction tolerates demographic motion as readily as it tolerates growth of the product per worker. The point cuts two ways for readers. It makes the schemas more capacious than their frozen figures suggest, since several magnitudes can move at once provided the crossing conditions are recomputed. And it exposes how much of any observed expansion is mere widening, more workers with the same equipment and the same dinner, as against the deepening that raises the product per head, a distinction development economics would later formalise as the difference between capital widening and capital deepening. The schemas contain the distinction implicitly in the division of accumulation between additional constant capital for new workers and the rising mass of equipment per worker when technique changes; drawing it out is left, characteristically, to the reader's pencil.

## A note on aggregate social capital

One terminological trap awaits the unwary in the secondary literature, and a glossary exists to spring such traps harmlessly. Marx's aggregate social capital, the Gesamtkapital of the third part, means the sum total of the individual capitals in a society, treated as one movement; it has nothing to do with the modern sociological phrase social capital, which denotes networks of trust and association, and the two expressions share only words. Confusion between them has produced examination answers and even published paragraphs in which the schemas are credited with a theory of civic networks. The correction takes one sentence, and it is the kind of sentence that justifies the glossary genre: in this article and its sources, social capital means society's capital, the whole stock and flow of productive wealth whose circulation the schemas trace, and any other sense belongs to a different shelf of the library.

## Departments without borders: the geography of the two sectors

The schemas' departments are defined by the destination of products, not by territory, and nothing in the construction requires both departments to sit inside one country's frontier. Historically they often did not. The nineteenth-century world economy arranged a rough spatial version of the scheme, with the industrial core exporting means of production and importing food and raw materials that entered its consumption and its furnaces alike, while whole regions were specialised, by investment and by coercion, into supplying the consumption goods and primary products of the system. Read through the schemas, that geography is a single reproduction process with the crossing exchanges stretched across oceans, and its balance conditions reappear as conditions on trade: the industrial region's exports of equipment must be matched by its partners' capacity to pay, in goods or in debt, and a failure of the match registers as a crisis of the exchanges rather than of any single nation's production. The stretching clarifies several things at once. It shows why the closed-economy assumption, so artificial geographically, was analytically shrewd, since it isolates the conditions that trade then redistributes among territories without abolishing. It gives Luxemburg's external market its most precise restatement, as the claim that the crossing conditions cannot be met within the capitalist territories alone, a claim whose truth varies by epoch and is therefore empirical. And it warns modern readers against a nationalist bookkeeping in which each country's proportions are audited separately, when the reproduction that matters may be the system's, a warning the age of global supply chains has made vivid to audiences far from this literature. The schemas do not develop the geography; they license it, by defining their units functionally rather than territorially, and the licence is one more instance of the form out-travelling the doctrine.

## Could the conditions ever be measured?

A natural closing question for an empirical age is whether the balance conditions could be tested against data: whether an actual economy's departments stand in the proportions the schemas require. The exercise is possible in a rough form, and its difficulties are themselves instructive. Input-output tables permit the aggregation of an economy into two great branches by destination of output, and the crossing flows can be computed for a given year; but the comparison with the schemas' conditions founders on three reefs. The observed economy is open, so the foreign sector absorbs discrepancies the closed model forbids. The state stands inside every flow as taxer and purchaser, a third party the model excludes. And the conditions are stated in values, while the tables are kept in prices, with the divergence between the two systems of measurement being precisely the transformation problem's subject, so the test presupposes a solution to the cluster's other great construction. What can be measured without those presuppositions is the structure itself: the share of the equipment industries in total output, the weight of the internal traffic within Department One, the sensitivity of the whole to the bottleneck branches, and such structural facts have real diagnostic value, as every supply shock that propagates through an input-output network reminds its analysts. The schemas thus end where this article's rule always said they would: as the grammar of a set of questions, some answerable from statistics, some only from theory, and none answerable by quotation. The empirical ambition they inspire is legitimate, and its proper home is the research programme that the companion study of empirical testing surveys.

## From the one to the many and back: the method of the construction

A last word on method, because the schemas exemplify a procedure that recurs across Marx's economics and that students can reuse wherever they meet it. The procedure moves in three steps. It begins with the individual unit, the single capital and its circuit, analysed until its internal necessities are clear. It then ascends to the aggregate, where the units' external relations become internal to the object, and conditions appear that no single unit can see, the proportions among departments being the present case. Finally it returns to the units with the aggregate conditions in hand, re-reading their behaviour as constrained by a whole they collectively compose and individually obey without knowing. The reproduction schemas are the second step executed in isolation, which is why they can feel bloodless: the firms in them have no strategies because the step brackets strategy. The third step, in which capitals chase profit across the proportions and thereby enforce or violate them, belongs to the theory of competition and crisis that the later volumes and the unwritten books were to supply. Judging the schemas for lacking the third step is like judging a map's legend for lacking the journey; the complaint identifies a real absence and a wrong target simultaneously. What the second step contributes, and what no amount of third-step realism can replace, is the knowledge of what the whole requires, without which the units' collisions are just noise. Economics has repeatedly had to relearn the value of that step, in national accounting, in network analysis, in the macroeconomics of sectoral shocks, and each relearning has found the schemas waiting at the point of need, patient as only arithmetic can be.

## Moral depreciation: the second kind of wear

Fixed capital suffers two kinds of consumption, and the schemas' replacement problem is incomplete until the second is named. Physical wear consumes a machine gradually as it works; moral depreciation, Marx's term for obsolescence, consumes it suddenly when a better or cheaper machine appears, devaluing the installed stock regardless of its remaining physical life. A mill whose looms are outclassed by a rival's new models has lost value that no depreciation fund foresaw, since the fund was assembled to replace like with like at the end of a working life, not to write down the stock mid-course. The consequence for reproduction is a distinctive disturbance: capitals carrying devalued plant find their accumulated replacement funds inadequate at the moment of replacement, while the producers of the new equipment face a surge of demand that the proportions did not predict. Innovation, which the fixed catalogue of goods excluded at the level of products, re-enters here at the level of equipment, and the schemas can register it only as another of their exhibited coincidences failing. The point has a modern ring that needs no labouring: every technological transition strands assets somewhere, and the stranding is legible in the schemas' terms as a replacement fund arriving at the wrong size for the equipment the market now demands. Teachers may find moral depreciation the most classroom-friendly of the construction's difficulties, since every student has owned a device it happened to.

## The party debate: orthodoxy answers Luxemburg

The reception of Luxemburg's book inside German social democracy deserves a paragraph of its own, because it determined how the schemas were read by the movement that owned the vocabulary. The party's leading theorist, Karl Kautsky, had himself written on the market question years earlier and had treated capitalist expansion as requiring an ever-widening field of demand, a position close enough to Luxemburg's to make his public coolness towards her book instructive: reviewing her argument in the party press, he resisted its conclusion that accumulation was impossible without an outside, while the party's right wing treated the whole construction as a gift to their opponents and its left rallied to the author. The dispute was never only scholarly. Her theory carried a strategic payload, since a capitalism that must expand into non-capitalist territory or choke is a capitalism with a built-in terminus, whose collapse the movement need only organise to inherit, and the party's custodians of orthodoxy heard the payload and answered the arithmetic accordingly. Historians of the debate note the asymmetry that followed: her book was answered in reviews, in Bauer's constructions, and eventually in Bukharin's full-length assault from the rival party, yet the questions it posed about the demand for the accumulating surplus kept resurfacing in each generation's crisis literature, reformulated by writers who sometimes did not know their source. A glossary records the debate's existence and its stakes without retrying it; the trial has already occupied a century, and the evidence is the dispute section above.

## Saving and investment in the departments' mirror

One further translation of the schemas into a familiar vocabulary is worth making explicitly, since it connects the construction to the categories in which modern macroeconomic argument is conducted. Saving, in the schemas' world, is the surplus value that owners do not consume; investment is that same magnitude embodied, as orders placed with the equipment department for additional plant and materials plus the wage advances for additional hands. The two are equal in the models by construction, not by market achievement, because the constructor sets the accumulation rate and the departments' proportions in one act. The equality's triviality inside the frame throws the non-trivial question outside it into relief: in an economy where savers and investors are different people making uncoordinated plans, what makes the magnitudes match, and what happens in the interval when they do not? Keynesian economics made that interval its home, locating fluctuations in the gap between intended saving and intended investment, and Kalecki, starting from the schemas themselves, had already located it there by a parallel road. The schemas therefore stand behind the modern debate in a double capacity, as the frame in which the equality is definitional and as the provocation that forced later theorists to explain the equality's real-world counterpart. Readers meeting the saving-investment identity in a textbook are meeting the crossing condition wearing modern dress, with the departments folded away and the same logical skeleton underneath: something must match something, in exact magnitudes, for the circuit to repeat, and a theory of the economy is largely an account of what does the matching or suffers the mismatch.

## Hilferding and organised capitalism: proportions under management

Rudolf Hilferding's Finance Capital of 1910 carried the schemas into a new question: what happens to the proportion problem when capitals combine? Cartels and trusts, on Hilferding's analysis, do within their branches what the schemas' constructor does on paper, fixing output and investment by agreement rather than by the blind pressure of prices, and a fully cartelised economy, he argued in a famous speculative passage, would approach a consciously regulated production in which the anarchy that enforces proportions through crisis is suspended, while the social character of the system, its foundation in wage labour and private appropriation, persists untouched. The passage founded the theory of organised capitalism and a long argument within the workers' movement about whether combination had changed the system's nature. For the schemas, the interest of the episode is formal: Hilferding saw that the proportion conditions define a task, and that the task can in principle be performed by agreement among producers as well as suffered through disequilibrium, which is the Kugelmann letter's distinction between the forms in which the proportion law asserts itself, restated for the age of the cartel. His critics replied that cartels regulate proportions inside branches while worsening them between branches, protecting their own returns at the expense of the uncartelised remainder, so that the enforcement problem migrates rather than dissolves. Both claims are compatible with the schemas, which specify what must be achieved and remain silent, here as everywhere, about the achiever.

## Buffers: the quiet variable in every proportion

Between the schemas' razor-edge balance and the real economy's tolerance for imbalance stands a variable the construction mentions only in passing and experience puts at the centre: stocks held as buffers. Inventories of materials, finished goods awaiting sale, spare capacity in plant and in the labour force, credit lines undrawn, all absorb the shocks that the models transmit instantly, letting a department produce out of proportion for a season while its stocks fall or rise in compensation. The history of production management can be written as an argument about how much buffer to hold. The craft and early factory systems held large stocks against every uncertainty; the logistics doctrines of the later twentieth century, prizing the release of capital locked in warehouses, drove inventories down towards just-in-time delivery, in which each stage produces against the next stage's immediate call. The schemas illuminate the trade-off exactly: a low-buffer economy approximates the construction's conditions, its proportions binding tightly and its disturbances propagating fast along the chain of deliveries, while a high-buffer economy purchases sluggishness at the price of idle stock. Neither setting is a refutation of the other or of the model; they are different distances from the model's limit case, and the distance is measurable in the stock-to-output ratios that statistical offices publish. A reader who has followed the argument this far will recognise the pattern one last time: the construction states the limit, practice chooses a distance from it, and the choice, like every choice the article has examined, is invisible until the frame makes it visible.

## Where services sit: transport, storage, and the immaterial product

The departments classify commodities, and a reader is entitled to ask where services fall, since so much modern employment produces no durable object. Marx's own treatment, given early in the second volume, assigns the transport industry to the sphere of production: its product is the change of location of things and persons, consumed in the very act of production, and freight transport adds value to the goods it moves exactly as a further stage of their manufacture would, its costs entering the schemas as part of the producing departments' outlays. Storage that preserves goods on their way to market is treated likewise, as a continuation of production within circulation, while purely personal services, the haircut, the concert, the lesson, are consumption purchased out of revenue and belong to the second department's world as final demand. The classification is more than tidiness. It determines which expenditures reproduce the economy's material basis and which merely draw on its surplus, and it explains why the schemas can absorb a service-heavy economy without structural change: the departments sort by destination, and a service consumed by a household and a service consumed by a production process fall on opposite sides of the line as cleanly as coal for the grate and coal for the furnace. Modern accounts, which wrestle with the same boundary in measuring output, reproduce the difficulty and, in their treatment of transport margins and distribution, much of the solution.

## A condition, not an identity: the crossing equality and Say's law

The last precision this article owes its readers is the distinction between the schemas' central equality and the identity associated with Say's law, since the two are persistently confused and the confusion reverses the construction's meaning. Say's identity asserts that supply creates its own demand in aggregate, that the value of what is produced is necessarily spent on products, so that general overproduction is impossible by definition. The crossing equality asserts something narrower and stronger: that one specific magnitude, the first department's income fund, must match another specific magnitude, the second department's replacement requirement, in a system where the aggregate identity can hold while the crossing fails. An economy can spend every penny of its income and still jam its reproduction, if the spending arrives in proportions the material structure cannot use, too little reaching the equipment trades, too much chasing consumption goods whose production was calibrated to a different wage bill. The schemas are thus not an elaboration of Say's law but its refutation in the only form that matters, a worked counterexample in which total demand equals total supply throughout and reproduction breaks anyway, on composition. Ricardo's side of the glut debate, which rested on the identity, cannot accommodate the example; the example is the schemas' quietest and most durable polemical achievement, and it is fitting that a glossary article should end its substantive work by isolating it, since a reader who can state the difference between an identity and a condition can be trusted with the rest of the literature unaided.

## The allocation share: Feldman's lever stated in words

The growth models descended from the schemas turn on a single decision variable, and it can be stated without a symbol. In any period, the economy's new investment capacity, itself the output of the capital-goods sector, must be divided between two destinations: part is installed in the capital-goods sector itself, enlarging the economy's ability to produce equipment in the future, and part is installed in the consumer-goods sector, enlarging the flow of goods households enjoy. The share assigned to the first destination is the lever. Give it a high setting and the economy sacrifices present consumption to compound its productive base, each period's equipment sector growing into a larger supplier for every later period, until the accumulated base supports a consumption flow far above the starting level. Give it a low setting and consumption rises early and pleasantly, while the base grows slowly and the later periods inherit the modesty of the early choice. Feldman's demonstration was that the economy's long-run growth rate is an increasing function of that share, a result the schemas' chained years already display in arithmetical form, since the first department's expansion is what permits every subsequent expansion. The lever also states the political economy of planning in one line: the share cannot be set by the market signals of the present, because its costs fall on present consumers and its benefits on future ones, and the entire controversy between the machine-first strategists and the wage-goods critics, recorded above, was a controversy over its setting conducted in the schemas' own vocabulary. No reader who understands the lever needs the algebra to follow any of the historical debates this article has surveyed, which is the notation policy's justification in miniature.

## Departments inside a single firm

A final clarification removes a misunderstanding the diagrams invite. The departments are functions, not firms, and a single vertically integrated enterprise can house both: a conglomerate that mines its own ore, smelts its own steel, builds its own machinery, and also operates canteens, housing, and retail outlets for its workforce performs the first department's work and a slice of the second's under one ownership. The crossing exchange then occurs inside the firm's accounts, as transfer prices rather than market sales, and the balance conditions bind those internal transfers exactly as they bind market exchanges, since the material requirements are indifferent to the legal form of their satisfaction. The point matters beyond tidiness. It shows that the schemas analyse the economy's material organisation, not its corporate map, and that changes in industrial structure, integration, outsourcing, the redrawing of firm boundaries, leave the proportion problem untouched even as they rearrange who experiences it as a market event and who as a planning decision. A century that has watched production reorganise itself across firms, platforms, and borders without the proportion problem ever dissolving has confirmed the abstraction's soundness in the most practical way available.

## For students and exam candidates

The schemas reward a specific study discipline, and it is not reading. Take the simple reproduction figures and recompute every exchange until the circuit closes in your hands without the text open; then perturb one figure, as this article's worked disruptions do, and trace the damage in words before checking it against the model. Examiners on this topic set two families of question, the mechanical and the judgemental, and they reward different preparations. For the mechanical family, definitions, conditions, and the location of the schemas in the second volume, the [marxist economics exam answers](/2016/08/15/marxist-economics-exam-answers/) in this series drills the expected forms. For the judgemental family, whether the schemas prove stability, breakdown, or neither, the winning habit is the demonstration not description rule applied aloud: name the construction, name the added premise in each famous reading, and decline to let arithmetic carry more than arithmetic can. Keep a card with the two conditions stated in words, front and back, in a [VaultBook study card on the reproduction schemas](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html), and test yourself on which department's magnitude stands on which side of each equality, since transposing them is the classic examination error and the easiest to eliminate.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The numerical example is your best instrument: set the simple reproduction figures as a worked exercise, require students to close the circuit unaided, and then assign each seminar member one perturbation, a changed coefficient, a refused accumulation, a mistimed replacement, to follow through two periods in writing. The exercise exposes in an hour what lectures conceal for weeks, namely which students hold the construction and which hold its vocabulary. The dispute then teaches itself if it is staged honestly: give Tugan-Baranovsky, Luxemburg, and Bauer one seminar each, with the rule that every claim must be labelled as arithmetic or as added premise before it may be debated, and let Grossmann's extension of Bauer close the sequence, since nothing else so efficiently teaches what a model is. Warn the room, early and in terms, against the two seductions of the topic, the harmony reading and the breakdown reading, and against the descriptive fallacy that feeds both. Assessment should ask for the conditions in words before it asks for opinions about them, and should credit the answer that states what the schemas cannot settle as highly as the answer that states what they can.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research frontier on this topic is documentary and statistical rather than exegetical. The manuscripts' stratigraphy, now accessible in the historical-critical edition, allows the schemas' development across Marx's drafts to be dated and compared, and several familiar interpretive quarrels change shape when the layers are separated. The lineage into Leontief's tables, Feldman's model, and the material balances is documented in archives on three continents and remains underexploited by historians of economics, particularly the Soviet statistical milieu of the nineteen twenties in which the balance method and the young Leontief's formation intersect. For journalists and for researchers approaching from other fields, the transferable asset is the proportion discipline itself: the schemas supply the questions to put to any economy's accounts, what replaces the capital stock, which branches supply the investment goods, where the internal traffic of the producing sector runs, and they join naturally to the empirical programme surveyed in the article on [testing marxist economics](/2016/08/01/testing-marxist-economics/). Cite the schemas as a construction, never as a finding; attribute the famous conclusions to their actual authors, the disputants, rather than to the arithmetic; and treat any source that reports the models as describing a real economy as unreliable on this topic until independently verified.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What are the reproduction schemas?

They are a pair of arithmetical constructions in the second volume of Capital in which all social production is gathered into two departments and the exchanges between them are traced for a year. One department turns out goods destined for further production, the other goods destined for personal consumption, and each product is resolved into replacement value, wage value, and surplus. By choosing figures that fit together, Marx exhibits the terms on which a capitalist economy can renew its activity at an unchanged scale, and then the altered terms on which it can renew activity at a growing scale. The constructions are analytical instruments, not portraits of any historical economy.

### Q: What are Department I and Department II?

Department One is the collection of all branches whose output serves as an input to production: mining, metallurgy, machine building, industrial chemicals, construction of plant, and the like. Department Two gathers the branches whose output serves personal consumption: food processing, clothing, household goods, and services sold to individuals. The sorting rule follows the buyer and the use, not the physical character of the thing sold, so output from a single works can fall on both sides of the line when part is bought by factories and part by families. Marx additionally separates the second department into a branch supplying necessities and a branch supplying luxuries, because their purchasers differ.

### Q: What is simple reproduction?

It is the hypothetical case in which the scale of production repeats without growth, every owner consuming the whole surplus as personal revenue and no part of it being turned into fresh capital. Marx adopts the case as a starting point even though he regards it as foreign to capitalism's real tendencies, because the exchanges needed merely to keep the system standing are already intricate, and isolating them first makes the further complications of growth easier to follow. Under this case the year's product must be so composed that each department can replace exactly what it used up and feed exactly the people attached to it, with nothing left over and nothing missing.

### Q: What is expanded reproduction?

It is the case in which owners set aside a portion of the surplus and convert it into additional means of production and additional labour power, so that the next period begins from a larger base. Marx treats this as the normal case for the system under study. For the enlargement to proceed without a hitch, the equipment-producing department must turn out more than both departments need for mere replacement, since the material for growth has to exist before it can be installed. The consumer-goods department is then obliged to enlarge in step, hiring more hands whose wages create the market for its extra output, and the two expansions have to be matched in size.

### Q: What conditions must hold for reproduction to work?

At constant scale, the spending power generated inside the equipment department, its wages together with its surplus, has to match, in value, the plant and materials that the consumer-goods department has used up and must restore; when the two magnitudes coincide, the cross-department exchange clears exactly. For growth, the first magnitude has to be the larger of the two, and the margin between them is what finances enlargement on both sides. Behind those headline equalities stand quieter requirements that the text works through: money laid aside for replacing durable plant has to match spending on such plant in the same year, and reserves accumulated for future investment have to be offset by other owners drawing theirs down.

### Q: Do the schemas prove crisis is impossible?

No, and the construction itself says as much. The models display configurations in which every exchange is completed, which establishes that no contradiction inside the accounting makes breakdown unavoidable; it does not establish that real economies locate those configurations. Marx remarks in the same chapters that the requirements of undisturbed reproduction double as a catalogue of ways reproduction can be disturbed, and he flags the replacement of durable equipment as a point where the required coincidence is especially fragile. Later writers who claimed a guarantee of stability from the models were reading a possibility result as a promise, against the warning built into the text.

### Q: How did Luxemburg use the schemas?

Rosa Luxemburg subjected the growth case to a sustained interrogation in her book on the accumulation of capital, asking where the purchasers were to come from for the swelling portion of output that represented surplus earmarked for reinvestment. Surveying the closed society of owners and wage earners that the models assume, she judged the demand insufficient and concluded that accumulation feeds necessarily on buyers beyond the capitalist pale, in peasant, artisan, and colonial milieux that the system progressively absorbs. The schemas served her as the prosecution's chief exhibit: their failure, as she read it, to close the circle of demand was the formal root of her theory of imperialism as an economic compulsion rather than a policy choice.

### Q: Are the schemas the ancestor of input-output analysis?

Yes, by documented descent rather than by resemblance alone. Wassily Leontief, who built the first statistical input-output table for the American economy, had written his doctoral study on the economy as a circular flow and placed his project in a line running from Quesnay's Tableau through Marx's two-department constructions. What he took over was the conception of an economy as a system of interlocking deliveries whose consistency can be checked as a whole. What he added was measurement: dozens of industries instead of two departments, coefficients estimated from statistics, and a computational method for working out the total output any final demand implies. The ancestry is acknowledged in his own writings and in the standard histories.

### Q: Where are the reproduction schemas in Capital?

They occupy the closing portion of the second volume, the part devoted to the reproduction and circulation of society's aggregate capital rather than to the fortunes of a single firm. That part begins by restating the problem at the level of the whole economy, continues with a critical review of earlier treatments by Quesnay and by Adam Smith, and then gives one extended chapter to reproduction at a constant scale and a closing chapter on accumulation and enlarged reproduction. The second volume as a whole was assembled by Engels from Marx's drafts and published after Marx's death, and the reproduction chapters show the joins between drafts of different dates more plainly than most.

### Q: Are the reproduction schemas still used?

Their direct descendants are in daily use, and the original constructions retain their place in teaching. National statistical offices compile inter-industry tables in the input-output tradition for their accounts and for policy simulation, and growth theorists work with multisector models whose ancestry runs through the two-sector constructions that Soviet and Indian planners adapted. Within economics education, the original schemas are set as the classical statement of inter-sectoral balance and as an exercise in what formal models establish. Scholars also continue to argue over the interpretations the models attracted, the harmony, breakdown, and external-market readings, so the text functions simultaneously as an instrument, a syllabus item, and a historical document.

### Q: How does the demonstration not description rule change the schemas' use?

The rule confines every legitimate inference to the register of possibility. A user bound by it may cite the schemas to refute a claim that capitalist reproduction is arithmetically incoherent, and to identify the proportions on which smooth reproduction depends, but may not cite them as evidence that any economy has achieved those proportions or must fail to. Applied consistently, the rule dissolves most of the famous quarrel at a stroke: the harmony reading is exposed as an inference the construction cannot carry, the breakdown reading likewise, and what remains in dispute is a set of empirical and institutional questions about adjustment, credit, and demand that the models were never designed to answer. The rule therefore converts a sterile controversy into a research agenda.

### Q: What should a student separate between simple and expanded reproduction?

Three contrasts carry most of the examinable weight. First, the destination of the surplus: wholly consumed as revenue in the one case, partly capitalised in the other. Second, the headline equality: at constant scale the equipment department's wage-and-surplus fund matches the consumption department's replacement needs exactly, while in the growth case it must exceed them, the excess being the fund of enlargement. Third, the logical status of each case: constant-scale reproduction is an admitted fiction used to isolate the exchanges, whereas the growth case models the system's normal tendency and therefore attracts the substantive disputes. A student who can state all three contrasts, and give one reason the fiction is methodologically useful, holds the distinction in the form examiners reward.

### Q: Why do the two departments have to exchange in exact proportions?

Because each holds, in physical form, what the other needs and cannot make for itself. The equipment department ends the year with machinery and materials but nothing its people can eat or wear; the consumption department ends it with food and clothing but with its own plant worn down by a year's work. Trade between them is not a convenience layered on production but a stage of production itself, and it can only clear at quantities set by their respective surpluses and deficits. Overshoot on either side leaves one department with unsold stock and the other with unmet needs in the same movement, which is why proportion, rather than total output alone, governs whether the year can repeat.

### Q: How does the two conditions table state the balance requirement?

The table arrays the two cases, constant scale and growth, across the two departments, giving for each row the composition of the product, the purchase that department must make from its partner, the requirement expressed in words, and the failure that follows if the requirement is missed. Its figures are the round thousands of Marx's own illustration, chosen so the equalities can be checked mentally: the reader can see the equipment department's spending fund of two thousand meeting the consumption department's replacement bill of two thousand in the stationary case, and exceeding a smaller bill by the growth margin in the other. The verbal statement is deliberate; the requirement is a relation among named magnitudes, and the table keeps the names attached.

### Q: What separates a schema from a growth model in this tradition?

A growth model contains a mechanism that generates a path through time: rules of behaviour for saving and investment, a treatment of technical change, and some account of how the economy moves from one period's position to the next. A schema contains none of those; its rates of accumulation are assumptions entered by the constructor, its techniques are frozen within the period, and it certifies only that the positions it displays are mutually consistent. The difference explains the historical division of labour in this literature. Marx supplied schemas; successors who wanted growth theory, Feldman with his two-sector planning model and von Neumann with his expanding equilibrium, had to add the mechanisms themselves, and their additions, not the schemas, are what their models stand or fall by.
