## A Political Reading of the Feud

Cultural materialism reads the feud in Romeo and Juliet as a contest of power before it reads the lovers as a romance, and the verdict of the method is plain from the first page of the play. The Prologue announces "two households, both alike in dignity" (Prologue, line 1) locked in "new mutiny" (Prologue, line 3), and the tragedy that follows is the record of what that mutiny costs a city whose ruler cannot rule it. A cultural materialist reading holds that the deaths in the tomb are produced by institutions: two great households competing for standing, a prince whose law threatens more than it performs, a marriage market that prices a daughter, and a church that can bless a union but cannot protect one. The love story is real, and the method never denies its force. The claim is narrower and stronger. Whatever the lovers feel, the conditions that decide whether feeling becomes marriage or burial are political conditions, set by the distribution of authority in Verona, and the play knows it. Its final speech is given not to a parent, a priest, or a survivor of the romance but to the head of state, and that speech is an audit of government: "All are punished" (5.3.295).

The method that produces this reading was named in Britain in the middle of the 1980s, in the foreword to Political Shakespeare: Essays in Cultural Materialism (1985), edited by Jonathan Dollimore and Alan Sinfield. Its founders defined it against two opponents at once. Against the older criticism, it refused to treat the play as a vessel of permanent human nature floating free of the moment that made it. Against quieter forms of historicism, it refused to pretend that the critic stands outside politics. The foreword states the commitment without decoration: cultural materialism "registers its commitment to the transformation of a social order which exploits people on grounds of race, gender and class." A reading built on that sentence will ask of any text three questions. What order does the text show? Where does that order crack under its own contradictions? And whose side does the reading take? Applied to Verona, those questions turn a feud usually treated as background scenery into the play's central political machine.

One clarification belongs at the head of the account, because the record is often misstated. Neither Dollimore nor Sinfield published a freestanding reading of Romeo and Juliet. Sinfield's Faultlines: Cultural Materialism and the Politics of Dissident Reading (1992) builds its Shakespeare arguments on Othello, Macbeth, Henry V, and Julius Caesar. Dollimore's Radical Tragedy (1984), the founding study of the school, reads the drama of Shakespeare and his contemporaries for its subversion of orthodox ideology, and its sustained Shakespearean exhibits are the great tragedies rather than the Veronese play. The cultural materialist account of the feud is therefore a method's account, assembled from a published programme and tested against the text scene by scene. That is not a weakness. A method that can only repeat its founders' examples is a commentary tradition; a method that can be carried to a play its founders left alone, and made to yield findings the text confirms, is a working instrument. The pages that follow carry it to Verona and report where the text resists as well as where it yields.

## What Cultural Materialism Is: History, Theory, Commitment, Text

The foreword to Political Shakespeare describes cultural materialism as a combination of historical context, theoretical method, political commitment, and textual analysis, and each of the four elements does distinct work in a reading of the feud. Historical context means the play is placed inside the practices of its own period: household government, patriarchal marriage negotiation, civic order and its enforcement, the church's jurisdiction over clandestine unions. Theoretical method means the reading works with explicit concepts, above all ideology, the set of representations through which an order makes itself seem natural, and dissidence, the pressure that an order generates against itself. Political commitment means the reading declares its interest in the present distribution of power rather than disguising preference as neutrality. Textual analysis means none of the other three licenses inattention to the words on the page. The method stands or falls on lines, and a cultural materialist reading of the feud must be won from the Prologue, the street scenes, the Prince's speeches, and the tomb, or not won at all.

### What does the word materialism commit the method to?

Materialism commits the method to explaining culture by the conditions that produce it: institutions, property, labour, law, and the bodies that live under them, rather than by spirit, genius, or an unchanging human essence. A feud, on this account, is not a weather of the soul.

It is a practice maintained by households with retainers, reputations, marriageable children, and honour to defend, and it persists because the arrangement rewards the men who sustain it. The word rules out readings in which hatred floats free of everything that feeds it. Where a traditional account sees ancient grudge as a given of the story, a materialist account asks who is paid, in coin or standing, to keep the grudge warm, and the play answers that question in detail, from the servants who open the action to the fathers who close it in gold.

The term itself was adapted from Raymond Williams, whose Marxism and Literature (1977) used cultural materialism for a theory of culture as a productive process, made of institutions and formations rather than reflected from an economic base at a distance. Dollimore and Sinfield took the term into Shakespeare studies and sharpened it for polemic. Graham Holderness, a contributor to the same movement, called the result "a politicised form of historiography," and the description is exact: the past is recovered, but recovered with its conflicts left in and its winners' descriptions treated as evidence to be cross-examined rather than conclusions to be repeated. In the second edition of Political Shakespeare (1994), Dollimore's introduction, "Shakespeare, Cultural Materialism and the New Historicism," restated the programme for a profession that had begun to merge the British school with its American counterpart, and insisted on the differences: an inheritance from Marxism and feminism, an attention to subcultures and dissident groups, and a refusal to surrender the critic's present commitments as the price of historical rigour.

The movement's wider shelf confirms the programme. Dollimore's Radical Tragedy argued, against a long orthodoxy, that Renaissance tragedy does not simply ratify the providential and hierarchical beliefs of its age; at its strongest it subjects those beliefs to pressure, staging the failure of the consolations that official ideology offered for suffering and death. Sinfield's Literature, Politics and Culture in Post-War Britain (1989) carried the same method into the critic's own century, treating the postwar settlement's idea of good culture as an instrument of class power. Faultlines then gave the school its most precise instrument for reading early modern texts: the claim that a dominant order cannot help producing fault-lines, points where its own criteria of plausibility contradict one another and dissident sense can be made. Each of these books matters for the feud, because each supplies a tool the reading uses: ideology as a lived arrangement rather than a set of propositions, the text as a site where an order's story about itself can fail, and the critic as a declared partisan of the order's casualties.

A frequent misunderstanding should be cleared away before the reading begins. Cultural materialism does not claim that the playwright sat down to write a political pamphlet, and it does not need to. The method's claims are about what the text does within the arrangements it represents, not about a message smuggled inside it. A play can register the contradictions of household government, princely weakness, and the marriage market with perfect fidelity while its author holds whatever private opinions he holds. The evidence is the staging: which speeches are given authority and then shown failing, which institutions are trusted with the lovers' fate and then default, which deaths the plot prices as necessary. Verona's government fails on stage in full view. A criticism forbidden to discuss power would have to describe that failure as atmosphere. Cultural materialism describes it as the subject.

## The Founders' Instruments: Dollimore and Sinfield

A method is known by its tools, and the two founders forged theirs in books that a reader of the feud should have in hand. Dollimore's Radical Tragedy (1984) appeared a year before Political Shakespeare and supplied the school's historical thesis. The orthodoxy it opposed held that Renaissance drama, whatever its surface turbulence, finally endorses the providential order of its age: rebellion is punished, hierarchy is restored, and the audience is reconciled to the arrangement of the world. Dollimore's counter-reading, sustained across Shakespeare and the Jacobean dramatists, finds the drama working the other way at its most serious: staging the collapse of providential consolation under the pressure of power, suffering, and death, and relocating value in human defiance rather than cosmic design. His term for the process was decentring, the displacement of the supposedly fixed centres, God, king, and essential human nature, from which the period's official meanings were issued. Transposed to Verona, the thesis predicts a play in which the centres fail one by one: the Prince's law, the fathers' authority, the church's sacrament, and finally the heavens themselves, whose stars are blamed by the Prologue and never once intervene. The prediction fits the text with unusual exactness, and the fit is evidence that the instrument was forged on this drama's true grain, even though its maker spent his pages on other plays.

Sinfield's instrument is finer and, for the feud, more decisive. Faultlines (1992) begins from a problem in the theory of ideology: if the dominant order supplies the very language in which its subjects think, how is dissent so much as formulable? His answer relocates the question. A dominant ideology is not a seamless web but a set of stories an order tells to make its arrangements plausible, and the stories must be assembled from the order's own commitments, which do not cohere. The early modern state preached degree and sponsored advancement; the church preached charity and administered exclusion; the household preached obedience and required enterprise. Where the stories conflict, plausibility cracks, and through the crack a subject of the order can formulate a reading of it that the order did not authorize. Sinfield calls such unauthorized sense-making dissidence, and he is careful about its scope: dissidence operates with reference to the dominant, borrows its concepts, and can always be partly reabsorbed, but its existence is structural, not accidental, because the fault-lines that permit it are produced by the order itself. A regime does not get to choose whether its plausibility structure has seams. It only gets to police them, and policing, as Verona shows, is the confession that the seams are there.

Two consequences follow for the feud. The first is a reading protocol: hunt the seams. In practice this means attending to the moments where Verona's official stories contradict one another in a single speech or a single scene, the Prince fining what he cannot hang, Capulet commanding the obedience he suspends at his own feast, the Friar preaching the sacrament he must hide. The second consequence is a defence of the lovers that the romance tradition cannot mount. On the entrapment model, the marriage is a licensed transgression the order tolerates and then exhibits. On the fault-line model, the marriage is what an order's incoherence makes thinkable: the church's own doctrine of consent, that a marriage is made by the parties' vows rather than the fathers' contract, is one of Verona's official stories, and the lovers simply act on it at full strength. The Friar does not import a foreign doctrine into the city. He applies the city's own sacramental teaching at the point where it contradicts the household's property teaching, and the catastrophe is the sound of the two stories colliding. That collision, not a star and not a flaw, is the tragedy's mechanism in the school's mature account.

The founders' later work kept the instruments pointed at the institutions that certify readings. Sinfield's chapters on the profession itself, on Shakespeare in the United States and on the cultural politics of the academy, argue that editions, syllabi, and heritage celebrations are also arrangements of power, deciding which Verona each generation is permitted to see. Dollimore's Sexual Dissidence (1991) extended the method into the history of sexuality, tracing how categories of deviance are manufactured by the orders that police them. Neither development is a digression from the feud. A method that reads the Prince's proclamations as performances of a failing authority will read its own profession's proclamations the same way, and the romance packaging of the play, its marketing as the supreme love story, becomes on this account one more plausibility structure with seams: the packaging must promise transcendence and sell tickets, celebrate the lovers and warn against them, and the contradictions are available to any student who has been taught to look. Teaching the feud by this method is therefore partly an exercise in showing readers the seams in the book in their own hands.

## The Feud as a Contest of Power, Not a Private Quarrel

The standard account of the feud treats it as a premise: an ancient grudge, cause unstated, that exists so the love story can be forbidden. The cultural materialist account begins by refusing the premise's passivity. What the play actually stages is an armed competition between two corporations for precedence in a city, conducted by retainers in the streets, restrained intermittently by a nervous state, and reproduced in each generation as an inheritance. The full anatomy of that competition, episode by episode, is set out in [the account of the feud between the Capulets and Montagues and the hatred that sustains it](/2022/04/01/capulet-montague-feud-explained/); the reading here concentrates on what the competition is made of. Its materials are visible in the first scene, and the first scene belongs not to the lovers, not to the fathers, but to servants.

Sampson and Gregory, Capulet's men, open the play by converting employment into grievance. Their banter runs on the house's quarrel as a condition of service: the Montague men are to be thrust to the wall, the maids to the wall as well, and the exchange of thumb-biting that follows is a negotiated provocation in which each side tries to start a fight the law will blame on the other. Sampson's care is legal before it is martial: "Let us take the law of our sides; let them begin" (1.1). The line is a small masterpiece of the feud's real mechanics. The violence is corporate, the liability is managed, and the men performing it own no part of the quarrel they are about to bleed for. The materialist point is not that the servants are cynical. It is that the feud has a labour force, and the play insists on showing the labour before the leadership. When the citizens arrive with clubs, crying "Down with the Capulets! Down with the Montagues!" (1.1), the city itself enters as a third party whose patience is a political fact the Prince will spend the play failing to organize into order.

The households' equality is the feud's engine, and the Prologue states it as a structural fact: "both alike in dignity" (Prologue, line 1). Rivals who are unequal settle; the weaker submits, the stronger absorbs. Rivals who are exactly matched can only continue, since every encounter is a referendum on a precedence neither can concede. The comparative anatomy of that parity, rank by rank and servant by servant, is drawn in [the study of the two households and the distribution of power and class in Verona](/2022/04/22/two-households-class-power-verona/). What the materialist reading adds is the dynamic: dignity in this play is not a possession but a performance that must be renewed in public, which is why the feud keeps returning to streets, squares, and feasts, the places where standing is witnessed. Tybalt's fury at the feast is not temper. It is quality control. A Montague dancing unchallenged in the Capulet hall is a depreciation of the house's stock, and Tybalt prices it exactly so: "This, by his voice, should be a Montague. / Fetch me my rapier, boy" (1.5).

### Why does the feud have no stated cause, and why does the omission matter?

The feud has no stated cause because its function in Verona's order does not depend on a cause. A grievance with an origin could be audited, compensated, and closed; a grievance without one can only be inherited, and inheritance is the point.

The Prologue reaches back to "ancient grudge" (Prologue, line 3) and stops there, and no character in five acts ever names the originating offence, because none of them needs it. The feud justifies the households' retinues, disciplines their young men, prices their daughters, and gives two old men a public identity larger than age. A cause would be a liability to every one of those uses. The omission is therefore not a gap in the playwright's information. It is the most precise political fact in the play: a competition for standing that has outlived its pretext and continues on the energy of the arrangement itself, consuming servants, nephews, and heirs as running costs.

Against this machinery, the love story's position changes. In the romance reading, the feud is the obstacle and the love is the subject. In the materialist reading, the love is the probe and the feud is the subject: the marriage is the one event that tests every institution in the city, and each institution fails the test in its own way. The Friar wagers that a union of the heirs can convert the competition into alliance, "to turn your households' rancour to pure love" (2.3), and the wager is political before it is pastoral. The Prince's law, the fathers' authority, the church's sacrament, and the city's peace are all staked on two teenagers, and all default. What dies in the tomb is not only a pair of lovers but a hypothesis: that Verona's order could reform itself from inside, by absorption, without the order itself being called to account. The ending calls it to account instead.

## Honour: The Story That Makes the Arrangement Plausible

Every durable arrangement requires a story that makes obedience to it feel like self-respect, and Verona's story is honour. The materialist reading treats honour neither as a timeless masculine essence nor as a decorative code pasted onto the feud's surface. It treats honour as the feud's ideology in Sinfield's precise sense: the plausibility structure through which two corporations recruit the bodies that fight for them, converting a competition for precedence, in which the fighters own no share, into a personal obligation from which refusal marks a man as ruined. The conversion is visible in the play's smallest transactions. A bitten thumb is nothing; a bitten thumb interpreted through honour is a debt that must be collected in public or carried as shame. The code's genius is accounting. It denominates every slight as a liability and every act of violence as a payment, so that the feud's participants experience an economic system as a moral one, and audit themselves by its ledger without ever asking who issued the currency.

Act 3 Scene 1 is the ledger's audit scene, and it shows the ideology surviving direct refutation, which is what ideologies are for. Romeo, married that morning into the Capulet house, attempts to resign from the code in terms the code cannot process: "I do protest, I never injured thee, / But love thee better than thou canst devise / Till thou shalt know the reason of my love" (3.1). The speech is a fault-line made audible. By the church's story, Tybalt is now Romeo's kin, and the feud's central relation has been abolished by sacrament; by honour's story, a Montague declining a challenge is a coward advertising his shame. Tybalt answers from inside the code's arithmetic, "Boy, this shall not excuse the injuries / That thou hast done me; therefore turn and draw" (3.1), and the injuries he cites are real entries, the gatecrashing of the feast above all. What he cannot perceive is that the ledger itself has been repudiated by a higher jurisdiction his world also professes. Mercutio perceives only the shame, and pays Romeo's refused debt with his own body, cursing the currency as he dies of it. The scene's horror, for the materialist reading, is its rationality: every man acts correctly by the story he inhabits, and the stories are incompatible, and the incompatibility is the city's constitution.

The code's distribution across the city completes the exhibit. Honour governs the young men's swords, but it also governs the marriage market, where a daughter's reputation is a house's tradable credit; it governs the feast, where precedence at table is audited by every guest; it governs the tomb, where the fathers discharge their final obligations in gold because gold is honour's most legible denomination. Even the Prince is inside the story he polices: his kinsmen fight, his fine is assessed in the language of amercement, and his closing grief is for a brace of kinsmen lost, the sovereign as one more householder whose kin the feud has taxed. A code that reaches from the servants' thumbs to the sovereign's family is not a theme in the play. It is the operating system of its world, and the cultural materialist reading of the feud is, at bottom, the claim that the play knows its operating system is a made thing: issued by interested parties, maintained by payments, and open, at its seams, to the refusal the lovers embody. What criticism owes such a play is not admiration for the code's glamour, which the verse supplies in abundance, but an account of the code's manufacture and price. The feud's young men die beautifully and in verse. The reading's task is to keep the bill attached to the beauty.

The bill, once itemized, has a further use. It explains the play's durability in argument rather than in sentiment: generations of readers have sensed that the catastrophe is overdetermined, that too many hands are on the rope, and the honour economy is the mechanism that puts the hands there, each pair convinced it is only settling an account.

## The Prologue as Official History: Star-Crossed as an Alibi

Before any character speaks, the play's events are narrated to the audience in a fourteen-line summary, and a materialist reading begins by asking the unglamorous question: who benefits from this version? The Chorus's account is complete and confident. Two households, an ancient grudge, a pair of star-crossed lovers, a death-marked love, and a burial that ends the strife: "the continuance of their parents' rage, / Which, but their children's end, nought could remove" (Prologue). Every term of the summary assigns causality upward to the heavens or backward to the grudge, and none of it assigns causality to the arrangements the play is about to exhibit: the Prince's tolerated disorder, the households' marriage market, the church's divided doctrine. The Prologue is, in the strictest sense, an ideology in sonnet form, the story the order would tell about the catastrophe if the order got to write the programme notes. In a sense it did: choruses were the theatre's official voice, and this one speaks with the serene fatalism of a government communique.

The play then spends five acts auditing its own programme notes. The stars, invoked in the Prologue and again by Romeo at the crisis, "Then I defy you, stars!" (5.1), are the explanation of last resort for characters inside the machine; the audience, who has watched the machine work, is positioned to know better. Every link of the catastrophe has been shown with its human and institutional fastenings: a statute unenforced, a sentence bargained, a treaty accelerated, a letter quarantined, a poison legally sold to a starving man by a shopkeeper the law made desperate. To call the outcome star-crossed after that exhibition is not an interpretation. It is a refusal of the evidence, and the play's structure makes the refusal feel like one: the Chorus's serenity is never recovered, and the closing scene replaces the Prologue's tidy fatalism with inquests, confessions, and a prince itemizing his own negligence. The sonnet said the children's end would remove the rage. The scene shows the rage's owners converting the end into monuments and the state converting it into narrative, "For never was a story of more woe" (5.3), the official history already in production before the bodies are cold.

This is the point at which the school's founding polemic and the play most completely coincide. Dollimore's decentring thesis held that Renaissance tragedy at its strongest withdraws its assent from the official centres of meaning, providence above all, and the present play performs the withdrawal in its architecture: it opens with the providential account, in the period's most prestigious lyric form, and then dismantles that account scene by scene until nothing is left standing but institutions and the people they failed. A reader who finishes the play still believing the Prologue has been contained by the text's first fourteen lines, and the materialist reading exists to break exactly that containment. The feud's first victory is over its audience's explanation of it. Teaching the play whole means refusing the victory.

## Escalus: A Prince Whose Law Threatens More Than It Performs

No figure in the play gains more from the materialist reading than Prince Escalus, and none is more diminished by the romance reading, where he dwindles into a device for banishments and epitaphs. Read politically, he is the play's study of a state that has lost the monopoly of violence and governs by proclamation. He appears three times, and the three appearances form a descending graph of authority. In the first he threatens death and is defied by events. In the second he bargains a death sentence down to exile under pressure from both houses. In the third he presides over the wreckage and includes himself in the indictment. The feud's deepest political fact is not that two households are violent. It is that Verona's sovereign can be measured, scene by scene, failing to be sovereign.

The first entrance sets the pattern. Escalus arrives over a street brawl with a speech of genuine grandeur, "Rebellious subjects, enemies to peace" (1.1), and its rhetoric concedes the reality it protests: "Three civil brawls, bred of an airy word, / By thee, old Capulet, and Montague, / Have thrice disturbed the quiet of our streets" (1.1). Thrice. The sentence that follows, "If ever you disturb our streets again, / Your lives shall pay the forfeit of the peace" (1.1), is a capital statute proclaimed by a ruler who has already tolerated three breaches of the peace it retroactively declares. The materialist reading does not mock the Prince. It notes the structure: a law whose penalty escalates as its credibility falls, addressed to men rich enough to treat proclamation as weather. Capulet and Montague are dismissed on their own recognizance, summoned to judgement at leisure, and the scene's last word on enforcement is the old men's exit, unhurried and unbound.

### Why does Escalus commute the death sentence to exile after the street fight in Act 3?

Escalus commutes the sentence because his state cannot afford its own law. Executing the Montague heir under the first act's statute would convert a brawl into a blood debt between the throne and a great house; pardoning the killer of Tybalt would confess that the statute was theatre.

Romeo has killed Tybalt, the Prince's kinswoman's son and the Capulet heir in waiting, hours after Mercutio, the Prince's own kinsman, died under Romeo's guard. Exile is the instrument of a ruler purchasing quiet from both corporations at once, priced in the currency of a young man's life chances. The Prince says as much in the act of sentencing: "I have an interest in your hate's proceeding, / My blood for your rude brawls doth lie a-bleeding; / But I'll amerce you with so strong a fine / That you shall all repent the loss of mine" (3.1). Government by fine is government that has conceded the streets.

The second appearance rewards a closer look at who petitions and who prevails. Lady Capulet demands the statute: Romeo slew Tybalt, Romeo must not live. Montague pleads the feud's own jurisprudence, that Romeo only executed the law's sentence on the man who killed Mercutio. Benvolio narrates. The Prince's verdict splits every claim, and the splitting is the politics. Romeo is exiled "for that offence" (3.1), the houses are fined in prospect, and the actual killer of the Prince's kinsman is punished by a penalty whose enforcement depends on Romeo's voluntary departure before sunrise. Within hours the machinery of private arrangement, the Friar's plot, the Nurse's errands, Capulet's wedding contract, is running again at full speed, and the state's one intervention has had the single effect of removing the one man whose presence the plot to save Juliet requires. A stronger state would have made the tragedy impossible. That is the materialist verdict on Escalus: not a tyrant, not a fool, but the presiding officer of an order whose weakness is the tragedy's enabling condition.

The third appearance completes the graph. Over the bodies, the Prince's language turns judicial and theological at once, and its most remarkable feature is its distribution of guilt. "Capulet! Montague! / See what a scourge is laid upon your hate, / That heaven finds means to kill your joys with love" (5.3) arraigns the fathers; then the sentence widens: "And I for winking at your discords too / Have lost a brace of kinsmen. All are punished" (5.3). The head of state enters his own name in the charge sheet, for the precise offence the materialist reading has tracked from the first scene: winking, the toleration of disorder that a weak authority practises and calls peace. It is a finer political moment than any reconciliation that follows, because it locates the catastrophe not in a star, a flaw, or a feud alone, but in a structure of government that knew, tolerated, fined, and buried.

## The Household as an Institution: Marriage, Obedience, and the Price of a Daughter

If the feud is a competition between corporations, the household is the corporation's internal government, and the play examines that government with the same cold attention it gives the Prince. A great Veronese household in the drama is a unit of property, labour, and alliance: it holds a palace, maintains servants and retainers, trades on a name, and reproduces itself through marriages negotiated as treaties. Juliet is the Capulet succession. Her brotherless position makes her the vessel of the house's future, and every scene of her courtship is conducted in the vocabulary of transfer. Paris, the Prince's kinsman, sues for her as a connection to the throne; Capulet first delays the treaty on grounds of her age, "My child is yet a stranger in the world" (1.2), then accelerates it in Act 3 as an instrument for managing a household in grief, and finally enforces it as a test of sovereignty: "An you be mine, I'll give you to my friend. / And you be not, hang, beg, starve, die in the streets" (3.5). The ultimatum's sequence is the household's constitution stated aloud. A daughter who is his is an asset to be bestowed; a daughter who is not his is refuse to be expelled. Affection does not enter the clause.

The feast scene exposes the same government under internal strain, and it is the finest single exhibit for the method's claim that Verona's order is fissured rather than monolithic. Tybalt, the house's sword arm and its most ideologically reliable young man, identifies the intruder and reaches for enforcement. Capulet overrules him in terms that reveal the actual constitution of household authority: the master will be obeyed in his own hall, the guest list is his prerogative, and the nephew's zeal is recoded as insolence, "Am I the master here, or you? Go to" (1.5). Two principles of the house collide in the exchange. Tybalt embodies the feud as doctrine, in which an unchallenged Montague is a loss; Capulet embodies the feud as strategy, in which a feast is a display of magnificence whose credit a brawl would destroy, and whose guest, moreover, is rumoured an honest gentleman. The house survives the evening by its master's pragmatism and stores up its catastrophe in the nephew's humiliation: Tybalt withdraws with the feud's logic intact and sharpened, "I will withdraw; but this intrusion shall, / Now seeming sweet, convert to bitterest gall" (1.5). A criticism that reads the household as a single will misses the scene entirely. A materialist reading finds in it the whole model in miniature: authority as negotiation among ranked interests, ideology enforced selectively, and dissent inside the dominant institution itself.

Juliet's part in this government is the play's most exact study of ideology meeting its limit, and the orchard scene gives it philosophical form. "Deny thy father and refuse thy name" (2.2) is usually read as the purest lyric utterance of the romance, love dissolving the social into the essential: "What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other word would smell as sweet" (2.2). The materialist reading does not dispute the lyricism; it identifies the argument. Juliet is conducting, in soliloquy, a critique of the nominal order of Verona, the system under which a name is not a label but a bundle of enforceable claims: debts, duties, enmities, marriageability. Her proposal is that the bundle be refused, that Romeo shed "Montague" as one sheds a garment, and her reasoning is faultless except for one premise, that names in Verona are words. The play spends three acts correcting the premise. Names there are institutions with servants, swords, and marriage contracts attached, and the correction is administered by Tybalt's rapier, the Prince's statute, and her father's ultimatum in turn. The tragedy, on this reading, is not that two young people loved across a divide. It is that the divide was load-bearing.

The Nurse and the Friar complete the institutional map, and the method reads both as officeholders before it reads them as characters. The Nurse is retained labour inside the household, a servant whose intimacy with Juliet is real and whose counsel is finally governed by the terms of her employment: when the marriage to Romeo becomes a liability, her advice reverses to the house's new line, that Paris is the better man and the first husband as good as dead. The reversal shocks readers of the romance; the materialist reading finds it the most predictable event in the play, the moment a dependent aligns her counsel with the power that feeds her. The Friar represents the second estate, the church, whose jurisdiction over marriage makes him the only official in Verona whose authority can create the fact the lovers need. His motives are stated as policy, the conversion of rancour to love, and his instruments are the period's technologies of the sacrament and the sickroom: a clandestine ceremony, a feigned death, a letter. Each fails against a material obstacle the romance reading calls chance. The letter is stopped by a plague quarantine, a public-health cordon, the state's other face. What the romance tradition assembles as a chain of accidents, the materialist reading assembles as an inventory of institutions, each functioning exactly as its structure dictates, and crushing the marriage between them.

## The Counter-Household: The Marriage as an Act of Dissidence

Every account of the play must decide what the marriage is, and the schools divide on the decision. The romance tradition makes it a sacrament of feeling, the feud's opposite in kind: private, spontaneous, and pure, destroyed by contact with a public world it never belonged to. The materialist reading makes it an institution founded inside the enemy's jurisdiction, and the difference reorganizes the middle acts. Romeo and Juliet do not merely fall in love and then, regrettably, marry. They marry within days, in secret, before a priest, with a witness, in a rite whose form is impeccably official and whose publication is indefinitely deferred. The marriage is a counter-household: a unit of two, founded on consent rather than contract, claiming the church's authority against the fathers', and attempting to live inside Verona while owing Verona's central institution, the feud, nothing. Its brief existence is the play's experiment in whether an alternative arrangement can be constituted from the order's own materials.

The experiment's design explains its failure better than the accident chain does. A counter-household needs what every household needs: secure communication, a defensible residence, an income or a patron, and recognition by the surrounding order. The marriage has none of these. Its communications run through a servant whose loyalty is retained by the opposing house and a friar whose nerve is retained by his office. Its residence is a borrowed cell and, for one night, a bride's chamber inside the enemy palace, entered by a rope ladder that is also its evacuation route. Its patron is the church acting without its hierarchy's knowledge. Its recognition is nil: in the eyes of Verona the bride is unmarried and available, which is why the Paris treaty can proceed in Act 3 as though the counter-household did not exist, and why the crisis of that act is not emotional but constitutional. Two incompatible marriages claim the same person, one founded on consent and concealed, one founded on contract and proclaimed, and the order resolves the contradiction by the only means it recognizes: the proclaimed marriage is enforced, the concealed one is driven to counterfeit death. Juliet's potion is the counter-household's last instrument, a fiction of the body deployed against a fiction of the law, and it very nearly works.

Seen this way, the lovers change status in the argument. They are not the feud's victims in the passive sense, casualties of a machine that would have ground on identically without them. They are its dissidents, the two subjects of Verona who act on the order's own counter-story, the doctrine that marriage is made by consent, and carry it to the conclusion the order cannot afford. Their dissidence is not a programme; neither of them drafts a manifesto, and Romeo in particular spends long stretches trying to live inside the feud's codes even after renouncing them, weeping in the Friar's cell in the approved posture of a banished Montague. Dissidence in Sinfield's sense does not require a programme. It requires a refusal that the order must answer, and the measure of the refusal is the size of the answer: a prince's confession, a church's disgrace, two houses bankrupted of heirs, and a settlement in gold. The counter-household is destroyed. The order that destroys it is arraigned in the act, by its own officers, in its own square. That exchange rate, a household of two against the legitimacy of the whole arrangement, is the tragedy's political mathematics, and no reading that treats the marriage as merely private feeling can reproduce the sum.

## Mercutio: The Kinsman Who Belongs to Neither House

The play's casualty list contains one name that unlocks the whole political geometry, and the materialist reading is the one that insists on weighing it. Mercutio is kin to the Prince and friend to Romeo; he holds no brief for either household, draws no wages from the feud, and owes its quarrel nothing. He is, structurally, the nearest thing Verona has to a free citizen, a man whose standing comes from the state itself rather than from a corporation. His death is therefore the feud's most revealing transaction. The order's violence, licensed for use between the houses, kills the one prominent man who belongs to neither, under the guard of the Montague heir who had married into the enemy the day before. When the materialist reading calls the feud a system rather than a sentiment, Mercutio's corpse is the proof it reaches for first: the system does not distinguish between its enemies and its bystanders, because in a city governed by two corporations there are no bystanders, only unassigned casualties.

His speech is built to the same geometry. Mercutio's wit is the play's freest intelligence precisely because it is unretained; the Queen Mab set piece, whatever else it does, inventories the dreams of every rank in Verona, courtiers, lawyers, parsons, soldiers, and finds appetite under every costume, a one-man survey of the city's plausibility structures delivered as entertainment. Romeo's party piece, by contrast, is a borrowed Petrarchanism, and Tybalt can barely speak outside the fencing manual's imported vocabulary of honour. The freest tongue in the play belongs to the man with no house to speak for, and the feud's answer to free speech is the rapier under Romeo's arm. His dying curse, "A plague o' both your houses!" (3.1), is usually absorbed into the romance reading as a flourish of bitterness. The materialist reading restores its constitutional precision. It is a judgement delivered by the state's own kinsman against both corporations at once, seconded by the facts: he has been made, as he says, food for worms by a quarrel in which he held no share, between houses whose equality in dignity has just been measured in his blood. The curse is the closest the play comes to a popular verdict on the feud, spoken by the only man positioned to deliver it, and the plot spends two acts executing its sentence on everyone who hears it.

The Prince's grief in the final scene closes the circuit. "I have lost a brace of kinsmen" (5.3) counts Mercutio and Paris, the two men through whom the state was connected by blood to the younger generation, one killed by the feud directly and one killed defending the marriage the feud made necessary. The state's losses are the measure of the corporations' war: the feud, left unwinked at, consumes the sovereign's own family as readily as it consumes servants and heirs. A romance reading registers Mercutio's death as the end of the play's comedy, the moment the tone darkens. The materialist reading registers it as the moment the feud's true constituency is exposed: not two families but an entire city, taxed in blood at every rank, whose head of state will eventually have to confess that the tax was levied with his knowledge. Both readings notice that the play changes at his death. Only one of them can say what changed: the violence stopped being deniable.

## Where the Order Cracks: The Fault-Lines, Scene by Scene

The fault-line protocol earns its keep only when it is run across the whole action rather than illustrated from a single speech, and the play obliges with a regularity that begins to look like design. Five seams run through Verona's order, each visible in a set scene, and the tragedy is what happens when a marriage is threaded through all five.

The first seam is the state's: law that proclaims death and practises fines. Its Act 3 form has been examined; its Act 5 form is quieter and more damning. Mantua has a law too, and Romeo recites it in the apothecary's shop: death is the penalty for selling poison, and the shopkeeper is starving under the statute's protection. "My poverty but not my will consents" (5.1) is the whole critique in nine words, a subject of the law distinguishing between the order's command and his own judgement, and finding the command affordable only at the price of his life by hunger rather than by rope. Romeo's commentary prices the transaction in the feud's own metal: "There is thy gold, worse poison to men's souls, / Doing more murder in this loathsome world / Than these poor compounds that thou mayst not sell" (5.1). The state that could not police Verona's streets has policed Mantua's pharmacy into a black market, and the instrument of the tragedy's final act is purchased across the counter of its failure. When Romeo then cries "Is it even so? Then I defy you, stars!" (5.1), the materialist reading hears the period's official explanation clearing its throat: the stars are what a political catastrophe is called by those who would rather not name its authors. The play has spent four acts naming them.

The second seam is the church's: a sacrament that constitutes marriage by consent, administered by an institution that dares not publish the marriage it has made. Friar Laurence is no rebel against his order. His herb speech in the cell garden is the period's orthodoxy in its most balanced form, the doctrine that created things hold good and evil in suspension and that the will decides between them: "In man as well as herbs, grace and rude will" (2.3). The tragedy grows from the gap between that balanced doctrine and the Friar's practice, which is the practice of a dependent. He marries the heirs in secret because the sacrament authorizes what the households forbid, and then, because secrecy was the condition of the rite, he must defend the rite with fictions: a sleeping potion, a counterfeit funeral, a letter entrusted to a single courier. Each fiction is reasonable. Their sum is the catastrophe, and the materialist point is that the sum was structural. An institution whose true doctrine contradicts the property order, and which lacks the power to say so in the square, will produce exactly this pattern: correct sacraments and catastrophic administration. The Friar's final confession accepts personal blame, "I am the greatest, able to do least" (5.3), and the reading should decline the offer. The play has shown the office failing, not merely the officer.

The third seam is the household's: obedience preached as absolute, practised as negotiation. Capulet's feast-day suspension of Tybalt's enforcement has been examined; the seam widens in Act 3, when the same father who counselled Paris to win his daughter's consent, "My will to her consent is but a part" (1.2), discovers in bereavement a use for speed, fixes the wedding day as an exercise of authority, and meets his daughter's refusal with the ultimatum that converts her from heir to refuse. The contradiction is not hypocrisy in the simple sense. It is the household's two theories of itself colliding: the theory of the marriage as alliance, in which the bride's managed consent is part of the treaty's value, and the theory of the household as monarchy, in which a subject's refusal is treason. Juliet's tragedy inside the house is that she is governed by whichever theory the moment requires, and her response is the play's boldest act of dissidence: she takes the church's theory, consent, and the feud's own technology, secrecy, and builds a counter-household of two in the Friar's cell. That the counter-household cannot survive contact with the state's quarantine and the father's calendar is the verdict of Acts 4 and 5. That it could be founded at all is the fault-line's doing.

The fourth seam is gender's, and the play states it by distribution of speech. The feud is an affair of men competing in public, yet its costs are administered through women who are excluded from its councils: a bride priced in a market she may not address, a wife who plots in the only register left to her, a mother who dies of the grief the feud assigns her sex to carry. Lady Capulet, refused any public instrument, reaches for a private one, proposing to send poison to Mantua so that Romeo "shall soon keep Tybalt company" (3.5), and the proposal's horror is the measure of the exclusion: the household's women may commission violence only as crime, never as policy. Lady Montague does not survive to the settlement at all; the Prince's roll of the punished is read over her absence, her death from grief reported by her husband in the tomb scene as an aside of war. A criticism that treats the feud as a contest between two houses and stops there has accepted the houses' own account of who counts as a combatant. The materialist reading counts the casualties instead, and the count redistributes the play's politics: the order that prices its daughters and buries its mothers is arraigned by its own casualty list before the Prince speaks a word.

The fifth seam is the one the play's ending exposes, between the order's account of the catastrophe and the catastrophe's account of the order. Verona's official explanation is ready-made and the Prologue supplies it: star-crossed lovers, death-marked love, a pair of children sacrificed to their parents' rage, after which the rage conveniently expires. The materialist reading treats that explanation as the settlement's first clause, the story the survivors agree to tell, in which the feud dies of grief and no institution stands charged. Against it the play files its own evidence in the same scene: a Prince confessing negligence, a Friar confessing concealment, two fathers converting grief into competitive gold, and a promised inquest whose findings the audience never hears. The fault-line runs through the final tableau itself. The audience is shown a reconciliation and simultaneously shown the instruments by which the reconciliation will be remembered, statues and a prince's narrative, "the story of more woe" (5.3) already being prepared as the official text. Dissident reading begins at exactly that seam, with the suspicion that the story of more woe is the last service the order performs for itself, and that the play, by staging the manufacture of its own epitaph, has taught its audience to read the epitaph against the evidence. Few tragedies hand their critics so complete an exhibit of ideology at work. The school's claim on this play rests finally on that exhibit.

## Subversion and Containment: The Debate the Method Was Born In

Cultural materialism defined itself in an argument about whether power in a text, and behind a text, always wins. The argument's other party was the American school, and its classic statement is Stephen Greenblatt's essay "Invisible Bullets," first published in 1981 and collected both in Political Shakespeare itself in 1985 and in Greenblatt's Shakespearean Negotiations (1988). Reading the Henriad, Greenblatt proposed that the Elizabethan state and its theatre stood in a relation of production and containment: subversive energies were not merely suppressed but generated, displayed, and finally absorbed by the order they appeared to threaten, as a regime might tolerate or even stage dissent the better to demonstrate its management. The model was brilliant and bleak. On its logic, the theatre's Licence to question power was itself an instrument of power, and apparent resistance became evidence of the system's suppleness.

The British reply came from inside the same historicist turn and is the founding gesture of the school this article describes. Sinfield named the target precisely: new historicism, he argued in Faultlines, too often worked from an "entrapment model" of ideology and power, "whereby even, or especially, manoeuvres that seem designed to challenge the system help to maintain it." Against entrapment he set the fault-line. A dominant order must present itself as plausible, and plausibility is manufactured from materials that contradict one another: a church preaching mercy and administering property, a household preaching obedience and requiring initiative, a state preaching peace and recruiting killers. At those seams, Sinfield argued, "the criteria of plausibility fall into contest and disarray," and dissidence, his preferred term, becomes possible: not subversion, which in his usage prejudges a bid to overthrow, but the part of the dominant that has not been consented to, held open "without prejudice to the outcome." The full map of the American position, its anecdotes and its account of social energy, belongs to [the study of Stephen Greenblatt and the new historicism brought to this play](/2027/01/01/greenblatt-new-historicism-reading/); the contrast matters here because the feud is a nearly perfect test case for the quarrel, and the quarrel can be adjudicated on Verona's evidence.

### What is the difference between dissidence and subversion in Sinfield's usage?

Subversion, in Sinfield's usage, names an attempt to overturn an order, and builds the attempt's likely fate into the analysis, since the studies he was answering tended to find such attempts contained. Dissidence names something prior and more durable: a refusal of consent by those whom an order has failed to convince, whether or not the refusal mounts a campaign.

A dissident reading therefore does not ask first whether a challenge succeeds. It asks where the order's own story fails to persuade the people living inside it, and it treats that failure as a permanent structural feature rather than an accident. The distinction governs the feud's ending. Measured as subversion, the lovers' marriage attempts an overthrow and is crushed. Measured as dissidence, the marriage is a refusal that the order must spend its heirs, its credit, and its Prince's conscience to answer, and the answering is the exposure.

The adjudication the evidence supports is a split verdict with a clear tilt. Greenblatt's model describes one real mechanism in the play: the ending does absorb the lovers into the order's monument economy, converting the marriage that defied the households into statues that advertise them, and any reading that calls the close simply triumphant dissent has not looked at the gold. But containment in Greenblatt's strong form predicts that the absorbing order emerges with its authority confirmed, and Verona's order emerges indicted by its own head. The Prince's self-arraignment, the fathers' handshake conducted as a bidding war in funeral expenditure, and the closing promise of proceedings, "Some shall be pardoned, and some punished" (5.3), describe a settlement in which the state survives by confessing. Sinfield's model predicts exactly this configuration: dissidence that does not overthrow, and an order that persists, but persists with its fault-lines on the public record, available to every later audience as the terms on which the peace was bought. On the play's evidence, dissidence is the better instrument.

## The Difference from New Historicism, Drawn Clearly

Because the two schools arrived together, share ancestors in Foucault, Williams, and the Marxist tradition, and are shelved together in every survey, the contrast between them is often blurred into a transatlantic difference of style. Dollimore's 1994 introduction was written against exactly that blur, and its distinctions survive scrutiny. Three are decisive for the feud.

The first concerns the shape of power. New historicism's characteristic figure is circulation: power moves through a culture as energy moves through a system, exchanged between court and theatre, and the critic's task is to trace the exchanges. Cultural materialism's characteristic figure is contest: power is held by identifiable institutions at identifiable others' expense, and the critic's task is to name the holders, the held, and the points where the held push back. Verona rewards the second figure. Nothing in the play circulates innocently. The feud's violence is commissioned by households, its peace is proclaimed by a prince, its marriages are contracted by fathers, and its costs are invoiced to servants, nephews, daughters, and heirs in a legible order. A reading that begins from circulation will find the tomb mysterious, a convergence of energies. A reading that begins from contest finds the tomb accounted for.

The second distinction concerns the present. The American school's founding gestures bracket the critic's own politics as an embarrassment to be managed by irony; its famous openings confess implication and then proceed as analysis. The British school's founding gesture is the opposite: the foreword's declaration of commitment is a methodological claim, that a reading's present interest is a fact about it whether declared or not, and that declaring it is the honest procedure. For the feud this yields a concrete difference in what a reading is for. A cultural materialist reading of Verona is written to be used: in classrooms where the play arrives wrapped in the romance packaging of the heritage industry, in theatres deciding whether the street scenes are decoration, in any setting where the question of who pays for an order's peace is a live one. The method measures its success partly by that use.

The third distinction concerns the state of the text's own moment. New historicism tends to reconstruct a period's dominant ideology as a coherent system and then locate the text inside it. Cultural materialism, formed in a Britain arguing about its own settlement, expects incoherence: orders are coalitions, ideologies are patchworks, and the seams are where history happens. The play bears the British expectation out at every level. Verona's state preaches peace and fines brawlers it cannot hang; its church marries the heirs it should reconcile in public and hides the sacrament it should proclaim; its households preach obedience and depend on the initiative of daughters, nurses, and friars who disobey as a condition of the plot. A coherent-ideology model would have to treat these as local colour. The fault-line model treats them as the subject, and the subject, once seen, organizes the whole play.

A fourth distinction, of method rather than doctrine, follows from the other three and shows in the texture of the writing each school produces. The new historicist essay characteristically opens with an anecdote, a strange document or episode from the period archive, and reads the play as its resonance. The cultural materialist essay characteristically opens with a position: a claim about the text's politics, stated in advance and then fought for across the evidence. The difference is not a quirk of temperament. It follows from each school's theory of its own work. A criticism that believes power circulates will write essays that circulate, moving between archive and play without declaring a destination. A criticism that believes power is contested will write essays that contest, naming the opposed reading and the stakes of the opposition. The feud rewards the second texture. Verona's evidence does not resonate into a pattern on its own; it arraigns, and an essay form built for arraignment, charge, evidence, rebuttal, verdict, fits the material as the anecdotal form, for all its brilliance elsewhere, does not.

None of this makes the American school dispensable, and the adjudication should not be allowed to harden into a caricature. The containment essay taught a generation to ask who profits from tolerated dissent, a question the materialist reading of the statues uses with gratitude. The verdict is about fit. For a play whose action is an audit of institutions conducted in public, whose sovereign indicts himself, and whose lovers' refusal is neither absorbed silently nor crowned, the British instrument, built for contest, declared commitment, and structural incoherence, returns the fuller account.

## The Ending Reframed: A Settlement, an Indictment, and a Price in Gold

The last scene is where readings of the play declare themselves, and the materialist reading of the close can be stated as a sequence of transactions. The state arrives to find its failure accomplished. The church's officer confesses his plot and offers his life to the law's rigour. The fathers, meeting over the bodies of the succession each has lost, negotiate. Capulet opens with the language of the marriage market he had tried to run in Act 3: "O brother Montague, give me thy hand: / This is my daughter's jointure, for no more / Can I demand" (5.3). A jointure is the settlement a bride's family receives, the property counterweight to a dowry; Capulet is stating, in the vocabulary of contract, that the handshake itself is the entire marriage portion now payable, the whole yield of the alliance the Friar had hoped to build. Montague answers in the same currency and raises it: "But I can give thee more: / For I will raise her statue in pure gold" (5.3), and Capulet caps the bid with a statue of Romeo in kind. The feud's final act is an auction.

### Why do the fathers bid against each other in gold over the bodies?

The fathers bid in gold because gold is the feud's native language and the settlement must be spoken in it to be binding. Each house has conducted its rivalry for a generation as competitive display: retinues, feasts, marriages, monuments, and tombs contested in public.

A reconciliation transacted in plain words would be a private emotion, revocable at the next insult; a reconciliation transacted in matched statues is a public contract, witnessed by the Prince and the city, in which each house's honour is staked on the permanence of the peace. The statues are therefore not a failure of feeling. They are the feud's machinery turned, at ruinous cost, to its opposite purpose, and the price of the conversion is the measure of what the machinery was worth to the men who owned it. The love that the order could not accommodate alive is affordable to it as treasure.

What the settlement does not do is equally part of the record. It does not restore the Prince's authority, which closes the play in the past tense of confession and a promise of further proceedings. It does not address the labour force of the feud, the servants and retainers whose brawls opened the action and whose masters' peace leaves their employment's purpose abolished without comment. It does not enfranchise the city's citizens, whose clubs in the first scene were the nearest thing Verona showed to a popular politics, and who vanish from the state they twice assembled to defend. And it does not survive its own morning: "A glooming peace this morning with it brings" (5.3) is the Prince's weather report on a settlement whose terms are two statues and an inquest. The materialist verdict on the ending is therefore double, and the doubleness is the finding. The reconciliation is real, and it is an instrument of the order it reconciles: the houses are united as shareholders in a monument, the state ratifies the merger, and the cost is itemized in the only ledger the play keeps, the bodies of the young. Exposure and containment occur in the same gesture, and the critic's task is not to choose between them but to show the gesture whole. On that showing, the exposure is the larger fact, because the play makes its audience watch the pricing.

## The Reading in Use: Stage, Classroom, and the Heritage Verona

A method that declares its commitments must expect to be judged by its effects, and the cultural materialist reading of the feud has a practical record to inspect. Its first effect is on the weight of scenes. In the romance production tradition, the street brawls are choreography, energetic preliminaries to be danced through on the way to the balcony, and the Prince is a herald with a crown. A production that has absorbed the materialist reading cannot afford either economy. The brawls become the play's recurring referendum on order, staged with the servants' names and grievances legible; the Prince's entrances acquire the tension of a government spokesman at a disaster briefing, each appearance measuring a further loss of control; and the tomb scene lengthens at its end, because the negotiations over the statues are no longer an epilogue to be trimmed but the verdict the evening has been building toward. Directors need not have read Faultlines to make these choices, and many arrived at them by instinct. The method's contribution is to supply the rationale that defends the choices against the counter-pressure of the romance brand, which always prefers the lovers lit and the politics dimmed.

The second effect is on the classroom, where the play's packaging is most aggressive. For generations of students the tragedy has arrived pre-interpreted as a celebration of young love defeated by an unfortunate feud, and the examination questions have obediently asked about fate, haste, and the lovers' characters. The materialist reading changes the questions before it changes the answers. Who employs the men who open the play, and what are they fighting about? What does the Prince threaten, what does he do, and why is there a difference? What is Juliet worth to her house, in the precise sense that her father can state her price in a marriage negotiation and her mother can be assigned her grief as a duty? Students who have been asked those questions have not been given a doctrine; they have been given the play back, with the parts the packaging cropped restored to the frame. The method's founders, both formed in a British education system arguing about whose culture the curriculum would carry, intended exactly this use. A reading of the feud that cannot survive a classroom of sceptical sixteen-year-olds, required to point at lines, is not a reading the school would recognize as its own.

The third effect is on the heritage Verona itself, the composite product of tourism, film, and festival marketing in which the play survives as a brand: a balcony in a courtyard, a club of Juliets answering letters, a city monetizing a fiction about itself. The materialist reading does not begrudge the brand its pleasures, but it refuses the brand's editorial policy, which is to sell the romance and suppress the audit. On the brand's terms, the feud is local colour and the tomb is a shrine. On the text's terms, the feud is a system that killed six people in four days, three of them the Prince's kin or the houses' heirs, and the tomb is a crime scene at which the responsible institutions are still negotiating their exposure as the lights go down. A criticism that can hold the second account open in the presence of the first is doing the work the foreword promised: refusing to mystify its perspective, and refusing to let a social order, Verona's or the heritage industry's, present its preferred version of events as the natural one.

## The Editor's Verona: Gibbons and the Claims of Design

The mandatory presence of an editor in this argument is not a formality, because the materialist reading's most instructive disagreement is with the editorial tradition's account of what kind of object the play is. Brian Gibbons, editing the play for the Arden second series in 1980, five years before Political Shakespeare, mounted the modern defence of the play's design: against the older condescension that treated the tragedy as a brilliant apprentice work, its beauties lyric and its architecture accidental, Gibbons argued from the text's patterning, its handling of the source in Brooke's poem, and its management of comic and tragic registers that the play is a made thing, deliberately and skilfully constructed. The defence has held. It is also, from the materialist position, incomplete in a revealing way, because it locates the design in the playwright's artistry and stops there, as if pattern were its own explanation.

The disagreement can be adjudicated cleanly, because both parties need the same evidence. The materialist reading depends on Gibbons being right: an accidental play would not distribute its failures so symmetrically among Prince, church, and households, would not rhyme the opening brawl with the closing auction, would not give its sovereign the first threat and the last confession. Where the readings divide is over the design's object. For the editorial tradition the pattern vindicates the maker; for the materialist the pattern is the maker's instrument for arraigning the arrangements the pattern connects. A design that rhymes a servants' brawl with a princes' settlement is making an argument about where violence is manufactured and where it is memorialized, and the argument is political in content whatever its author's ballot of private opinions. The verdict, then, is adoption with amendment: the school takes the editor's play, constructed and deliberate, and declines the editor's inference that construction is a refuge from politics. In this text the construction is the politics, load-bearing and exact.

There is a second editorial fact the method prizes, and it returns the argument to the schools' shared ground. The play that editors construct is a material object with its own history of production: quartos printed for a commercial theatre, a text transmitted through the decisions of compositors and revisers, and a modern edition, Gibbons's included, that mediates every line quoted in the present article. Cultural materialism's attention to the conditions of a text's making and remaking, the fourth element of the foreword's programme, is the point at which the method and the editorial discipline cease to be opponents and become the same inquiry pursued at different scales. The feud's Verona was manufactured, in a playhouse, for money, before an audience whose city had its own households, statutes, and marriage markets. An editor establishes the words. The materialist asks what the words were for. Neither question survives the other's neglect.

## The Objections: Theory Imposed, Poetry Forgotten

The school has never lacked prosecutors, and two charges recur with enough force to require answers rather than acknowledgements. The first is anachronism: that a method formed in the arguments of the 1980s reads its own politics into a play of the 1590s, finding Thatcher-era contests in a Veronese fiction. Brian Vickers, in Appropriating Shakespeare (1993), pressed the general case against the period's theoretical schools, that they appropriate texts to programmes and read past the words in their haste to reach the verdict. The charge has a true and a false form. In its true form it warns every practitioner, and this reading accepts the discipline: the findings offered here stand on the Prologue's wording, the Prince's three speeches, the feast scene's collision of authorities, and the closing auction, all of which are in the text for any reader to check. In its false form the charge assumes that a reading without declared politics is a reading without politics, and that the romance tradition's Verona, a timeless backdrop for young love, is the play's natural state rather than an interpretation with its own history and its own institutional sponsors in the theatre and the classroom. Cultural materialism's answer is the foreword's: all criticism has commitments; the method is the one that prints its own on the title page.

The second charge is aesthetic: that the political reading flattens the play's poetry into evidence, trading the orchard scene's radiance for a thesis about names. The answer begins by conceding the risk and then disputing the instance. The orchard scene loses nothing when its argument about names is taken seriously; it gains its plot. Juliet's reasoning is the play's most rigorous thinking, and treating it as decoration rather than argument is the true flattening, the romance tradition's habit of admiring the heroine's lyricism while declining to follow her logic to the conclusion the institutions impose on it. Where the charge does bite is at the level of surplus: the play's language generates pleasures and resonances, Mercutio's wit above all, that no inventory of arrangements exhausts. The method at its best knows this. Dollimore's own writing on tragedy is saturated with the period's theology and metaphysics precisely because ideology in his account is lived at that depth. A cultural materialist reading that cannot hear Queen Mab has not refuted the aesthetic objection; it has illustrated it. The standard the school sets itself is a reading that returns the poetry to the world that made it dangerous, not a reading that confiscates the poetry as contraband.

A third objection comes from the school's own left, and it deserves the same directness. Readers formed by the older Marxist tradition sometimes find cultural materialism too literary: too much ideology, too little base; a politics of reading substituted for a politics of production. The fuller economic anatomy of Verona, its money, wages, and property, is the province of [the survey of Marxist readings of the play and its class conflict](/2027/02/12/marxist-readings-romeo-and-juliet/), and the two approaches are complements rather than rivals. The division of labour is real. Where the Marxist account explains the feud's Verona by its property relations, the cultural materialist account explains how those relations were lived, justified, and cracked open in representation, and why a play could be one of the places the cracking happened. An account of the feud that omitted either term would be describing half a machine.

## Verdict: What the Method Reveals

Cultural materialism reveals the feud as the play's governing institution and the tragedy as the audit of a political order. Its findings can be carried away in four sentences. The feud is a competition for precedence between matched households, maintained by labour, honour, and inheritance, and it needs no cause because the arrangement is its own reward. The state that should suppress it is too weak to do more than proclaim, fine, and confess, and the Prince's three appearances measure the weakness exactly. The marriage that might have converted the competition into alliance is destroyed not by fortune but by the sequential default of every institution entrusted with it, household, church, and law. And the peace that ends the play is a settlement negotiated in gold over the bodies of the heirs, ratified by a sovereign who includes himself among the punished. None of these findings requires the love story to be diminished. They require it to be located: inside a city, inside an economy of honour, inside a failing state, where its destruction becomes legible as a political event rather than a meteorological one.

The method also returns a dividend to readers who will never use its vocabulary. Once the feud has been read as a contest of authority, the play's familiar beauties rearrange themselves without losing their lustre. The balcony scene becomes a young woman dismantling, in perfect logic, the registry that owns her, and failing only because the registry has swords. The feast becomes a summit at which two theories of power collide over a guest list. The tomb becomes a shareholders' meeting held over the assets that the rivalry consumed. Readers who came for the romance keep it; they simply stop mistaking it for the whole event. That is the practical meaning of the foreword's promise, borne out wherever the reading is taught at full strength: a reading that refuses to mystify its own perspective ends by demystifying the play's packaging as well, and the play that emerges from the packaging is larger, angrier, and more exact than the one the heritage industry sells.

The method's limit is the obverse of its strength. It is a reading of arrangements, and the play also contains what arrangements cannot contain: the inwardness of the lovers' language, the sense, which no production loses, that something in the orchard exceeds the city that forbids it. The school's honest answer is that excess is itself socially produced, that private inwardness is one of the things a certain kind of order manufactures and then punishes, and the answer is good as far as it goes. A reader who wants the last word on the play will need other instruments as well. A reader who wants to know why the lovers die, and who in Verona is answerable, and why the audience is shown the bill, will find that cultural materialism, alone among the schools the feud has attracted, insists on answering all three questions in the same terms. That insistence is its distinction, and on the evidence of the text, it is earned.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch materialism primer, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch materialism primer is the table in this article that sets eleven features of the play in three columns: the feature itself, the reading the inherited romance tradition gives it, and the reading the cultural materialist method returns. Its pattern is a reversal of explanation. Wherever the romance reading assigns an event to feeling, fortune, or temperament, the materialist column assigns the same event to an arrangement: the opening brawl to a labour relation, the exile to a negotiated sentence, the Paris marriage to a treaty, the statues to competitive expenditure. The primer is built for reuse. Run down its rows against any scene and the question it teaches is constant: not what a moment means in the abstract, but what distribution of power the moment transacts, who ordered it, who performs it, and who pays.

### Q: Where and when was cultural materialism named as a critical programme, and by whom?

Cultural materialism was named as a critical programme in 1985, in the foreword to Political Shakespeare: Essays in Cultural Materialism, edited by Jonathan Dollimore and Alan Sinfield and published by Manchester University Press. The term was adapted from Raymond Williams, who had used it in Marxism and Literature (1977) for a theory of culture as a material productive process. Dollimore and Sinfield sharpened the term for Shakespeare studies and for polemic: a reading practice combining historical context, theoretical method, political commitment, and textual analysis, aimed at the contradictions of the order a text inhabits. A second edition of the collection in 1994 carried Dollimore's introduction restating the programme and its differences from American new historicism, by which time the label named a recognized British school with its own books, among them Dollimore's Radical Tragedy and Sinfield's Faultlines.

### Q: What four commitments does the foreword to Political Shakespeare build into the method?

The foreword builds in historical context, theoretical method, political commitment, and textual analysis. Historical context places the text inside the practices of its own period rather than above them. Theoretical method requires explicit concepts, above all ideology and dissidence, instead of impressions dressed as common sense. Political commitment is the element that distinguishes the school most sharply: the foreword declares that the method "registers its commitment to the transformation of a social order which exploits people on grounds of race, gender and class," treating a declared interest as more honest than a concealed one. Textual analysis binds the other three to the page. A cultural materialist claim about the feud must be won from the Prologue, the Prince's speeches, the feast scene, and the tomb, or it is not a finding of the method but a slogan in its vocabulary.

### Q: What does Raymond Williams have to do with the term cultural materialism?

Raymond Williams supplied the term and the intellectual inheritance behind it. In Marxism and Literature (1977) he argued that culture is not a reflection produced at a distance by an economic base but a material process in its own right, carried on in institutions, formations, and practices, and he named the study of that process cultural materialism. Dollimore and Sinfield acknowledged the debt when they adopted the term for Political Shakespeare in 1985, and Williams contributed the afterword to that collection, a fitting arrangement: the elder critic of the British New Left handing the instrument to its Shakespearean practitioners. The inheritance shows in the method's reflexes. Where an older Marxism might have treated the play as ideology to be unmasked from outside, the Williams line treats the play as cultural production from inside, one of the places where a society makes and contests the meanings it lives by.

### Q: Did Dollimore or Sinfield publish a freestanding reading of this play?

No. Neither founder published a freestanding reading of Romeo and Juliet, and any account implying otherwise misstates the record. Sinfield's Faultlines (1992) builds its Shakespeare case on Othello, Macbeth, Henry V, and Julius Caesar. Dollimore's Radical Tragedy (1984) sustains its argument across the great tragedies and the Jacobean drama of Shakespeare's contemporaries. The cultural materialist account of the feud is therefore the method's account rather than a master's: the programme from the Political Shakespeare foreword, the decentring thesis from Dollimore, and the fault-line model from Sinfield, carried to a play they left alone and tested against its lines. The test is the point. A commentary tradition repeats its founders' exhibits; a working method generates findings on new material, and the feud, with its weak prince, its corporate households, and its indicted ending, confirms the instruments at nearly every scene.

### Q: Why does a cultural materialist reading of the play begin with the servants in the first scene?

Because the first scene shows the feud's material base before its leadership appears. Sampson and Gregory are Capulet's retained men, and their opening banter converts employment into grievance: the quarrel they are about to fight is a condition of their service, not a conviction of their hearts. Sampson's care to "take the law of our sides" before the thumb-biting begins shows violence administered as corporate policy, with liability managed in advance. Beginning here reverses the romance reading's priorities, in which the street brawls are decoration before the love story starts. For the materialist, the brawl is the feud in its working clothes, performed by the only people in the scene who will gain nothing from its outcome. A reading that starts with the servants has already learned the method's central habit: ask who performs an order's work, and at whose expense, before asking what the order proclaims.

### Q: Why does Escalus threaten death for the next brawl and then never carry out the threat?

Because the threat is addressed to men the state cannot afford to execute. Escalus proclaims in the first scene that any further disturbance of the streets will be paid for with the offenders' lives, and the proclamation follows three tolerated brawls, which fixes its value before events test it. When the test comes in Act 3, Romeo has killed the Capulet heir in waiting after the Prince's own kinsman died in the same fight, and the statute's strict application would make an enemy of a great house whose heir stands condemned. The Prince bargains the sentence down to exile and a fine, describing the transaction himself as an amercement. The pattern is the reading's central exhibit for the Verona state: penalties escalate on paper as credibility falls in practice, and government degenerates into the pricing of offences committed by corporations too strong to punish. The unexecuted threat is not a detail of plotting. It is the constitution of the tragedy.

### Q: What does the Mantuan law against selling poison accomplish in the tragedy's final act?

The Mantuan law manufactures the instrument of the catastrophe while pretending to prevent it. Mantua punishes the sale of poison with death, and its apothecary is starving in full view of his unsold stock: the statute has made him desperate enough to break it and poor enough to need to. His sentence, "My poverty but not my will consents" (5.1), is a subject's audit of the law that governs him, distinguishing the order's command from his own judgement. Romeo's gold completes the transaction, and his comment on it, that gold does more murder in the world than the compounds the law forbids, extends the audit to the feud's own currency. For the materialist reading the scene is the state's second failure, twin to Verona's: a government strict where strictness produces a black market and helpless where helplessness produces brawls, supplying by its own design the poison, the banished man, and the desperation that meet in the tomb.

### Q: Why does Capulet first delay and then suddenly accelerate the Paris marriage?

Because the marriage serves different functions in the household's government at different moments, and both decisions are exercises of the same authority. In Act 1 Capulet delays, telling Paris his daughter is yet a stranger in the world and making her consent part of the suit's terms; the treaty with the Prince's kinsman is valuable, and a managed courtship protects its value. In Act 3, with Tybalt dead and the house in mourning, Capulet accelerates: the wedding is refunctioned as an instrument for restoring order and cheer inside a grieving household, and its date is fixed by the father's fiat. When Juliet refuses the accelerated treaty, the underlying constitution surfaces in the ultimatum of Act 3 Scene 5. The materialist reading draws the moral plainly. The daughter's consent was never a right; it was a clause in the negotiation, honoured while the negotiation was leisurely and struck out the moment the household's government required speed.

### Q: What argument is Juliet making about names in the orchard scene, and why does the play prove her premise wrong?

Juliet argues that a name is a label separable from the thing it names: "What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other word would smell as sweet" (2.2). On that premise Romeo can shed "Montague" as a garment is shed, and the lovers can meet as persons outside the feud's registry. The materialist reading takes the argument seriously as the play's most rigorous thinking, an attempt to think a human relation outside the nominal order of Verona. The play then corrects the premise rather than the reasoning. In Verona a name is not a word but a bundle of enforceable claims: retainers, debts, enmities, marriageability, and the liability to be killed on sight in the public street. Tybalt's rapier, the Prince's statute, and her father's ultimatum administer the correction in turn. The tragedy is that Juliet's philosophy is sound and her city is built to refute it.

### Q: How does the cultural materialist reading answer the claim that the lovers are star-crossed?

It treats the star-crossing as the official explanation the play itself supplies and then audits. The Prologue assigns the catastrophe to the stars and the ancient grudge, a formulation that charges the heavens and the past while acquitting every institution in the present. The action that follows assigns the same catastrophe, link by link, to identifiable arrangements: a statute proclaimed and unenforced, a sentence bargained down, a marriage treaty accelerated, a letter stopped by a plague cordon, a poison sold under a law that starved its seller. Romeo's cry in Mantua, "Then I defy you, stars!" (5.1), is on this reading the language of a man inside the machine reaching for the only explanation his culture stocks. The audience has been shown the machine. The materialist answer is therefore not that fate is an illusion but that fate is an alibi, the name an order gives to outcomes it has arranged and would rather not sign.

### Q: What is the entrapment model, and why did Sinfield reject it?

The entrapment model is Sinfield's name, in Faultlines, for the account of power he found dominant in new historicist writing: the model "whereby even, or especially, manoeuvres that seem designed to challenge the system help to maintain it." On that account every apparent act of resistance is absorbed in advance by the order it resists, and criticism becomes the repeated demonstration that escape was impossible. Sinfield rejected the model on two grounds. Empirically, it totalizes power beyond what the evidence of texts and history shows, since dominant orders demonstrably fail, fracture, and lose. Politically, it functions as a sedative: if dissent always maintains the system, the rational critic need never ask which dissent might not, and the analysis ratifies the quietism it describes. His alternative is dissidence along fault-lines, the refusal of consent at the points where an order's own plausibility structure contradicts itself, held open without prejudice to the outcome.

### Q: What does Dollimore's Radical Tragedy argue about Renaissance tragedy?

Radical Tragedy (1984) argues that Renaissance tragedy does not finally endorse the providential and hierarchical ideology of its age, as the older orthodoxy maintained, but subjects that ideology to destructive pressure. Where the orthodoxy read the drama as a machine for reconciling audiences to degree, providence, and the consolations of religion, Dollimore reads its strongest work as decentring those authorities: staging the failure of providential explanation in the face of power and suffering, and relocating value in human knowledge and defiance. The book's exhibits are Shakespeare's great tragedies and the Jacobean dramatists rather than the Veronese play. Its bearing on the feud is structural. The present play performs the decentring in its architecture, opening with the providential account in the Prologue's sonnet and dismantling it scene by scene until the closing audit is delivered by a prince confessing negligence, a friar confessing concealment, and two fathers pricing their grief in gold.

### Q: Why does Capulet restrain Tybalt at the feast, and what does the moment expose about the household?

Capulet restrains Tybalt because the feast is an instrument of policy whose success a brawl would destroy. Tybalt, the household's most doctrinal young man, identifies Romeo's voice and reaches for enforcement; the master overrules him in the language of sovereignty, "Am I the master here, or you? Go to" (1.5). Magnificence displayed before Verona's society is worth more that evening than a point of feud discipline, and the intruder is in any case reputed a virtuous gentleman. The moment exposes the household as a fissured government rather than a single will. The feud exists inside the house in two forms, as doctrine in the nephew and as strategy in the master, and the two forms are incompatible: doctrine demands the challenge, strategy forbids it. Tybalt withdraws storing the humiliation as gall, and the catastrophe of Act 3 is brewed in that storage. The order's seams, the scene teaches, run through its own headquarters.

### Q: Is the secret marriage an escape from Verona's politics or a move inside them?

A move inside them, executed with the order's own instruments. The marriage is performed by a priest under the church's doctrine that consent makes the union, witnessed, sacramental, and canonically binding; nothing about its form is improvised or private in the romantic sense. What is withheld is publication, and publication is withheld because the fathers' treaty system would annul by force what the church's doctrine creates by right. The marriage therefore founds a counter-household on the fault-line between Verona's two official stories, the sacramental and the proprietary, and its destruction demonstrates the balance of force between them: the proprietary story commands fathers, statutes, calendars, and swords, while the sacramental story commands two teenagers, a friar, and a nurse. The materialist reading honours the marriage precisely by refusing to call it an escape. It was a constitutional act, and it was answered constitutionally, by ultimatum, exile, and a forced second wedding.

### Q: What does the Prince include in his own charge sheet when he says that all are punished?

He includes himself, for the offence of toleration. The closing speech arraigns the fathers first, "See what a scourge is laid upon your hate" (5.3), and then widens the indictment: "And I for winking at your discords too / Have lost a brace of kinsmen. All are punished" (5.3). The verb is the speech's precision. Winking is not ignorance and not violence; it is the knowing indulgence of disorder by the authority charged with suppressing it, the policy the play has tracked from the first scene's thrice-disturbed streets through the bargained sentence of Act 3. The Prince's losses, Mercutio and Paris, are entered as his fine. For the materialist reading this self-arraignment is the ending's largest political fact, larger than the reconciliation it frames: the head of state closes the tragedy by testifying that the catastrophe was a failure of government, his own included, and no account of the ending that omits the testimony has read the speech to its end.

### Q: Why is the play's last speech given to the Prince rather than to either father?

Because the play's closing business is the state's, and the fathers have already completed theirs in the handshake and the statues. The settlement between the houses is a private treaty conducted in gold; what remains is public: the inquest, the pardons and punishments the Prince promises, and the official narrative under which the catastrophe will enter the record. Giving those to Escalus completes the arc the materialist reading traces from his first entrance. The sovereign who began by proclaiming a law he could not enforce ends by composing the story the city will tell, "For never was a story of more woe" (5.3), and the promotion from failed legislator to successful narrator is itself a political finding: the order that cannot prevent the tragedy can still own its meaning. The fathers get the monuments. The Prince gets the epitaph, and the audience, taught by five acts of auditing, is positioned to notice the division of spoils.

### Q: What can cultural materialism not explain about the play?

It cannot exhaust the play's inwardness, and the honest practice of the method says so. The reading explains the conditions under which the lovers live and die: the institutions that price, forbid, exile, and bury them. It explains less completely the experience the poetry creates from inside those conditions, the sense in the orchard scene that the language exceeds every arrangement sent to contain it, or the surplus of Mercutio's wit over any function the plot assigns him. The school's considered answer is that inwardness and surplus are themselves historically produced, that a certain kind of order manufactures private selves and then punishes their privacy, and the answer carries real weight. It does not convert the reading into a total account, and the method's best practitioners have not claimed one. Cultural materialism is the strongest available answer to why the lovers die and who is answerable. The question of what their living felt like requires other instruments alongside it.

### Q: How does the reading change the way the street brawls should be staged?

It promotes them from choreography to evidence. In the romance production tradition the brawls are energetic preliminaries, danced through on the way to the balcony, with the servants as anonymous colour. A staging informed by the materialist reading gives the brawlers their names, their liveries, and their grievances, and lets the audience register that the men fighting are employees performing a condition of service for houses that will not bury them with statues. The citizens' intervention in the first scene gains weight as the city's one appearance as a political body. The Prince's entrances, paced as a government's declining press conferences, measure the order's loss of control across the evening. None of this requires a concept imposed on the text from outside. It requires only that the street scenes be played at the value the script assigns them, as the feud's working machinery rather than its wallpaper, which is the value the romance packaging has traditionally talked directors out of paying.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch materialism primer to argue that the feud is a contest for authority in Verona?

Begin from the primer's strongest rows and build outward. The opening brawl establishes the feud as a labour relation, violence performed by retained servants; the Prince's speeches establish the state as a party too weak to end the contest it condemns; the Paris negotiation establishes the households' internal government, in which a daughter is the succession and her marriage a treaty. An essay should then test the argument against the ending, where the contest is settled in the primer's own terms: authority over Verona is redistributed over the bodies of the heirs, the fathers convert their rivalry into matched monuments, and the Prince ratifies the settlement while confessing his share of its cost. The primer's three columns supply the essay's discipline. For every event claimed as a contest for authority, the romance reading of the same event should be stated fairly in the second column's terms before the materialist reading answers it, so that the conclusion is earned by comparison rather than asserted by vocabulary.
