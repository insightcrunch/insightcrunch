Ask a room of educated readers who Friedrich Engels was, and the answers arrive in a settled order. He was Karl Marx's friend. He was the wealthy manufacturer who paid the bills while Marx wrote in the British Museum. He was the loyal second who edited the unfinished volumes, answered the correspondence, defended the doctrine against its enemies and asked for nothing except to stand a step behind the greater man. Every element of that portrait contains some truth, and the portrait as a whole is false, because it arranges the two lives in the wrong sequence at the one point where sequence decides everything. When the partnership began in earnest in the late summer of 1844, the younger man from Barmen had already published a critique of political economy that his future collaborator praised in print, and he was carrying in his luggage the notes for a study of English industrial life that no one else in their circle could have written. Marx came to political economy through Engels. The empirical portrait of the industrial working class that grounded their shared politics was drawn by Engels, from streets he had walked for nearly two years. The assistant, on inspection, turns out to have been a founder who arrived early.

That reversal has a name in this article, because a claim that cannot be named cannot be tested. The chronological correction is the simple discipline of restoring dates to a story that habit tells backwards: Engels published a critique of political economy before Marx had written one, and he completed an empirical investigation of industrial conditions before the collaboration existed, so any account that casts him as a follower who attached himself to a finished doctrine has the founding years upside down. The correction does not demote Marx, whose theoretical achievement dwarfs everything around it and needs no protection from a calendar. It promotes the record over the legend. What follows is an attempt to read Engels whole: the pietist manufacturer's son who became a revolutionary, the clerk who mapped Manchester's slums with a thoroughness later generations of sociologists would recognise as fieldwork, the military analyst whose family nickname was the General, the businessman who spent two decades inside a cotton firm he despised and used its profits to keep a research programme alive, and the elderly editor who, after 1883, became the tradition's chief expositor and, in the eyes of its critics, its first distorter. Each of those figures is documented. Together they describe a thinker whose independent contribution can be listed, dated and weighed, which is precisely what the junior partner legend prevents anyone from doing.

## A manufacturer's son in the Wupper valley

Friedrich Engels was born on 28 November 1820 in Barmen, a textile town in the Wupper valley whose chimneys, dye works and crowded workers' districts would supply the scenery of his earliest writing. His father, also Friedrich Engels, owned cotton mills in Barmen and held a partnership in the Manchester firm of Ermen and Engels, and the household was prosperous, disciplined and devout. The family's pietism was not a decorative faith. It was a demanding evangelical Protestantism that treated worldly success as a sign to be examined and pleasure as a snare to be watched, and the son's rebellion against it, conducted first in private letters and then in print, gave his intellectual formation its emotional charge. A boy raised to scrutinise his own soul for evidence of grace learned habits of self interrogation that survived the loss of the doctrine itself, and readers of his mature correspondence will find the confessional intensity redirected rather than extinguished.

His formal education ended early, and the manner of its ending matters for everything that followed. He attended the gymnasium in Elberfeld, a respectable school, but his father withdrew him a year before the final examination so that he could enter the family business, and the university education that Marx received as a matter of course was never offered to him. Engels was an autodidact by compulsion before he was one by conviction. He trained in the counting house, learned the mechanics of trade, and educated himself in languages, literature, history and philosophy in the margins of a working day, a pattern that left permanent marks on his style. Where Marx's prose carries the architecture of the seminar and the dissertation, Engels writes like a man explaining something across a desk to a colleague who must grasp it before the post leaves: quickly, concretely, with the example arriving before the abstraction. The difference in their educations is the first and most innocent source of the later hierarchy between them, since academic culture habitually mistakes the credentialled route for the serious one.

The young clerk's apprenticeship took him to Bremen in 1838, to the office of a merchant consul, and Bremen gave him two gifts that Barmen could not. The first was distance from the paternal household sufficient to read dangerously. The second was a public. Writing under the pen name Friedrich Oswald, he began publishing letters and poems in Hamburg's Telegraph for Germany, pieces that mixed literary ambition with a young man's fury at the narrowness of his home valley. The Letters from Wuppertal, published in 1839 when he was eighteen, already contain the germ of the Manchester book: a walk through his own town's mill districts, an inventory of disease, drunkenness and stunted children, and an argument connecting the misery not to the workers' character but to the factory system and the hypocrisy of owners who preached temperance while profiting from exhausted labour. He had never left Germany. He had simply looked at the streets he grew up on with the piety stripped away.

Philosophy reached him through the same door it reached every restless young German of his generation. He read Strauss, absorbed the shock of the Life of Jesus, and gravitated toward the Young Hegelians, that loose company of journalists and lecturers who were turning Hegel's dialectic against the Prussian state and the Christian doctrine Hegel had been hired to reconcile with it. In 1841 he moved to Berlin for his year of military service, choosing the artillery, and used the posting as an unofficial studentship, attending Schelling's lectures in order to attack them in pamphlets defending the Hegelian left. The episode displays a trait that recurs across his life: Engels learned a subject by volunteering for the fight about it. The military year also left a residue of professional competence. He took soldiering seriously, mastered the technical literature, and filed away an expertise that would resurface decades later in war correspondence read across Europe.

By 1842 the family had a plan for its difficult heir. He would go to Manchester, to the offices of Ermen and Engels at Southgate House, and learn the cotton trade at its imperial centre. The intention was commercial rescue: steady work, English partners, distance from Berlin radicals. The result was the opposite of rescue, because Manchester in 1842 was the most instructive city on earth for a young man already convinced that the factory system was a moral catastrophe and already armed with a philosophy that told him history moved through contradiction. His father, in effect, posted his rebellious son to the one address where rebellion could become research.

## What Barmen taught and what Bremen added

The Wupper valley deserves a slower look, because Engels's first subject was not England but home, and the habits formed there explain the method he later carried across the Irish Sea. Barmen and Elberfeld were twin towns strung along a river blackened by dye works, where ribbon, yarn and cloth production bound thousands of families to merchants and mill owners in a web of putting out work and factory labour. Pietism governed the culture of the owning class, and its social teaching held that poverty reflected divine ordering and personal failing in roughly equal measure. The teenage Engels walked to business through districts where children worked at looms in cellars, where typhus moved through overcrowded housing, and where the sermons he heard on Sunday assigned the blame to drink and idleness. His letters from the valley are furious precisely because they are local: he names the dissonance between the chapel's account and the street's evidence, and he treats the street as the authority.

That priority of observed fact over received doctrine survived every later change in his thinking. When he turned to philosophy he remained impatient with systems that deduced the world from a principle, and when he turned to economics he trusted the ledger, the wage book and the factory inspector's report over the elegant theorem. Critics who later charged him with vulgarising a subtle method rarely reckoned with this formation. Engels was an empiricist by upbringing long before he was a materialist by argument, and the England book reads the way it does because its author had been practising its method, on a smaller stage, since adolescence.

Bremen contributed the craft of publication. The young Oswald learned to write for editors, to compress an argument into a feuilleton, to review books he had skimmed and operas he had genuinely studied, and to sustain a pseudonymous double existence between the counting house and the press. He also encountered, in the circle around the Bremen pastor and the radical journalism of the day, the first rumours of communism as a living movement rather than a French memory. Wilhelm Weitling's writings circulated among German artisans; Moses Hess was preaching a communism derived from philosophy; and by the time Engels left for England he had declared himself, in correspondence, a communist, though the word still named a moral allegiance more than a programme. What England supplied was the missing term: a working class in motion, organised, striking, petitioning, and visibly becoming a historical actor rather than a passive object of pity. Chartism would teach him what Wuppertal pietism and Berlin philosophy between them could not, that the sufferers in the mill districts were also protagonists.

## Manchester: the making of an investigator

Engels arrived in Manchester in November 1842 and stayed, with interruptions, until the summer of 1844. His official duties were light enough to leave him dangerous amounts of freedom, and he used that freedom methodically. He read the city's newspapers across the political spectrum, from the Manchester Guardian to the Chartist Northern Star. He attended lectures, haunted the reading rooms, and gathered the reports of factory inspectors, medical officers and parliamentary commissions with a completist's appetite. Above all he walked. The districts he traversed, Ancoats, Little Ireland, the courts behind Deansgate, the cellar dwellings along the Irk, were places that respectable Manchester preferred to know through statistics, and Engels insisted on knowing them through his boots.

His guide into those districts was Mary Burns, an Irish working woman employed in the mills, whom he met in Manchester and with whom he remained, in a union never formalised by marriage, until her death in 1863. Burns gave him what no report could: entry into homes, pubs, sickrooms and the informal economy of the poor, and an interpreter for a world whose speech and customs marked him as an outsider however sympathetic his questions. Any honest account of the England book must record that its knowledge was mediated by a working class woman whose name the official record nearly lost, and that Engels's marriage, late in life, to her sister Lizzie Burns, acknowledged in private what Victorian convention forbade in public. The partnership with Mary Burns stands behind the research the way the partnership with Marx stands behind the theory, and it was entirely his own.

The investigation widened beyond Manchester. He visited London, Leeds and other manufacturing towns, interviewed Chartist leaders including Julian Harney, wrote for the movement's press, and began contributing to the Owenite New Moral World, which carried his early reports on the continent's communist movements to an English radical readership. He also kept a businessman's eye on the machinery of the trade itself: the mills, the warehouses, the exchanges where raw cotton from slave worked plantations in the American South was priced for spinners in Lancashire. That double vantage, counting house above and cellar below, is the book's signature. Engels knew how the profits were made because making them was his day job, and he knew what they cost because his evenings belonged to the people who paid.

### What did Engels find in the Manchester districts?

He found a population housed in courts and cellars unfit for animals, fed on adulterated provisions, worked from childhood at machinery that maimed, and buried early from diseases that followed the housing as predictably as rent followed wages. He also found organisation, intelligence and political anger of a sophistication the middle class press denied existed among operatives.

The research taught him a lesson in method that he never unlearned. Official inquiry in industrial England was not scarce; it was abundant, blue bound and unread. Chadwick's sanitary report, the factory commissioners' volumes, the Poor Law inquiries, all contained devastating material, quoted by no one because quotation required a reader willing to assemble the indictment and accept its conclusions. Engels's innovation was assembly and acceptance. He trusted the bourgeois state's own documents against the bourgeoisie's self image, a technique Marx would later raise to genius in the chapters of Capital built from factory inspectors' reports. The pupil in that technique, again, was the senior partner; the teacher was the clerk from Barmen who had spent two years learning which shelves in Manchester held the evidence.

He left England in August 1844 with the investigation substantially complete and a reputation, among the small international world of advanced radicals, as the young German who knew England. The book itself was written in Barmen over the following winter, in German, for a German audience he hoped to convert, and published at Leipzig in 1845 under the title Die Lage der arbeitenden Klasse in England. Its English career came absurdly late: an American edition in 1887 and a London edition in 1892, by which time the districts it described had been partly rebuilt and its author was an old man annotating his own youthful fury with corrections and a famous, rueful preface. The delay shaped the book's reception for a century, since the English speaking world met it as a historical document rather than as what it had been, a contemporary intervention that arrived before the theory it is usually said to illustrate.

## The book that walked the streets

The Condition of the Working Class in England deserves treatment as a work in its own right, because the legend files it as juvenilia and the record will not support the filing. Its author was twenty four. Its architecture is deliberate: an introduction framing the industrial revolution as the decisive event of the age, a survey of the great towns district by district, examinations of housing, clothing, food, health and mortality, studies of particular trades from miners to handloom weavers, accounts of working class movements from trade unions to Chartism, and a closing assessment of the future he expected the system's contradictions to produce. Dedication, contents and argument all address the English workers directly, in a preface written in English, which announced that the author had lived among them, shared their hopes and intended the book as a weapon placed in their hands.

Its method repays attention from anyone interested in how social investigation was invented. Engels combined three kinds of evidence that the scholarship of his day kept apart. He used official statistics and reports, quoted extensively and checked against each other. He used the press, including hostile newspapers whose admissions against interest he prized. And he used direct observation, recorded with a surveyor's particularity: street widths, court layouts, the depth of filth in a cellar, the smell of a river, the price of potatoes in a particular market. Later urban sociology would dignify the combination as triangulation, and the great Victorian investigators who followed, Mayhew in London, Booth a generation later, worked recognisably in the territory Engels had paced out as a foreign clerk in his early twenties. Historians of the social sciences, when they attend to him at all, concede the priority while marvelling at the circumstances.

The book's judgements are its author's own and sometimes raw. He wrote of the Irish immigration with the period's prejudices only partly escaped, a failing he shares with most English commentary of the decade and which honest readers should register rather than excuse. He predicted revolution in England with a confidence the following decades disappointed, and he said so himself in later prefaces, trading prophecy for analysis as the facts accumulated. He described the degradation produced by the factory system in language of moral horror that the drier Marx of Capital would refine into the analysis of the working day. Yet the analytic core, the argument that competition among workers and among capitals alike drives conditions toward a floor set by subsistence, and that the system's casualties are structural products rather than personal failures, is political economy conducted through reportage, and it predates any comparable work from Marx's pen by years.

Reception at first was German and muted, a few reviews, some notice in socialist circles, silence from the respectable press whose documents he had raided. Its afterlife was larger. When the English editions finally appeared in the 1880s and 1890s they were read as a founding text by a new generation of labour movement readers, and twentieth century historians of industrialisation, from the Hammonds onward, mined it as a primary source while arguing with its conclusions. The Hammonds treated it as evidence of the first importance from a witness whose hostility made his admissions valuable. Later revisionists questioned his mortality figures and his selection of districts, and the argument over his accuracy continues in the journals, which is itself a kind of tribute: books that merely illustrated someone else's theory do not sustain a research literature of their own.

### Was the Manchester study derivative of other reports?

No. Its raw materials included official reports, as any investigation of the period required, but the synthesis, the street level observation and the governing argument were assembled by Engels from his own residence and walking in the districts. Contemporaries who knew the city recognised the descriptions, and no earlier work combines the evidence in his form.

What the book settles, for the purposes of this profile, is the question of standing. Before he had written a line jointly with Marx, before the Manifesto, before the name of their doctrine existed, Engels had produced a durable work of social investigation that scholars still cite, dispute and teach. Whatever is later said about simplification or codification must somehow accommodate the fact that the tradition's second figure began as an originator of empirical research, working a method that the tradition's first figure would subsequently make famous.

## The outline that turned Marx toward economics

In the same crowded season that produced the Manchester investigation, Engels wrote the short text on which the chronological correction principally rests. The Outlines of a Critique of Political Economy appeared in the Deutsch-Französische Jahrbücher, the journal Marx co-edited with Arnold Ruge, in 1844, and it attempted something no one in their circle had yet done: a systematic attack on the categories of classical economics from inside the science's own literature. Engels had read Smith, Ricardo, Malthus, Say, James Mill and McCulloch, and he turned their premises against their conclusions with an undergraduate's relish and a pamphleteer's pace. Private property, he argued, was the unexamined assumption smuggled into every category; competition, preached as freedom, produced monopoly as its necessary twin; value theory contradicted itself between the English appeal to production costs and the French appeal to utility; and the Malthusian doctrine of population was exposed as a device for blaming the poor for the poverty that the system manufactured.

The Outlines is a young man's essay, brilliant and uneven, moral in its energies where the mature Marx would be analytic. Its economics contains confusions its author later outgrew, and he knew it, referring to the piece in later years with the indulgence reserved for first attempts. None of that touches its historical function. It announced, to the tiny readership of an exiled German journal, that the critique of society must pass through the critique of political economy, that the categories of the economists were historical products rather than natural laws, and that communism required an economic argument and not merely a moral protest. Marx read it as a revelation of direction. In the preface to his 1859 Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy, looking back over the whole road to Capital, Marx described the Outlines in terms any author would frame: a brilliant sketch of the critique of the categories of political economy. The phrase matters because of who wrote it and when. It is the mature Marx, at the height of his powers, assigning priority in print.

The documentary trail supports the assignment. Marx's Paris manuscripts of 1844, the notebooks from which the doctrine of alienated labour was quarried, engage the economists through questions the Outlines had posed, and his excerpt notebooks show him beginning the systematic reading of Smith and Ricardo that would occupy twenty years. The two men had met briefly in Cologne in 1842, when Marx edited the Rheinische Zeitung and Engels passed through its office on the way to England, an encounter both remembered as cool. Their real meeting came in Paris at the end of August 1844, at the Café de la Régence, and it lasted ten days. They discovered, in Engels's later account, complete agreement in all theoretical fields, and from that summer their joint work began. The order of discovery should be noted without varnish. The German philosophy was Marx's ground, conquered in his doctoral years. The English economy and its critique were Engels's ground, conquered in Manchester. The partnership pooled two conquests, and the pooling is unintelligible if either man is cast as the other's pupil.

### Did Engels write about economics before Marx?

Yes. His outline of a critique of political economy appeared in 1844, before Marx had turned seriously to the subject, and Marx praised it. Engels also completed his study of English working conditions in 1845, based on direct observation in Manchester.

The snippet answer states the case in miniature, and the full record enlarges rather than qualifies it. Consider what Marx had published on economics before the Paris meeting: journalism on the law on thefts of wood, which had forced him, by his own later confession, into embarrassed engagement with material interests, and polemics against Hegel's philosophy of right. Consider what Engels had published: a critique of the whole categorial apparatus of political economy and, in manuscript, a book length empirical study of the world's most advanced industrial economy. On the subject that would define both their lives, the ledger of 1844 is not close. Engels himself, modest to a fault in later years, insisted he had merely given Marx a push along a road Marx would have found alone, and the insistence has been gratefully repeated by every writer who prefers the legend. It may even be true as counterfactual biography. It is irrelevant as history, because the road was in fact found, signposted and first travelled by the man from Barmen, and the correction simply declines to let modesty rewrite dates.

## From Paris to Brussels: the partnership forms

The ten days in Paris set a pattern that lasted forty years. The two men complemented each other with an almost theatrical completeness. Marx worked slowly, deeply, in spirals, returning to foundations, reluctant to publish anything that had not survived his own cross examination. Engels worked fast, outward, in straight lines, drafting at speed, happy to publish the provisional and correct it in the next number. Marx's health and finances were chronically precarious; Engels, once established in Manchester, was solvent and organised. Marx's prose could be labyrinthine; Engels had a journalist's gift for the lucid exposition that hostile readers could not misunderstand. Their correspondence, thousands of letters across four decades, reads as a single research seminar conducted by post, in which each man routinely assigned the other the tasks his own temperament avoided. Marx asked for facts about trade, for military judgements, for money, for summaries of books he had no time to read. Engels asked for the theoretical verdict, the deepening of an argument, the final architecture.

Their first joint book, The Holy Family, published in 1845, was a polemic against their former Young Hegelian comrades, and its division of labour already favoured Marx, who wrote the bulk of it while Engels contributed early chapters before leaving Paris. The German Ideology, written in Brussels in 1845 and 1846 and abandoned, in Marx's famous phrase, to the gnawing criticism of the mice, settled their accounts with German philosophy and stated the materialist conception of history in extended form for the first time. Who contributed what to that manuscript is a genuine scholarly question, pursued in the article devoted to the [marx engels authorship question](/2013/06/01/marx-engels-authorship-question/), and it is signposted rather than settled here. What can be said without entering that dispute is that the Brussels manuscripts emerged from dictation, discussion and shared notebooks, a mode of production in which the category of sole authorship fits badly, and that both men regarded the exercise as self clarification rather than publication.

Brussels also made them organisers. They built a Communist Correspondence Committee to link the scattered groups of the European left, quarrelled productively with Weitling and Proudhon, and in 1847 joined and transformed the League of the Just into the Communist League, whose second congress commissioned the document that made them immortal. The Manifesto of the Communist Party, published in London in February 1848, was drafted by Marx in its final form from a catechism draft, the Principles of Communism, written by Engels in the autumn of 1847. The draft survives, and its relation to the finished text is a matter of public record: question and answer transfigured into some of the most famous prose of the century. Engels's own verdict on the transaction was characteristic. He proposed the change of form, supplied the skeleton, and spent the rest of his life directing credit for the flesh to Marx, a generosity that biographers have too often mistaken for an accurate org chart.

For the life that formed the senior partner in that exchange, readers should turn to the companion profile of [karl marx intellectual biography](/2013/05/01/karl-marx-intellectual-biography/), which treats Marx's formation in the depth it deserves. The point of the present article is narrower. By the eve of the 1848 revolutions, Engels had contributed the partnership's economic initiation, its empirical foundation, its organisational energy and the draft of its founding document. The legend of the talented amateur assisting a genius cannot survive contact with that inventory. What survives is something more interesting: a collaboration between two originators with different gifts, in which the division of labour was real, acknowledged and, for the most part, cheerfully accepted by both.

## Revolution, exile and the correspondent's trade

The revolutions of 1848 gave Engels his first experience of armed politics and his second education. He helped edit the Neue Rheinische Zeitung in Cologne, where Marx was editor in chief and Engels the indispensable lieutenant who wrote much of the paper's military and Hungarian coverage. When the Prussian counter revolution closed in, he joined the uprising in the Palatinate and Baden as aide de camp to August Willich, fought in four engagements, and crossed into Switzerland with the defeated revolutionary army in July 1849, one of the last to leave German soil. The campaign confirmed two lifelong convictions: that revolutions are decided by material organisation as much as by enthusiasm, and that he personally was good at war. Both convictions fed the military writing that would make his European reputation in the following decade.

Exile reassembled the partners in London, and the 1850s distributed their roles in the form the legend remembers. Marx settled into the British Museum and the long gestation of his economic work, writing journalism for the New York Daily Tribune to keep his household alive. Engels returned, in November 1850, to the Manchester office of Ermen and Engels, to the counting house he had escaped in 1844, and stayed for twenty years. The arrangement was deliberate and was understood by both men as a sacrifice made to a common project. Marx's letters acknowledge it with gratitude and guilt in roughly equal measure. Engels performed the sacrifice with a discipline that deserves emphasis: he rose inside the firm, mastered its business, became a partner in 1864, and converted a hated occupation into the financial engine of a research programme being conducted two hundred miles away.

His own writing continued under cover of the double life. He studied languages with a philologist's appetite, adding Russian, Spanish, Italian and a shelf of others to a repertoire his friends estimated in dozens. He wrote military analysis for the Tribune, sometimes under Marx's byline in the paper's early years when Marx's English was still forming, a ghost writing arrangement the authorship scholarship has since unpicked. He collaborated on the articles about revolution and counter revolution in Germany, published under Marx's name. And he began, in the Manchester evenings, the self education in the natural sciences, mathematics, chemistry, physiology, that would eventually produce the manuscripts on the dialectics of nature. The clerk who mapped Little Ireland had become, by slow accretion, one of the most formidably read men in Europe, and almost none of his readers knew his name.

### Why did Engels return to the firm he hated?

Because the partnership's economics required it. Marx's research could not feed his family, journalism paid irregularly and badly, and a Manchester salary converted directly into rent, books and time in London. Engels treated the counting house as his contribution to a joint enterprise, endured it for two decades, and sold his share the moment retirement became possible.

The terms of that endurance are recorded in the correspondence with a candour that forbids romanticising them. He complained about the office in language that scorched the page, counted the years, drank against the boredom, and rode to hounds with the Cheshire hunt as the price of commercial respectability among the mill owners whose world he was documenting for destruction. His enemies later called the hunting hypocrisy. His letters call it camouflage, and the record of remittances to London supports the letters. Between the early 1850s and Marx's death, Engels's subsidies, regular allowances, emergency rescues, payment of debts, settlement of the household's crises and, after 1869, a settled annual income, kept the Marx family in the middle class decency its head's research required. The sums passed without contract or complaint, and when they are totalled by biographers the conclusion is unavoidable: Capital was underwritten in Manchester, and its author knew it, and said so.

## The General: a second profession in war

Within the family circle of the Marx household, Engels was known as the General, a nickname earned by the military expertise he had been accumulating since his artillery year in Berlin and his campaigning in Baden. The expertise was professional in substance. He followed campaigns the way other men follow markets, with maps, returns and the technical journals, and he wrote about war for publication across three decades: analyses of the Crimean War, of the American Civil War, whose outcome he and Marx called earlier and more accurately than most professional observers, of the Austro Prussian war of 1866 and, most famously, of the Franco Prussian war of 1870, when his anonymous articles for the Pall Mall Gazette, signed only with his initials, predicted the campaign's course, including the catastrophe awaiting the French army at Sedan, with an accuracy that set London talking about the retired officer who must be writing them. There was no retired officer. There was a cotton merchant in Manchester whose hobby had become a second profession.

The military work matters to the profile for reasons beyond colour. It demonstrates, first, that Engels's authority in the partnership rested partly on a domain where Marx deferred entirely, asking for judgements on campaigns and commanders the way he asked for trade figures, and accepting the answers. It demonstrates, second, a cast of mind: operational, logistical, attentive to railways, mobilisation timetables, supply and terrain, the material substrate of events rather than their heroic narration. When Engels later wrote about the state, about violence, about the peasant war of the sixteenth century, the same attention to the mechanics beneath the proclamations distinguishes his pages. The General was not a hobbyist bolted onto a theorist. He was the partnership's minister for the material world in its hardest form, and his judgement in that ministry was tested in public, repeatedly, against events.

His one book length military historical work shows the method at full stretch. The Peasant War in Germany, written in 1850 from the recent revolutionary defeat, treated the rising of 1525 as the first act of a German bourgeois revolution strangled by its own allies, and it did so with research into Münzer and the Anabaptist movements that professional historians of the period had not attempted. The book was reissued in the 1870s with a preface drawing the parallel between the peasants' leader and the modern movement's tasks, and it founded a historiographical tradition in its own right. Engels returned to the German past again in his studies of the Mark, the ancient communal landholding institution, and in his contributions to the history of early Christianity, where his comparison of the primitive Christian communities with the modern workers' movement scandalised the pious and delighted his friends. The range is the point. Between the Peasant War and the late essays on early Christianity stretches a body of historical writing, on subjects Marx never touched, which no theory of Engels as amanuensis can explain.

### How accurate were the General's published war predictions?

Accurate enough to make his reputation. His Pall Mall Gazette articles on the Franco Prussian war, written as the campaign unfolded, foresaw the French army's encirclement at Sedan and the war's broad course while professional commentators hesitated. His American Civil War judgements, shared with Marx, likewise called the conflict's transformation earlier than most observers.

War also shaped his political judgement in ways the doctrine's later custodians flattened. Having seen insurrection defeated by professional armies with modern weapons, Engels grew steadily more sceptical of barricade romanticism, and his 1895 introduction to a reissue of Marx's writings on France, which counselled legal mass organisation and the ballot where the ballot could be won, caused a scandal among militants who mistook the old General's arithmetic for apostasy. The party leadership of the German Social Democrats published the introduction in softened extracts, and Engels protested in private letters that his argument had been trimmed into its opposite. The episode is a useful corrective to two legends at once: the legend of Engels the rigid codifier, and the legend of Engels the mere echo. On the military question, at the end of his life, he was the tradition's most experienced voice, and the tradition preferred not to hear him.

## The businessman as fact, not embarrassment

No question follows Engels through the literature like the question of the factory. Was Engels a factory owner? The short answer is that he was a partner in the firm of Ermen and Engels, owners of the Victoria Mill at Weaste near Salford, where the company spun sewing thread, and that between 1850 and 1869 he drew his income from the exploitation his writings condemned. The longer answer, the one this profile is obliged to give in full, is that the arrangement was neither secret nor comfortable, that both men understood its contradictions precisely, and that the uses to which the income was put are documented in bankable detail. Victorian scandal sheets would have loved the story. The record, less lurid and more interesting, shows a man who treated commerce as conscription and spent the proceeds on the movement that proposed to abolish commerce in its existing form.

His position in the firm evolved across the two decades. He arrived in 1850 as a clerk in the Manchester office, the son of a partner, with a revolutionary past that his father's partners had every reason to deplore. He worked, by all accounts including his own gloomy ones, extremely well. The Ermen brothers, who ran the enterprise, valued his languages, his continental connections and his grasp of the trade, and in 1864 he was taken into partnership, acquiring the standing and the income share of an owner. Five years later he negotiated his exit, selling his share to the Ermens on terms that left him with capital sufficient for a gentleman's independence. He left Manchester for London in 1870 with the air, recorded in his letters, of a man escaping a sentence that had run its full term. The fox hunting, the dinner parties, the respectable club life of those years were the uniform of the posting, worn without conviction and discarded without regret.

What the firm funded must be stated plainly, because the funding is usually mentioned as gossip when it belongs in the history of ideas. From Manchester went the money that paid the Marx household's rent in Dean Street and later in Grafton Terrace and Maitland Park Road, that settled the pawnbroker's tickets and the butcher's bills, that kept the daughters at their lessons and the household in the black coat respectability Marx required for his self respect. From Manchester, after the retirement settlement, went a regular annual allowance that freed Marx from journalism altogether in his last productive decade. And from Manchester went the small, constant stream of five pound notes in envelopes, the emergency wires, the assumed debts, without which the archive of Capital would have been scattered by bailiffs sometime in the 1850s. Intellectual historians are trained to follow arguments. They are less trained to follow money, and the money in this story is not a footnote to the arguments. It is one of their material conditions, supplied by the man the legend remembers chiefly for supplying it, as though the pipeline and not the reservoir were the contribution.

### What did partnership in the firm actually involve?

From 1864 it involved shared ownership of the Victoria Mill enterprise, responsibility for continental correspondence and markets, a partner's share of profits, and the social standing of a Manchester manufacturer. Before that, from 1850, he was a salaried clerk and correspondent. He sold the share in 1869 and left the trade entirely.

The hypocrisy charge deserves its strongest statement before its answer. A man who denounces the factory system while drawing partnership profits from a factory, who hunts with the county while plotting the expropriation of the hunters, who lives as a Victorian gentleman on surplus value extracted from Lancashire operatives, stands, on the face of it, convicted by his own theory. Admirers who flinch from the charge and change the subject do Engels no service, because he never flinched from it himself. His defence, spread across the correspondence, runs as follows. The theory analyses a system, not the souls of its participants; a capitalist who understands the system remains a capitalist, and the analysis predicts his behaviour rather than excusing it. The relevant question is what a person does with the position the system assigns. Engels's answer was to convert commercial income into the material basis of the system's critique, at the cost of twenty years of his own life in an occupation he loathed, and to exit the position at the first moment the accumulated capital permitted. Whether that answer acquits him is a judgement readers must form for themselves; the neutrality this series observes requires only that the charge and the defence both appear at full strength, and that the facts, the partnership, the profits, the remittances, the exit, not be trimmed by either side.

One further fact belongs in this section because it disciplines both the scandal and the hagiography. Engels's knowledge of the cotton trade was not a dilettante's. He understood markets, credit, the American supply, the effects of the Civil War's blockade on Lancashire, the machinery of the exchange, with a practitioner's fluency, and that fluency flows through the partnership's correspondence whenever Marx needed the world economy explained at the level of the firm. The third volume of Capital, which Engels later edited, is full of the commercial and credit phenomena he had handled daily for twenty years. The factory, in short, was also a research post, held at personal cost, whose yield included some of the most concrete economic knowledge either man possessed.

## Retirement in London and the second career

Engels settled in London in 1870, at 122 Regent's Park Road, a short walk from Marx's house, and the final quarter century of his life divides into two careers that overlapped until 1883 and then merged into one. The first career was that of Marx's closest collaborator in the years of the International's aftermath and Capital's slow advance: adviser, factotum, fellow conspirator in the battles against Bakunin and the Lassalleans, host of the Sunday gatherings where the movement's visitors, exiles and delegations were received, vetted and encouraged. The second career, after Marx's death in March 1883, was that of executor, editor and patriarch, a role no one elected him to and no one else could have filled, which consumed his remaining twelve years and fixed the form in which the tradition would be transmitted to the century that followed.

The London household itself was an institution. Engels kept open house with a regularity that astonished visitors accustomed to Marx's more guarded domesticity. Socialists from every country found their way to Regent's Park Road, were fed, were interrogated about their movements with a staff officer's demand for numbers and facts, and left with letters of introduction, small subsidies and the sense of having been received at a headquarters. The young leaders of the German party, Bebel and Liebknecht above all, treated his judgements as a court of appeal. Eleanor Marx, Karl Kautsky, Eduard Bernstein and a generation of lesser figures passed through his study. When historians speak of Engels as the first Marxist, they usually mean the codification charge considered later in this profile. The phrase fits better here, in the sociological sense: he was the first person to live as the custodian of a doctrine that bore another man's name, and to build the networks, rituals and reading lists by which such a doctrine becomes a tradition.

His working habits in retirement astonished everyone who recorded them. He rose late, dealt with a correspondence that arrived from every continent and in a babel of languages, read the newspapers of several countries, worked at his desk until the small hours, and sustained the whole performance on formidable quantities of claret, Pilsener and good humour. The correspondence alone, preserved in the archives, amounts to a parallel oeuvre: thousands of letters advising parties, settling disputes, correcting proofs, recommending reading, analysing elections, in French, German, English, Italian, Spanish and beyond. Much of his late influence travelled by post rather than by book, which is one reason the printed record understates him. The letters to Sorge in America, to Bernstein, to Kautsky, to the Danes, Italians, Russians and French, are state papers of a movement that had no state, and their author functioned, in effect, as its foreign ministry and its supreme court combined.

## Anti-Dühring and the making of a system

The work that did most to shape what the world would call Marxism was written as a favour and published as a polemic. Eugen Dühring, a blind Berlin lecturer, had built a following inside the young German Social Democratic Party for a system of his own that mixed socialism with racial theory and a metaphysics of his invention, and the party leadership, anxious about the infection, asked Engels to answer him. Engels resented the assignment, which interrupted his studies in natural science, and discharged it in a series of articles in the party paper Vorwärts during 1877 and 1878, collected in 1878 as Herr Eugen Dühring's Revolution in Science, known ever since as Anti-Dühring. Marx followed the manuscript, contributed a chapter on the history of economic theory, and gave the enterprise his public blessing, a fact that matters greatly when the book's later reputation is weighed.

Anti-Dühring is three books wearing one coat. It contains a defence and exposition of the materialist conception of history, a summary of the economic argument of Capital compressed for readers who would never open the original, and a philosophy of nature and knowledge that ranges across astronomy, physics, chemistry, biology and mathematics, asserting that dialectical patterns govern change in every domain. The first two books made it the movement's textbook. Generations learned their Marxism from Engels's lucid, combative, funny summary, and learned it in his order, with his emphases, his examples and his jokes at Dühring's expense. The third book made it the target. The claim that the same laws run from the falling stone to the falling ministry, that negation of the negation can be illustrated by a grain of barley and by the history of philosophy alike, struck later critics as the moment a critical method hardened into a cosmology, and the argument about that hardening occupies a later section of this profile at the strength it deserves.

Three chapters of the polemic, detached and reworked in 1880 at the request of the French socialist Paul Lafargue, became Socialism: Utopian and Scientific, the most widely read text either man ever produced. Its account of the utopian socialists, Saint Simon, Fourier and Owen, treated with a generosity that surprised readers expecting abuse, and its compressed statement of historical materialism and the economics of Capital, carried the doctrine into languages and reading publics that the larger works never reached. Pamphlet editions multiplied across Europe and America; translations followed in a procession that continued for a century. If the question is which single text taught the nineteenth and early twentieth century what Marxism was, the answer is not Capital, read by thousands, but Engels's eighty page extraction from a polemic, read by millions. The transmission of the doctrine was, to a degree the legend never quite faces, Engels's work.

### What did the polemic against Dühring accomplish?

It defeated a rival system inside the German party, and became the first connected exposition of the whole doctrine by either founder: history, economics and philosophy of nature in one volume. Marx read and approved the manuscript and contributed a chapter. Its extraction as Socialism: Utopian and Scientific became the most widely circulated Marxist text of its era.

The Housing Question, published as articles in 1872 and 1873, shows the same expository gift turned on a single problem. Answering a Proudhonist proposal to solve working class housing misery by converting tenants into owners, Engels argued that the housing shortage was a necessary product of the same system that produced the factories, that it would be abolished with that system and not before, and that palliatives which left the wage relation untouched merely relocated the misery. The pamphlet's asperity delighted Marx, and its argument, that a social problem generated by a structure cannot be dissolved by schemes addressed to its symptoms, has been quoted by housing campaigners in every generation since. It is also, incidentally, the work in which Engels's Manchester knowledge does the heaviest lifting: the descriptions of working class districts in the industrial cities rest on the walking he had done thirty years before, refreshed by the reports of a new generation of investigators whose grandfathers he might have interviewed.

Ludwig Feuerbach and the End of Classical German Philosophy, published in 1886, performed a different office. Written for a journal and expanded into a pamphlet, it gave the movement its authorised genealogy: Hegel's dialectic rescued from Hegel's system, Feuerbach's materialism honoured and surpassed, the whole German philosophical inheritance audited and closed. Appended to it, at Engels's insistence, was Marx's set of theses on Feuerbach from 1845, published for the first time, which Engels called the first document in which the germ of the new world outlook was deposited. The gesture is characteristic of the man and fatal to the legend. The executor used his authority to publish his dead friend's most compressed statement of their shared philosophy, forty years after its composition, and to frame his own essay as its commentary. Self effacement of that order is not the behaviour of a disciple who never had ideas. It is the discipline of a co founder who had decided, early and finally, where the credit should bank.

## The family, the state and the deep past

In 1884, a year after Marx's death, Engels published The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State, built from Marx's notebooks on the American anthropologist Lewis Henry Morgan, whose Ancient Society had reached them as a revelation. The book argued that the monogamous family was a historical institution rather than a natural one, that it arose with private property and the need to transmit inheritance to known heirs, that the wife's position within it reproduced in miniature the relation of the propertyless to the propertied, and that the state emerged as the manager of the resulting antagonisms rather than as their arbiter. It drew on Bachofen's work on mother right, on Morgan's stages of savagery, barbarism and civilisation, and on the ethnographic record as the nineteenth century possessed it. A full treatment of the book as a text lives in the companion guide to [engels origin of the family guide](/2016/11/01/engels-origin-of-the-family-guide/), and the present profile confines itself to the book's place in its author's development.

That place is singular. The Origin is the one major work in which Engels, writing alone and late, attempted for anthropology what the Manchester book had attempted for industrial England: a materialist reordering of an entire field's evidence around the production and reproduction of life. Its anthropology has aged unevenly, as any work resting on Morgan's sequences was bound to age, and modern scholarship has discarded the unilinear stages while continuing to argue with the central provocation, that family forms are historical, variable and bound to property. Feminist readers of several generations have found in its pages both an indispensable opening, the insistence that women's subordination has a history and therefore can have an end, and a framework that subordinates gender to property in ways they have had to correct. The argument continues, which is more than can be said for most books of 1884, and it continues on terrain Engels staked out alone, in his sixty fourth year, from another man's notebooks that he alone had the learning and the standing to convert into a book.

His late historical writing ranged wider still. The essay on the role of force in history, left unfinished, worked through the Bismarck era with a staff officer's respect for the material preconditions of political violence. The studies of the Frankish period and the German Mark reconstructed communal institutions from legal and linguistic evidence. The two essays on early Christianity applied the materialist method to the sacred history of his own pietist childhood with a coolness that still startles: the early church read as a movement of the dispossessed, its doctrines and organisation compared, point by point, with the workers' movement of his own century, not to mock either but to explain both. A reader who follows Engels across these late works meets a mind enlarging its range as it aged, the opposite trajectory from the rigidification the codification charge describes, and the tension between the two pictures is one this profile refuses to resolve by slogan in either direction.

### What did Engels argue about the origins of the family?

He argued that the monogamous family arose historically with private property, to secure inheritance through known heirs, and that it carried the subordination of women inside it as a structural feature rather than a natural fact. The state, in the same account, emerged to manage the antagonisms of a society divided by property, not to stand above them.

Behind the family book stands a habit of thought worth naming, because it connects the young investigator of Manchester with the old scholar of the anthropologists. In both cases Engels trusted a mass of recalcitrant detail, gathered by other hands for other purposes, to yield a pattern when arranged by the questions his theory asked. Morgan's kinship tables were collected to serve a science of race and progress; Engels read them as a history of property. The factory inspectors wrote to serve administrative reform; Engels read them as an indictment of a system. The method has a name in his own vocabulary, the materialist reworking of an existing science from inside its evidence, and it may be the most transferable thing he ever practised. Marx performed it supremely on political economy. Engels performed it on urban conditions, on military history, on anthropology and, in the manuscripts considered next, attempted it on the whole of natural science.

## The manuscripts on nature

Engels's scientific studies began in Manchester, in the evenings stolen from the counting house, and continued in London with the help of scientifically trained friends, above all the chemist Carl Schorlemmer, whose advice steadied the chemistry and whose company steadied the chemist manqué. The project, pursued in fragments between 1873 and 1882 and abandoned when Marx's death imposed the executor's duties, aimed to show that the dialectical patterns the two men had found in history, the transformation of quantity into quality, the interpenetration of opposites, the negation of the negation, were discoverable throughout the natural world as it was being reconstructed by Darwin, by the cell theory, by thermodynamics and by the new chemistry. The manuscripts, published only in 1925 under the title Dialectics of Nature, arrived in a world already quarrelling about them, and the quarrel has never really stopped.

This profile signposts that quarrel rather than re fighting it, because the series gives the dispute its own full treatment in the guide to [engels dialectics of nature guide](/2012/09/01/engels-dialectics-of-nature-guide/), where the examples are audited one by one and the scientific verdicts are graded. What belongs here is the place of the project inside its author's life. The nature manuscripts were not a senile hobby. They were the work of the decade in which their author also produced Anti-Dühring and the family book, they grew from thirty years of disciplined reading, and they represented his conviction that a materialism which stopped at the factory gate, which had nothing to say about the world the factories were built in, would surrender half of reality to its opponents. Even readers who judge the project a failure usually concede the scale of the ambition and the reading. The man who is supposed to have been a mere populariser spent his best decade attempting the hardest synthesis available to his century, in manuscript, for no audience, at the desk of a retired cotton merchant in Primrose Hill.

The ecological recovery of these manuscripts belongs to the reassessment treated later in this profile, but its biographical kernel belongs here. Engels's attention to metabolism, to the revenge taken by nature on human projects that ignore her interconnections, illustrated by his famous warning that each victory over nature takes its revenge in unforeseen second and third order effects, grew from the same sensibility that had mapped the Irk's poisoned banks in 1844. The investigator who watched a river die of dye works did not need the twentieth century to teach him that production reorganises the natural world and is answered by it. The line from the Manchester walking to the nature manuscripts is continuous, and it is his line, walked at his pace, across fifty years.

## Executor: the editing of Capital

Marx died on 14 March 1883, leaving the second and third volumes of Capital as a mountain of manuscripts in varying states of readiness, some fair copied, some dictated, some sketched in a hand that only two people alive could read fluently, and of those two one was now dead. Engels understood at once what his remaining life was for. He set aside the nature project, set aside his own historical work, and gave the decade from 1883 to 1894 to the construction of Capital volumes two and three from the papers on his desk, deciphering, selecting, ordering, bridging, and writing the prefaces in which he explained, with a candour the scholarship has since verified in detail, exactly what he had done and what he had left alone.

Volume two appeared in 1885, volume three in 1894, a year before his own death, and the achievement should be weighed before it is questioned. Without the editing there is no second volume on the circulation of capital and no third volume on the process as a whole, no falling rate of profit argument in its canonical form, no analysis of merchant and interest bearing capital, no rent theory in its full deployment. The architecture of Marxist economics as the twentieth century received it is, in considerable part, an editorial construction, assembled by the one man who combined the decipherment, the economic knowledge, the authority and the devotion the task required. Engels compared himself, in the prefaces, to a literary executor discharging a trust, and the modesty of the comparison conceals the scale of the intervention: he chose among variants, imposed chapter divisions, drafted transitional passages and converted notebooks into a book, while striving, as the manuscript scholarship confirms, to intervene as little as the chaos of the materials allowed and to mark his interventions where he could.

The extent of that intervention is the subject of its own article in this series and is signposted here rather than adjudicated. Modern critical editions, above all the complete works edition known as MEGA, have placed the manuscripts and the edited volumes side by side, allowing scholars to measure the distance between what Marx left and what Engels published, passage by passage. The measured distance is neither negligible nor scandalous. Engels emerges from the comparison as a faithful editor working under impossible instructions, whose smoothing occasionally clarified and occasionally hardened the argument, and whose decisions, once printed, acquired the authority of Marx's own text for a century of readers who could not consult the originals. That last fact is a historical datum of the first importance, and it returns, in a different costume, when the codification charge is weighed. The tradition read Marx through Engels's editing before it read him through Engels's summaries, and both mediations were acts of love performed by the person best qualified to perform them. Posterity's task is not to resent the mediation but to see it clearly, which the new editions at last permit.

## The qualifying letters and the old man's caution

The last five years of Engels's life produced a set of letters that have been quoted in every subsequent argument about what the doctrine actually claims. Writing to Joseph Bloch in September 1890, to Conrad Schmidt the following month, to Franz Mehring in 1893 and to others who pressed him, the elderly Engels corrected, with visible exasperation, the mechanical reading of the materialist conception of history that younger adherents were already propagating. The economic element, he told Bloch, is ultimately determining, but it is not the only determining element; the political forms of the class struggle, constitutions, legal traditions, the very ideological reflexes in the heads of the participants, exercise their own influence on the course of events and in many cases preponderate in determining their form. History makes itself, he insisted, through an endless parallelogram of forces, individual wills colliding and producing a resultant none of them intended. To Mehring he confessed a share of blame: he and Marx, fighting opponents who denied the economic factor altogether, had stressed it beyond its due proportion, and had neglected the formal side, the manner in which ideas arise and react, for the content they were defending.

These letters, the fullest discussion of which belongs to the treatment of the [economic determinism debate](/2010/03/15/economic-determinism-debate/), are the executor's own correction of the codification he is accused of authoring, delivered while the ink of the codification was still wet. They complicate every simple version of the charge against him. The man who supposedly turned a supple method into an iron determinism spent his final years writing, to anyone who would listen, that the determinism was a caricature, that he had helped cause the misunderstanding by polemical overemphasis, and that anyone who twisted the materialist method into an excuse for not studying history would produce only nonsense. He even supplied the movement with a standing joke against itself, remarking that if materialist history were as simple as the mechanical version claimed, it would be very easy to apply, and it is not. The letters did not prevent the caricature from ruling much of the following century, partly because they travelled as private correspondence while the textbooks travelled as doctrine. But they stand in the record, and any verdict on Engels the systematiser must enter them as evidence for the defence from the defendant's own hand.

His death came on 5 August 1895, in London, from cancer of the throat and oesophagus that had silenced the famous voice before it stopped the famous pen. He had asked for no religious service and no monument. His ashes were scattered at sea off Beachy Head, near Eastbourne, in accordance with his wishes, from a small boat carrying the friends and comrades of a lifetime. The movement he had bankrolled, edited and advised buried him in the only manner he would accept, and then set about the century long argument concerning what, exactly, he had done to it.

## The codification charge at full strength

The strongest objection to everything argued so far must now be stated as its holders state it, without softening, because the profile's neutrality rules require the objection at full strength before any reply. The objection, associated with the Western Marxist tradition and given classic form by Georg Lukács in a famous footnote of 1923, by Karl Korsch, and later by scholars such as Norman Levine, who argued that Marxism was substantially Engels's creation and distortion, runs as follows. Whatever Engels achieved before 1844 or in the Manchester streets, his expository works after Marx's death, and Anti-Dühring above all, performed a fateful transformation. A critical method, whose home was the analysis of social forms and whose dialectic described the self movement of a society divided against itself, was recast as a universal doctrine with laws valid for nature, history and thought alike. The dialectic, in Marx a way of presenting the inner logic of capital, became in Engels's hands a cosmology, illustrated by barley seeds and boiling water, guaranteed by the sciences and available for memorisation. It was this version, portable, systematic and teachable, that the mass parties absorbed, that the Second International canonised, and that the Third International and the Soviet state after it hardened into an official philosophy with textbooks, examinations and heresies. On this account the chronological correction is a biographical nicety that evades the real charge: not that Engels arrived second, but that what he made of arriving first in the classroom deformed the inheritance for a hundred years, and that Marx, who never published a philosophy of nature and never called his method by the later label, bears no responsibility for the deformation because the deformation was the disciple's work.

The charge has genuine evidence, and honesty requires the evidence to be exhibited rather than gestured at. The phrase dialectical materialism is indeed not Marx's; it enters the tradition through the generation after him, with Joseph Dietzgen and Georgi Plekhanov as its carriers, and the systematised doctrine built under that name descends from Engels's expositions rather than from Marx's texts. The examples in Anti-Dühring and the nature manuscripts do sometimes proceed by analogy, finding the negation of the negation in a plant's life cycle with a confidence that later philosophy of science found embarrassing. The transmission claim is simply true as history: the parties taught Engels's summaries, the states canonised the system, and the system in its official Soviet form, with its catechism of laws, owes more to the structure of Anti-Dühring than to the structure of Capital. A defence that denied any of this would forfeit the right to be heard on the remainder.

The reply begins where the objection is weakest, in the documentary record of Marx's own conduct. Marx read the Anti-Dühring enterprise in manuscript, contributed the chapter on the history of economic theory to its second part, recommended the polemic in correspondence, and never, in the five years between its appearance and his death, recorded a reservation about its philosophy of nature in any letter or note that has survived. He had himself sketched, in early writings and in asides across the economic manuscripts, a conception of nature as man's inorganic body and of natural science as a historically developing human activity, which gave Engels's extension a foothold in the master's work rather than none. The division of labour, moreover, was the partnership's own constitution, agreed across forty years: Engels took the polemical, expository and scientific fronts because Marx asked him to, needed him to, and lacked the time, health and in some domains the knowledge to do it himself. To convict the expositor of betraying a method whose exposition he was assigned, whose drafts the method's author approved, requires a standard of evidence the objectors rarely apply elsewhere.

The reply then concedes what must be conceded and distinguishes what must be distinguished. Conceded: the transmission point. Engels's texts were the conduit, the conduit shaped what flowed through it, and the official doctrine of the following century is unthinkable without his systematising hand. Distinguished: authorship of the hardening is not the same as authorship of the summary. The catechism form, the examination syllabus, the state enforcement, belong to institutions of the next century that selected from Engels what suited them and discarded, among much else, the qualifying letters in which he warned against exactly the mechanical doctrine they built. A writer is answerable for his arguments. Whether he is answerable for the uses of readers who canonised half his work and suppressed the other half is a question in the ethics of reception that the objection usually assumes rather than argues. The verdict this profile is permitted by its evidence is therefore a differentiated one. The codification charge stands against the transmission and fails against the intention; it identifies a real transformation and assigns it a single author where the record shows a founder who approved the exposition, a partner who performed it, and a posterity that froze it. Readers who need a villain will find the record uncooperative. Readers who need the truth will find it, as usual, in the chronology.

## Misreadings that the record corrects

Around a figure this mediated, errors accumulate like iron filings around a magnet, and several deserve individual correction because each blocks a door the evidence opens. The first misreading casts Engels as Marx's follower, a talented recruit converted by the master's writings. The chronology disposes of it: the recruit had published the critique of political economy and completed the Manchester study before the conversion could have occurred, and the café meeting in Paris was a recognition between equals in adjacent territories, recorded as complete agreement in all theoretical fields. Followers are not usually described by their supposed masters as the authors of brilliant sketches that set the master's own agenda.

The second misreading reduces his contribution to money. Money he certainly contributed, in quantities and across decades that shamed richer men's charity, and the funding mattered, as this profile has insisted. But the reduction performs a quiet theft: it converts the author of the England book, the Outlines, the Peasant War, the family study and the movement's textbook into a wallet with a signature. The test is simple. Remove the money from the story and Capital is imperilled, but remove the writings and the doctrine loses its first economics, its empirical foundation, its anthropology, its authorised self exposition and its correctives against determinism, which is to say it loses body parts no wallet supplies.

The third misreading holds that he simplified a theory he did not understand. The simplification charge has been weighed in the previous section; the misunderstanding charge cannot survive his correspondence with Marx, in which the two men test arguments against each other as peers for forty years, nor his editing of the later volumes of Capital, a task no uncomprehending man could have performed to the standard the manuscript comparison has since verified. Disagreement with his expository choices is legitimate and often justified. Incomprehension is a verdict the documents refuse to sign.

The fourth misreading concerns the Manchester book, occasionally described as derivative hackwork or even as substantially the work of others. It was written in Barmen in the winter of 1844 and 1845 from his own notes, his own walking and his own reading of the official reports, and its descriptions were recognised by contemporaries who knew the districts. Mary Burns guided the research, as guides do, and guidance is not ghost writing; the synthesis, the structure and the argument are the author's, and the scholarly argument about his figures and selections proceeds, as it should, on the assumption of his authorship.

The fifth misreading credits him with inventing dialectical materialism, a coinage that postdates his main expositions and belongs to the systematising generation of Dietzgen and Plekhanov. Engels called his outlook materialist, spoke of the modern materialism and of the dialectical method, and left the isms of system to successors. Precision here is not pedantry. The label is usually produced as evidence that he packaged Marxism into a doctrine with a brand name, and the evidence dissolves on inspection of the dates: the branding was done by other hands, after the supposed brander's chief works were already written, which is the chronological correction operating once again, quietly, in a corner of the argument where few think to apply it.

## The reassessment and its grounds

Scholarship has been revising the junior partner picture for a generation, and the revision now has monuments. A full biography by Tristram Hunt, published in 2009 under the title Marx's General in Britain and as The Frock-Coated Communist elsewhere, presented Engels whole to a general readership for the first time in decades: the revolutionary in a frock coat, the fox hunting communist, the serious military mind, the businessman whose double life was a strategy rather than a shame. Hunt's Engels is neither saint nor falsifier but a figure of formidable energies whose partnership with Marx appears as a collaboration of peers with a deliberate division of labour. The biography's commercial and critical success signalled that the reassessment had escaped the journals.

The ecological wing of the revision draws on the scientific manuscripts that the codification debate had made disreputable. John Bellamy Foster's The Return of Nature, published in 2020, situated Engels within a serious engagement with the natural science of his day, Darwin, Liebig, the cell theorists and the physicists, and argued that the materialism he shared with Marx carried an ecological reasoning, about metabolism, soil, deforestation and nature's revenge on careless production, that the twentieth century's caricature had rendered invisible. Related work by Foster and others on the metabolic rift had already revalued Marx's own ecological notebooks, a line of inquiry surveyed in the series guide to [ecological marxism complete guide](/2017/07/01/ecological-marxism-complete-guide/); the extension to Engels corrected the asymmetry in which the senior partner's nature writing was philosophy and the junior's was an embarrassment. Kohei Saito's studies of Marx's natural scientific notebooks belong to the same scholarly weather, reading the founders' engagement with science as continuing research rather than youthful error. The reassessment is not a consensus and should not be reported as one. It is an ongoing argument with named works, dated publications and identifiable claims, which is exactly the status the reader needs in order to use it: the junior partner picture is no longer the default of scholarship, and sources that still present it are presenting a position, whether they know it or not.

Older supports for the revision deserve mention because they predate its fashion. The manuscript scholarship associated with the complete editions has steadily documented Engels's editorial fidelity and his independent working methods. Terrell Carver's studies of the intellectual relationship argued, from the texts, that the two men developed distinguishable positions within a shared project and that the habit of citing them as a single author, MarxandEngels in one breath, conceals differences a careful reader should preserve. Historians of social science have recovered the Manchester book's priority in urban investigation. Military historians have never needed persuading about the General. The reassessment, seen whole, is less a reversal than a restoration of resolution: a blurred double portrait resolving, under better light, into two faces.

### Is the reassessment merely fashion?

No. Its strongest exhibits are documentary and predate its popularity: the dates of the 1844 Outlines and the 1845 England book, Marx's printed praise, the manuscript record of the Capital editing, and the qualifying letters themselves. Fashion may explain the timing of the revival. It cannot manufacture the evidence on which the revival rests.

What should a reader do with a figure whose standing was set by polemic and is being reset by scholarship? The practical answer is to read him in the order the table imposes: the Outlines for the founding gesture, the England book for the method in its first maturity, the family book for the independent synthesis, the letters of the 1890s for the self correction, and Anti-Dühring with the charge and the reply both in mind. Readers who keep their notes, cross references and quotations organised in a study system such as the [Marxism study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) will find the two oeuvres separating themselves on the page, which is the reassessment's whole demand made practical. The tradition he co founded spent a century citing him as a suffix. The record, examined without the suffix, shows a founder.

## Why the second founder keeps being misfiled

It is worth pausing over the mechanism of the misfiling, because the mechanism explains why the chronological correction has to be restated in every generation. Partnerships between a systematic genius and a versatile collaborator invite a literary template, the master and the disciple, and the template is reinforced by everything in the archive's presentation. The doctrine bears one name. The grave at Highgate bears one name, and Engels, by his own instruction, has no grave at all, only scattered ashes, a self effacement so complete it deleted the pilgrimage site by which traditions remember their saints. The correspondence, read carelessly, seems to confirm the hierarchy, because Engels's letters are full of deference to Marx's theoretical powers, and deference in a strong mind reads, to weaker readers, as confession of inferiority. In fact the deference had a precise object. Engels deferred on the theory of value and the architecture of Capital, where Marx's achievement was genuinely singular. He did not defer, because there was nothing to defer about, on the empirical England he had mapped, the wars he analysed, the anthropology he synthesised or the natural science he had read more deeply than his partner. The letters distinguish the domains with perfect clarity. The legend merges them, because legends prefer one mountain and one foothill to a range.

Engels himself collaborated in the misfiling, and the collaboration is the most attractive thing about him. He spent fifty years directing credit toward Marx with a consistency that no merely ambitious man could have sustained: in prefaces, in letters, in the famous comparison in which he described himself as playing second fiddle, and playing it, he allowed, remarkably well. The metaphor has been quoted against him ever since, usually by readers who miss its structure. A second fiddle is not a spectator. It is an instrument in the same orchestra, playing a different line without which the harmony thins, and its player's estimate of the first violin says nothing about whether the second part was composed by a mediocrity. Engels knew the value of his own line. His will left his estate to Marx's daughters and to the movement. His prefaces to Marx's works defend the master and document the disciple with the same pen. The self effacement was a moral choice made by a man secure in the record, and the appropriate response of scholarship is not to repeat the courtesy but to audit the accounts, which is what the reassessment, at its best, is doing.

There is a final reason the junior picture persists, and it is the least respectable: it is useful. Movements that wished to discard Engels's systematisation while keeping Marx's critique found the disciple template ready made, a way to blame the messenger for the message's career. Scholars who wished to defend the system entire found the same template serviceable in reverse, treating criticism of Engels as criticism of a subordinate whose errors could not touch the founder. Both conveniences require the chronology to stay blurred. The correction is therefore not an antiquarian courtesy to a neglected man. It is the refusal of a convenience, in the name of the only tribunal either founder consistently recognised, the evidence arranged in its order.

## The polyglot and the self taught scholar

Engels's learning had a texture that university men recognised with respect and sometimes with envy, because it had been acquired without syllabus or examination and therefore followed appetite rather than requirement. Languages were its foundation. He read, by the usual counts, more than twenty, and corresponded in at least a dozen, moving between German, English and French as working tongues and reading Russian, Polish, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Dutch and the Scandinavian languages for the movement's business. He studied Persian for pleasure and joked about the difficulty of its script. He read Old Norse and Gothic for the Germanic philology behind his work on the Mark and the Frankish dialects. The polyglot range was not ornament. A movement scattered across a continent conducted its affairs in letters, and the man who could read a Danish party dispute, an Italian strike report and a Russian student's pamphlet without intermediaries held an institutional power that the printed works only partly reveal.

The sciences filled the second shelf. From the Manchester years onward he read chemistry with a manufacturer's practicality and then with a theorist's ambition, followed physiology through the cell theory and the energy debates, worked at mathematics up to and into the calculus whose philosophical treatment in his manuscripts still provokes argument, and tracked Darwin from the first edition onward, grasping both the theory's power and its Malthusian packaging, which he and Marx criticised in their letters with a precision later commentators have had to acknowledge. His friendship with Schorlemmer, a professional chemist of standing who shared his politics, gave the reading a laboratory conscience; manuscripts went to Carl for correction before they went anywhere else. None of this made Engels a scientist. It made him the best scientifically informed layman in the movement, which was precisely the qualification the Anti-Dühring assignment required and precisely the qualification the caricature of the dabbling amateur denies him.

His reading in history and literature ran as deep. He knew his Goethe and his Shakespeare the way his generation's educated Germans did, by heart and by quotation, championed Balzac alongside Marx as the novelist from whom more could be learned about French society than from its historians, followed Ibsen and the Scandinavian drama in his last years, and wrote literary criticism in his youth sharp enough to suggest the career not taken. The breadth matters for the profile's central argument because it destroys the remaining prop of the junior legend, the notion of a narrow technician beside a universal mind. The two minds were differently shaped. Marx's was deeper in the single channel of political economy, bored through rock for thirty years. Engels's was wider across the whole landscape of nineteenth century knowledge, and the partnership's output, encyclopaedic in its reach from cotton prices to early Christianity, required both shapes. A tradition founded by the deep mind alone would have been a treatise. Founded by both, it became a world view with a reading public, which is what in fact it became.

## Ireland, America and the wider world

Engels's interests were never confined to the German and English stages on which he performed. Ireland engaged him for decades, first through Mary Burns and the Irish Manchester of the mills, then through his own study of Irish history and language, undertaken with the intention of writing a history of Ireland that the events of 1870 interrupted and left as fragments. He supported Fenianism's right to its goal while criticising its methods with a soldier's realism, visited Ireland with Eleanor Marx, and understood earlier than most English radicals that the Irish question was not an embarrassment to English politics but a key to it. The unfinished Irish history, with its chapters on the land, the conquest and the communal institutions of the clans, shows the mature Engels working exactly as the young one had: from the evidence of a people's material life toward the structures that governed it.

America reached him through the Tribune years, when explaining European revolutions to New York readers had taught him the republic's politics from outside, and then, overwhelmingly, through the Civil War. He and Marx followed the campaigns in their letters with maps open, assessed generals, quarrelled with the Northern command's slowness, predicted the war's transformation into a revolutionary war against slavery, and advised the International's address to Lincoln. The war correspondence is among the best evidence for the partnership's real division of labour in political judgement: Marx supplied the constitutional and economic reading, Engels the operational one, and each man's letters correct the other's enthusiasms. After the war his American correspondence with Florence Kelley, with Friedrich Sorge and with the German American movement extended his advisory web across the Atlantic, and his prefaces for American editions of the England book and the Manifesto show him, in his seventies, still adjusting the doctrine's presentation to audiences an ocean away from Primrose Hill.

The colonial world entered his writing through the journalism of the 1850s, on India, China, Persia and Algeria, much of it written for the Tribune under the pressures of weekly copy. Those pieces are uneven, marked by the period's European assumptions in ways modern readers register immediately, and they also contain analyses of colonial conquest, of the destruction of communal property and of the revolts conquest provoked, which later anti colonial movements read with profit. The profile records the range without adjudicating the whole of it, since the adjudication belongs to studies of the founders' journalism. What the range establishes, again, is the working pattern of the partnership: when a subject demanded treatment, the treatment very often came from the desk in Manchester or Regent's Park Road, at speed, in publishable prose, while the deeper work in London proceeded at its own geological pace.

## Engels inside the movement he advised

Between the dissolution of the First International and his death, Engels occupied a position in European socialism that had no title and no precedent. He was not a party member in any ordinary sense, held no office, commanded no votes, and was nevertheless consulted by the leaderships of parties in half a dozen countries as the surviving founder whose memory reached back to the 1840s and whose judgement on men and moments carried a weight no congress could confer. The German party, the largest and most organised, treated him as its patriarch and its censor: he intervened against the anti socialist law's temptations to conspiracy, advised on programmes, and in 1891 performed his most startling act of textual authority by publishing, against the wishes of the party executive, Marx's suppressed critique of the Gotha programme, forcing the Erfurt programme's drafters to confront the founder's actual words on the state and the transition. The episode displays the executor's power at its most constructive and most dangerous in a single gesture. The archive he controlled made him the doctrine's living editor, and he used the archive, on that occasion, to discomfort the doctrine's own custodians.

His relations with the movement's second generation mixed generosity with steel. He financed younger comrades, found them publishers, corrected their manuscripts and demolished their errors in letters whose wit barely softened the demolition. Karl Kautsky he educated and, on occasion, despaired of. Eduard Bernstein he treated as a son and argued with as an equal. The Russian Marxists reached him through correspondence that reached back to the 1870s, when he had debated the Russian commune's future with the populists and learned the language to do it in the original; Vera Zasulich's famous question about the commune had been addressed to Marx, and the drafts of the reply show the two men's shared caution, but it was Engels who sustained the Russian correspondence after 1883 and Engels whose letters on Russia's development were quoted in the factional struggles of the next century. The French movement knew him as Lafargue's father in law in all but law and as the author, with Marx, of the programme's preamble that the Guesdists carried into their congresses. No single thread runs through all of this except the thread of the correspondence itself, thousands of letters constituting an unpublished collected works of advice, which historians now mine as the operating manual of international socialism's heroic age.

### How did Engels exercise influence without office?

Through the correspondence, the archive and the authority of survival. Party leaders wrote to Regent's Park Road for judgement on programmes, tactics and disputes, and his letters, grounded in fifty years of memory and in control of Marx's unpublished papers, functioned as rulings. He held no post and sought none; the influence was personal, textual and, on the evidence, considerable.

The picture that emerges from the movement years reinforces the profile's thesis from an unexpected direction. Institutions do not consult secretaries. The parties of Europe, staffed by men of real ability and considerable ego, deferred to Engels's judgement on history, on texts and on strategy because they had tested it and found it the best available. The deference of Bebel, of the young Russians, of the French leadership, is evidence about the quality of the mind being consulted, tendered by witnesses with every incentive to resent the consultation. A legend that makes him a wealthy amateur must explain why the professionals of a continent wrote to the amateur for rulings, and it cannot.

## The last introduction and the argument over his intentions

In 1895, months before his death, Engels wrote an introduction to a new edition of Marx's Class Struggles in France, surveying the prospects of the movement he had served for half a century. The text argued that the conditions of street fighting had been transformed by modern rifles, artillery, broad boulevards and professional armies; that the era of successful barricade insurrection by minorities was closing; that the legal mass party, growing through elections toward an eventual majority, now held the stronger hand; and that the movement should preserve its forces, avoid premature collisions and let its enemies be the ones to break legality first. The argument was the General's last piece of staff work, applied to the movement itself. The German party leadership, nervous of the text's effect and of prosecution, published extracts shaped to suggest a simple preacher of legality at any price, and Engels protested sharply in letters that his meaning had been inverted, insisting that he had argued tactics for a phase, not surrender of the objective.

The episode has been fought over ever since, by reformists who claimed the introduction as their charter, by revolutionaries who blamed the editors, and by scholars who have established the full text and the circumstances of its trimming. For this profile its significance is biographical and methodological at once. Biographically, it shows the elderly Engels still revising doctrine against evidence, counting rifles and railway gauges where a mere codifier would have quoted scripture. Methodologically, it shows what became of his revisions once they entered the machinery of transmission he himself had built: the movement's institutions edited their founder for convenience, within weeks, in his lifetime, over his protest. Readers who blame Engels alone for the rigidity of the doctrine that bears Marx's name should sit with that episode until its lesson settles. The freezing of the tradition was an institutional process, practised on Engels's own text before he was buried. He was its first victim as often as he was its agent.

His intentions, so far as a profile may summarise them, were consistent from the Paris meeting to the last introduction: to equip a real movement with a true account of the society it proposed to change, to keep that account answerable to evidence, and to subordinate his own name to the account's survival. The first intention produced the writings. The second produced the corrections, the prefaces revising his youthful prophecies, the letters qualifying the determinism. The third produced the legend, and it is the argument of this article that the legend has now cost more truth than the modesty was worth.

## How to read Engels without the suffix

A reader persuaded that the second bibliography deserves direct acquaintance faces a practical problem, because the works differ enormously in kind and the order of reading decides what is found. The England book should come first, not because it is the earliest but because it shows the method before the system: evidence, structure, judgement, in prose a modern reader can still race through. The Outlines belong beside it, read as a founding pamphlet whose errors are part of its interest, the record of a mind arriving at the critique of economics by main force. Socialism: Utopian and Scientific remains the shortest road into the doctrine's self understanding, and its generosity toward the utopians models the tradition's best manner toward its predecessors. The family book rewards reading as an argument to be tested rather than a scripture to be absorbed, its central historicising move surviving its dated ethnography. Anti-Dühring should be read last among the majors and read whole, including the natural philosophy, with the codification charge in one hand and Marx's approval in the other, so that the reader's verdict is earned rather than inherited.

The letters are the advanced course, and the 1890s letters on historical materialism, short, quotable and endlessly argued over, are the natural entry point, followed by the correspondence with Marx himself, in which the partnership's real texture, the jokes, the money, the requests for facts, the theoretical cross examination, dissolves every plaster statue the tradition has erected. Readers assembling such a course for examination purposes will find the student layer of this profile addressed to them directly below, and the examination boards, which increasingly set Engels as a topic in his own right, reward exactly the discriminations the chronological correction teaches: which text, which date, which man. The suffix habit fails examinations as reliably as it fails history.

What emerges from the direct reading, finally, is a style of mind the tradition could use more of: impatient with cant, loyal to evidence, funny at the expense of pretension including its own, and willing to be corrected by the facts in public, in prefaces written in old age to books written in youth. The young man who walked Manchester corrected his prophecies when Manchester declined to fulfil them. The old man who edited Capital corrected his determinism when the disciples simplified it. Between those corrections stretches a life's work that no honest table of contents can file under another man's name.

## The correspondence as a second collected works

Any profile of Engels that confined itself to his books would miss the medium in which he may have done his largest quantity of original thinking. The letters, to Marx above all, and after 1883 to the whole European movement, constitute a running commentary on the nineteenth century written at the speed of events. The Marx and Engels correspondence of the 1850s and 1860s is a working seminar on the world economy: crises are tracked from the money markets to the cotton ports, wars are assessed, books are demolished or praised within days of publication, and the theory of Capital can be watched assembling itself under the pressure of a friend's questions. Engels's letters in that exchange are not secretarial. He reports the Manchester trade from inside, corrects Marx's military judgements, supplies the chemistry and the philology, proposes arguments, and registers the partnership's most famous private verdicts, including the assessment of Darwin's book as the natural historical foundation their view of history had been waiting for.

The later correspondence has a different character and a different evidentiary value. After 1883 the letters become instruments of government for a movement without a government, and they show the executor thinking, case by case, about questions the books never reached: the prospects of the Russian commune, the tactics of the French factions, the programme disputes of the Germans, the prospects of the American movement, the reading lists of promising students in four countries. Historians reconstructing any national socialism of the period find his letters in the archive whether they look for them or not, because the principals wrote to London before they acted and quoted the replies when they defended themselves. A thinker whose influence required that volume of consultation was not functioning as anyone's clerk.

The letters also preserve the man at his most attractive, which is a scholarly point and not merely a human one, because the attractiveness did political work. The humour is constant and often directed at himself: the polyglot jokes, the mock despair over the office, the delight in a good vintage or a bad opponent. The generosity is structural rather than occasional, money and time and introductions flowing outward in a stream that the recipients' memoirs unanimously record. And the intellectual candour is bracing even now: he tells correspondents when he does not know, directs them to better authorities, and revises his published positions in private before revising them in print. The tradition that ossified in his name was served, in his lifetime, by a correspondent of unusual suppleness, and the contrast between the letters and the catechism built from the books is the single most efficient refutation of the notion that the ossification began with the man himself.

## The England book among the historians

Because the Manchester study is the foundation stone of the chronological correction, its standing among professional historians deserves a direct audit rather than a reverent paragraph. The audit begins well for Engels. The Hammonds, whose trilogy on the labour movement set the terms of the debate for a generation, used the book as a principal witness for the prosecution in the case over industrialisation's human costs. Asa Briggs, mapping the Victorian city, treated its Manchester chapters as a founding act of urban observation. E. P. Thompson, in the most influential work of British labour history written in the twentieth century, engaged Engels as a predecessor whose framework he inherited even where he corrected its details. The book entered the discipline's bloodstream early and has never left it; syllabuses in urban history, public health history and the history of sociology all find room for extracts, usually beside Chadwick and Mayhew, in an arrangement that quietly concedes the clerk from Barmen a seat at the founders' table of empirical social research.

The criticisms are real and have improved the book's readers if not the book. Economic historians of the revisionist school questioned his selection of the worst districts as representative, recalculated his mortality claims against the parish data, and noted the polemical compression by which a varied reality became an indictment. Social historians have marked his treatment of the Irish, compassionate in structure and prejudiced in some of its language, as the period's failing imperfectly escaped. Defenders reply, in the main successfully, that his figures track the official returns he cited, that his worst districts existed and housed tens of thousands, and that a work of intervention written at twenty four should be graded against the investigative literature of 1845 rather than against the cliometrics of a later century. The argument continues in the journals with undiminished energy, which is the surest sign of the book's vitality: it is treated as a live contribution to a research field, not as a relic of a doctrine.

One further reception deserves record because it closes a circle. When the English edition finally appeared in London in 1892, Engels wrote a preface measuring his youthful text against the transformed city, noting the cleared slums, the embanked river, the improved mortality, and drawing the moral that the worst abuses he had described had been remedied precisely because they had become impossible to defend once described. The investigator thus audited his own investigation across forty seven years and reported, with an empiricist's satisfaction, that exposure had worked. Few founders of research traditions have been granted so long a view of their first fieldwork, and fewer still have used it to revise rather than to celebrate themselves.

## Pietism's long shadow: Engels on religion

The son of the Wupper valley pietists never stopped writing about religion, and the trajectory of that writing maps his intellectual life as faithfully as the economics does. The adolescent crisis is documented in letters of extraordinary anguish, in which the gymnasium student records his passage from his father's faith through Strauss's criticism to unbelief, praying, in one famous passage, for the ability to believe what he could no longer honestly affirm. The passage matters because it establishes that Engels's materialism was bought at a personal price his more comfortably raised contemporaries rarely paid. He did not drift out of religion. He fought his way out, against love and duty, and the fight left him with a permanent respect for belief's power over human beings that purely secular formations seldom achieve.

The mature work on religion is therefore analytical rather than contemptuous. His writings on the German Peasants' War read Münzer's theology as the necessary dress of a revolutionary programme in an age that thought in scripture, and his late essays on early Christianity extended the method to the tradition's founding texts. In the pieces on the Book of Revelation and on Bruno Bauer and early Christianity, written in the 1880s and 1890s, he treated the primitive church as a movement of slaves, freedmen and the dispossessed, traced its transformation as its social composition changed, and drew the parallel with the workers' movement of his own day explicitly: both began among the oppressed, both promised a transformation of the world, both developed orthodoxies and heresies as they grew. The essays scandalised the devout and have been mined by historians of early Christianity ever since, partly for their errors, which are the errors of the criticism of his day, and partly for a sociological instinct that the discipline later made its own.

There is a biographical symmetry here that the legend, once again, cannot accommodate. The supposed vulgariser of a subtle philosophy spent his last decade applying that philosophy, with learning and without malice, to the sacred history of his own childhood home, and published the results for a movement readership that found them among the most gripping things he wrote. The pietist's son had become the tradition's historian of religion almost by inheritance, and the inheritance shows in the work's refusal of the easy satisfactions of mockery.

## Darwin, Schorlemmer and the scientific circle

Engels's engagement with the natural sciences was social as well as solitary, conducted inside a circle of scientifically trained socialists whose existence complicates the picture of a doctrine sealed against the laboratory. Carl Schorlemmer, professor of organic chemistry at Owens College in Manchester and a fellow of the Royal Society, was his closest scientific friend, a man whose professional standing was unimpeachable and whose politics were the partnership's own. The two walked, drank and argued chemistry together for decades, and Schorlemmer's influence runs under the surface of everything Engels wrote about science, supplying both facts and a professional's sense of what a fact is. When Engels prepared the eulogy at Marx's graveside, it was the Darwin comparison he reached for first: as Darwin had discovered the law of development of organic nature, so Marx had discovered the law of development of human history. The sentence has been quoted into banality, but its placement was deliberate, the considered judgement of the partner who had read both bodies of work most closely.

His direct dealings with the scientific establishment were slight and occasionally bruising, and he preferred it so, remarking that the professors would come to the movement's conclusions by their own road or not at all. What he sought from the sciences was never endorsement but material: the cell theory and the conservation of energy, Darwin's mechanism and the new chemistry's demolition of fixed species and elements, each arrived in his notebooks as a further dissolution of the static world picture his philosophy opposed. Whether the dialectical patterns he discerned in that material were discoveries or impositions is the dispute the companion guide audits in full. What the profile records is the quality of the attention. The manuscripts cite the literature of several sciences in several languages, distinguish carefully between established results and fashionable speculation, and pause, repeatedly, to confess the limits of a layman's competence. Amateur the project may have been. Slapdash it was not, and the distinction is one the reassessment has had to teach the literature again.

## What Engels did not write

A profile concerned with accuracy should mark the boundaries of the oeuvre as carefully as its contents, because the tradition's habit of citing Marx and Engels as a single author has filled the gaps in each man's work with the other's. Engels wrote no systematic treatise on value, no analysis of the capitalist production process rivalling the work he edited, and no philosophy of history in treatise form beyond the expositions the polemics required. He wrote nothing on aesthetics to set beside his early literary journalism, though his letters on the novel, above all the famous judgement that he learned more from Balzac than from the professed historians and economists of the period, and his defence of tendenceless realism in fiction, have been quarried by literary critics ever since. His projected history of Ireland remained fragments. His philosophy of nature remained manuscripts. The Dialectics of Nature's unfinished state is not a detail: the executor chose his friend's completion over his own, and the choice, made in 1883 and held for a decade, is the most eloquent single fact about the partnership's internal economy of honour.

The boundaries matter for the argument of this article in a way that may surprise. A mere disciple's oeuvre would be expected to cluster around the master's subjects, echoing and applying. Engels's clusters elsewhere: in the empirical investigation the master never attempted, in the military analysis the master wholly delegated, in the anthropology and the natural science the master left to him, in the religious history neither of them had planned. The map of what he wrote is the map of a complementary intelligence, and the map of what he left unwritten is the record of a loyalty that cost his own bibliography dearly. Both maps belong in any verdict on his standing, and neither is visible from inside the legend.

## Transmission: parties, textbooks and the state doctrine

The objection considered earlier in this profile identified transmission as the codification charge's strongest ground, and the mechanics of that transmission deserve a section of their own, because they show how much of the freezing happened downstream of the author. The first vehicle was the party school and the pamphlet literature of German Social Democracy, the movement's powerhouse, where Anti-Dühring and its extraction served as the standard introduction for organisers who needed a doctrine they could teach on a Sunday. Kautsky's commentaries and Bernstein's problems extended the exposition; Plekhanov carried it east and gave it the name dialectical materialism in the early 1890s; Lenin studied the summaries in exile and defended their philosophy against the movement's empiricist critics with a vehemence that fixed the canon for the Russian tradition. Each relay simplified. Examples hardened into proofs, qualifications fell away as excess baggage, and the texts' polemical occasions, a Berlin lecturer here, a party dispute there, faded from memory until the arguments read as timeless decrees.

The second vehicle was the state. After 1917, and decisively after the codification of the 1930s, the doctrine Engels had expounded became an official philosophy taught to millions as a condition of education and employment, with his works, edited and excerpted, at the centre of its curriculum. The Short Course's chapter on dialectical and historical materialism, the era's most consequential summary, descends from his architecture while departing from his cautions at exactly the points where his letters had warned against departure. A profile bound to neutrality does not adjudicate the states that claimed the doctrine; it records the documentary chain, because the chain is the evidence on which the codification argument stands or falls. On that evidence the chain has many links, the first forged by Engels in works his partner approved, the last forged by institutions that censored his qualifications and trimmed his final introduction while he still lived to protest. Assigning the whole chain to its first link is not history but bookkeeping convenience, and the chronological correction, applied to the transmission itself, dissolves the convenience.

For the scholarly reader the practical consequence is a reading rule: Engels should be cited from his texts and dated editions, not from the summaries of summaries through which the parties transmitted him, and Marx should be read in the manuscripts where the editorial mediation is in question rather than assumed away. The critical editions exist precisely to make that rule followable. A generation of scholarship has begun to follow it, which is why the reassessment, treated in this profile as an ongoing argument rather than a fashion, rests on documents any reader may now inspect.

## The plain style and what it was for

Engels wrote the most readable prose in the tradition he co founded, and the readability was a doctrine before it was a talent. His formation was journalistic: the Bremen feuilletons, the Tribune's deadlines, the party papers' appetite for copy that a working reader could finish on a tram. He distrusted the academic German of his generation, its subordinate clauses nested like coffins, and he cultivated instead a style of short approaches, concrete nouns, examples arriving early and abstractions made to earn their place. The polemic against Dühring is funny, which in a work of philosophy and economics is a strategic choice as much as a gift: a reader laughing at the victim's system has already stopped fearing it. Marx, whose own style could blaze and could also bury, recognised the gift and used it, routing the movement's public explanations through the pen that the public could follow.

The style had costs that the codification debate has catalogued. Lucidity flattens what it carries; a distinction that survives compression into a pamphlet is not always the distinction the original analysis needed. Engels knew the trade and accepted it as the price of a doctrine meant for millions rather than for seminars. He also built safeguards into the plain style that his excerptors later stripped away: the hedged example introduced as an illustration rather than a proof, the qualification entered in the same paragraph as the claim, the joke that signals a simplification in progress. Reading him whole, in context, the safeguards are visible on every page. Reading him in the catechisms, they are gone, and the difference between those two readings is a fair measure of how much of the codification happened to his prose rather than in it.

The plain style also had a democratic theory inside it, which Engels stated whenever the movement's writing was discussed. A doctrine addressed to working readers owed those readers sentences they could test as they went, examples drawn from the world they worked in, and arguments whose joints showed. Obscurity, in his judgement, was rarely a sign of depth; it was more often a tariff levied by the educated on the attention of the busy, and he had watched the German philosophical manner levy it all his life. His own pages pay the opposite tax willingly. They surrender the prestige of difficulty in exchange for the discipline of being understood by a hostile reader at a first reading, and any writer who has attempted the exchange knows it to be the more expensive of the two. The movement's enemies complained for decades that Engels made dangerous ideas sound like common sense. The complaint, examined, is the style's certificate.

His letters on literature show the same stylistic conscience turned outward. Advising younger writers, he argued for realism that lets its politics emerge from the situation rather than from the author's trumpet, praised Balzac's honest reporting against Balzac's own royalist sympathies as the triumph of the method, and warned a novelist of his acquaintance against turning characters into mouthpieces. The advice is Engels's exposition problem stated for fiction: how to carry a world view in a form that respects the evidence. That he solved it better in counsel than the movement's later cultural officials solved it in practice is one more instance of the pattern this profile keeps encountering, the founder subtler than the foundation myth.

## Paris, the Owenites and the education of a communist

The years between the Bremen apprenticeship and the Paris meeting repay a closer reconstruction, because they contain the making of Engels the communist before Marx enters the story as more than an editor whose office he had visited. In Manchester he sought out the Owenite movement, attended its Halls of Science, wrote for its New Moral World, and absorbed the practical English socialism of co operation and rational community building that German communism, derived from French theory and artisan conspiracy, entirely lacked. He met the Chartists on their own ground, wrote for the Northern Star, and learned the discipline of a mass movement with newspapers, delegates and a programme. He studied the economists in the Manchester libraries and the trade in the Manchester exchange. By the time he boarded the boat for the continent in 1844 he carried a synthesis no one else in the German emigration possessed: French communism's goal, German philosophy's method and English experience's evidence, assembled by a twenty three year old who had tested each component against the others.

The Cologne encounter of 1842, often dramatised as a snub, deserves its documentary deflation. Engels passed through the Rheinische Zeitung's office while Marx, beset by censors and contributors, received the young Berlin partisan of a rival Young Hegelian faction with the coolness factional etiquette prescribed. Neither man yet had anything the other needed. Two years later the positions had reversed in the most instructive way: the editor had been driven from journalism into theory and was starving in Paris over the manuscripts of a philosophy he had not yet grounded, while the traveller arrived with the grounding in his luggage, the Outlines printed and the England notes complete. The ten days at the Café de la Régence and in Marx's rooms were the recognition of a complementarity that the preceding years had manufactured independently in two cities. Partnerships of this quality are not made by meetings. They are revealed by them, and the revelation required both men to have become, separately, what the other lacked.

## The International years and the Hague

Engels's move to London in 1870 placed him, for the first time since 1849, at the operational centre of the movement, and the General Council of the International Working Men's Association gained its most industrious corresponding secretary. He took charge of correspondence with Spain, Italy, Portugal, Denmark and Belgium at various points, drafted resolutions, reported on strikes and quarrels from Lisbon to Milan, and supplied the council with the continental intelligence its London artisan members could not gather for themselves. The work was unglamorous and indispensable, the administrative substrate of an international organisation in the age of the telegraph and the penny post, and it fell to Engels because the partnership's division of labour assigned him the world's correspondence as it assigned Marx the world's theory.

The struggle with Bakunin and the anarchist Alliance consumed those years and displayed his polemical method at its most systematic: dossiers compiled, circulars drafted, the record of the rival organisation assembled document by document until the Hague congress of 1872 expelled the Alliance's leaders on evidence rather than on denunciation. Whatever judgement later history passes on the split, the procedure was Engels's, archival and prosecutorial, the counting house habits of verification applied to factional war. The transfer of the General Council to New York, which he and Marx engineered to keep the International from falling into hostile hands, closed the organisation's European career and freed both men for the decade of writing that followed, in which his share, Anti-Dühring, the housing polemic's afterlife and the nature manuscripts, was the larger. The legend remembers the 1870s as Marx's decade of Capital's foreign editions and the International's glory. The record shows the partnership's public face, its controversies and its correspondence increasingly carried by the partner whose name the legend files second.

## The editor of the master's shorter works

Between the great labour of the Capital volumes, Engels performed a quieter editorial service that shaped the canon as surely as the big construction did. He prepared the new editions through which the reading public actually encountered Marx: the reissue of The Poverty of Philosophy with its notes on Rodbertus, the editions of Wage Labour and Capital, to whose 1891 printing he attached an introduction distinguishing Marx's terminology of labour and labour power with a care that generations of students have silently absorbed, the reissue of the Eighteenth Brumaire and of Revolution and Counter Revolution, and the publication from the archive of the Critique of the Gotha Programme in 1891, timed, against the party executive's wishes, to correct the Erfurt draft. Each edition carried his preface, and the prefaces together form a running history of the movement written by its surviving founder, anecdotal, precise and deliberately pedagogical.

The Gotha episode in particular belongs in any honest account of the executor's power and its limits. The party leadership begged him to delay or soften a document whose criticisms of Lassallean phrases embarrassed their negotiations. He published it entire, in the party's theoretical journal, with a letter explaining that the founders' words were the movement's property and not the executive's. The leadership swallowed the rebuke because his standing made swallowing it cheaper than resisting. No subordinate in any movement has ever held such power over his own organisation, and the fact that he used it to publish a text rather than to promote a faction tells the reader where his loyalties were mortgaged. The archive was a trust, and he administered it, on the evidence, with a trustee's indifference to embarrassment, including his own side's.

His own books he treated with the same editorial conscience in reverse. The prefaces to successive editions of the England book, of the Manifesto, of the Peasant War, correct his youthful errors, register the changed facts, and warn new readers against quoting the young enthusiast against the old analyst. The 1892 English preface's audit of Manchester's improvements has already been noticed in this profile. The 1885 preface to the second German edition of Anti-Dühring enters reservations about the state of his scientific knowledge and the provisional character of its examples, reservations the catechism tradition duly ignored. An author who annotates his own fallibility across fifty years is an awkward patron for a doctrine of infallibility, and the awkwardness is documented in his own hand, in print, for anyone who reads the prefaces the excerpts omit.

## The estate, the ashes and the verdict of the will

The material settlement of Engels's life closes the argument about his intentions as neatly as any text. He died possessed of a considerable fortune, the accumulated capital of the Manchester partnership prudently invested across his London decades. His will directed the bulk of it to Marx's surviving daughters and their families and to the movement's purposes, with provision for the household that had served him and for the comrades who had not. The revolutionary who had been accused of enjoying the bourgeois life he condemned thus ended by returning its proceeds, for the second time, to the cause: once across fifty years of subsidy, and once at the end, in a single instrument. The fortune's destination is a fact of probate, not of propaganda, and it has embarrassed the hypocrisy charge ever since in the quiet way that documents embarrass slogans.

His instructions for his own remains completed the self effacement he had practised in life. No grave, no monument, no relic for pilgrimage: cremation, and the ashes scattered on the sea off Beachy Head, a spot he had loved on holiday walks. The movement complied, and the consequence, visible on any map of socialist pilgrimage, is the asymmetry this profile has noted before. Highgate receives its visitors; Engels is everywhere in the tradition's texts and nowhere in its geography, dispersed, in accordance with his materialism, into the elements he had studied. Biographers have sometimes read the instruction as modesty. It reads at least as well as doctrine: a man who believed that individuals are the products and the instruments of their time had no use for a shrine asserting the opposite, and he arranged his disappearance with the same clarity with which he had arranged everything else.

Those who knew him best left the verdicts that the reassessment has been relearning ever since. Bebel, the German party's leader, called him the most universal scholar of the movement. Eleanor Marx, who grew up calling him her second father, treated his judgement as the family's court of last resort in scholarship and in life. The obituaries in the European press, including papers that had fought him for decades, recorded the range with a respect that surprised nobody who had corresponded with him. It took the twentieth century's quarrels, fought over his summaries by men who needed a disciple to blame or to hide behind, to shrink him to the dimensions of the suffix. The documents were never shrunk. They waited in the editions and the archives for the correction this article has attempted to state, in the only form he himself would have trusted: dates, texts and the table.

A last word belongs to the proportion of things, because the chronological correction is easily misheard as a claim to equal billing in every respect, and it claims nothing of the kind. Marx's achievement in the critique of political economy stands where it stood, singular and unapproached by anyone in the partnership, Engels first of all in saying so. What the correction restores is the remainder of the map: the founding of the economics before the economist, the mapping of the industrial city before the theory that explained it, the military, anthropological and scientific territories held in one man's name, the editing that gave the master's work its completed form, and the advice that steered a continental movement through its formative decades. A tradition is not diminished by having two founders accurately described. It is diminished by the laziness that files one of them as staff, and the file, on the evidence assembled here, was always a misfiling.

## Crisis letters, the Grundrisse years and the economist in reserve

When the commercial crisis of 1857 broke, the partnership's correspondence catches fire, and the distribution of expertise in that exchange is the cleanest available X ray of the two men's economic standing. Marx, closeted with the notebooks that became the Grundrisse, demanded from Manchester a running account of the crisis as it moved through the cotton market, the banks and the exchanges, and Engels supplied it letter by letter: the state of the mills, the discount market's seizures, the American failures as they arrived by steamer, the prospects of the Indian and China trades. The reports are a professional's, precise about instruments and dates, and Marx built with them at his elbow. The arrangement rebukes two errors at once. Against the legend, the supposed amateur was the partnership's resident expert on the actual economy through which its theory had to pass. Against the opposite inflation, Engels never mistook his expertise for the theory itself; the same letters press Marx for the manuscript, celebrate its arrival, and treat the synthesis as the friend's unique work. Reserve economist and first reader in one person, he stands in the 1857 letters exactly where the chronological correction places him in 1844: ahead on the evidence, behind on nothing except the single commanding theory, and content that it should be so.

His own economic writing of the middle years is scattered through the journalism and the reviews, and its quality is consistently underrated because it lacks a title page. The survey of the English Ten Hours Bill, written for the partners' review in 1850, handled factory legislation with the Manchester book's authority updated by a clerk's daily knowledge of the mills' evasions. His contributions to the partners' journalistic output on trade, on the Indian revolt's economics, on the American war's blockade and cotton famine, carried the same signature: the world market treated as a single mechanism whose motions could be followed from a Lancashire warehouse to a Mississippi plantation and back. Readers who want Engels the economist should not wait for a treatise that never came. They should read the correspondence and the reviews, where the economics lives in working form, and where the line between his knowledge and Marx's theory is drawn by the participants themselves with unusual honesty on both sides.

## Russia, the commune and the long eastern correspondence

Engels's engagement with Russia began as a linguist's exercise and became one of his largest advisory commitments. He learned Russian in the 1870s, corresponded with Pyotr Lavrov and with the populist publicist Nikolai Danielson, who was also Marx's translator and the partnership's indispensable informant on Russian economic data, and in 1875 published his polemic On Social Relations in Russia against the populist Tikhomirov, arguing that the Russian commune could serve as a starting point for a higher development only if revolution in the west arrived to rescue it, and that capitalism was in the meantime dissolving it from within. The argument's structure, a conditional rather than a prophecy, is the mature Engels at his most characteristic, and the 1894 afterword to that polemic, written months before his death, restated the conditionality against a new generation of disputants with the evidence of two further decades entered in the margin.

The Russian correspondence matters to the profile because it shows the executor's authority operating at its fullest reach and because it corrects a lazy assumption about his rigidity. The man accused of freezing the doctrine into stages through which every nation must pass spent twenty years arguing, in Russian, with Russians, that Russia's road was an open question turning on the commune's survival and the west's example, and he changed his emphases as the zemstvo statistics and Danielson's reports accumulated. The Zasulich drafts, in which Marx had weighed the commune's chances in 1881, were known to him intimately, and his own letters continue their caution rather than the catechism's certainty. Scholars of the Russian debates reconstruct his positions from archives in three languages; the catechism version of Engels, dispensing iron laws to a backward empire, is not among the positions the archives contain.

## The British movement and the second homecoming

Engels's last political education was administered by the country he had first studied as a young man. Returning to London in 1870 a figure of the continental movement, he watched British labour through its long liberal decades with growing impatience, wrote for the Labour Standard in 1881 a short series on trade unions and wages that stated the partnership's view for English readers in English terms, and despaired in his letters of a working class bought off, as he put it, by its share in Britain's world monopoly. The revival of the late 1880s, the new unionism of the unskilled, the dock strike of 1889, the legal eight hours agitation, delighted the old investigator beyond measure. He followed the dockers' organisation with a staff officer's attention to its logistics, celebrated the May Day demonstration of 1890 in Hyde Park as the reawakening of the class he had mapped in 1844, and wrote for the continental press the reports of an England stirring again that his younger self would have envied.

His relations with the British organisations of the day were those of an elder whose advice was sought more often than it was taken. He distrusted Hyndman's Social Democratic Federation for its sectarian temper and its leader's vanity, kept his distance from its adventures, and placed his hopes in the slow conversion of the unions and the new independent labour politics forming in the north. Eleanor Marx, his closest collaborator in these years, worked the same ground as organiser and journalist, and the correspondence between them shows the old General tutoring the movement's most gifted English agitator in the history she had inherited. The British chapters of the life close the circle the profile opened in Manchester. The country that had made him a communist by showing him its factories received him back as the doctrine's elder statesman, and his final judgement on its workers, delivered in the prefaces and letters of the 1890s, was that the class of 1844 had begun, at last, to move again on the lines his youthful book had prophesied and his maturity had learned to doubt and then, cautiously, to hope.

## Lassalle, Eisenach and the German party's long apprenticeship

Engels's relations with the German movement that would become his largest responsibility began in wary correspondence with Ferdinand Lassalle, the brilliant organiser whose agitation built the first mass workers' organisation on German soil. The partners distrusted Lassalle's state aided schemes and his flirtations with Bismarck, said so in letters of memorable asperity, and yet recognised the organisational fact he had created, a recognition Engels sustained after Lassalle's death in a duel in 1864 by advising the rival Eisenach current of Bebel and Liebknecht, whose paper he supplied and whose leaders became his closest German comrades. The dual inheritance, Lassallean organisation and the Eisenachers' programme, merged at Gotha in 1875 over the founders' private objections, which Marx recorded in the critique Engels later published, and the Anti Socialist Law of 1878 then drove the united party into the semi illegality in which Engels's counsel, delivered by letter and through the Zurich and London exile press, acquired its patriarchal weight.

Those twelve years of illegality were his generalship applied to party politics. He advised against the provocations that would justify repression and against the despair that counsels conspiracy, kept the exile paper supplied and disciplined, quarrelled with the anarchist and the ultra left temptations in turn, and emerged in 1890, when the law lapsed and the party's vote mounted toward the millions, as the one figure every faction acknowledged. His critique of the Erfurt programme's draft in 1891, circulated in manuscript among the leadership, pressed for republican demands and against opportunist silences with a frankness the published programme only partly absorbed. The German party that dominated European socialism at his death was, in its theory, its programme disputes and its sense of its own history, substantially his pupil, a fact its leaders stated in their obituaries without embarrassment. The pupil's later custodians would freeze the lessons. The teaching itself, recovered from the correspondence and the critiques, is supple, empirical and impatient with phrase making, which is to say it resembles its teacher's prose.

## The Manifesto prefaces and the history he wrote for it

Between 1883 and 1893 Engels wrote, alone or as the surviving signatory, the prefaces that framed the Communist Manifesto for its successive national editions: English in 1888, German in 1883 and 1890, Polish, Italian and others in train. The prefaces are minor masterpieces of the genre he had made his own, each one a compressed history of the International and of the movement in the edition's country, each one adjusting the presentation of a text from 1848 to readers for whom its world had vanished. The English preface of 1888 contains his most quoted act of attribution, the statement that the Manifesto's fundamental proposition, the economic interpretation of history in its first programmatic form, belongs solely and exclusively to Marx. Generations have cited the sentence as the disciple's abdication. Read in place, it is something else: the executor performing the attribution discipline he demanded from everyone, assigning the theory's core to its author while the same preface records, without fuss, the document's joint production and his own draft beneath it. Precision about credit was his lifelong habit, and the sentence is the habit's monument, not its contradiction.

The prefaces also show him writing the movement's history as its archive opened, correcting dates, naming the dead, settling the record of the League and the International while witnesses still lived to check him. Historians of the Manifesto's reception treat the sequence as a primary source of the first rank, which it is, and as something rarer, a founder's running commentary on his own founding document across the half century in which it conquered the world. No comparable commentary exists for any other revolutionary text of the century, because no other revolutionary text had a surviving founder of Engels's longevity, memory and literary conscience to supply it. The suffix figure of the legend could not have written those prefaces. Their author had to know where the bodies were buried because he had helped, in several cases, to bury them, and the knowledge is on every page.

## The catechism he asked to discard

The Principles of Communism, Engels's draft of the League's programme in the autumn of 1847, survives as twenty five questions and answers, and it rewards reading as more than a quarry for the famous text that replaced it. In catechism form, because the League's traditions required it, Engels set out the doctrine entire: the proletariat's origin in the industrial revolution, the distinction between the proletarian and every earlier labouring class, the course of crises, the abolition of private property and its consequences for the family, for education, for the nations. The answers are lucid, unadorned and remarkably complete, a whole world view carried in a pamphlet a worker could finish in an evening. Then, in a letter to Marx in November 1847, the draft's author proposed its own supersession: the catechism form should be dropped, he argued, because the document had to contain history as well as doctrine, and history cannot be catechised. The finished Manifesto, with its narrative sweep from the bourgeoisie's revolutionary rise to its gravediggers, is the consequence of that proposal, Marx's prose executing a design change Engels had requested and supplied the material for.

The episode concentrates the profile's whole argument into a single transaction. The junior partner of legend is discovered, on inspection of the letters, drafting the movement's founding programme entire, diagnosing its form's inadequacy, commissioning its transformation and then spending fifty years assigning the glory of the result to the colleague who performed it. Literary history has few parallels for the combination of origination and renunciation, and none in which the renunciation succeeded so completely that the originator's draft had to be recovered by scholars from the archive his own modesty stocked.

## The Commune's interpreter and his late introduction

When the Paris Commune rose and fell in the spring of 1871, the partnership's public voice was Marx's address on the Civil War in France, and Engels's share of that episode was the characteristic one: military correspondence on the Commune's defence, whose failings he analysed with a professional's grief, and logistical counsel to the International's council. Twenty years later he performed the episode's second act. Preparing the German edition of Marx's address in 1891, he wrote an introduction that drew the Commune's lessons for a new generation, aimed its famous warning at the state worshippers inside his own movement, and delivered, in passing, the definition of the dictatorship of the proletariat as the Commune's form that a century of argument has quoted. The introduction is late Engels in full command: historically exact, tactically pointed, and willing to use the founder's text as the occasion for the survivor's correction of the founder's church.

The pattern completes the record of the executor years. In the Gotha publication, in the edition prefaces, in the Commune introduction, the same hand is at work, treating Marx's texts neither as relics nor as property but as instruments to be reissued, sharpened and aimed at the errors of the moment, including the errors of the movement that owned them. A disciple guards the master's words. Engels deployed them, and the deployment, with its risks accepted and its protests from the orthodox duly received, is the final evidence this profile offers for the standing it has argued throughout. The second founder was still founding, in his seventy first year, with another man's book in his hands and his own name, as ever, in the smaller type.

## Sunday at Regent's Park Road

The human record of the London decades survives in the memoirs of visitors, and its consistency across hostile and friendly witnesses alike gives it evidentiary weight. The Sunday gatherings at 122 Regent's Park Road were the movement's informal court: an enormous midday table, food and wine of a quality the host, a connoisseur formed in the Rhineland and hardened in Manchester clubland, supervised personally, and conversation that ranged across every language the company commanded until the small hours. Liebknecht's memoirs, Bernstein's recollections, the letters of visitors from a dozen countries, all return to the same impressions: the host's memory for the movement's personnel across fifty years, his demand for facts before opinions, his laughter, and the absence, remarked by everyone, of the pomposity that power usually secretes. Younger comrades arrived trembling before the surviving founder and left as though from an elder brother's table, which was, the record suggests, precisely the effect intended.

The household's private architecture deserves its sentence in a complete profile. After Lizzie Burns's death in 1878, weeks after the marriage that had regularised their union at her wish, Engels kept the house with the help of Mary Ellen Rosher, Pumps, whose presence in the correspondence and the memoirs steadies the domestic background of the executor years, and the Marx daughters were its constant family. He stood to Eleanor, Jenny and Laura as the second father his letters show him being, advising, financing, teasing and, at the end, remembering them in his will as his heirs. The affections were not a distraction from the work. They were the medium in which the work's last decade was done, the table at which the movement's business and the family's business were transacted in the same conversation, and they explain, better than any doctrine, why the correspondence reads as it does: the patriarch of European socialism governed, insofar as he governed at all, as the head of a household.

## For students and exam candidates

Engels is an evaluation resource, and treating him as one converts a footnote into marks. The discriminations examiners reward on this material are chronological: the 1844 Outlines before Marx's economic turn, the 1845 England book before the partnership's joint works, the editorial construction of Capital's later volumes after 1883, the qualifying letters as a correction issued from inside the tradition. A candidate who can deploy the chronological correction can evaluate the codification charge rather than merely recite it, conceding the transmission point, which is documented, while testing the intention point against Marx's approval of Anti-Dühring and the evidence of the letters. Read the Outlines and Socialism: Utopian and Scientific first, keep the independent works and their dates in a revision table, and practise the two sided answer: his expository role in transmitting the doctrine, stated plainly, against his independent contributions, stated with dates. Answers that mention Mary Burns, the Manchester residence and the General's military writing signal the wider reading markers are trained to reward.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

Teach the partnership as a division of labour between two originators and let the documents make the argument. A strong seminar sequence opens with the Outlines beside Marx's 1859 preface, so students watch the senior partner assign priority himself, then moves to paired extracts from the England book and the factory inspectors' reports, where the method of assembly can be seen at work. The codification debate teaches best as a structured controversy: the Lukács footnote and its successors against the qualifying letters and the record of Marx's approval, with the class required to distinguish the transmission claim from the intention claim before voting on either. The factory owner question sustains a full session on its own, provided the facts are tabled first: the partnership dates, the remittances, the exit in 1869, and Engels's own defence, so that discussion addresses evidence rather than atmosphere. Close with the 1895 introduction and its trimming by the party press, which lets students see transmission happening to the transmitter, and usually ends the seminar arguing about institutions rather than statues.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The live research frontiers on Engels are documentary, and they are unusually well supplied. The critical editions have made the Capital editing measurable, and the remaining work is granular: passage level comparison, the correspondence's full publication, the reception history of the summaries in the parties and states that canonised them. Historians of social investigation have a priority question to settle in the England book's favour or against it, and the Manchester archives, the firm's records and the Burns family's traces still reward attention. The reassessment literature, Hunt's biography of 2009 and Foster's ecological reading of 2020 at its head, has reopened the nature manuscripts as a serious engagement with nineteenth century science, and the next work there is audit: example by example, science by science, of what the manuscripts got right. Journalists covering the tradition's afterlives will find the qualifying letters the most useful documents Engels left, since every contemporary argument about determinism, structure and agency replays, usually unknowingly, the corrections an old man posted from Primrose Hill to disciples who were already simplifying him.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was Friedrich Engels?

Friedrich Engels was a German philosopher, social investigator and revolutionary who co founded the Marxist tradition with Karl Marx. Born in 1820 in Barmen into a textile manufacturing family, he worked in the family firm's Manchester office, where he researched and wrote his study of the English working class, published in 1845. He reached the critique of political economy before Marx did, drafting an outline of it in 1844 that Marx praised in print. Across fifty years he produced independent works on economics, housing, the family, military history and philosophy, financed Marx's research from his business income, and after Marx's death in 1883 edited the second and third volumes of Capital and served as the movement's chief adviser and correspondent.

### Q: What did Engels contribute that was his own?

His own contributions begin with the Outlines of a Critique of Political Economy in 1844, which opened the economic front of the doctrine before Marx had engaged it, and The Condition of the Working Class in England in 1845, a pioneering work of empirical social investigation based on his Manchester residence. He added the study of the German peasant war, the polemic on the housing question, Anti-Dühring and its extraction Socialism: Utopian and Scientific, the book on the origin of the family, private property and the state, an unfinished philosophy of nature, respected military analysis across three decades, and the letters of the 1890s qualifying economic determinism. The editorial construction of Capital volumes two and three from Marx's manuscripts was his work too, though of a different kind, as this profile's table distinguishes.

### Q: Did Engels write about economics before Marx?

Yes, and the dates are the whole argument. Engels's Outlines of a Critique of Political Economy appeared in the Deutsch-Französische Jahrbücher in 1844, attacking the categories of Smith, Ricardo and their schools from the standpoint of private property's abolition. At that moment Marx had written journalism and a critique of Hegel's philosophy of right, but no political economy; his serious engagement with the economists began in Paris that same year, through questions the Outlines had framed. Marx acknowledged the priority himself in the preface to his 1859 Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy, describing Engels's essay as a brilliant sketch of the critique of economic categories. The acknowledgement, from the one man with every reason to omit it, is the strongest evidence the chronology possesses.

### Q: What is The Condition of the Working Class in England?

It is Engels's book length study of industrial England, written in German after nearly two years of residence in Manchester and published at Leipzig in 1845. Drawing on official reports, the press and his own walking of the factory districts, guided by Mary Burns, it documented housing, health, wages, diet and mortality among the operatives, examined the chief trades in turn, and assessed the unions and Chartism as the class's political answer to its conditions. Its governing argument held that the misery it recorded was a structural product of competition and the factory system rather than a failure of the workers' character. English editions arrived only in 1887 and 1892, yet historians still treat it as a pioneering work of empirical social investigation and an indispensable primary source.

### Q: Was Engels a factory owner?

He was, in the precise sense the record supports. Engels worked in the Manchester office of Ermen and Engels from 1850, rose through the firm on commercial merit, and became a partner in 1864, sharing the ownership of a cotton spinning business whose profits his writings condemned in principle. In 1869 he sold his share and retired to London on the capital it realised. During the partnership years he lived the double life the record shows: respectable merchant and fox hunting clubman by day, revolutionary correspondent and funder of Marx's household throughout. He never disguised the position, and his defence was that the theory indicted a system rather than souls, and that his own conduct within it, twenty years of hated work converted into the material basis of the system's critique, was the relevant fact.

### Q: Why is Engels called the General?

The nickname was the Marx family circle's tribute to his military expertise. Engels served a year with the artillery in Berlin, fought in the Baden campaign of 1849 as an aide de camp, and thereafter studied war as a second profession, writing analyses of the Crimean War, the American Civil War and the wars of German unification. His anonymous articles on the Franco Prussian war for the Pall Mall Gazette in 1870 predicted the campaign's development, including the disaster awaiting France at Sedan, so accurately that London speculated about the retired officer behind them. Marx consulted him on every military question of their lifetimes and deferred to the judgement entirely, which is why the family title, half affectionate and half professional, stuck for life.

### Q: What did Engels write after Marx died?

After March 1883 his writing divided between the executor's labours and works of his own. He edited Capital volume two for publication in 1885 and volume three in 1894, wrote the prefaces explaining his editorial method, and published new editions and introductions to Marx's earlier works. His own major production of the period was The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State in 1884, followed by Ludwig Feuerbach and the End of Classical German Philosophy in 1886, which printed Marx's theses on Feuerbach for the first time. He revised his earlier books with corrective prefaces, sustained an enormous advisory correspondence with the European parties, and in the letters of the 1890s issued the tradition's own qualifications of economic determinism.

### Q: Was Engels a lesser thinker than Marx?

On the theory of value and the architecture of Capital, Engels himself awarded Marx the palm, and no serious reader reverses that verdict. Greatness, however, is not a single ladder. Engels preceded Marx in political economy, surpassed him in empirical investigation, military analysis, languages and the exposition that carried their shared doctrine to its public, and worked territories, from anthropology to natural science, that Marx never entered. The comparison also distorts both men, since the partnership was designed as a division of labour between different gifts rather than a contest between similar ones. The reassessment in scholarship treats the lesser thinker question as badly posed: the record shows a co founder with a distinct oeuvre, not a disciple with a smaller share of the same one.

### Q: What did Engels say about the family?

In The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State, published in 1884 from Marx's notebooks on Lewis Henry Morgan, Engels argued that the family is a historical institution whose forms change with the organisation of production and property. The monogamous family, in his account, arose with private property and the need to transmit inheritance to known heirs, and it embodied the subordination of women as a structural feature rather than a natural arrangement, the first class opposition in history appearing, in his phrase making, as that between man and woman within marriage. Earlier communal and mother right forms preceded it, and its transformation awaited the socialisation of domestic labour and childcare. Feminist scholars have argued with the framework ever since, while using its founding move, the historicising of the family, as common ground.

### Q: Is Engels being reassessed by scholars?

Yes, as an ongoing argument with named monuments rather than a settled consensus. Tristram Hunt's full biography of 2009 restored the independent figure to general readers, presenting the partnership as a collaboration of peers. John Bellamy Foster's The Return of Nature in 2020 read the scientific manuscripts as a serious ecological engagement with nineteenth century science, extending earlier work on metabolism that had revalued Marx's notebooks. Terrell Carver's textual studies had long argued that the two founders held distinguishable positions within a shared project, and the critical editions' documentation of the Capital editing has replaced legend with measurement there too. The junior partner picture persists in older sources and popular summary, but scholarship's centre of gravity has moved toward the co founder the chronology always showed.

### Q: How does the chronological correction change who founded the tradition?

It converts the founding from a solo act with assistance into a documented collaboration between originators. If the critique of political economy was opened by Engels in 1844, if the empirical portrait of the industrial class was drawn by Engels before the partnership existed, and if the doctrine's transmission, editing and self correction after 1883 were Engels's work, then the tradition has two founders with distinguishable oeuvres, and questions of authorship, responsibility and interpretation must be argued text by text rather than settled by the suffix habit. The correction does not diminish Marx's singular achievement in Capital. It removes the assumption, built into the phrase Marxism itself, that everything else in the tradition is commentary on one man's work, and replaces it with the archive's own picture: two men, two conquests, one project.

### Q: What should a student read first among Engels's independent works?

Begin with Socialism: Utopian and Scientific, the short extraction from Anti-Dühring, because it states the doctrine's self understanding in eighty pages of his clearest prose and treats the utopian predecessors with a generosity that teaches the tradition's manners. Move next to The Condition of the Working Class in England, where the method appears before the system and the Manchester evidence can be checked against the reports he used. The Outlines of 1844 repay reading third, as the founding gesture in economics, most valuable when read beside Marx's acknowledgement of it in 1859. The family book and the 1890s letters complete a first course: the independent synthesis and the self correction, the two poles between which the debate about his standing continues to move.

### Q: How did Engels's business life fund the partnership with Marx?

Systematically, across three decades. During the 1850s and 1860s his Manchester salary and then his partnership profits carried the Marx household through its worst years: rent, debts, medical bills, the children's schooling and the emergencies that journalism could not meet arrived in his letters as requests and left Manchester as remittances, five pound notes in envelopes and larger settlements when the crises deepened. After he sold his share in 1869 and retired to London, the arrangement was regularised into an annual allowance that freed Marx from journalism for the last decade of work on Capital. The funding was never formalised by contract and never, on the evidence of the correspondence, resented by the giver or forgotten by the receiver, who understood that his research programme had a Manchester material base and said so in his letters.

### Q: What did Engels do as Marx's literary executor?

He inherited a chaos of manuscripts and converted it into the canonical completion of Marx's work. Engels deciphered the nearly illegible drafts, selected among variants, ordered the material of Capital volume two, published in 1885, and performed the far larger construction of volume three, published in 1894, imposing chapter divisions and writing transitional passages where the notebooks broke off, while explaining his interventions in prefaces whose candour the later manuscript scholarship has verified. He also prepared new editions of Marx's earlier books with historical introductions, published the theses on Feuerbach and the Gotha critique from the archive at decisive moments, and managed the Nachlass as a trust for the movement rather than as property. The critical editions now let scholars measure his editing exactly, and the measurement has generally raised his reputation for fidelity.

### Q: Where does Engels's writing on nature belong in his own development?

It belongs at the centre of his middle years, not at the margin of his old age. The scientific studies began in Manchester in the 1850s, deepened through his friendship with the chemist Carl Schorlemmer, and occupied the decade from 1873 until Marx's death recalled him to the executor's desk, leaving the Dialectics of Nature manuscripts unfinished. The project extended the materialism the partners had built for history into the domain the nineteenth century's sciences were revolutionising, and its examples fed directly into Anti-Dühring, which is why the codification debate fastens on it. Within his development it is continuous with the England book's attention to the material world, the poisoned river and the metabolic costs of production, and the ecological reassessment reads it as the mature form of that early attention rather than as an aberration.
