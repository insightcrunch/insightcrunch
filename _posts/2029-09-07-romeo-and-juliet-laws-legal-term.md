## Romeo and Juliet Laws: When a Play's Title Enters the Criminal Law

Romeo and Juliet laws are real provisions in American criminal codes, and the phrase that names them appears in none of the statutory texts themselves. The term is a nickname, carried by court opinions, legislative debate, news coverage, and legal commentary, for a family of rules that treat two young people close in age differently from an adult who targets a child. A Texas defendant raises the idea as an affirmative defence. A Florida petitioner raises it as a plea to be removed from a public registry. An Indiana statute builds it into a defence that turns on whether the pair were dating. A Kansas court struck words out of its version on constitutional grounds, and in doing so called the provision, in a published opinion, the Romeo and Juliet statute. The lovers lend their names to all of it, and the lending is the subject. What work does a tragedy's title do inside a criminal code, and does the borrowing survive an honest reading of the play it borrows from?

![A courtroom bench beside an open edition of the play - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-51.webp)

The verdict of the pages that follow has two halves, and neither half cancels the other. The provisions themselves are defensible, even necessary, repairs to a body of law that for decades made little distinction between a predatory adult and a pair of schoolmates. The nickname is a different matter. It is the most effective piece of literary advocacy in modern American statute commentary, and it works by editing the play: it keeps the love, the youth, the hostile world, and it cuts the coercion, the haste, the Friar's warning, and the tomb. Naming a protective statute after a thirteen-year-old who dies by her own hand, in a marriage arranged in secret against her father's command, is an irony no amount of legislative good intention dissolves. The name should be kept, because no plainer label has ever moved a legislature as reliably. It should also be understood, because a name that argues is a name that can mislead, and this one argues constantly.

The structure of the inquiry matters. A close-in-age exemption is not one mechanism but at least four, and the differences among the mechanisms decide real cases: whether a young person is never charged, is charged and acquitted, is convicted and sentenced lightly, or is convicted and left to petition for relief from a registry years afterwards. The statutes examined in detail here are the Texas affirmative defence in Section 22.011 of the Texas Penal Code, Florida's registration-relief section 943.04354, Indiana's defence at Indiana Code section 35-42-4-9(e), the Kansas unlawful voluntary sexual relations statute at the centre of State v. Limon, Georgia's misdemeanor provisions rebuilt after the Genarlow Wilson case, and Connecticut's offence definition in section 53a-71. Each is stated from its text. Each carries dates, age bands, and gaps exactly as the cited provision gives them. Where the play's own legal world is used for contrast, the contrast is drawn from the text of the play, cited by act and scene. General information is the aim throughout; nothing here is advice about a particular case.

## What a Close-in-Age Exemption Is, and the Problem It Was Built to Solve

Start with the architecture that made the exemptions necessary. Every American state sets an age of consent, a bright line between sixteen and eighteen in most jurisdictions, below which a younger person is treated by the criminal law as incapable of consenting to sexual activity at all. The line is deliberately blunt. It does not inquire into affection, maturity, or the other party's age. Under the classic statutory rape model, the offence is one of strict liability as to age: the Texas sexual assault statute, to take the example examined in full below, applies "regardless of whether the person knows the age of the child at the time of the offense." A mistaken belief about a partner's age, even a belief the partner engineered, is in most jurisdictions no answer to the charge. The bluntness has a rationale. It spares child witnesses a cross-examination about consent, it removes a predatory adult's favourite courtroom story, and it gives police and prosecutors a rule that can be applied without reconstructing a relationship's emotional history.

The cost of the bluntness falls on a population the rule was never aimed at. Adolescents form relationships with each other, across the small age differences of a school cohort: a senior of eighteen and a sophomore of fifteen, two students whose birthdays straddle a line the legislature drew for other purposes. When the older partner crosses the age of majority inside such a relationship, the blunt rule can convert a school romance into a felony carrying years of imprisonment and, in the registration era, a place on a public sex offender registry. The United States Department of Health and Human Services survey of state statutory rape laws, published in 2004 under the title Statutory Rape: A Guide to State Laws and Reporting Requirements, documented how unevenly the states handled that conversion. Some states had written age-gap provisions into their codes for decades. Others had none, and relied on prosecutorial discretion to separate the schoolyard case from the predatory one, a reliance that failed in public, repeatedly, in cases that became scandals of disproportion.

### What failure in the older law were these provisions built to correct?

The failure was a category error with a sentence attached: one offence definition, built for adults who exploit children, swept in adolescents whose conduct differed from the target conduct in every feature except the younger partner's birthday. The correction is a sorting rule.

A close-in-age provision redraws the category so that proximity in age, the single most reliable marker distinguishing peer relationships from predatory ones, becomes an element of the offence, a defence to it, or a ground for relief from its collateral consequences.

The sorting idea is older than the nickname. The Model Penal Code, the American Law Institute's great mid-century restatement of criminal law, adopted a provision under which sexual conduct with a person under sixteen was decriminalized where the younger person willingly engaged in the acts with a partner not more than four years older. The Georgia Supreme Court, quoting the Code's commentary in Humphrey v. Wilson in 2007, repeated the drafters' reasoning: the criminal law should not target "sexual experimentation among social contemporaries." That sentence, written by law reformers in the middle of the twentieth century, contains the whole philosophy of the close-in-age exemption in nine words. Social contemporaries. The exemption does not declare adolescent sexuality harmless. It declares that the relevant legal fact about a pair of teenagers is their contemporaneity, the shared corridor of age they occupy, and that a legal system which ignores that fact punishes a resemblance rather than a wrong.

Provisions built on the idea differ along three axes, and a reader comparing jurisdictions has to hold all three apart. The first axis is the size of the permitted gap, most often three years or four. The second is the youngest age at which protection begins, most often thirteen or fourteen. The third, and the one with the largest human consequences, is the mechanism: where in the machinery of prosecution the sorting happens. A state can write the gap into the offence definition itself, so that conduct inside the gap is no crime at all. It can leave the offence intact and hand the defendant an affirmative defence, which acquits but only after charge, arrest, and trial, and only if the defendant proves the qualifying facts. It can reduce the grade of the offence, felony to misdemeanor, for pairs inside the gap. Or it can leave conviction and sentence untouched and offer relief afterwards from the registry, the consequence that outlasts every sentence. Connecticut chose the first mechanism. Texas and Indiana chose the second. Georgia chose the third. Florida chose the fourth, the weakest of the set, and the contrast among the four choices is the real map of how much protection the nickname buys in each place it is used.

One more preliminary distinction matters, because commentary on the nickname habitually blurs it. None of these provisions lowers a state's age of consent. The line stays where the legislature put it. What changes is the treatment of a defined band of conduct near the line, between partners near each other. A general legal reference counts roughly thirty states with some form of close-in-age exception in their codes, which means a substantial minority of states still leave the sorting to prosecutors. The exemption is therefore best understood not as a national settlement but as a patchwork of state-level judgments, made at different times, under the pressure of different cases, sharing a philosophy and a nickname rather than a text.

## Texas: An Affirmative Defence the Defendant Must Prove

Texas runs two parallel close-in-age defences, and the pair of them shows how much fine print the nickname conceals. The first sits in the sexual assault statute, section 22.011 of the Penal Code. The offence in subsection (a)(2) reaches sexual conduct with a child, defined as a person younger than seventeen, and it applies, in the statute's own words, "regardless of whether the person knows the age of the child at the time of the offense." Against that strict-liability offence, subsection (e) grants an affirmative defence on two alternative grounds: that the actor was the spouse of the child, or that a cluster of age and record conditions is met. The age conditions are that the actor "was not more than three years older than the victim" and that the victim "was a child of 14 years of age or older." The record conditions look backward at the defendant's own history: at the time of the offence the actor must not have been required to register for life as a sex offender, and must not have had a reportable conviction or adjudication for an offence under the same section. A final condition excludes victims the actor was barred from marrying under the bigamy statute or from sexual contact under the prohibited-sexual-conduct statute, the code's way of keeping family relationships outside the defence.

The second Texas defence, at section 21.11(b), protects against prosecution for indecency with a child, the contact offence that sits beside sexual assault in the code. Its text differs from its sibling in ways that reward close reading. The actor must have been "not more than three years older than the victim and of the opposite sex." The actor must not have used "duress, force, or a threat against the victim at the time of the offense." The same registration-clean conditions follow. What the subsection does not contain is equally striking: it states no minimum age for the younger person. Where section 22.011(e) writes a floor of fourteen into the defence, section 21.11(b) is silent, leaving its protection bounded only by the gap, the sex condition, and the absence of coercion. Two defences, one nickname, two different shapes of mercy, separated by a single section break in the Penal Code. A defendant whose case falls under the indecency statute can invoke the defence without regard to a floor the sexual assault statute insists on, and a defendant in a same-sex case can invoke the sexual assault defence, whose text carries no sex condition, while the indecency defence remains closed to him. The Kansas litigation described later in this article turned on exactly that kind of condition, and Texas carries a version of the condition in one of its two provisions to this day, in the amended text of the code.

The mechanism deserves emphasis, because Texans who describe section 22.011(e) as the state's Romeo and Juliet law often speak as though the provision decriminalizes the conduct it covers. An affirmative defence does nothing of the kind. The conduct remains a second-degree felony on the face of the statute. The defence is a fact-bound plea that the defendant must raise and support at trial, after arrest, after charge, after the machinery of prosecution has done most of its damage to a young defendant's schooling, family, and record. If the defence succeeds, the acquittal is complete, and that is a great deal more than Florida offers. But the sorting happens at the end of the process rather than at its start, and the price of the sorting is borne by the person the provision exists to protect. Texas practice softens the point: prosecutors screen cases, and a documented three-year gap with a fourteen-year-old or older complainant is a strong reason to decline a charge. Screening, though, is a habit, not a right. The statute gives the young defendant a shield to raise in court. It does not give him a rule that keeps him out of court.

## Florida: Relief from the Registry, and Only from the Registry

Florida's provision is the most frequently misdescribed of the set, and the misdescription runs in a predictable direction: people who call section 943.04354 the state's Romeo and Juliet law tend to describe it as a defence, and it is not one. Enacted in 2007 as section 3 of chapter 2007-209 of the Laws of Florida, the section bears a title that states its function with bureaucratic candour: "Removal of the requirement to register as a sexual offender or sexual predator in special circumstances." The special circumstances are a closed list. As enacted, a person qualified for consideration only if the conviction or delinquency adjudication rested on a violation of section 794.011, the sexual battery statute, or section 800.04, the lewd or lascivious offences statute; only if registration was required "solely on the basis of this violation"; only if the person had no other conviction or adjudication under those sections; and only if the person "is not more than 4 years older than the victim of this violation who was 14 years of age or older but not more than 17 years of age at the time the person committed this violation." Fourteen to seventeen. Four years. One offence, one registration, nothing else on the record.

The procedure is as significant as the criteria. For violations committed on or after July 1, 2007, the qualifying person moves the sentencing court to remove the registration requirement, alleging both eligibility and that removal "will not conflict with federal law." The state attorney receives at least twenty-one days' notice and may present evidence in opposition. The court's power is discretionary even when the criteria are met: the statute says the court "may grant the motion," and a denial closes the door, since a person whose motion is denied "is not authorized under this section to petition" again. For violations committed before the July 2007 date, the section supplies a parallel petition to the court that imposed the sentence, again with notice to the state attorney, again discretionary, again with a denial made final. If the motion or petition succeeds, the person delivers a certified copy of the order to the Department of Law Enforcement, the registration requirement falls away, and the department removes the person's information from the public registry, though the order does not seal the underlying criminal history, which remains a public record by other routes.

### Why does a granted Florida motion still leave a criminal record behind?

Because the section was drafted to repair a consequence, not a conviction. Registration in Florida follows from the conviction automatically, and the 2007 legislature chose to build a narrow exit from the registry while leaving the conviction, the sentence, and the record of both entirely intact.

A successful petitioner walks away still convicted of sexual battery or a lewd or lascivious offence, still carrying that felony into every background check that asks about convictions rather than registration, and still subject to every other consequence the conviction carries. The relief is real and it is partial, and the distance between relief and record is the distance between what the nickname promises and what the section delivers.

Amendment widened the section without changing its character. Legislation effective October 1, 2014 lowered the younger person's threshold age from fourteen to thirteen and broadened the list of qualifying offences, so that the section in its amended form covers violations of sections 794.011, 800.04, 827.071, and 847.0135(5), together with similar offences from other jurisdictions, with the age test restated as a victim who "was 13 years of age or older but younger than 18 years of age" and an offender not more than four years older. The federal-law condition is not boilerplate. The federal registration statute, the Sex Offender Registration and Notification Act enacted as part of the Adam Walsh Act of 2006, excludes from its definition of a registrable sex offence, at 34 U.S.C. section 20911(5)(C), consensual conduct where the victim was at least thirteen and the offender not more than four years older. Florida's criteria track that federal carve-out almost exactly, which is the practical reason the state section demands a finding that removal will not conflict with federal law: the state exit was built to fit inside the federal definition, and a petitioner whose facts fall outside the federal band cannot be removed without putting the state's registry out of compliance. The lovers' names float above all of this in commentary. Underneath, the provision is an exercise in intergovernmental drafting, fitting a state discretion inside a federal floor.

The honest grading of the Florida model follows from its mechanism. It concedes the prosecution, concedes the conviction, concedes the sentence, and then negotiates over the registry, the single consequence most likely to govern the rest of a young person's life: where he can live, where he can work, what a search of his name returns. Reformers who fought for the section were not wrong to prize that negotiation. Registration is the punishment that never finishes. But a law that begins helping only at the registry is a law that has accepted every earlier stage of the harm, and the nickname's warmth should not be allowed to blur that acceptance. Florida did not decriminalize the schoolyard case. Florida built a door out of the registry and stationed a judge in front of it.

## Indiana: The Only Statute in the Set That Asks Whether the Pair Were Dating

Indiana's provision, at subsection (e) of Indiana Code section 35-42-4-9, protects against prosecution for sexual misconduct with a minor, the offence committed by a person of eighteen or older who engages in sexual intercourse or other sexual conduct with a child of at least fourteen and under sixteen. The offence is a Level 5 felony in its basic form, rising to a Level 4 felony when committed by a person of twenty-one or older and to a Level 1 felony where force, a weapon, serious bodily injury, or surreptitious drugging is involved. Subsection (e) then states, in the code's formula, that "it is a defense to a prosecution under this section if all the following apply." The first condition is the familiar gap: "the person is not more than four (4) years older than the victim." The second condition is the distinctive one in American law: "the relationship between the person and the victim was a dating relationship or an ongoing personal relationship," with the statute pausing to define the second term by exclusion, since an "ongoing personal relationship" does "not include a family relationship." The third condition is a bundle of negatives: the crime was not committed by a person of at least twenty-one, was not committed by using or threatening deadly force, was not committed while armed with a deadly weapon, did not result in serious bodily injury, was not facilitated by furnishing the victim, without the victim's knowledge, with a drug or controlled substance, and, completing the list in the amended text, was not committed by a person holding a position of authority or substantial influence over the victim.

The dating condition deserves a section of its own in any honest account, because it makes the Indiana statute the only provision in this set that writes the nickname's premise into the elements of the defence. Texas asks about ages and records. Florida asks about ages and offences. Kansas asked about ages and sexes. Indiana asks about the relationship. The legislature in Indianapolis effectively enacted the question the other statutes leave to implication: were these two people a couple? A dating relationship, or an ongoing personal one, is the statutory translation of the idea the nickname carries in two names, that the conduct under review grew out of an attachment rather than an encounter. The translation is not costless. It hands juries a question of social fact, was this dating, that teenagers themselves argue about without agreement, and it withholds the defence from a pair whose single encounter was willing on both sides but who cannot prove a relationship's duration. A first meeting that both parties entered freely is outside subsection (e). A standing relationship with a four-year-and-one-month gap is outside it too. The statute protects couples, and the boundary of its protection is the boundary of couplehood as a court can be persuaded to see it.

Set the three defence-model states in a row and the design philosophies stand out. Texas trusts arithmetic plus a clean record. Indiana trusts arithmetic plus a narrative. Kansas, in the statute examined next, trusted arithmetic plus a sex classification, and lost the classification in court. Florida trusted arithmetic plus judicial discretion, exercised after conviction. None of the four trusts the proposition that consent in fact should end the inquiry, because the age-of-consent architecture forbids the inquiry: the younger person's consent is legally void, and every close-in-age provision is a workaround that leaves the void in place while routing around its hardest consequences. That is the deep structure the nickname conceals. The laws named for the most famous consenting pair in English literature operate inside a legal framework that refuses, as a matter of definition, to hear the younger party's consent at all. The statutes do not say the fifteen-year-old agreed. They say the gap was small, the record clean, the relationship real, the registry consequence disproportionate. Romeo and Juliet, whose agreement with each other is the entire engine of their play, would find the reasoning foreign. The law protects them by declining to ask them anything.

## Kansas: The Nickname Tested in a Courtroom

Kansas built its close-in-age provision as a separate, lesser offence rather than a defence, and then attached a condition that carried the provision into the United States Reports' orbit and out again. The statute, K.S.A. 21-3522, defined unlawful voluntary sexual relations: engaging in voluntary sexual intercourse, sodomy, or lewd fondling or touching with a child of fourteen or fifteen, where the offender is less than nineteen years of age and less than four years older than the child, where the child and the offender are the only parties involved, and, in the words the legislature enacted, where the pair "are members of the opposite sex." Inside those conditions, the conduct drew shorter prison terms, shorter postrelease supervision, and lighter registration consequences than the general rape, sodomy, and lewd touching statutes imposed. Outside them, the general statutes applied at full force. The Kansas Supreme Court, in the opinion that decided the provision's fate, introduced it with a sentence that belongs in any history of the nickname: "The statute subject to this challenge, commonly referred to as the Romeo and Juliet statute," and then recited the four conditions, the fourth being the sex classification. Commonly referred to. The court did not pause over the name, because courts rarely do, but the sentence records the nickname doing its quiet work at the summit of a state's judiciary: the provision arrived in the opinion pre-framed as the lovers' law.

Matthew Limon's case exposed what the framing hid. In February 2000, a week after his eighteenth birthday, Limon took part in a voluntary act of oral sex with a boy of fourteen; both were residents of a school for young people with developmental disabilities, and the difference in their ages was three years, one month, and some days. Measured against the Kansas statute's conditions, Limon's conduct satisfied every element but one. The younger boy was fourteen. Limon was under nineteen and less than four years older. The two were the only parties involved. The failing element was the sex classification: the pair were members of the same sex, so the Romeo and Juliet statute stood closed to Limon, and the state charged him instead under the criminal sodomy statute, K.S.A. 21-3505(a)(2). He was convicted and sentenced to seventeen years and two months in prison, with sex offender registration and five years of supervision to follow release. Had the partner been a girl, the same conduct would have fallen under the lesser offence, and the maximum sentence would have been fifteen months. Seventeen years against fifteen months, on identical facts of age, interval, and willingness, separated only by the sex of the younger partner: that ratio is the case.

The litigation that followed ran through the decade's constitutional revolution in the background. The Kansas Court of Appeals first upheld the conviction in reliance on Bowers v. Hardwick, the 1986 federal decision sustaining sodomy laws. The United States Supreme Court then decided Lawrence v. Texas in June 2003, striking the Texas sodomy statute and overruling Bowers, and the day after Lawrence the Court granted Limon's petition, vacated the Kansas judgment, and remanded for reconsideration. The Kansas Court of Appeals upheld the conviction a second time, by two votes to one in January 2004, reasoning that Lawrence concerned adults and did not control a case involving a minor. The Kansas Supreme Court took the case and ruled unanimously on October 21, 2005, in an opinion by Justice Marla J. Luckert. The court held that Lawrence controlled the analysis and that, measured under the equal protection guarantees of the Fourteenth Amendment and of the Kansas constitution, the state had no rational basis for the classification the statute created. The opinion's remedy is as instructive as its holding. Rather than void the lesser offence, the court severed the offending words, striking "and are members of the opposite sex" from K.S.A. 21-3522 and leaving the Romeo and Juliet statute standing, extended to the same-sex pairs it had excluded. Limon was released from prison on November 3, 2005.

The Limon litigation matters to the nickname's history for two reasons. The first is doctrinal: the nickname's warmth had licensed a legislature to distribute leniency by a classification that could not survive the mildest constitutional scrutiny, and the case stands as the record of a court refusing the licence. The Kansas justices were not hostile to close-in-age leniency; they preserved it, at the price of its discriminatory boundary. The second reason is rhetorical, and it cuts the other way. The case was litigated in public under the nickname from start to finish: the American Civil Liberties Union, which represented Limon, headlined its 2001 press release "'Romeo & Juliet Law' Gives Gay Teen 16 Years More in Prison than Heterosexual Would Serve." The lovers' names, invoked by the statute's defenders as the emblem of sympathetic leniency, became in the challengers' hands the emblem of the leniency's unequal distribution. A nickname that argues can be argued back, and Limon is the proof. When the Kansas court wrote "commonly referred to as the Romeo and Juliet statute," it was preserving, in the neutral diction of an opinion, the trace of a fight over what the name meant and to whom its sympathy extended.

## Georgia: The Case That Moved a Legislature, and the Court That Finished the Job

Georgia's provisions arrived by the route the Kansas case nearly took: a single prosecution so disproportionate that it reorganized the politics of the statute book. The defendant was Genarlow Wilson, a seventeen-year-old honours student and athlete in Douglas County. At a New Year's Eve gathering, a fifteen-year-old girl willingly performed oral sex on him; the encounter was videotaped, the tape reached the authorities, and Wilson was charged with aggravated child molestation, an offence whose definition turned on the act of sodomy involved. In February 2005 a jury convicted him. The sentence was fixed by law: a mandatory minimum of ten years in prison, with no possibility of probation or parole, a maximum of thirty, and registration as a sex offender. The anomaly at the centre of the case was an accident of drafting. Georgia's statutory rape law had long treated intercourse between teenagers close in age as a misdemeanor, so the same two young people, in a different act, would have faced a maximum of twelve months and no registration. The oral act fell under a different statute written with adult predators in view, and the different statute carried a decade.

The Georgia legislature responded in 2006, while Wilson sat in prison. The amendment, Ga. L. 2006, p. 379, rewrote section 16-6-4 to create misdemeanor grades of the molestation offences for close-in-age pairs. In the amended text, a person convicted of aggravated child molestation is guilty of a misdemeanor where the victim "is at least 13 but less than 16 years of age," where the person convicted "is 18 years of age or younger and is no more than four years older than the victim," and where the charge rests on an act of sodomy rather than physical injury; a companion provision grades simple child molestation as a misdemeanor where the victim is at least fourteen and under sixteen and the same age conditions hold, and a parallel amendment to the registration statute, section 42-1-12, relieved the misdemeanor class of registration. Reporting on the change, including the United Nations Chronicle's account of the case, called the 2006 measure the state's "Romeo and Juliet" law. The legislature then made the choice that defined the case's second act: the amendment was not made retroactive. Wilson's conduct, regraded by his own legislature from a ten-year felony to a twelve-month misdemeanor, remained punishable at the old grade in his case, because the old grade was the law when he acted. He stayed in prison.

### What kept Wilson behind bars after the amendment passed?

The nonretroactivity decision, compounded by appellate failure. The legislature that regraded the conduct expressly declined to extend the new grade to cases already sentenced, and Wilson's direct appeals had been exhausted under the old law. His remaining route was habeas corpus, a collateral attack on the lawfulness of his detention rather than on his conviction.

It was on that narrow path, through a county habeas court's order in June 2007, that his sentence finally came before the Georgia Supreme Court as a question of constitutional punishment rather than legislative grace.

The decision, Humphrey v. Wilson, 282 Ga. 520, came down on October 26, 2007, by four votes to three, in an opinion led by Chief Justice Leah Ward Sears. The court held that Wilson's ten-year sentence constituted cruel and unusual punishment. Its reasoning leaned directly on the legislature's own change of heart: legislative enactments, the court wrote, are "the clearest and best evidence of a society's evolving standard of decency," and the 2006 amendments represented, in the opinion's most quoted phrase, "a seismic shift in the legislature's view of the gravity of oral sex between two willing teenage participants." The court noted that the legislature had substituted misdemeanor punishment for the felony, relieved the class of registration, and thereby declared that the severe punishment imposed on Wilson made "no measurable contribution to acceptable goals of punishment." The habeas court's disposition was corrected in one respect: rather than resentencing Wilson under the new grade, the Supreme Court ordered his discharge from custody. He had served more than two years of the ten. The dissent rested chiefly on the legislature's decision against retroactivity and on the deference owed to legislative sentencing judgments, which is to say the dissent took the 2006 line at face value, while the majority treated the amendment as evidence that convicted the old sentence under the constitution.

Georgia's sequence is the cleanest demonstration in the set of how the nickname's world actually changes law. A drafting accident produced an indefensible sentence. A defence attorney, B. J. Bernstein, carried the case to the legislature and the press, saying in substance that the aggravated molestation statute "wasn't made for teens like Genarlow." The legislature regraded the conduct and attached the lovers' name in public discussion, then stopped one step short of the prisoner whose case had moved it. A court then used the legislature's own regrading as the constitutional instrument to free him. At no point in the sequence did the statutory text carry the lovers' names. At every point, the name did political work: it told legislators, reporters, and judges which story they were inside, the story of young lovers and an uncomprehending legal order, before a single element of any offence was discussed.

## Connecticut: The Age Gap Written into the Offence Itself

Connecticut chose the mechanism that Texas, Florida, Indiana, and Georgia all declined, and the choice deserves attention precisely because it attracts so little. Section 53a-71 of the Connecticut General Statutes defines sexual assault in the second degree. Its first subsection makes a person guilty when he engages in sexual intercourse with another person who "is thirteen years of age or older but under sixteen years of age and the actor is more than three years older than such other person." The gap is not a defence to the offence. The gap is an element of it, a fact the state must prove, like the act and the ages themselves. Where the interval between the partners is three years or less, the offence defined in that subsection does not exist, and no arrest, charge, or trial can be built on it. The same architecture recurs down the section's neighbouring provisions: sexual assault in the fourth degree, the contact offence, is defined for a younger person of thirteen or fourteen only where the actor is more than three years older, and for a child under thirteen only where the actor is more than two years older. The whole Connecticut scheme is graduated by interval, the code measuring relationships in years the way the other states' defences do, but measuring them at the definition stage, before the machinery starts.

The margin has moved within the statute's own history. The 1993 act, Public Act 93-340, restated the first subsection to specify a younger person of thirteen or older with an actor more than two years older. In the restated text carried through the 2011 and 2013 substituted versions of the section, the requirement stands at more than three years, so the protected interval widened by a year across those decades, quietly, in the ordinary traffic of penal code revision, without a scandal case or a nickname-driven campaign attached to it in the record. Connecticut's approach also allocates the burden correctly for the protected class: a defendant in a defence-model state must assemble proof of birthdays, records, and relationships, while a Connecticut defendant inside the interval need prove nothing, because the state cannot state the offence. The difference between an element and a defence is a semester of criminal procedure in the abstract. In the life of an eighteen-year-old with a fifteen-year-old partner, it is the difference between a case that cannot be opened and a case that must be won.

Connecticut rarely appears in commentary under the nickname at all, and the absence is itself evidence about how the name spreads. The nickname attaches to drama: to defences raised in courtrooms, to petitions, to cases with names of their own, Limon and Wilson. A state that solves the problem inside its offence definitions generates no defendants, no appeals, no press releases, and consequently no occasions for the lovers' names to be spoken over its statute. The most protective mechanism in the set is the one the nickname passes by, because the nickname is a creature of litigation and legislation in crisis, and Connecticut's design leaves little of either. Any verdict on the provisions has to grade that silence properly: by results for the young people concerned, the offence-definition model is the strongest of the four, and its invisibility in the nickname's career is the nickname's failure of coverage, not the model's failure of effect.

## Floors, Gaps, and Ceilings: The Arithmetic Inside the Nickname

Every provision in the set is built from three numbers, and the numbers repay attention because they encode, in arithmetic, judgments that the commentary prefers to leave in the warmth of the nickname. The floor is the youngest age at which the protected band begins. The gap is the widest interval the band tolerates. The ceiling, present in some designs and absent in others, is the oldest age at which the older partner can still claim the band's shelter. A legislature choosing the three numbers is deciding, in effect, which school corridor it is willing to protect, and the choices cluster tightly enough to show a shared intuition at work underneath the states' independence.

The floors divide at thirteen and fourteen, and the division tracks the two sources of the modern design. Thirteen is the federal number: the Adam Walsh Act's registration definition, at 34 U.S.C. section 20911(5)(C), draws the consensual-conduct carve-out at a victim of at least thirteen with an offender not more than four years older, and the states that legislated in the federal law's shadow adopted its floor. Florida's amendment of 2014 lowered its floor from fourteen to thirteen, into alignment with the federal band its section already had to respect through the no-conflict condition. Georgia's regraded aggravated offence and Connecticut's offence definition also begin at thirteen. Fourteen is the older state number, held by Texas in its sexual assault defence and by Indiana in its misconduct defence, and it has the longer pedigree in state codes: fourteen is the age at which several of the older statutory schemes began their own gradations, and it is, by the play's arithmetic, the birthday Juliet has not yet reached. A floor is a refusal disguised as a boundary. Below it, the law declines the entire inquiry into intervals and relationships, and treats every case as the predatory case, whatever the birthdays say.

The gaps divide at three and four. Four years is the span of a four-year school: the freshman and the senior, the widest pairing a single secondary institution ordinarily contains, and the Model Penal Code's drafters had already chosen four as the interval that bounded social contemporaneity in the provision the Georgia court quoted. Four is also the federal number, in the registration definition, and the number in Florida, Indiana, Georgia, and Kansas. Three is the stricter judgment, chosen by Texas in both its defences and built into Connecticut's offence definition, and it draws the protected corridor more narrowly: a three-year band covers most of a school cohort while excluding the pairing of its oldest and youngest members at the extremes. Neither number has a developmental science behind it that fixes it to the year. Both are legislative intuitions about where contemporaneity ends, expressed in the only units a code can use, and the day-exactness with which courts apply them, Florida measuring its four years to the birthday, is the price of expressing a social judgment in arithmetic. A relationship does not change its character on a birthday. The codes pretend otherwise because a code must draw the line somewhere, and the pretence is defensible exactly to the extent that the line is drawn in the open, in the text, where Limon's judges and Wilson's could find it.

The ceilings are the least discussed number and the most revealing. Kansas capped its lesser offence at an offender under nineteen, so the statute protected a school-age pair and stopped at the schoolhouse door. Georgia capped its misdemeanor grades at an offender of eighteen or younger. Indiana's defence fails if the crime was committed by a person of at least twenty-one, a ceiling set one notch higher, at the edge of the college years. Texas and Florida, by contrast, wrote no ceiling into their provisions at all: the Texas defence turns on the three-year interval, the victim's age of fourteen or more, and the defendant's record, so an offender of twenty with a partner of seventeen stands inside the arithmetic, and Florida's relief section likewise tests only the interval, the victim's band of years, and the record. A ceiling expresses a judgment that the nickname's sympathy belongs to the young. Its absence expresses a different judgment, that the operative fact is the interval alone. Since the underlying offence requires a younger party below the age of consent, the absent ceiling matters only in the narrow band where the younger party is sixteen or seventeen and the older party just past twenty, a pairing the interval test shelters in Texas and Florida while the Georgia and Kansas ceilings exclude it by design. The variation is a reminder that the nickname unifies designs that disagree about first principles: whether the law is protecting youth as such, or proximity as such.

Indiana's code carries the design one step further, into territory the nickname's commentary rarely visits. The same chapter of the Indiana Code that holds the misconduct defence holds a sibling defence for the image offences, the possession, production, and dissemination provisions: where a cellular telephone, another wireless device, or a social networking site was used, where the defendant is not more than four years older or younger than the person depicted or the person who received the image, where the relationship was a dating relationship or an ongoing personal relationship, where the crime was committed by a person under twenty-two, and where the person depicted acquiesced in the conduct, a defence lies. The architecture is the misconduct defence rebuilt for the camera phone: the same four-year interval, the same relationship condition, the same insistence that the instrument was the ordinary technology of adolescent social life. The extension matters to the nickname's history because it shows the design escaping the fact pattern that named it. The lovers exchanged no images. The principle the codes extracted from their story, that proximity in age plus a real relationship changes the legal character of adolescent sexual conduct, turned out to be portable to conduct the sixteenth century could not have imagined, and Indiana's drafters carried it across without needing the name at all.

## The States Without a Provision: Discretion, the Unwritten Exemption

A general legal reference counts roughly thirty states with some form of close-in-age exception, which leaves a large minority of states, at any given count, without one. In those states the sorting between the predatory case and the peer case still happens. It happens in prosecutors' offices, in charging conferences and plea negotiations, invisible to the public record and unreviewable by any court. The 2004 survey by the United States Department of Health and Human Services documented the resulting landscape: a patchwork in which the legal consequence of the same two birthdays depended on the state, and within the no-provision states, on the county and the office. Discretion is the unwritten exemption, and its defenders make a real argument for it: a prosecutor can weigh facts no statute can anticipate, the texture of a relationship, the presence or absence of pressure, the wishes of the younger party and the younger party's family, and can decline the cases that deserve declining without a legislature's permission. The written provisions exist because that argument kept failing in public.

The failures have a pattern, and the cases in this article exhibit it. Discretion did not save Genarlow Wilson, because the anomaly in his case was not a prosecutor's invention; the aggravated molestation statute genuinely covered the act, the mandatory sentence genuinely followed, and the discretionary actors in the system, the jury that convicted him and the judge who sentenced him, were bound by a text that gave them nothing to exercise discretion with. Discretion did not save Matthew Limon, because the charging decision in his case was dictated by the boundary of the lesser offence itself: the prosecutor did not choose severity over leniency in the abstract, but applied the general sodomy statute to conduct the lesser statute excluded on its face. In both cases the harm flowed from texts, and only texts could repair it, a regrading in Georgia and a severance in Kansas. The lesson generalizes. Prosecutorial discretion operates inside the space statutes leave, and the space the age-of-consent statutes leave is the decision whether to set the machinery in motion, not the power to redesign its consequences. Once the machinery moves, its gears are statutory: offence definitions, mandatory minima, registration triggers. A discretion regime can spare the sympathetic defendant an arrest. It cannot spare him the sentence or the registry if the case proceeds, and the cases that proceed are disproportionately the ones involving a complaining parent, a racial or social mismatch that draws official attention, or a local politics of toughness, which is to say the cases where neutral criteria were most needed.

The Illinois campaign shows the conversion of discretion into rule being attempted in the open. Reform advocates in that state argued that the code criminalized relationships between consenting teenagers where one partner was under seventeen, the state's age of consent, and that the sorting depended entirely on which cases came to official attention. Representative Bob Pritchard's bill proposed to write the sorting down: registry relief for an offender not more than four years older than a victim of fourteen or more, on the Florida pattern. Whether or not the bill's particular design was the right one, the campaign's premise is the premise of every provision in this article, that a judgment exercised daily by prosecutors under no standard should be stated as a rule, with numbers, in a text, where defendants can invoke it and courts can police its boundaries. The nickname's role in that conversion is the subject of the sections that follow. First, though, the consequence that gives the conversion its urgency needs its own examination, because the registry is the reason a Florida-style exit, the weakest mechanism in the set, was worth a legislature's session and a governor's signature.

## Registration: The Consequence That Outlives Every Sentence

Sex offender registration began as a law-enforcement tool and became, across the decades in which the close-in-age provisions were written, the dominant fact in the lives of the people the provisions protect. The mechanics are visible in the Florida text. A person required to register is listed in a public registry maintained by the Department of Law Enforcement; the 2007 section's machinery for removal runs through a certified copy of a court order delivered to the department, after which "the registration requirement will not apply to the person and the department shall remove all information about the person from the public registry." The same subsection adds the limit that defines the relief: removal from the registry "does not mean that the public is denied access to information about the person's criminal history or record that is otherwise available as a public record." Registration is a publication regime. Its burden is not the act of registering, which takes an afternoon, but the standing fact of being findable, by landlords, employers, neighbours, and search engines, under the state's own label of sexual offender or sexual predator, for years or for life. The Florida section's coverage of persons "adjudicated delinquent" as well as convicted records the regime's reach into juvenile court: a delinquency adjudication, the juvenile system's supposedly non-criminal disposition, could carry the same public label as an adult conviction.

The federal layer locked the state regimes into a common frame. The Adam Walsh Child Protection and Safety Act of 2006 enacted the Sex Offender Registration and Notification Act, setting national minimum standards for state registries and defining the offences that trigger registration. Inside that definition sits the carve-out at 34 U.S.C. section 20911(5)(C), excluding consensual conduct where the victim was at least thirteen and the offender not more than four years older, the federal system's own close-in-age judgment, expressed as a matter of definition rather than defence. The carve-out is the reason Florida's section conditions relief on consistency with federal law, and it illustrates the interlock that governs the whole field: a state may be more lenient than the federal floor in its own registry practice only within the space the federal definition leaves, and a state petitioner whose facts fall outside the federal band can win a state motion and still fail the section's own federal condition. The lovers' names are spoken in none of these instruments. The arithmetic of thirteen and four does the work, in Washington as in Tallahassee.

The human cost of the registry for the young is the subject of the documentary record the scholars cite. Human Rights Watch's 2013 report, Raised on the Registry: The Irreparable Harm of Placing Children on Sex Offender Registries in the U.S., assembled case histories of people registered for offences committed as children and adolescents, including consensual peer conduct, and documented the consequences that follow the label: exclusion from housing and schooling, barriers to employment, public notification to neighbours, and the psychological weight of a public identity fixed at the age of the offence. The report's thesis is in its title, that the harm is irreparable in kind, because the registry publishes a adolescent act as an adult identity. Set beside the report, the close-in-age provisions divide by their relationship to registration. The defence and offence-definition models prevent the label from attaching. The regrading model in Georgia expressly relieves the misdemeanor class of registration. The Florida model accepts the label and litigates its removal, one petitioner at a time, under criteria that mirror the federal band. The nickname treats the four designs as one idea. The registry knows the difference, and so does every young person who has had to explain a search result to an employer: prevention and removal are not the same mercy, and the distance between them is measured in the years a name spends on a public list while a petition waits.

## The Name That Appears in No Statute

Consider the documentary record assembled in the preceding sections. The Texas defence sits inside a section titled Sexual Assault. The Florida section is titled Removal of the Requirement to Register as a Sexual Offender or Sexual Predator in Special Circumstances. Indiana's is Sexual Misconduct with a Minor. Kansas called its offence Unlawful Voluntary Sexual Relations. Georgia's provisions live inside Child Molestation; Aggravated Child Molestation. Connecticut's is Sexual Assault in the Second Degree. Six jurisdictions, seven provisions, and the words Romeo and Juliet occur in none of them. The lovers' names are not a short title, not a section heading, not a legislative finding, not a codifier's note. The name lives entirely outside the texts it names: in the Kansas Supreme Court's "commonly referred to," in the American Civil Liberties Union's headlines, in the United Nations Chronicle's description of the Georgia amendment, in the explanations of defence attorneys and the copy of reporters, in the questions constituents ask legislators and the answers legislators give back. A general legal reference can state flatly that no state statute titles itself the Romeo and Juliet law, and the flat statement is correct. The term is a folk name for a family of provisions that share a philosophy and almost nothing else, not even a mechanism.

The folk name has nonetheless reached the highest levels of American legal writing, and its most remarkable appearance predates most of the provisions examined here. In Ashcroft v. Free Speech Coalition, 535 U.S. 234, decided April 16, 2002, the Supreme Court struck down provisions of the Child Pornography Prevention Act of 1996 that criminalized images appearing to depict minors. Justice Anthony Kennedy's opinion for the Court paused, in the middle of a First Amendment analysis, to place the play. "Both themes, teenage sexual activity and the sexual abuse of children," the opinion reads, "have inspired countless literary works. William Shakespeare created the most famous pair of teenage lovers, one of whom is just 13 years of age. See Romeo and Juliet, act I, sc. 2, l. 9 ('She hath not seen the change of fourteen years'). In the drama, Shakespeare portrays the relationship as something splendid and innocent, but not juvenile." The opinion goes on to count the play's film progeny, "no less than 40 motion pictures," some suggesting that the teenagers consummated the relationship. A constitutional holding about virtual images turns, for a paragraph, on the cultural standing of the Veronese lovers, and the standing the Court assigns them is splendour and innocence. The paragraph does no doctrinal work that a lesser example could not have done. It does rhetorical work of a high order: it anchors the Court's protection of disturbing speech in the culture's most honoured story of adolescent desire, and it treats that story's innocence as a premise no reader will contest.

Legislators work the same seam at lower altitude. In Illinois, where reform advocates campaigned for a close-in-age provision against a code that offered none, the case was opened in the press under the sentence, "If Romeo were alive today, he would be a sex offender," and Representative Bob Pritchard, the Republican sponsor of a bill built on a four-year gap and a floor of fourteen, compressed his side of the argument into a distinction: "We're all disgusted with older people who prey on young children, but that's not what we're talking about." The bill's fate is beside the point here; the rhetoric is the point. Pritchard's sentence performs the sorting that close-in-age law exists to perform, and it performs the sorting by invoking the shared understanding the nickname supplies, that there exists a class of young lovers whom the predator statutes were never talking about. The name is that understanding, portable. A researcher assembling the comparative record across fifty codes, tracking which states chose an element, a defence, a regrading, or a registry exit, is doing work the nickname makes harder in one specific way: the folk name promises a single thing and the codes deliver four. Scholars who keep that comparative file in order, in [a private research vault built for organising study notes across a long project](https://vaultbook.net/), find the discipline the nickname lacks waiting in the texts themselves, each with its own title, none of them borrowed from a play.

### Why do legislators reach for two fictional names instead of a plain description like age-gap exemption?

Because the plain description describes and the names persuade. "Age-gap exemption" states a mechanism and invites a technical debate about years and intervals, a debate in which the protective purpose can be made to sound like a loophole. The lovers' names import a complete narrative instead, with sympathetic protagonists and an unreasonable opposing order.

The narrative arrives with a known ending, in which the legal and familial machinery destroys the innocent, so a legislator who says Romeo and Juliet has already argued the case before the elements are read, and has argued it in the only register, story, that moves votes on a subject where every participant fears being quoted on the wrong side.

That is the honest account of the nickname's power, and it is also the beginning of the case against it. A name that argues in advance argues selectively. The narrative the name imports stops at the balcony. It does not continue to the Friar's cell, the banquet hall, the apothecary's shop, or the tomb, and the parts of the story it omits are not decorative. They are the parts in which the lovers' relationship is shadowed by exactly the features modern protective law exists to police: a great disparity of power between a child and the adults arranging her future, a secret marriage conducted by an adult who hopes to profit politically from it, a pace of decision that every adult voice in the play warns against, and a conclusion in which both young people are dead by their own hands within days of meeting. The cultural critic Joseph Pearce, a biographer of Shakespeare, attacked the Supreme Court's paragraph on precisely this ground, calling the splendid-and-innocent reading a misreading of a cautionary tragedy and the suggestion that the play glorifies underage sexual activity an absurdity; the play, in Pearce's account, is written from the perspective of parental concern at the destructive consequences of naive adolescent passion. The legal scholar Paul Finkelman, reviewing the same paragraph in the Tulsa Law Review, registered the irony in a drier register, referring to Juliet in a footnote as "the most famous thirteen-year-old female fornicator in western culture." Splendid and innocent; a cautionary tragedy; a thirteen-year-old fornicator. The same paragraph of the United States Reports drew all three responses, and the spread of the responses is the nickname debate in miniature. The name depends on the first reading. The play supports the second and third at least as well.

## Why the Name Persuades: The Argument Hidden Inside a Nickname

Strip the nickname to its logical skeleton and it makes four claims at once, each contestable, none ever stated. The first claim is symmetry: the two parties are alike, in age, in station, in desire, as Romeo and Juliet are alike. The second is mutuality: the attachment runs in both directions and was sought by both. The third is innocence of intent: nothing predatory, calculating, or exploitative is present, as nothing predatory is present in the orchard scene. The fourth is misdirection of blame: whatever has gone wrong in the case has gone wrong because an external order, feuding houses or felony statutes, refused to accommodate a harmless thing. A legal audience that accepts the nickname has, without deliberation, accepted all four claims for the case in front of it. That is why prosecutors resist the name and defence advocates reach for it. It is a directed verdict disguised as a label.

The four claims explain both the nickname's victories and its failures of coverage. Where the claims hold, the name fits like a glove: Georgia's Wilson, Kansas's Limon measured by age and willingness, the Indiana couple the statute imagines, dating, within four years, hurting nobody. Where any claim fails, the name becomes a liability to its own side, which is why the statutes, as distinct from the commentary, build their protections out of verifiable proxies rather than the story's qualities. No provision examined here asks whether the parties loved each other. Indiana's dating condition comes closest, and even Indiana defines its term by what it excludes and surrounds it with arithmetic. The codes translate symmetry into years, mutuality into the younger party's age and the absence of force, innocence into clean records and the absence of authority, and misdirection of blame into the quiet premise that a registry or a felony grade would be disproportionate. The translation loses the story's warmth. It gains the only thing a criminal code is entitled to trade in, which is tests that can be applied to strangers.

There is a fifth claim, quieter than the other four, embedded in the name's grammar. Romeo comes first. The provisions the name covers protect, in the run of litigated cases, an older male partner against the consequences of a relationship with a younger female one; Limon and Wilson were both young men, and the Florida petitions and Texas defences of commentary and case reporting skew the same way. Yet the name yokes the pair symmetrically, and the statutes, to their credit, are written in sex-neutral terms almost everywhere: the Texas sexual assault defence, the Florida section, the Indiana defence, and the Georgia regrading all speak of persons, victims, and offenders without sex, and the one provision that wrote sex into its conditions lost the condition in court. The nickname romanticizes a symmetry that the litigated reality often lacks and that the texts, after Limon, decline to enact. Even the name's word order argues. Shakespeare's title is a conjunction of equals, and the law that borrows it has spent two decades being corrected toward the equality the title merely asserts.

## The Scholars' Quarrel: Development, Registration, and the Half-Finished Fix

The scholarly argument about close-in-age provisions is not between friends and enemies of the provisions. It is between scholars who think the provisions repair the wrong variable and scholars who think they repair a real one, and the disagreement is best seen through the two bodies of work that frame it. Franklin E. Zimring of Berkeley Law published An American Travesty: Legal Responses to Adolescent Sexual Offending in 2004, the first scholarly book in half a century on its subject, and its thesis is developmental. American law, Zimring argues, responds to sexual misconduct by adolescents on the template built for adult offenders, ignoring the developmental status of the young people involved; the clinical, predictive, and moral significance of the conduct all change with age, and a legal response that refuses to take age into account produces results he calls, in the book's governing charge, both foolish and unjust. The empirical core of the argument is that young offenders, judged by later arrest histories, resemble undifferentiated delinquents rather than incipient predators: adolescent sexual misconduct is a weak predictor of adult sexual offending, and policies built on the contrary assumption misclassify the population they govern.

Catherine L. Carpenter of Southwestern Law School directs the attack one level deeper, at registration itself. Across a series of works, including "The Constitutionality of Strict Liability in Sex Offender Registration Laws" in 2006 and "Against Juvenile Sex Offender Registration" in 2014, Carpenter argues that the registration regime rests on a false narrative: an exaggerated account of recidivism, embraced by courts and legislatures, that sweeps children and strict-liability offenders into a system designed for the dangerous few. Her formulation of the regime's psychology is that the public is afraid for its children and afraid of its children at the same time, and that the second fear writes the laws. On her account, the teenager convicted of a strict-liability offence who lands on a registry, like the Indiana teenager Zach Anderson, whose 2015 case, arising from a dating-app meeting with a Michigan girl who had misrepresented her age, Carpenter cites in her registration critique, is not an accident of the system. The teenager is the system working as designed, registering first and assessing dangerousness never.

Between Zimring's developmental frame and Carpenter's registration frame, the close-in-age provisions occupy an awkward middle. They answer Zimring's proportionality objection at the level of the offence: a well-drawn provision refuses to grade peer conduct on the adult template, and Connecticut's design refuses the most completely, by declining to define the offence at all. They answer Carpenter hardly at all, and in the Florida design they confirm her critique by construction, since a provision whose entire office is to negotiate exits from the registry concedes the registry's claim on everyone who falls outside its narrow door, including every young person whose gap was four years and a month, whose record carried a second count, or whose judge said no. The provisions also leave untouched the strict-liability core that both scholars identify as the engine of the hardest cases: none of the six jurisdictions examined here makes the younger person's actual willingness, or the older person's reasonable belief about age, an element of the protected band, and Indiana, which writes the relationship into its defence, routes around willingness rather than recognizing it. The law will ask how many years separate the birthdays. It will not ask what the younger party wanted. On the scholars' shared ground, that refusal, more than any gap size, is the measure of how far the reforms fall short of their own premise.

### Do close-in-age provisions answer the strongest scholarly objection to the older law?

Only the proportionality half of it. The provisions concede that grading peer conduct as predatory offending was unjust, and the best of them, the offence-definition model, withdraw the conduct from the criminal process entirely. That concession is real, and it is where the agreement ends.

They do not concede the registration scholars' deeper claim, that a system which registers the young on the strength of a category rather than an assessment of danger will keep producing the cases the provisions exist to relieve, one gap measurement at a time.

The adjudication this article reaches between the reformers and the scholars is therefore split, and the split follows the mechanism line the table draws. As offence design, the provisions succeed: they are the developmental correction Zimring demanded, enacted piecemeal, and the Limon court showed that constitutional law will patrol their boundaries when legislatures distribute the correction unequally. As registration policy, they are palliative: they relieve the sympathetic and leave the regime. As rhetoric, finally, the nickname is implicated in both verdicts, because the same story that built the political will for the offence-design reforms also taught legislatures to stop at the sympathetic case, to draft for Romeo and Juliet and leave the unromantic registrant, the one without a balcony in his story, exactly where the false narrative put him. The name opens the door for the couples who resemble its heroes. The scholars' point is that a just system would not need the resemblance.

## Thirteen in Verona: The Irony at the Centre of the Name

The play states its heroine's age with a precision it grants almost nothing else. In Act 1, Scene 2, Capulet resists Paris's suit on the ground of years: "She hath not seen the change of fourteen years; / Let two more summers wither in their pride / Ere we may think her ripe to be a bride." Paris answers with the market's voice: "Younger than she are happy mothers made." Capulet's rejoinder concedes the market and states its cost: "And too soon marred are those so early made." In Act 1, Scene 3 the Nurse fixes the birthday to a feast day, Lammas Eve, and repeats the reckoning until the date is unmistakable: on Lammas Eve at night, Juliet shall be fourteen. She is thirteen. The play's own account of the lovers' ages, examined at article length in [the study of how old Romeo and Juliet are and why the question matters](/2022/02/18/how-old-are-romeo-and-juliet-age-question/), and in [the account of Juliet at thirteen as Shakespeare's daring heroine](/2022/07/01/juliet-thirteen-years-old-heroine/), gives Romeo no number at all: he is older, experienced enough to have conducted a prior suit to Rosaline, and the text leaves his years to inference. The nickname's symmetry, Romeo and Juliet as a matched pair of teenagers, is thus the culture's completion of a text that names only the girl's age and leaves the boy's blank. Even the famous pair's equality in age is an editorial act performed by the afterlife.

Set the statutes against the number thirteen. The federal registration definition draws its line at thirteen: consensual conduct is carved out of the registrable category where the victim was at least thirteen and the offender not more than four years older. Florida, after its 2014 amendment, draws the same line for registry relief. Georgia's regraded aggravated offence begins at thirteen. Connecticut's offence begins at thirteen. Indiana and Texas hold their floors at fourteen, the birthday Juliet has not reached. The statutes' lowest rung, in other words, is Juliet's age or one year above it, and the coincidence is not a coincidence of drafting so much as a collision of two systems measuring the same boundary: the age below which the law refuses, as a matter of definition, to treat a young person as a participant rather than a victim. Verona's marriage market treated thirteen as bridal. The modern codes treat thirteen as the outer edge of the zone in which a close-in-age partner might be spared, and below it no gap, no relationship, no record, and no nickname avails. The name borrowed the lovers. The codes quietly adopted the heroine's birthday as the floor of their mercy and built everything above it.

### How old is Juliet in the play, and what follows from that number?

Juliet is thirteen, one birthday short of fourteen, and the number is stated three times in the first act by the three adults disposing of her future: her father in negotiation, her nurse in reminiscence, and the text's own arithmetic of Lammas Eve.

What follows is that the play's romance is, by the categories of every code examined here, a relationship between a child at the floor of the protected band and an older partner of unstated years, arranged against paternal command and consummated in a secret marriage. The statutes that carry the lovers' names would examine that relationship with instruments the play never contemplates, and the examination would not obviously acquit it.

The marriage market context sharpens the point. Capulet's opening position, that his will to her consent is "but a part" of the matter, belongs to a household order in which a father's disposition of a daughter is a property and alliance decision, the order anatomized in [the analysis of patriarchy and the marriage market in Verona](/2024/02/23/patriarchy-marriage-market-verona/). Within days of stage time the same father is threatening disownment to compel the Paris match. The modern close-in-age provisions exist to protect a young person's partner from the state. Nothing in them protects a young person from a parent, a guardian, or a household, and the play's catastrophe is set in motion by exactly the parties the modern provisions do not reach: the fathers whose feud and whose marriage plans close every lawful door. The name imports the lovers as a unit against the law. The play presents them as a unit against their families, which is a different antagonist, and the law of Verona, when it finally acts, acts against the feud's violence rather than against the marriage. Modern commentary that blames statutory rape law for standing where Capulet stood has the play's geometry wrong, and the wrongness matters, because the provisions' actual antagonist, prosecutorial and legislative overreach against peer couples, is a problem of the state's instruments, not of any household's commands.

The Friar completes the irony. Lawrence marries the pair in secret, in his cell, in a ceremony the play stages in Act 2, Scene 6, and he does so on a political calculation he states openly: the alliance might turn the households' rancour to pure love. Before the ceremony he warns the bridegroom in the play's most quoted sentence of counsel: "These violent delights have violent ends / And in their triumph die, like fire and powder, / Which, as they kiss, consume." The warning is the play's own close-in-age provision, an adult voice measuring the interval between desire and judgment and finding the interval too small, and it is spoken by the one adult who proceeds anyway. The scene itself, [the secret wedding at the Friar's cell and the haste that governs it](/2023/09/01/act-2-scene-6-secret-wedding/), is the hinge on which the tragedy turns from comedy's materials to catastrophe. A legal culture that names its protective statutes for this couple has named them for a marriage that its officiant entered as a gamble, warned against in the ceremony's own dialogue, concealed from every parent, and followed within days by three deaths in a street fight's aftermath and two more in a tomb. The nickname's edit is not a detail. It is the difference between the play as written and the play as needed.

## Verona's Law Punished the Lovers' World; the Modern Law Shields Couples from Itself

The play's legal order, drawn at the passage level the subject allows here, is a study in protections pointing the wrong way. Prince Escalus opens the action with an edict aimed at the feud's public violence: after a third disturbance of the streets, the penalty is death, "Your lives shall pay the forfeit of the peace." The edict protects the peace, an abstraction, with great rhetorical force, and it protects no person in particular; it is unenforced against the households whose servants brawl, commuted without stated principle when Romeo kills Tybalt, and powerless to prevent any act it forbids. Mantua's law, in Act 5, is the edict's mirror: a statute forbidding the sale of poison on pain of death, which Romeo recites to the Apothecary even as he buys the poison, and which the Apothecary breaks out of poverty, answering with the play's bleakest sentence of legal realism, "My poverty, but not my will, consents." Verona's law is severe, public, and useless to the lovers at every point where a law might have helped them: no provision protects a daughter against a forced match, no provision shelters a secret marriage, no provision measures the difference between a brawl and a wedding. The legal order sees the feud's symptoms in the street and is blind to the catastrophe being arranged in the houses.

The modern provisions invert every term of that picture. The Prince's law existed to protect the city from the lovers' world; the close-in-age provisions exist to protect the lovers from the law itself. The Prince punished by category, all disturbers of the peace alike; the modern codes sort by interval and record, building the distinctions the edict refused. The Prince's one act of individualized judgment, the commutation of Romeo's death sentence to banishment, was arbitrary grace from a throne; the modern equivalents, the Texas defence, the Florida motion, the Indiana conditions, are grace converted into criteria, published in advance, available to strangers. Where the play's law failed upward, protecting abstractions and abandoning persons, the nickname's statutes at their best fail downward, protecting persons and abandoning the abstraction of a single undifferentiated offence. The inversion is the strongest argument that the borrowing, however ironic, is not frivolous: the modern provisions embody exactly the jurisprudence Verona lacked, a law that asks who the parties are to each other before it decides what their act was. What the borrowing still owes the play is the acknowledgment that the jurisprudence arrives under a false flag, flying the names of two children whom no law in their own story ever asked anything at all.

## The Provision the Nickname Would Write, If the Nickname Could Draft

The graded set invites a synthesis, and a verdict-led account should not refuse it. A provision drawn from the strongest element of each design would run as follows. The age gap would sit in the offence definition, on the Connecticut pattern, so that no charge can be framed inside the band and no young defendant must win a case to be free of it. The interval would be four years, the Model Penal Code's measure of social contemporaneity and the federal registry definition's measure, rather than three, because the narrower band excludes the oldest-and-youngest pairing inside a single school, the pairing the whole doctrine exists to recognize. The floor would be fourteen, the Texas and Indiana number, rather than thirteen: the federal floor of thirteen was set for the narrower purpose of defining registrable offences, and a state defining criminal liability itself should hold the band's edge at the higher line, the more so when the play behind the nickname puts its heroine at thirteen and thereby marks the age at which the culture's sympathy should stop and its protection become absolute. The classification would run on age alone, with no condition as to the parties' sexes, the Kansas court's correction in Limon being treated as a drafting rule rather than a litigation outcome, and no condition as to the relationship's form: Indiana's dating requirement, for all its fidelity to the nickname's premise, hands juries an unanswerable social question and withholds protection from the willing pair who cannot prove duration, and a rule that protects couples rather than contemporaries will distribute its mercy by narrative skill. Registration would follow the offence automatically, excluded for the band as a matter of definition on the federal pattern, so that no petitioner must litigate his way off a public list for conduct the code has declined to call an offence. Force, coercion, authority, and a secret drugging would remain outside the band entirely, as every design in the set already insists. No design in force combines all of those elements. The synthesis is therefore an indictment as much as a model: each state holds some of the pieces, the nickname claims the whole, and the distance between the claim and the codes is the measure of the work the name still has to do.

## The Verdict: A Borrowed Name, Honestly Earned and Honestly Faulted

The provisions first. Graded by mechanism, the set ranks itself. Connecticut's offence-definition model is the strongest protection, because it prevents the case. Kansas's lesser-offence model follows, grading the conduct rather than excusing it, and carrying the Limon correction inside its text. Georgia's regrading stands next, its misdemeanor ceiling and registration relief honest if born of scandal. The Texas and Indiana defences protect completely but late, at trial, on the defendant's proof, with Indiana's relationship condition adding a narrative burden no other state imposes and Texas's pair of defences diverging in floor and sex condition in ways the nickname's smoothness conceals. Florida's registry exit is the weakest instrument, real relief stationed at the far end of the process, though the consequence it relieves is the one that lasts longest. A reader choosing where the nickname's promise is kept has the ranking the texts compel: the promise is kept in proportion as the sorting happens early, by rule, without discretion, and it fades in proportion as the sorting is deferred to courtrooms, petitions, and judges.

The concept beneath the provisions is sound, and the scholars' quarrel does not unsettle it. Age proximity is the best single proxy the criminal law has for the difference between predation and peer attachment; the Model Penal Code's drafters saw it two generations ago, the federal registry definition adopted the same arithmetic of thirteen and four, and the states' independent convergence on gaps of three and four years is the kind of agreement that suggests a real boundary is being tracked rather than a fashion followed. The objections that survive are about completeness, not direction: the provisions should be elements rather than defences where the political will exists, they should reach the registry and not only the offence, and their boundaries should classify by age alone, as the Kansas court required, never by the sexes of the pair.

The name last. It is a mistranslation that tells a truth. It mistranslates the play, whose lovers are a child of thirteen and an older youth, whose marriage is a secret gamble, whose story ends in a tomb and whose own officiant predicted the ending in the ceremony. It tells the truth about the law's subjects, the pairs of near-contemporaries whom a blunt code would otherwise grade as predator and victim, and it tells that truth in the only idiom that has reliably moved legislatures to repair the grading. Keep the name, then, as the culture's shorthand and the advocate's instrument, and read it with the play open beside the statute book: the lovers for the sympathy, the texts for the facts, and the irony, thirteen in Verona against thirteen in the codes, as the standing reminder that a borrowed name is an argument, and arguments are owed scrutiny even when their conclusions are just.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is a Romeo and Juliet law?

A Romeo and Juliet law is a provision that treats sexual conduct between two young people close in age more leniently than the general age-of-consent offences would. The provisions share a nickname rather than a text. Depending on the state, the mechanism is an element of the offence, an affirmative defence, a reduced offence grade, or relief from sex offender registration after conviction. The shared conditions are a small age gap, usually three or four years, a youngest age for the protected band, usually thirteen or fourteen, and the absence of force or exploitation. The name is commentary's shorthand, borrowed from the play's lovers, for the judgment that peer relationships should not be graded as predation.

### Q: Is Romeo and Juliet law the official name of any statute?

No. Across the provisions examined in this article, the lovers' names appear in no statutory text, heading, or short title. Texas files its defence inside a section titled Sexual Assault, Florida's inside a section titled Removal of the Requirement to Register as a Sexual Offender or Sexual Predator in Special Circumstances, Indiana's inside Sexual Misconduct with a Minor, and Kansas called its offence Unlawful Voluntary Sexual Relations. The name lives in court opinions that call a provision "commonly referred to" by it, in legislative debate, in advocacy, and in reporting. A general legal reference states the position flatly: no state statute titles itself the Romeo and Juliet law.

### Q: How large an age gap do close-in-age exemptions usually allow?

Three or four years is the standard band. Texas allows an interval of not more than three years in both its sexual assault and indecency defences. Florida's registration relief, Indiana's defence, Georgia's misdemeanor grades, and the federal registry definition all use four years. Kansas required the offender to be less than nineteen and less than four years older than the younger person. Connecticut defines its offence by an interval of more than three years, which protects every pair inside three years. The arithmetic is strict: Florida courts measure the four years to the day, and a gap of four years and one day fails the test entirely.

### Q: How does an affirmative defence differ from registration relief?

An affirmative defence is raised at trial and, if proved, acquits: the defendant leaves without a conviction. Registration relief operates after conviction and changes only one consequence: the duty to register as a sex offender. Texas and Indiana use the defence model, so a successful young defendant has no conviction at all, though the defence must be proved after arrest and charge. Florida uses the relief model, so a successful petitioner keeps the conviction and the sentence and loses only the registry obligation, and even that requires a judge's discretionary order. The distance between the two models is the distance between never being convicted and being pardoned for one consequence.

### Q: What must a Texas defendant prove to use the Section 22.011 defence?

The defendant must prove the conditions of Penal Code section 22.011(e)(2). The actor was not more than three years older than the victim. The victim was a child of fourteen years of age or older. At the time of the offence the actor was not required to register for life as a sex offender and had no reportable conviction or adjudication for an offence under the same section. The victim was not a person the actor was barred from marrying under the bigamy statute or from sexual contact under the prohibited-conduct statute. An alternative ground exists where the actor was the spouse of the child. The defence is affirmative, so the burden of raising and supporting it rests on the defendant.

### Q: What does Florida's Section 943.04354 change for a person who qualifies?

It removes the requirement to register as a sexual offender or sexual predator, and nothing else. The conviction stands, the sentence stands, and the criminal record stands. As enacted in 2007, the section covered convictions under the sexual battery and lewd or lascivious statutes where registration rested solely on that violation, the person had no other such conviction, the victim was fourteen to seventeen, and the gap was not more than four years. Amendment effective October 1, 2014 widened the youngest age to thirteen and broadened the offence list. Relief comes by motion or petition, on notice to the state attorney, at a judge's discretion, and a denial cannot be refiled under the section.

### Q: What conditions must an Indiana defendant satisfy under subsection (e)?

Every condition in Indiana Code section 35-42-4-9(e) must hold at once. The person is not more than four years older than the victim, who is at least fourteen and under sixteen for the offence charged. The relationship between the pair was a dating relationship or an ongoing personal relationship, a term the statute defines to exclude a family relationship. The crime was not committed by a person of twenty-one or older, used or threatened no deadly force, involved no deadly weapon, caused no serious bodily injury, was not facilitated by secretly furnishing the victim a drug or controlled substance, and was not committed by a person in a position of authority or substantial influence over the victim.

### Q: What happened to Matthew Limon in Kansas?

Matthew Limon, eighteen years old in February 2000, took part in a voluntary sexual act with a boy of fourteen at the school both attended. The age gap was three years and one month, inside the Kansas Romeo and Juliet statute's limits, but the statute protected only opposite-sex pairs, so Limon was charged with criminal sodomy instead. He was sentenced to seventeen years and two months, against a maximum of fifteen months had the partner been a girl. After Lawrence v. Texas vacated the judgment once, the Kansas Supreme Court held the opposite-sex condition unconstitutional in 2005, struck those words from the statute, and Limon was released that November.

### Q: Why did the Kansas Supreme Court strike words from the statute in State v. Limon?

Because the opposite-sex condition failed equal protection review. The court, in a unanimous opinion by Justice Marla J. Luckert filed October 21, 2005, held that Lawrence v. Texas controlled the analysis and that the state had no rational basis for punishing the same voluntary conduct between teenagers far more severely when the pair were of the same sex. Rather than void the lesser offence and its leniency altogether, the court severed the words "and are members of the opposite sex" from K.S.A. 21-3522, extending the Romeo and Juliet statute to the pairs it had excluded. The remedy preserved the provision and removed its unconstitutional boundary.

### Q: What did Georgia change in 2006 after Genarlow Wilson's conviction?

Georgia regraded close-in-age molestation offences from felonies to misdemeanors. The 2006 amendment to section 16-6-4, Ga. L. 2006, p. 379, made aggravated child molestation a misdemeanor where the charge rested on sodomy, the victim was at least thirteen and under sixteen, and the person convicted was eighteen or younger and not more than four years older. A companion grade covered simple child molestation with a victim of fourteen or fifteen on the same age conditions, and an amendment to the registration statute relieved the misdemeanor class of registration. A misdemeanor carries a maximum of twelve months. The amendment was not made retroactive, which is why its author's most famous beneficiary stayed in prison.

### Q: Why could Georgia's Supreme Court free Wilson in 2007 when the 2006 amendment was not retroactive?

Because the court reviewed the sentence, not the statute, under the cruel and unusual punishment guarantee. In Humphrey v. Wilson, 282 Ga. 520, decided October 26, 2007 by four votes to three, the court treated the 2006 amendments as the clearest evidence of the community's evolving standard of decency, calling them "a seismic shift in the legislature's view of the gravity of oral sex between two willing teenage participants." A ten-year mandatory sentence for conduct the legislature had regraded as a twelve-month misdemeanor made, in the court's phrase, "no measurable contribution to acceptable goals of punishment." The habeas court was directed to discharge Wilson rather than resentence him.

### Q: How does Connecticut build the age gap into the offence itself?

Connecticut writes the interval into the definition of sexual assault in the second degree. Section 53a-71(a)(1) makes a person guilty only where the other person is thirteen or older and under sixteen "and the actor is more than three years older than such other person." The gap is an element the prosecution must prove, not a defence the accused must raise, so conduct inside a three-year interval is outside the offence from the start: no charge can be framed on it. The same architecture governs the neighbouring contact offence, with intervals of three years for victims of thirteen and fourteen and two years for children under thirteen. Protection arrives before the machinery of prosecution starts.

### Q: Do Romeo and Juliet laws lower the age of consent?

No. Every provision examined here leaves the state's age of consent exactly where the legislature set it, between sixteen and eighteen depending on the state. The provisions regulate the band of conduct near the line, between partners near each other in age, and they do so without declaring the younger party capable of consent in law. A qualifying pair in Texas is acquitted through a defence, not through a finding that the younger person consented. A Florida petitioner is relieved of registration while the offence remains an offence. The age of consent is the wall. The close-in-age provisions are doors cut into the wall for a defined class of pairs, and the doors do not move the wall.

### Q: What happens when the younger person lied about being older?

In most of these frameworks, the lie does not by itself protect the older partner. The Texas offence applies "regardless of whether the person knows the age of the child at the time of the offense," and the close-in-age defences measure the parties' actual ages, not their represented ones. A pair whose true ages fall inside the gap may still qualify for a defence or relief, since the arithmetic uses real birthdays. A pair outside the gap gains nothing from the misrepresentation under these provisions. Indiana separately provides a defence where the accused reasonably believed the child was at least sixteen, at subsection (c) of the same statute, but that belief defence is a distinct provision from the close-in-age defence at subsection (e).

### Q: Why did the United States Supreme Court quote Romeo and Juliet in a 2002 decision?

The play served as the Court's anchor for the cultural standing of adolescent love. In Ashcroft v. Free Speech Coalition, 535 U.S. 234 (2002), the Court struck down parts of a federal statute criminalizing images that appear to depict minors. Justice Kennedy's opinion observed that teenage sexual activity and the sexual abuse of children have both inspired countless literary works, called the play's pair "the most famous pair of teenage lovers, one of whom is just 13 years of age," quoted Capulet's line that she "hath not seen the change of fourteen years," and described the relationship as portrayed "as something splendid and innocent, but not juvenile." The citation supported protecting expression about the theme.

### Q: What does Joseph Pearce object to in the splendid and innocent reading of the play?

Pearce, a biographer of Shakespeare writing as a cultural critic, objects that the reading mistakes a cautionary tragedy for a celebration. In his account the play is written from the perspective of parental concern at the destructive consequences of naive adolescent passion, and the claim that it glorifies underage sexual activity is, in his word, absurd. The lovers' secrecy, haste, and deaths are the play's argument, and a reading that keeps the balcony and discards the tomb has edited the tragedy into an endorsement. The objection matters beyond literary criticism because the nickname for close-in-age laws imports the same edited version: the love story without the ending, cited as authority for a legal reform.

### Q: What does Franklin Zimring argue American law gets wrong about adolescent offenders?

Zimring argues that the law ignores development. In An American Travesty: Legal Responses to Adolescent Sexual Offending (2004), he contends that American policy judges adolescent sexual misconduct on the template built for adult predators, although the clinical, predictive, and moral significance of the conduct changes with the offender's age. Young offenders, measured by later arrest histories, resemble undifferentiated delinquents rather than incipient adult predators, so sanctions modeled on adult dangerousness are, in his governing phrase, both foolish and unjust. Close-in-age provisions are the piecemeal enactment of his correction: they build the offender's proximity in age to the other party into the legal response, where a developmentally blind code saw only the act.

### Q: Why does Catherine Carpenter treat registration as the deeper problem?

Carpenter argues that the registry, not the offence, is where the lasting damage is done and where the governing story is false. In works including "Against Juvenile Sex Offender Registration" (2014), she contends that registration regimes rest on a narrative of high recidivism that the evidence does not support, and that the narrative sweeps children and strict-liability offenders into public registration designed for dangerous adults. A close-in-age defence that ends at acquittal serves her critique. A relief provision like Florida's, which negotiates exits from the registry for a narrow class, confirms it: the sympathetic few are released by petition while the regime that registered them, and registers others one birthday outside the band, continues untouched.

### Q: Why do the provisions set their youngest ages at thirteen or fourteen?

Because the protected band needs a floor as much as a gap. The floor marks the age below which legislatures will not treat any sexual contact as a peer matter at all, whatever the interval: below it, protection of the child is absolute and no defence, regrading, or petition applies. Thirteen is the floor in the federal registry definition, in Florida's amended relief section, in Georgia's regraded aggravated offence, and in Connecticut's offence definition. Fourteen is the floor in Texas's sexual assault defence and Indiana's misconduct defence. The two numbers divide the map between states willing to extend the band to the youngest teenagers and states that hold the band's edge a year higher.

### Q: Should a person facing a charge rely on the nickname rather than the statute?

No. The nickname is a theme, and themes decide nothing in a courtroom. Outcomes turn on the provision a state actually enacted: its mechanism, its age floor, its gap measured in days, its record conditions, its deadlines, and whether relief is a right on proof of the facts or a judge's discretion. Genarlow Wilson's case turned on retroactivity. Matthew Limon's turned on a severed phrase. A Florida petitioner's turns on a federal-law finding and a discretionary order. Anyone facing a charge needs the text of the governing provision and a licensed attorney in that jurisdiction. The account given here is general information about a legal term and its history, not advice about any case.
