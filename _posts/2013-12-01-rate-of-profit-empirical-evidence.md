Ask a room of economists whether the rate of profit has fallen over the past half century and the room divides. Ask the same room to look at the national accounts of a single country, the United States say, and the division does not disappear; it sharpens. Two researchers of standing can work from the same published statistics, covering identical years and the same economy, and emerge with trend lines that slope in opposite directions across the decades after the early nineteen eighties. One reports a long decline that never reversed. The other reports a fall followed by a substantial recovery. Neither has made an arithmetic error. Neither has invented data. The difference between them was settled before either touched a spreadsheet, at the moment each decided how the capital stock in the denominator of the ratio would be valued. That decision, and the handful of decisions that surround it, are the real subject of the evidence on profitability, and they are the subject of this article.

![Evidence on the rate of profit, measuring the capital stock, valuation choices and profit series - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-85.webp)

The finding can be stated in one sentence, and the rest of this article exists to earn it. Whether the capital stock is valued at historical cost, meaning the prices actually paid for it when it was acquired, or at current replacement cost, meaning what it would cost to reproduce it at the prices of the year under study, determines the reported direction of the profit rate across the disputed decades more powerfully than any disagreement about events in the world. Series built the first way have generally shown a persistent long-run decline in the major economies. Series built the second way have generally shown decline into the early nineteen eighties and a partial recovery thereafter. The raw material beneath both families of series is largely shared. The conclusions are not. A reader who grasps that single point understands the empirical literature on profitability better than a reader who has memorised a dozen headline figures, because the reader who grasps it knows which question to ask of every figure quoted: on which side of the fork was this series built?

One claim organises everything that follows, and it deserves a name that can be carried through the argument. The claim is the valuation fork. At a definite point in the construction of any profit rate, the researcher must choose how to value the stock of capital against which profit is measured, and the path taken at that fork governs what the finished series can possibly say about the period since the early nineteen eighties. It follows that a profit rate cited without its valuation basis is not a piece of evidence at all; it is half a piece of evidence, the numerical half, with the interpretive half missing. The honest reporting standard for this entire field is therefore to state the convention before the number, a standard this article will apply to itself throughout: every finding reported below is reported as the finding of a named research tradition working under stated measurement choices, never as a bare fact about an economy.

Scope matters as much as method. This article is about the evidence and its construction: what has been measured, by whom, under which decisions, and with what degree of agreement. The theoretical mechanism that the measurements are meant to test, the tendency of the rate of profit to fall as the composition of capital rises, is treated in the companion article on the [tendency rate of profit to fall](/2013/10/15/tendency-rate-of-profit-to-fall/), and the formal controversy over the logic of that tendency is treated in the article on the [falling rate of profit debate](/2013/11/15/falling-rate-of-profit-debate/). Neither is re-argued here. What this article adds is the layer both of those discussions presuppose and rarely inspect: the workshop floor where series are actually built, the national accounts are reworked, and the conventions are chosen. Readers who finish it will not know whether the rate of profit has fallen in some ultimate sense. They will know something more usable: exactly which parts of that question the evidence has settled, which parts it has not, and why the unsettled parts resist settlement.

## The rate of profit as a ratio of two constructed quantities

A rate of profit is a fraction. Profit, or some measure of the surplus an economy generates, sits in the numerator; the capital advanced to generate it sits in the denominator; the quotient is expressed as a percentage. The simplicity of the form conceals the character of the exercise, because neither the top nor the bottom of the fraction is read off the world directly. Both are constructed, by statisticians first and by researchers second, and the construction involves decisions at every stage. National accounts do not contain a line labelled profit in the Marxian sense, still less a line labelled capital stock valued in the manner a given theory requires. They contain the outputs of an accounting framework built for other purposes, which the researcher must disassemble and rebuild before a single ratio can be computed. The published series that circulate in debate are the end products of that rebuilding, and their disagreements are located in the rebuilding, not in the underlying statistical material, which the rival camps mostly share.

Consider what this means for the status of the numbers. A temperature reading is, within instrument error, a property of the air. A profit rate is a property of a measurement procedure applied to an economy, and it varies as the procedure varies. This does not make it arbitrary: procedures can be defended, criticised, compared and improved, and some of the defences are strong. It does make the figure inseparable from its procedure in a way that temperature is not. When two series disagree, the first hypothesis to test is never that one researcher has falsified the world; it is that the two procedures differ, and the interesting work lies in locating the exact decision where they part. The empirical literature on profitability, read with this discipline, becomes unusually instructive, because its practitioners have been forced by the sharpness of their disagreement to document their procedures in detail. Few fields of economics lay their workshop so bare.

### Why does the same economy produce two different profit rates?

Because the accounts supply ingredients rather than a finished measure, and the recipe is chosen by the researcher. Profit must be defined before it can be counted, the stock must be valued before it can be entered, and the sector must be bounded before either can be assembled. Each choice is defensible, each changes the result, and the choices compound.

The compounding deserves emphasis, since casual readers often imagine a single disputed choice surrounded by agreed ground. In practice the researcher faces a sequence of decisions, and the decisions interact. A broad definition of profit paired with a narrowly bounded sector behaves differently from the same definition applied economy-wide. A valuation convention that matters little in a decade of stable prices matters enormously after a decade of inflation. The trend a series reports is a property of the whole chain of choices, which is why the literature cannot be summarised as a contest between two numbers. It is a contest between two families of construction, each internally coherent, each answering a slightly different question while claiming to answer the same one. Much of the confusion in popular discussion comes from treating the families as rival estimates of a single quantity, when they are closer to rival quantities sharing a name.

There is a further subtlety in the denominator that will recur throughout this article. The capital stock is not a flow of spending in a single year but an accumulated inheritance: machines, buildings, vehicles, software and inventories built up over decades, each vintage acquired at the prices of its own time. To state the stock as a single sum of money, the researcher must somehow make those vintages commensurable, and there are only two natural ways to do it. Add them at what they cost, and the stock is a sediment of historical prices. Revalue them at what their equivalents would cost now, and the stock is a statement about present reproduction. Both operations are performed on the same physical inheritance. They yield different sums, different movements over time, and, in an age that passed through a great inflation, dramatically different ratios when set against the profit of a single year. The fork is not a technicality bolted onto the side of the measurement. It sits at the centre of the denominator, which is why it dominates the outcome.

The numerator has its own layered construction. The accounts record various income aggregates: compensation of employees, taxes on production, consumption of fixed capital, and operating surplus in its several forms. To reach a profit figure the researcher decides which of these components belong, whether interest paid to lenders and rent paid to landowners count as shares of the surplus or as costs to be removed, whether the taxes of the corporate sector are deducted, and whether the profits of banks and insurers stand inside or outside the boundary. None of these decisions is dictated by the accounts themselves. Each has a theoretical rationale, and the rationales conflict. A researcher who regards interest as a portion of surplus value will build a numerator that a researcher who treats interest as a cost of production will not recognise, even before the denominator is reached. The numerator decisions move the level of the measured rate considerably and its trend somewhat; the denominator decision, as the coming sections show, can reverse the trend itself. That asymmetry is the empirical heart of the matter, and it is why this article gives the denominator the central place.

## What is agreed: the long postwar decline

Every survey of this literature, whatever the allegiance of its author, reports one finding with something close to consensus. Measured profit rates in the major developed economies fell, substantially and persistently, from the middle of the nineteen sixties through to the early years of the nineteen eighties. The decline appears in series built on historical cost and in series built on replacement cost. It appears for the United States and for the other large economies that have been studied in comparable depth. It appears for manufacturing and for broader sector definitions. Researchers who agree about almost nothing else in this field agree about that segment of the record, and the agreement is itself a datum worth pausing over, because it shows that the measurement problem, however severe, does not dissolve the entire subject into convention. Where the signal is strong enough, the conventions converge.

### Has the rate of profit actually fallen?

Measured rates in major developed economies fell from the mid-1960s to the early 1980s, which is broadly agreed. What happened afterwards is disputed, and the disagreement follows mainly from whether the capital stock is valued at historical cost or at current replacement cost.

That short answer is the whole article in miniature, and its two halves have different epistemic weights, a distinction the rest of this section and the next will develop. The first half reports a convergence of rival procedures on a single direction over a defined period. The second half reports a divergence, and locates its source. Readers who encounter the question in examination rooms or in journalism are usually offered one half or the other, depending on the commitments of the person answering. The settled segment is quoted by those who want the tendency confirmed; the disputed segment is quoted by those who want it refuted; and the methodological fact that joins the two halves, that the same procedures produce both, is quoted by almost nobody. Yet the joining fact is the one that disciplines both uses. It tells the first camp that their strongest evidence covers a bounded period, and it tells the second camp that the recovery they cite is a property of a convention they must be prepared to defend, not a deliverance of the data alone.

Why did the rival procedures converge over the postwar decades? Partly because the movement was large. When profitability falls across a long stretch that includes both the tight labour markets of the late nineteen sixties and the oil shocks and stagflation of the nineteen seventies, the fall registers through almost any reasonable accounting lens. The components moved together: wage shares rose in many economies during the earlier part of the period, productivity growth slowed, and the energy price shocks raised costs in ways no valuation convention could hide. A researcher would have had to select decisions perversely, at several points of the tree at once, to make that segment of the record point upward. Convergence under those conditions is what genuine robustness looks like, and the literature is right to treat the segment as established in the only sense empirical economics can establish anything: independent constructions, starting from shared accounts and diverging in method, deliver the same verdict.

The agreement also has a boundary that careful writers respect. It concerns measured rates, constructed from national accounts, for a set of large developed economies, over a period for which the accounts are comparatively rich and stable in definition. It does not extend backward into the sparse statistics of the nineteenth century, where reconstruction is heroic and contestable, and it does not extend sideways to economies whose statistical systems differ sharply in coverage. Nor does the agreement, by itself, adjudicate the theory the measurement was designed to test. A fall in the measured rate across those decades is consistent with the Marxian mechanism of a rising composition of capital, and it is also consistent with rival explanations centred on wage pressure, on the terms of trade, or on the specific shocks of the period. The empirical article and the theoretical article divide that labour between them: here the fall is documented and its robustness explained; there, the competing accounts of what drove it are weighed. Keeping the two apart is not pedantry. It is the difference between knowing a thing happened and claiming to know why.

One more feature of the agreed segment deserves attention, because it returns later as a diagnostic tool. The decline was steep enough, and long enough, to survive not only the valuation fork but also the other major decisions on the tree: tax treatment, sector scope, the handling of financial profits. Later sections will show each of those decisions moving a series by amounts that matter for the disputed decades. Against the postwar decline they move the depth of the fall, its exact dating, its distribution across countries, but not its direction. This gives the reader a calibration standard. A measurement choice can be assessed by asking how large its effects are relative to that benchmark: choices whose effects are small beside the postwar movement may be argued on convenience, while choices whose effects rival it, as the valuation choice does for the later period, carry the full weight of the conclusion and must be argued on principle.

## What is disputed: the decades after the early nineteen eighties

Beyond the trough of the early nineteen eighties, the families of series separate. On replacement-cost constructions, the typical finding in the literature is a recovery: profitability climbs back from its trough through the nineteen eighties and nineteen nineties, regaining a substantial part of the ground lost since the nineteen sixties, though the completeness of the recovery and its behaviour around the financial crisis of the late two thousands are themselves debated within that camp. On historical-cost constructions, the typical finding is that no comparable recovery occurred: the rate continues its long descent, or at best moves sideways at a depressed level, so that the whole era from the mid nineteen sixties onward reads as one extended decline punctuated by fluctuations. These are not two shadings of a single story. They are different stories about the character of modern capitalism, and they support different theoretical and political conclusions, which is precisely why the choice between the underlying conventions cannot be treated as a matter of taste.

### Where do the two trend lines part company?

They part at the trough of the early nineteen eighties, and the separation widens with distance. Through the decline the families descend together, the replacement-cost series falling further because inflation swells its denominator. After the trough, disinflation reverses the pressure: that denominator stops growing fast and its series turns upward, while the historical-cost series keeps descending.

The mechanics behind that parting are worth dwelling on, because they show the fork operating as a process rather than a slogan. During the great inflation of the nineteen seventies, the prices of capital goods rose rapidly. A stock valued at replacement cost is revalued upward each year in step with those prices, so its measured size grows even when the physical stock grows slowly, and the profit rate computed against it is pressed downward by the denominator alone. A stock valued at historical cost, by contrast, still carries old equipment at the low prices of calmer years, so its measured size lags behind. When inflation then subsides in the nineteen eighties, the revaluation pressure fades; the replacement-cost denominator grows more slowly, profits recover in the numerator, and the ratio climbs. The historical-cost stock, meanwhile, gradually fills with equipment bought at the high prices of the inflation years and after, so its denominator keeps catching up with reality and its ratio stays low. One physical economy, one set of accounts, two arithmetics of inheritance, two histories. Any reader who understands this paragraph understands why the argument between the camps is really an argument about which arithmetic corresponds to the economic quantity the theory cares about, a question the data alone cannot answer because both arithmetics are internally correct.

It is worth recording what the dispute is not. It is not a dispute about whether the underlying statistical agencies made errors, nor about the honesty of either camp, a point the conventions of this series require to be stated plainly: the leading figures on both sides are serious researchers who publish their procedures, defend them in print, and acknowledge the opposing construction even while rejecting its interpretation. Nor is the dispute a proxy war that maps neatly onto the political spectrum within Marxian economics, since the valuation question cuts across other allegiances and has divided researchers who stand together on many other questions. Treating the disagreement as a scandal, or as a sign that one side must be cooking its figures, is the characteristic error of outside commentary on this literature. The correct description is duller and more interesting: a genuinely hard measurement problem, arising from the fact that the theoretical concept, capital advanced, has two plausible empirical counterparts, and the counterparts diverged historically because the world passed through an inflationary episode that drove a wedge between them.

The stakes of the choice extend beyond the trend line itself. If profitability recovered substantially after the early nineteen eighties, then accounts of the subsequent era that rest on a profitability crisis lose their foundation, and the era must be explained by other means: by financialisation as an autonomous development, by globalisation and the relocation of production, by changes in labour market institutions, or by some combination. If profitability did not recover, the same era reads as a long defensive operation by capital against a depressed rate of return: wage restraint, intensified work, the opening of new labour reserves abroad, and the turn to financial channels all appear as counteracting influences in the classical sense, responses to a pressure that never lifted. The empirical series are load-bearing in both architectures, which is why their construction deserves the scrutiny this article gives it. A conclusion about the whole character of an era can rest, at the end of the chain, on how a statistician in a national accounts office and a researcher at a desk each decided to price a ten-year-old machine tool. That is not a reductio of the enterprise. It is a reason to do the enterprise carefully.

## The numerator: deciding what counts as profit

Before any question of valuation arises, the researcher must say what profit is for the purposes of the series, and the national accounts refuse to answer the question on the researcher's behalf. The accounts of a modern economy record the income generated in production under headings designed for macroeconomic management: the pay of employees, the taxes levied on production and imports, the consumption of fixed capital as plant wears out, and the operating surplus that remains. A Marxian profit measure must be assembled from these and related pieces, and the assembly instructions are contested. Is the relevant magnitude the surplus before any distribution to lenders, landlords and the state, on the ground that interest, rent and tax are all carve-ups of a surplus produced elsewhere? Or is it the profit retained by enterprises after those obligations, on the ground that what matters for accumulation is what firms actually command? The two numerators differ enormously in size, and they can differ in movement, because the shares going to interest and to the state have their own histories.

### What happens to the series when tax is taken out?

A post-tax numerator removes one of the largest and most variable distributions of the surplus, so the series records what enterprises kept rather than what production generated. Where corporate tax burdens fell across the disputed decades, as they did in several major economies, the post-tax series rises relative to the pre-tax one and any apparent recovery strengthens.

The choice ramifies further. Interest payments swelled as a share of corporate outgoings during the high-interest years around the turn of the nineteen eighties and then ebbed; a numerator measured before interest therefore follows a different path from one measured after, even with everything else held fixed. Rent is smaller but not negligible, and in economies with large commercial property sectors it grows in importance. Some researchers add back the income of unincorporated businesses, or a portion of it, reasoning that the earnings of the self-employed mix a wage element with a profit element that the accounts do not separate. Others restrict the numerator to the corporate sector on the ground that only there does capital confront labour in the required form. Each of these decisions has been defended in print, usually with care, and each shifts both the height of the measured rate and, in the sensitive decades, the steepness of its climb or fall. A reader who sees two series diverge should therefore interrogate the numerator first for level differences, and only then turn to the denominator for the trend difference that the fork produces.

The numerator has a second, quieter difficulty: depreciation. The accounts value the wearing out of capital, the consumption of fixed capital, according to schedules and service lives set by statistical convention. Profit measured after deducting that consumption is the standard net concept, but the deduction is itself an estimate resting on assumed lifetimes and retirement patterns for each class of asset. Lengthen the assumed life of a class of equipment and annual depreciation falls, measured profit rises, and the rate rises with it, without any machine in the world behaving differently. Statistical agencies revise these assumptions as evidence accumulates, and revisions rewrite history: the profit of a decade ago changes in the newest vintage of the accounts. Researchers who built series on successive vintages discovered that part of what they had been debating was the revision policy of the agencies. This is one more reason the field's reporting discipline matters: a series is a statement about a vintage of the accounts, processed under stated rules, and it dates as surely as the data do.

A third numerator problem concerns whose profit it is. Modern corporations earn income across borders, and the accounts of a country capture some mix of what its residents earn abroad and what foreigners earn within it. Transfer pricing within multinational firms moves declared profit between jurisdictions for tax reasons, shifting the numerators of national series in ways that have nothing to do with production in either place. For the mid-century decades that dominate the agreed segment, these distortions were modest beside the signal. For the disputed decades, in which multinational production and financial globalisation expanded rapidly, they are a further source of divergence between series built at different levels of consolidation, and one reason cross-country comparisons, treated in a later section, are more fragile still.

None of this makes the numerator a free choice. The decisions are constrained by the question being asked. A study of the driving force of accumulation may reasonably prefer the surplus before distribution, since the whole surplus is what the system generates and struggles over. A study of the incentive facing the individual firm may reasonably prefer what the firm retains. The error is not in choosing; the error is in quoting the result without the choice attached, or in comparing a pre-tax series from one author with a post-tax series from another as though they were rival readings of one dial. The literature at its best is meticulous here, and its tables of definitions, often skipped by readers in a hurry, are where the real argument starts.

## The denominator: two ways of valuing an inheritance

The capital stock confronts the researcher with a problem no amount of care can dissolve, because the stock is a physical inheritance of different vintages that must nevertheless be expressed as one sum of money. A factory built in one decade, a fleet of machines installed in another, a warehouse, a software system, a stock of inventories: to add them is already to have chosen a common measure, and the candidates for that measure embody different ideas about what capital is. Historical cost adds the items at the sums actually spent on them. The resulting stock is a faithful record of past advances, a sediment of the money capital actually committed, layer upon layer, each preserved at the price of its year. Current cost, or replacement cost, revalues every item at what an equivalent asset would cost in the year of measurement. The resulting stock is a statement about the present scale of the productive apparatus, expressed in present prices, as though the whole inheritance were to be reproduced at once.

### What exactly sits in the denominator of the ratio?

A sum of money representing the accumulated stock of produced assets, built up by cumulating past investment and deducting retirements and depreciation under a perpetual inventory method. The stock may be entered gross or net of accumulated depreciation, may include or exclude inventories and residential structures, and must be priced somehow, which is where the fork enters.

Statistical agencies construct the stock by the perpetual inventory method: investment flows of each past year are cumulated, assets are retired according to assumed service lives, and depreciation is deducted according to assumed profiles. The method is standard and, within its conventions, well executed. But the agencies publish the stock on a replacement-cost basis, because that serves the purposes of national accounting, where consistency with current price output measures is what matters. A researcher who wants the historical-cost stock must therefore rebuild it, revaluing the cumulated investment by the prices of the years in which it occurred, a laborious reconstruction that the historical-cost camp has undertaken with considerable ingenuity. The asymmetry of effort matters for the sociology of the field: the replacement-cost series can be built close to the published accounts, while the historical-cost series requires an act of reconstruction that only a committed researcher will perform, and the reconstruction itself involves subsidiary judgements that critics can and do contest.

### How does inflation drive the two valuations apart?

Inflation reprices the inheritance continuously. Under replacement cost, a year of rising capital-goods prices enlarges the measured stock even if nothing new is installed, pressing the profit rate down mechanically. Under historical cost, the old layers stay at their old prices, so the stock rises only as new investment arrives at the new prices, which may take a decade.

The wedge that inflation drives between the two stocks is the single most important material fact in this literature, because the postwar era contains one of the great inflations of the modern period. Between the mid nineteen sixties and the early nineteen eighties the price level in the major economies rose severalfold, and the prices of capital goods rose with it. At the start of that episode the two stocks stood reasonably close, since recent prices dominated both. By its end they stood far apart: the replacement-cost stock had been marked up repeatedly, while the historical stock still carried, in its depths, plant installed at the prices of a cheaper world. The measured rates therefore diverged in level throughout the inflation and, more consequentially, in behaviour when the inflation ended. Everything in the disputed segment follows from the unwinding of that wedge during the disinflation that followed, as the earlier section described. A reader who wants one image to hold onto should keep this one: two ledgers recording the same factory, one in the prices of its construction years, one repriced every year, agreeing while prices are calm and telling different stories ever after a storm.

There are subsidiary decisions inside the denominator, each with its own literature. Whether to include inventories, the stocks of materials and unsold goods that Marx counted as circulating capital, changes the size of the denominator and, because inventory behaviour is cyclical, the short-run movement of the series. Whether to include residential structures, usually excluded on the ground that housing is not capital in the productive sense, shifts the whole-economy measures. Whether land enters, usually excluded because it is not produced, raises conceptual questions some researchers answer differently. Whether the stock is taken gross, before deducting accumulated depreciation, or net, after it, interacts with the depreciation assumptions already met in the numerator. Working through the decision tree in the next sections means meeting each of these in turn, but none of them rivals the fork. They adjust the message. The fork, for the disputed decades, writes it.

The defenders of each convention argue from the theory the measurement serves, and both arguments deserve to be heard at full strength before any verdict is attempted. The case for historical cost begins from the concept of capital advanced: what the capitalist actually laid out, the money sum whose expansion the circuit of capital describes. On this view, the rate of profit in Marx's texts is the ratio of surplus value to the value advanced, and the value advanced is an historical magnitude, fixed at the moment of advance; repricing the advance at present-day replacement prices substitutes a different concept, the current scale of operations, and answers a question about reproduction rather than about the return on sums committed. Its defenders add a sharper charge: replacement-cost valuation imports the price changes of capital goods into the denominator, so that a cheapening of equipment, which in the theory is one of the counteracting influences that raise profitability, shows up in the replacement-cost accounts partly as a shrinkage of the measured stock that masks the very effect under study. The case for current cost begins from comparability and economic meaning: a sum that adds pounds or dollars of different decades treats unlike units as like, and in an inflationary age the historical stock becomes a monetary fiction whose movements reflect the age structure of the stock as much as its scale. On this view, the economically meaningful question is what return the existing apparatus, valued consistently with the current prices at which its output is sold, yields in the present; a numerator in current prices divided by a denominator in the prices of dead decades mixes two monetary languages in one ratio. Its defenders add that firms themselves, deciding whether to expand or exit, look to replacement costs and current returns, so the current-cost rate tracks the signal to which accumulation actually responds. Both cases are serious. Both will be met again, attached to the researchers who press them, in the section on the fork itself.

## The valuation fork, with the researchers attached

Abstract conventions acquire force when they are attached to the people who defend them, and in this literature the alignment between convention and conclusion is close enough to be the first thing a reader should learn. The most prominent defence of historical-cost measurement in the Marxian literature is associated with Andrew Kliman, whose book-length study of the United States economy reconstructed the capital stock at the prices actually paid and reported, on that basis, that the rate of profit had been in persistent decline since the postwar years, with no sustained recovery after the early nineteen eighties. Kliman's argument is not merely technical. It holds that replacement-cost measurement builds a bias into the series, because the revaluation of the stock with rising prices, and its slower growth when inflation subsides, manufactures first an exaggerated fall and then an illusory recovery; strip the revaluation away, on his account, and the underlying trajectory is one long descent that the crisis of the late two thousands interrupted but did not create. The title of his best known work announces the thesis plainly: the failure he examines is, in his reading, a failure of profitability that was never repaired.

### Who defends historic cost, and on what grounds?

Its best known defender in this literature argues that the theory concerns the return on capital actually advanced, and that the advance is an historical sum. Repricing the stock at replacement values, on this argument, lets price movements impersonate changes in profitability and manufactures a recovery out of disinflation alone.

On the other side stand Gérard Duménil and Dominique Lévy, whose long-running programme of research on the United States and France, built on the official accounts and therefore on replacement-cost valuation of the stock, reports the pattern described earlier in this article: a deep fall in profitability from the postwar level to a trough in the early nineteen eighties, followed by a recovery that, in their reading, restored a large part of the lost ground by the years before the financial crisis. Their interpretation is woven into a wider account of the era, in which the recovery of profitability is one element of a new social order favourable to capital, achieved through wage discipline, financial expansion and global restructuring. The measurement and the history illuminate each other in their work, which is part of its influence: a reader is not asked to accept a trend line but a trend line embedded in a narrative of class relations that gives the line meaning. Critics reply that the narrative inherits the convention, and that a recovery visible only through replacement-cost spectacles is a questionable foundation for so large a structure. The reply from that camp is equally structural: profitability as business experiences it, and as it guides investment, is a current magnitude, and a measure that cannot see the recovery also cannot see the investment behaviour that followed from it.

### Who defends current cost, and on what grounds?

The researchers most associated with the replacement-cost reading argue that numerator and denominator must speak the same monetary language: profit arrives in the prices of the year, so the stock must be priced in the prices of the year as well. Adding sums spent across decades of inflation, on this argument, produces a denominator with no coherent economic meaning.

Between and around these poles stand other major figures whose work complicates any simple two-camp picture. Anwar Shaikh, whose reconstructions of United States national accounts, undertaken with Ertugrul Tonak, remain a standard reference for converting official categories into Marxian ones, has generally worked with measures closer to the official valuation while subjecting the accounts to extensive adjustment, and his reported findings on the rate of profit describe a fall followed by a partial and troubled recovery, with emphasis on the difference between the ordinary rate and the rate corrected for capacity utilisation. Deepankar Basu, working with econometric methods on United States data, has reported evidence consistent with a long-run downward pressure on profitability while stressing the sensitivity of the result to specification, a caution that recurs in his writing on the subject. The presence of such intermediate and methodologically varied positions matters for the reader's judgement: the field is not two armies but a spectrum, and the spectrum is ordered, roughly but visibly, by proximity to the fork. The closer a construction stands to historical cost, the gloomier its verdict on the disputed decades; the closer to replacement cost, the stronger the recovery it reports. That ordering is the valuation fork stated as a fact about the literature itself.

It should be recorded, in fairness to all parties, that each camp has answered the other in detail and at length, in journal exchanges and in books, and that the exchanges have improved the constructions on both sides. Historical-cost researchers have refined their rebuilding of the stock and confronted the objection that their sums mix monetary units of different years. Replacement-cost researchers have confronted the objection that revaluation gains and losses contaminate their denominator, and some present their series alongside variants that strip out particular effects. The debate is, by the standards of empirical economics, unusually transparent, because the stakes attached to the trend line forced every participant to show the working. A reader who follows even one exchange from end to end will learn more about how economic measurement actually proceeds than from many textbooks, and will never again read a chart of the profit rate, from any source, without asking the fork question first.

## The measurement decision tree

The decisions surveyed so far, and several still to come, can be drawn as a single artifact. The measurement decision tree sets out each choice a researcher must make, the options at that choice, what each option does to the measured trend, who in the literature takes which branch, and the argument offered for taking it. It is the findable artifact of this article, and it is designed to be used actively: take any published series, walk it down the tree, and at each row identify the branch its author took. A series that cannot be walked down the tree, because its author has not disclosed the branches, is a series whose conclusions cannot be assessed, however impressive its charts. The tree converts the vague counsel to check the methodology into a checklist that takes minutes.

The middle rows modulate: they decide how strong a recovery appears, how deep a trough, how a crisis registers. The lower rows matter most for level comparisons between studies and for the finer features of timing. A reader equipped with the tree can also see why two honest series can differ without either being wrong in its own terms, and why the demand that the literature produce a single agreed number is a demand that the tree be collapsed by decree. The disagreements that remain after the tree has been applied are the real disagreements, and they are fewer and more interesting than the raw clash of headlines suggests.

One further use of the tree deserves mention. Examination candidates and journalists, the two groups most often required to state a conclusion about profitability in a sentence, can use the rows as a disclosure checklist for their own writing. A sentence that names the valuation basis, the sector and the profit definition has identified its series fully enough for a reader to place it; a sentence that names none of these has quoted a number in the dark. The persona sections at the end of this article return to that discipline from the standpoint of each audience.

## Sector choices and the boundaries of the economy being measured

Every profit rate is the profit rate of something, and the something must be bounded before the ratio can be built. The broadest boundary is the whole economy, which sweeps in government, households as landlords, and the non-profit sector alongside business, and requires the researcher to decide how much of the income recorded in those corners counts as profit at all. The corporate sector narrows the field to incorporated business, where the confrontation between capital and wage labour takes its most legible form and where the accounts are richest. The non-financial corporate sector narrows it further, setting aside banks, insurers and other financial firms whose profits raise the classification problems of the next section but one. Manufacturing alone, the traditional heartland of the theory and the best documented sector in the older accounts, offers the longest and cleanest series at the cost of describing a shrinking share of modern economies.

The choices are not interchangeable descriptions of one object; they are measurements of different objects that happen to share a name. Manufacturing profitability in the United States, on most constructions, fell harder and recovered differently from the profitability of the whole corporate sector, because manufacturing faced the direct force of international competition and the relocation of production, while services and finance followed other rhythms. A study of the tendency as Marx stated it may defend the manufacturing focus: the theory concerns the sphere of production, and manufacturing is where mechanisation, the engine of the rising composition of capital, is most visible. A study of the economy's overall return to capital will defend the broad boundary with equal justice. Disputes in the secondary literature frequently reduce, on inspection, to one author quoting a manufacturing series against another's economy-wide series, each unaware that the boundary, as much as the world, separates them.

The boundary problem has sharpened with time, because the structure of the economies under study has shifted beneath the categories. Sectors that the mid-century accounts treated as peripheral, above all finance, grew to command a large share of recorded profit during the disputed decades, so a boundary decision that barely mattered for the agreed segment can carry the conclusion for the later one. Outsourcing redrew boundaries from the other side: activities once performed inside manufacturing firms, cleaning, catering, logistics, design, computing, migrated to service firms, moving their workers and their margins across the statistical frontier without any corresponding change in the physical process of production. Measures built on fixed sector definitions across sixty years are therefore measuring a moving object with a stationary ruler. The careful researchers in this field acknowledge the drift and discuss its direction; the careless citation of their series, stripped of the discussion, presents a precision the originals never claimed. Here as everywhere in this article, the remedy is not despair but disclosure: name the boundary when the number is named.

## The productive and unproductive labour adjustment

A researcher who takes the theory's categories seriously faces a further reconstruction, one that reproduces inside the profit-rate literature a classification problem this series has already met in a neighbouring context. Marxian theory distinguishes labour that produces surplus value from labour that is paid out of it: the activities of circulation, selling, advertising, much of finance, and the supervisory apparatus are, on the classical account, necessary to the system but unproductive in the specific sense that they do not add to the surplus they help to realise or guard. The national accounts know nothing of this distinction. They record the wages of a machine operator and the wages of an advertising copywriter in the same column, and the profits of a manufacturer and the margins of a trading firm under the same heading. To build a profit rate in the theory's own terms, the researcher must reclassify the accounts activity by activity, moving whole industries and occupations across the line, and recompute both the surplus and the stock on the far side of the move.

The standard instrument for this reconstruction in the United States case is the work of Shaikh and Tonak, whose mapping from official categories to Marxian ones was built with unusual explicitness and has been adopted, adapted and criticised ever since. The adjustment matters to the trend and not merely to the level, because the unproductive share of employment and of costs grew substantially across the postwar decades in the major economies. A series that nets those costs out of the surplus will therefore show a deeper squeeze on the productive core than an unadjusted series shows for the economy at large, and the two can part company over exactly the decades in dispute. Researchers who decline the adjustment are not simply being lazy; some argue that the classification, defensible in principle, cannot be executed on published accounts without a chain of imputations so long that the result says as much about the imputer as about the economy, and others hold that the economy-wide return, unadjusted, is the magnitude that governs accumulation in any case. The parallel with the companion article on [measuring rate of exploitation](/2011/03/01/measuring-rate-of-exploitation/) is exact, and it is not a coincidence: the same boundary, between producing a surplus and living from it, must be drawn before either ratio can be computed, and every difficulty met there returns here wearing the denominator as an additional complication.

For the reader's purposes, the practical consequence is a further item for the disclosure checklist. Two series may share a valuation basis, a sector and a tax treatment, and still diverge because one has been rebuilt on productive activities alone while the other follows the accounts as published. When a study reports that the rate of profit in the productive sector stands far below the headline corporate rate, no contradiction with the headline series need be involved; a different economy, a theoretical subset of the visible one, is being measured. Whether that subset is the right object of the theory is a question for the theory's interpreters. That it is a different object is a matter of arithmetic.

## Financial profits and the sector that changed shape

No boundary decision has grown in consequence as steadily as the treatment of finance. In the decades of the agreed decline, the financial sector of the major economies was a modest claimant on total profit, and whether its earnings stood inside or outside the numerator moved the measured rate only slightly. Across the disputed decades the sector expanded dramatically, in employment, in value added and above all in its share of corporate profit, so that the same decision now moves the series by amounts that rival the movements under dispute. A measure that includes financial profits records the financialisation of the economy as, in part, a recovery of profitability. A measure that excludes them records the same decades as a period in which the productive core earned a low return while claims upon its surplus multiplied in the financial sphere. Both descriptions are arithmetically faithful to their definitions. They support sharply different readings of the era, and the difference is a choice, made at a desk, about what the word profit in the numerator shall cover.

### What changes when finance is left inside the measure?

The numerator absorbs the earnings of banks, insurers and securities firms, whose share of corporate profit grew greatly across the disputed decades. The measured rate then registers financial expansion as improved profitability, and any recovery in the series is partly a record of that sector's growth rather than of returns in production.

The theoretical arguments mirror those met at the sector boundary, sharpened by the character of financial earnings themselves. One tradition holds that financial profit is a deduction from a surplus produced elsewhere, a fee for circulation and risk-bearing that the productive economy pays, so that counting it as profit of the system double counts: the surplus appears once where it is produced and again where it is skimmed. On that view the correct system-wide numerator excludes financial earnings, or nets them against the interest the productive sector pays. The opposing tradition answers that the distinction, however venerable, cannot survive contact with the modern firm, whose treasury operations, captive finance arms and intellectual property holdings blur any line the statistician might draw, and that a measure excluding finance increasingly describes an economy that no longer exists. A middle practice has emerged in the better literature: publish the series both ways, with finance in and finance out, and let the reader see how much of the conclusion the decision carries. It is a practice this article commends as a model for the whole tree, since a conclusion that survives both branches of a decision is worth more than a conclusion that depends on one, and a conclusion that does not survive is thereby honestly labelled as conditional.

The financial question also governs how the crisis at the end of the disputed period appears in the data. Series including finance register the crash of the late two thousands as a collapse in the numerator itself, as bank earnings turned to losses, while series excluding finance register the same crisis mainly through its aftermath in the productive economy. The applied use of these series in explaining that crash is the business of the companion article on [marxism and the financial crash](/2013/12/15/marxism-and-the-financial-crash/), which draws on the evidence surveyed here; the point for present purposes is that even the shape of the crisis in the data is partly a function of decisions taken long before the crisis arrived.

## Tax, depreciation and capacity: the quieter decisions

Below the fork and the boundary sit decisions that rarely make headlines and never stop mattering. Tax has already appeared in the numerator, but its denominator shadow deserves a word: some constructions measure the rate on the stock net of tax provisions and deferred liabilities, others gross, and in periods when corporate tax law changed rapidly the difference shows in the trend. The general lesson of the tax rows is asymmetry of attention. Debates fasten on the valuation fork because it reverses conclusions, while tax treatments that shift a series by a fifth as much pass unexamined in the citing literature, so that two series described as using the same valuation may still not be the same series.

Depreciation, met earlier as a deduction in the numerator, returns in the denominator wearing a second hat. A net capital stock is what remains after accumulated depreciation has been written off, and the write-off follows the agencies' assumed service lives and decay profiles for each asset class. Those assumptions have a history: the shift toward shorter-lived assets, above all computing equipment and software, across the disputed decades changed the composition of investment toward assets that depreciate quickly, and measures differ in how promptly that shift is allowed to shrink the net stock and swell the annual deduction. A researcher using gross stock, before any deduction, sidesteps the assumptions and accepts a denominator swollen by assets long retired in economic substance if not in the ledger. Neither escape is costless, and the better studies say which cost they have chosen to pay.

Capacity utilisation introduces a different kind of adjustment, aimed not at the accounts but at the cycle. The measured rate falls in every slump partly because plant stands idle: the denominator is unchanged while the numerator collapses, and a reader of the raw series sees the tendency and the cycle fused together. Researchers in Shaikh's tradition therefore distinguish the ordinary rate from a measure adjusted toward the rate that would obtain at normal or full utilisation of capacity, arguing that the tendency the theory predicts should be sought in the corrected series, where cyclical idle capacity has been ironed out, rather than in the raw one. Others resist the correction as one imputation too many, or note that persistently low utilisation may itself be a symptom of the condition under study, so that correcting for it assumes away part of the phenomenon. The dispute is instructive because it is the numerator and denominator problem in miniature: an adjustment that clarifies on one theory of the data and distorts on another, with no tribunal above the theories to settle it.

## Going worldwide: cross-country and world measures

If national measurement is a workshop of contested decisions, the construction of a rate of profit for the world economy is that workshop multiplied by every statistical system on earth. Researchers have nevertheless attempted it, driven by a real consideration: the theory's tendency is stated for capital in general, capital migrates across borders, and a national recovery purchased by relocating production to lower-wage economies might register as health in one country's series while the world rate continued to fall. World-level studies in this literature have generally reported, on the constructions their authors defend, a long decline in the world rate across the postwar era with fluctuations around it, and those reports circulate widely in movement literature. They should be read with a firmer grip on the decision tree than any national series, because every national difficulty is compounded in the aggregate, joined by new ones that belong to aggregation itself.

### Can a world rate be more reliable than a national one?

No, and the appearance that it might be rests on a confusion between coverage and accuracy. A world aggregate inherits every national measurement decision, adds exchange-rate conversion and the patching of incompatible statistical systems, and buries the divergences under a single line. Its breadth is real; its precision is borrowed.

The compounding works through several channels. National accounts differ in their treatment of the informal economy, in the boundary of the government sector, in depreciation conventions and in the vintage of their methods, so the inputs to a world series are not commensurable in the way the inputs to a national series, for all their faults, roughly are. Conversion into a common currency forces a choice between market exchange rates, which swing with financial conditions and can move the world rate without any factory changing hands, and purchasing-power parities, which are built for comparing living standards rather than capital stocks. The large economies dominate any aggregate by weight, so the world series largely restates the experience of a few countries while wearing the authority of the planet. And the coverage of the earlier postwar decades is thin for much of the world, so the long comparisons that give world series their rhetorical force rest on the weakest data at exactly the end where the comparison begins. None of this makes the attempts worthless; the researchers who build them are generally candid about the scaffolding. It makes the citing practice around them the worst in the field, since the world figure, being the largest in scope, is quoted with the least qualification. The rule for readers inverts the rhetoric: the wider the territory a series claims, the longer the list of conventions that must travel with its number.

Cross-country comparison among the developed economies stands on firmer ground, and the literature comparing the United States, the large European economies and Japan has been valuable precisely because it works with a small set of comparatively compatible statistical systems and can discuss their differences explicitly. Even there, depreciation conventions, sector boundaries and the treatment of self-employment differ enough that the careful studies harmonise what they can and flag what they cannot. The broad finding that the postwar decline appeared across the major economies, rather than being an artefact of one country's accounts, owes its credibility to that harmonisation work, and it remains the strongest single piece of evidence that the agreed segment records something about the world and not merely about a statistical office.

## The data sources themselves

Every series in this literature descends from a handful of statistical sources, and a reader who knows them can place any study quickly. For the United States, the foundation is the national income and product accounts maintained by the Bureau of Economic Analysis, together with that bureau's fixed assets statistics, which supply the investment flows and the replacement-cost stock estimates on which the perpetual inventory constructions rest. Corporate profits by industry, capital consumption allowances, and the reconciliation items between book and tax concepts all live in those tables, and nearly every American study in the field is, at one remove, a reorganisation of them. For the European economies, researchers draw on national statistical offices and on harmonised collections: the AMECO database of the European Commission, the OECD's statistical programmes, and the EU KLEMS project, which was built expressly to supply comparable industry-level data on capital, labour and output across countries and has become a standard source for cross-country profitability work. At world level, the Penn World Table supplies the long international panels that aggregators adapt.

Two features of these sources shape everything built on them. The first is revision. National accounts are not carved in stone; they are re-estimated as better source data arrive, re-based as price indices are reweighted, and periodically redefined in comprehensive revisions that can rewrite the statistical past, moving profits between sectors, reclassifying activities, and altering the capital stock estimates for decades already studied. A series published from the vintage of the accounts available in one year is therefore not strictly comparable with a series published from a later vintage, even by the same author under the same rules, and part of the movement attributed in debate to the world belongs, on inspection, to the statisticians. The mature literature handles this by stating the vintage used; the citing literature almost never does, which is one more reason headline figures from different decades of scholarship cannot be placed side by side without caution.

The second feature is purpose. The accounts were designed to manage a modern economy: to measure output, income and spending consistently for macroeconomic policy. Their concepts answer the questions of that design. Profit in the accounts is what remains to corporations after the costs the framework recognises; capital is what serves the framework's need for a consistent stock at current prices. Nothing in the design is wrong, but nothing in it was arranged for the questions Marxian researchers bring, which is why the reconstruction work described throughout this article is unavoidable and why its results bear the marks of the reconstructor. The same dependence on borrowed instruments appears across empirical economics, in fields far from this one, and it is not a scandal. It is a standing condition of the discipline, which this literature, to its credit, discusses more openly than most.

## How a series is actually built

Abstractions about decisions become clearer when the construction is followed once, slowly, from raw tables to finished line. Imagine a researcher setting out to measure the rate of profit for the United States corporate sector across the postwar era, working from the published accounts, and watch where the branches of the tree present themselves. The work begins with the numerator. From the accounts she takes corporate profits, and immediately faces the inventory valuation and capital consumption adjustments by which the statisticians convert business book figures into economic concepts; whether to accept those adjustments as they stand is her first decision, and most accept them, noting the choice. She must then decide whether the interest paid out by corporations, recorded elsewhere in the accounts, belongs in her profit concept, and whether to work before or after the taxes on corporate income that the tables report. Already her numerator is a position, not a datum.

The denominator takes longer. The published fixed asset tables give her the stock at replacement cost directly, built by the agency's perpetual inventory. If replacement cost is her branch, her remaining work is alignment: matching the stock's coverage to her sector, choosing net or gross, deciding whether inventories from the income accounts join the fixed stock in her denominator. If historical cost is her branch, her real labour begins, because the agency does not publish what she needs. She must take the investment flows of every past year, price them as they were priced then, cumulate them under assumptions about retirement and decay, and thereby manufacture a stock the statistical system never recorded. The reconstruction is doable; the historical-cost literature has done it and published its methods. But every assumption inside it is a place where a critic can dig, and the critics have dug, which is why the historical-cost studies carry some of the longest methodological appendices in the field.

Then comes the arithmetic illustration that explains the fork better than any polemic, offered here as pure arithmetic with invented round numbers, not as data about any real economy. Suppose a single machine bought for one hundred units of money in a year of calm prices, and suppose prices of such machines double over the following decade while the machine still works. In the replacement-cost ledger the machine now stands in the stock at two hundred; in the historical ledger it stands at one hundred, less whatever depreciation each method has written off. Suppose the machine's annual profit contribution is unchanged in money terms. The rate computed on the historical denominator is roughly twice the rate computed on the replacement denominator, from the same machine, the same output and the same profit. Multiply the machine into an economy whose capital goods went through the great inflation, with vintages layered from cheap years and dear years, and the two national series inherit exactly this divergence, softened by depreciation, retirements and new investment, but never erased. The example is a toy. The mechanism it isolates is the engine of the entire controversy, and a reader who has followed it owns the key to every chart in the literature.

The final steps of construction are the ones least visible to readers and most revealing to auditors. The researcher aligns the timing of numerator and denominator, since profit accrues through the year while the stock is conventionally measured at a point; chooses whether to smooth the series or present it raw, with the cycle's teeth showing; and decides how to handle the breaks where the statistical agency redefined a category midstream, bridging them by splicing, by estimation, or by truncating the series and saying so. Each is a small decision. Together they are why replication in this field is a craft rather than a keystroke, and why two series that agree through all of it command a respect that no single series, however elegant, can claim for itself.

## The long history of the empirical debate

The measurement quarrel is not a recent growth on the theory; it is nearly as old as the postwar theory itself. Among the earliest systematic attempts to test the tendency against the statistics of the United States stands the work of Joseph Gillman, whose book of the nineteen fifties assembled the available data and reported a falling rate of profit in the earlier twentieth century, giving the young debate its first empirical landmark and its first methodological quarrels, since Gillman's categories were already reconstructions rather than readings. Shane Mage's doctoral work of the nineteen sixties extended the reconstruction of the American accounts and remains cited wherever the conversion of official categories into Marxian ones is discussed. The nineteen seventies, with profitability visibly squeezed across the developed world, brought a generation of empirical studies and fierce exchanges about their construction, in which the questions of unproductive labour, of the boundary of the productive sector and of the correct treatment of the state's take were all fought over in print.

Fred Moseley's work on the postwar United States economy, published in book form at the start of the nineteen nineties, shows the debate maturing: a full reconstruction in the Shaikh and Tonak spirit, reporting the fall in the measured rate and attributing the larger part of it to the growth of unproductive activities relative to productive ones, so that the controversy shifted from whether the rate had fallen toward which component of the construction carried the fall. The exchanges around Kliman's historical-cost reconstruction and around the Duménil and Lévy programme, described in the section on the fork, dominate the more recent decades, joined by econometric work such as that of Basu and Manolakos, who brought time-series methods to the American data and reported results they themselves characterised as sensitive to specification, a finding that will surprise no reader who has reached this page. Running beneath the whole history is a pattern this article has stressed throughout: each generation's quarrel about the world has resolved, on inspection, into a quarrel about a convention, and each resolution has left the conventions better documented than before. The literature's disagreements are not its failure. They are its method of self-correction, conducted in public, at the cost of never quite arriving.

## Levels against trends: a confusion to retire

Much mischief in the citing of this literature comes from comparing levels when only trends are comparable, and trends when the levels were the point. The level of a measured rate, its height in a given year, depends heavily on the gross or net basis of the stock and on the breadth of the profit concept: a gross-stock, narrow-profit construction can place the rate in single figures where a net-stock, broad-surplus construction places it several times higher, for the same economy in the same year, with both arithmetics correct. Levels across studies are therefore close to meaningless unless the constructions match row by row down the decision tree. Trends are hardier, because many level-shifting choices shift the whole line up or down without much altering its slope; that is why the literature's agreements and disagreements are almost always stated, by careful authors, as claims about direction over a period rather than about the height of a line in a year. The citing practice that lifts a level from one study and a trend from another, and assembles from them a conclusion neither study supports, is the commonest technical error in journalism on this subject, and the easiest to avoid once seen.

A corollary concerns the endpoints of periods. Because the measured rate is cyclical, falling in slumps and rebounding in booms, a trend measured from a peak year to a trough year will show decline where the same construction measured across whole cycles shows none, and the choice of endpoints can be made innocently, by the boundaries of a dataset, or strategically, by the needs of an argument. The better studies report their trends across several endpoint choices, or across full cycles identified on independent grounds, and the reader's corresponding duty is to ask of any two-point comparison what the endpoints are and who chose them. In a field where a valuation convention can reverse a forty-year verdict, a pair of endpoints can reverse a ten-year one even more cheaply. The defences are the same throughout: disclose the construction, disclose the endpoints, and treat any figure offered without them as a claim about its author's method until proved otherwise.

## The profit share is not the profit rate

One misreading deserves a section of its own because it appears wherever this subject touches public discussion. The profit share, the fraction of national income accruing as profit, is widely tracked, easily obtained, and frequently reported to have risen across the disputed decades in several major economies as wage shares fell. It does not follow that the rate of profit rose. The share divides profit by income; the rate divides profit by the capital stock; and the two denominators move on different logics entirely. Profit can claim a growing slice of a slowly growing income while the stock against which it is measured grows faster still, through capital deepening, through the accumulation of housing and infrastructure inside broad stock measures, or through the revaluation effects already examined, so that the rate falls while the share rises. Both movements can be, and in the literature on some economies and periods have been, reported together by the same authors without any contradiction, because there is none.

### Does a rising profit share settle the question?

No. The share compares profit with income, the rate compares profit with the capital stock, and the stock can grow faster than income for decades, through capital deepening or revaluation, pulling the rate down while the share climbs. The two series answer different questions and can move in opposite directions indefinitely.

The confusion is consequential because the share is the statistic that general commentary reaches for, being published prominently and requiring no reconstruction. Debates conducted in terms of the share therefore proceed as though the empirical question about profitability had been answered, when a different and much easier question has been answered instead. A rising profit share is evidence about the distribution of income between classes, important in its own right and central to neighbouring parts of this series. It is not evidence, in either direction, about the return on the accumulated stock, and the researcher or journalist who needs the second quantity must do the second quantity's work, down the whole decision tree, rather than borrow the first quantity's answer.

## What a careful study does: robustness as a standard

Out of the quarrels surveyed here, a standard of good practice has crystallised, and it can be stated as a set of expectations a reader is entitled to bring to any new contribution. A careful study names its branches on every row of the decision tree, and names them in the text rather than in an appendix that citing authors can pretend not to have seen. It reports sensitivity: how the headline trend changes when the tax treatment is flipped, when finance is removed, when the sector is narrowed, when the endpoints are moved. It states the vintage of its accounts and the bridging it applied at the breaks. Above all, on the evidence of this literature's history, it reports the rival branch of the fork: not necessarily by building the rival series in full, which is a labour of years, but by acknowledging the valuation on which its conclusion depends and the direction in which the rival valuation would push it. A conclusion stated with its dependency attached is a scientific claim. A conclusion stated without one is an advertisement for a convention.

The standard cuts both ways, and readers should apply it to the canonical studies as well as to newcomers. The strongest works in this field pass it because their authors wrote under fire and learned to disclose everything an opponent might demand. The weaker works, including some widely cited ones, fail it in the specific way this article has taught the reader to detect: a chart with no visible scaffolding, a rate quoted to a decimal place whose construction would not fill a paragraph, a forty-year verdict resting on endpoints nobody chose for a stated reason. Precision of presentation is not precision of knowledge, and in this field the two are often inversely related, the decimal places flourishing exactly where the conventions are least examined. The reader's defence is the checklist, applied without respect of persons: branches named, sensitivity shown, vintage stated, fork dependency acknowledged. A study that survives the checklist has earned the right to be argued with. A study that fails it has not yet earned the right to be believed.

## The pattern repeats: measurement dominance across this series

Readers of this series have met the shape of this problem before, and the recurrence is itself worth naming. The companion article on [labour theory of value evidence](/2010/10/01/labour-theory-of-value-evidence/) surveyed the literature correlating prices with labour values and found the same geometry: a family of studies reporting strikingly strong correlations, a family of critics replying that the result is sensitive to the conventions of aggregation, sector definition and the treatment of joint production, and a mature position in which the convention must be stated before the coefficient means anything. The article on the rate of exploitation found it again, in the classification of productive and unproductive labour that this article has also had to cross. Three times now, at three different sites of the theory, the empirical programme has arrived at the same destination: a methodological decision, taken upstream of the data, moves the reported result further than any plausible movement in the world being measured.

That recurrence is a finding about Marxist empirical work that no single-site study could have delivered, and it deserves a name of its own alongside the valuation fork: measurement dominance, the condition in which the choice of measure dominates the measured. Naming it is not a counsel of despair, and the articles in this series have each resisted that conclusion for the same reason. In each case something survived the conventions: here, the agreed postwar decline, visible through every lens; in the value studies, correlations too strong to be manufactured by any single convention; in the exploitation estimates, a range rather than a point, but a range that excludes the values casual discussion assumes. The pattern disciplines the programme without dissolving it. What it dissolves is a style of citation, the single triumphant number, and in its place it installs the style this article has practised: findings reported as intervals across conventions, agreements reported where conventions converge, and dependencies reported where they do not. The wider programme of [testing marxist economics](/2016/08/01/testing-marxist-economics/) takes up the question of what such a discipline implies for the theory's scientific standing as a whole; the present article's contribution is the worked example, the site where the discipline can be watched operating on the theory's most contested number.

## The strongest objection, stated fairly

The objection to all of this must now be given its full force, because it is the most serious challenge the literature faces and it comes not from the theory's enemies but from the philosophy of measurement itself. It runs as follows. A quantity whose measured trajectory reverses when an accounting convention changes is not being measured at all; what is being measured is the convention. Science advances by constructing instruments whose readings constrain the world to answer one way rather than another, and an instrument that can be tuned, by a defensible choice at a single dial, to report either decline or recovery across forty years has abdicated that function. The persistence of both conventions, each defended by intelligent researchers for decades without convergence, is itself the evidence: where a question is empirically decidable, fields converge as instruments improve, and where they do not converge, the reasonable conclusion is that the question, as posed, has no empirical answer. On this objection, the entire empirical literature on the rate of profit is a prolonged exercise in discovering what its practitioners already believed, with the national accounts as an elaborate mirror.

The objection should be conceded more than its targets usually concede, before it is answered. It is true that the post nineteen eighties segment, the segment on which the largest theoretical claims depend, cannot be settled by the accounts alone, because the accounts do not determine their own valuation basis and the choice between bases is a theoretical act. It is true that both camps have held their conventions through decades of exchange, and that the exchange has refined rather than narrowed the disagreement. It is even true that a researcher's theoretical commitments predict the branch of the fork he or she defends with uncomfortable accuracy, which is exactly the symptom the objection cites. Any honest survey must record all of that, and this one does.

The answer begins by refusing the objection's premise that convergence on the agreed segment counts for nothing. The same instruments, tuned to opposite conventions, jointly report a substantial fall in profitability between the mid nineteen sixties and the early nineteen eighties across the major economies. A mirror does not do that; a mirror tuned two ways would flatter two prejudices, and the postwar segment flatters neither camp's whole story, since the recovery camp must explain why its recovered rate needed recovering from, and the decline camp must explain the trough's depth on its own construction. The conventions constrain each other across the agreed segment, and the constraint is information about the world, delivered jointly by rivals who intended no such collaboration. The objection also proves too much. National income itself, unemployment, productivity, the price level: every macroeconomic magnitude rests on conventions that are contestable in the same structural way, and several have known variants that move their trends. If convention-dependence voided measurement, empirical macroeconomics would close, not merely its Marxian corner. The defensible conclusion is narrower and harder: the profit rate is measurable relative to a stated concept of capital, the concepts are two, the theory that wants its tendency tested must say which concept the tendency concerns, and until it does, the evidence will continue to return the split verdict it has honestly returned all along. That is not the abdication of measurement. It is measurement reporting, accurately, that the question put to it was ambiguous.

## How to cite a profit rate without overclaiming

The practical yield of everything above is a discipline of citation, and it can be made concrete. The sentence a writer can safely set down about profitability names the economy, the period, the construction and the direction: measured profit rates in the United States, on replacement-cost national accounts for the corporate sector, fell from the mid nineteen sixties to the early nineteen eighties and recovered part of the fall thereafter. Every clause of that sentence is doing work, and removing the construction clause converts a true sentence into a contestable one without changing a single figure. The sentence a writer cannot safely set down is the one that circulates: the rate of profit fell, or rose, full stop, as though a single dial had been read. Between those two sentences lies the whole difference between using this literature and being used by it.

### What should a citation of a profit rate always state?

It should state the valuation basis of the capital stock, the definition of profit including its tax treatment, the sector covered, and the period with its endpoints. A citation carrying those four disclosures can be placed, checked against rival constructions and argued with; one missing them quotes a number in the dark.

Researchers keeping project notes on this literature will find the discipline easier to sustain with a system than with good intentions. Each series encountered should be logged with its branches on the tree, its vintage and its endpoints, so that months later the dependency of a conclusion on a convention can be recovered at a glance rather than reconstructed from memory. A study companion such as the [Marxism study notes tool](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) serves exactly this purpose, binding each extracted finding to the construction that produced it, so that the notes preserve what the citing literature throws away. The habit sounds clerical. In a field where the conclusion is the convention, the clerical habit is the scholarship.

## What would count as decisive evidence

The long-tail question this literature is asked most often, in seminars and in correspondence, is what could possibly settle it. The honest answer has two parts. The first is that no purely statistical discovery can settle the disputed segment, because the divergence is not caused by missing data; the data are abundant and shared. New vintages of the accounts will refine both families of series without reconciling them, since the fork is not a gap in the record but a choice about the record's meaning. Expecting a future dataset to end the argument misunderstands the argument, and predictions that the next release will vindicate one camp should be read as symptoms of the citation style this article exists to correct.

The second part is more constructive. The question could be settled from the theory side, if the tradition reached an argued consensus on which concept of capital the tendency law quantifies over: the value advanced, fixed historically at the moment of commitment, or the current scale of the apparatus whose reproduction the system must finance. That is a question about Marx's texts and about the economic function of the magnitude, and it is arguable, with evidence, in the way textual and theoretical questions are arguable. A subsidiary route runs through behaviour: if the two candidate magnitudes diverge in their power to explain investment, crisis timing and the other phenomena profitability is invoked to explain, the one that earns its keep in those contests gains a claim that pure accounting cannot give it, and parts of the literature have begun exactly that comparative work. Until one of those routes delivers, the mature position is the split verdict, stated without embarrassment: the fall is agreed, the sequel is conditional, and the condition has a name. A field that can state its own undecided question that precisely is not failing at empirics. It is showing the rest of economics how empirics, honestly pursued, reports its limits.

## Misreadings this survey exists to correct

Gather the errors together, since they travel together. The first is that a single profit-rate series exists, waiting to be looked up; the survey has shown a family of series, ordered by their distance from a fork, and the look-up mentality is the root error from which the others grow. The second is that the disagreement among Marxian researchers on this evidence reflects their politics, when the documentary record shows a methodological dispute conducted with published procedures and mutual acknowledgment, in which the political valences of the conclusions do not line up as the accusation requires. The third is that the postwar decline is itself disputed, a claim that survives only by never opening the rival literatures side by side, since the decline is the ground on which the rivals stand to disagree about everything else. The fourth is that world series, by their scope, outrank national ones, when the reverse ordering of reliability holds. The fifth, corrected in its own section above, is the slide from a rising profit share to a rising profit rate. Each error is a way of avoiding the decision tree. The article's whole argument can be recovered from that observation: the evidence on the rate of profit is usable, rich and in part settled, and it becomes unusable only at the moment a reader declines to ask how any of it was built.

## Inside the agreed decline: what the fall was made of

The agreement that profitability fell across the postwar decades conceals a second literature, almost as large, disputing what the fall consisted in, and a reader of the evidence should know its main positions because they determine what the disputed sequel would even mean. Decompose the rate and three moving parts appear: the share of profit in output, the ratio of output to the capital stock, and the valuation terms that translate between them. A fall in the measured rate must come through some combination of a squeezed profit share and a deteriorating output-to-capital ratio, and the rival explanations of the postwar era are, at bottom, rival weightings of those components. The profit squeeze account, developed most influentially for the British case by Andrew Glyn and Bob Sutcliffe at the start of the nineteen seventies and extended by many hands since, locates the fall in distribution: full employment strengthened labour's bargaining position, wage growth outran productivity, and the share of profit was compressed from the side of income. The composition account, closer to the classical mechanism, locates it in accumulation itself: capital deepening raised the stock faster than the surplus it generated, so the output-to-capital ratio deteriorated even where distribution was stable. The shock account assigns the decisive role to the energy price explosions of the nineteen seventies, which raised costs and rendered part of the existing stock, built for cheap energy, economically obsolete before its physical life ended.

The decomposition literature's mature finding, reported across many studies with differing constructions, is that the components' relative weights vary by country and sub-period, which is why each single-cause account can exhibit a country where it shines and why none has carried the whole field. In the British case the distributional squeeze is prominent in most reconstructions; in the American case the deterioration of the output-to-capital ratio and the growth of unproductive employment, emphasised in Moseley's reconstruction, claim a larger share; everywhere the nineteen seventies shocks sit on top of trends already established. For the purposes of the present article the weights matter less than the discipline the decomposition imposes on the sequel. If the fall was substantially a squeeze of the profit share, then the wage restraint of the disputed decades addresses its cause directly, and a recovery of the share is the expected signature, visible in both valuation families. If the fall was substantially compositional, no distributional offensive repairs it, and the historical-cost series' refusal to recover is the signature to expect. The two families of series are thus not merely two arithmetics; each aligns with a diagnosis, and the diagnosis with a reading of the whole era that followed. Evidence, mechanism and interpretation arrive as a package, which is the deepest reason the packages are hard to prise apart.

## Country variation inside the agreement

The agreed decline is an agreement about a family of national experiences, not about a uniform path, and the variation inside it is part of the evidence. The United States supplies the most studied case and the longest continuous debate, partly because its accounts are the richest and partly because its postwar profitability stood high at the start of the period, giving the decline a long way to run. The British case entered the literature early and polemically, through the profit squeeze debate, and British reconstructions have generally emphasised the distributional component and the weakness of manufacturing profitability under international competition. The continental European economies present the pattern with national inflections: rapid postwar reconstruction and catch-up growth sustained profitability through the nineteen fifties and into the sixties in several of them, after which the common squeeze arrived, mediated by different wage-bargaining institutions whose effects the comparative literature has traced with care. Japan's trajectory stands partly apart in most accounts, its extraordinarily high investment rates sustaining a different configuration of stock growth and profitability, and its later stagnation posing the measurement questions in a distinct form.

Two lessons follow from the variation. The first is that the agreement is stronger than any single national story, because the constructions that deliver the decline in one statistical system deliver it, with local differences of timing and depth, in the others; an artefact of one country's accounting would be unlikely to reproduce so faithfully abroad. The second is that timing differences are informative rather than embarrassing. Economies whose wage institutions compressed distribution later show the distributional component later; economies that mechanised fastest show the compositional component earliest; and the comparative studies that exploit those differences, using the variation as a natural laboratory, represent some of the most persuasive work in the field precisely because they explain the divergences instead of averaging them away. The reader who meets a claim about the rate of profit should therefore ask not only which country it concerns but whether its author treats the other countries as confirmation, as exceptions, or as silence. Each treatment is available in the literature; they are not equally honest.

## The capital the stock omits

A further frontier of the denominator debate concerns the assets the conventional stock leaves out, and it has grown more important as the character of investment has changed. The traditional stock counts produced tangible assets: structures, equipment, inventories, and in newer vintages of the accounts, software. Land is excluded, being unproduced, though its price movements dwarf those of many included categories and though rents drawn from it enter some numerators. Government infrastructure is usually excluded from the business stock, reasonably for a corporate rate, less reasonably for an economy-wide one, since roads and grids condition the productivity of private capital without appearing in its denominator. Most consequential for the disputed decades is the rise of intangible investment: research and development, design, branding, organisational development, the training and software-like assets that firms increasingly accumulate and that accounting long treated as current expenses. The international statistical standards were revised in the two thousands to capitalise research and development, and the major agencies, the American bureau among them, incorporated the change in comprehensive revisions, enlarging the measured stock across the whole postwar span and rewriting the denominators of earlier studies retroactively.

The intangibles question cuts into the fork debate from an unexpected angle. If a growing share of what firms advance is intangible, and the accounts historically expensed it, then the measured stock in the older vintages understates the advance increasingly over time, and the measured rate is biased upward increasingly over time, flattering the disputed decades in a way neither camp's standard construction corrects. Researchers have begun the work of capitalising the broader intangible categories on the lines developed in the growth literature associated with Corrado, Hulten and Sichel, whose estimates of intangible investment in the United States economy are a standard reference, and the early indications reported in that literature are that intangible-inclusive stocks grow faster and intangible-inclusive rates behave differently from their conventional counterparts. The findings are too young, and too entangled with imputation choices of their own, to adjudicate the fork. Their service at present is disciplinary: they demonstrate that the denominator debate is not closed by either convention, because the list of things the denominator might contain is itself still moving, and a series is always, among other things, a bet on what capital is.

## Decomposing the rate: exploitation, composition and the cross-checks

The profit rate does not stand alone in the theory's arithmetic, and the empirical literature gains credibility where its central magnitude is cross-checked against its relatives. Algebraically, the rate of profit can be decomposed into the rate of exploitation, the share of the working day or of value added appropriated as surplus, set against the composition of capital, the ratio of the stock to the wage bill or to value added. A rising rate of exploitation with a falling rate of profit implies a composition rising faster still; a falling rate of exploitation can deliver a falling profit rate with no compositional movement at all. The empirical studies that estimate the components jointly, rather than the headline rate alone, therefore test the theory at a joint where its mechanism lives, and the decomposition results reported in the literature, including the finding across several reconstructions that the postwar fall combines a squeezed or stagnant exploitation measure with a rising weight of capital and of unproductive costs, are among the most informative products of the whole programme. They are also the most convention-sensitive, since the exploitation rate inherits the productive labour classification and the profit rate inherits the fork, so that the decomposition can be read as a map of exactly where the conventions bite hardest.

The cross-checks extend outward. Profitability series can be set against the subsequent behaviour of investment, on the principle that a magnitude claiming to govern accumulation should show some grip on accumulation's course; against the timing of crises, where the literature on the financial crash, already linked from this article, draws its inferences; and against the profit share series of the official accounts, which bound what any numerator can claim. No single cross-check is decisive, and the literature's more reflective practitioners treat them as a web of mutual discipline rather than a tribunal. The point for the reader is that the field, at its best, does not ask to be believed on the strength of one line on one chart. It offers an articulated structure in which the fork is the largest single joint, the classification of labour the second, and the sector and tax decisions the smaller bones, and it invites the reader to stress each joint and watch what moves. That invitation, taken up, is the difference between studying this literature and merely citing it.

## Dating the trough

Even the turning point of the agreed decline refuses to sit still under examination, and its behaviour teaches a final measurement lesson. The trough of profitability in the early nineteen eighties is a feature of every major construction, but its precise dating migrates: a series dominated by the wage squeeze finds the lowest point where distribution was most compressed, a series dominated by utilisation finds it in the depths of the recession at the decade's opening, a replacement-cost series finds its descent prolonged by the revaluation of the stock while inflation persisted, and a historical-cost series may print no sharp trough at all, only a ledge on a long staircase down. Studies that date the recovery's beginning differently are therefore not necessarily disagreeing about any event; they are reporting the turning points of different magnitudes that share a name. The practical moral generalises the endpoint warning of an earlier section. In this literature, dates are conclusions, not premises: the year a study gives for the turn is an output of its construction, and quoting the date without the construction repeats, in miniature, the error of quoting the rate without its valuation.

The migration of the trough also explains a recurring puzzle in the secondary discussion, where partisans cite the same recession and draw opposite morals. If profitability bottomed with the great disinflation and then recovered on the measures that guide business decisions, the era of wage restraint and financial expansion that followed appears as a successful, if harsh, restoration. If the trough was merely a ledge, the same era appears as a holding action whose expedients, debt, global labour arbitrage, the financialisation of the firm, testify to a pressure never relieved. The data of the accounts sit beneath both readings, patient and identical. What differs is the construction, and behind the construction the concept of capital the constructor believes the theory intends. This article has argued from its first page that naming that dependency is the beginning of literacy in this field. The dating of a single trough, followed through the constructions, is as good a final examination in that literacy as the literature affords.

## The two concepts of capital beneath the fork

The fork persists because it is not, at bottom, a statistical disagreement. Beneath the two arithmetics stand two concepts of what capital is, each with roots in the theory's own texts, and the empirical debate is the surface disturbance of that deeper duality. On the first concept, capital is an advance: a sum of value committed to a circuit, thrown into production in order to return expanded. The magnitude that matters is the sum committed, and it is fixed by the historical act of committing it. A machine bought for a hundred and now replaceable only for two hundred still embodies an advance of one hundred; the owner's fortune turned on the hundred, the loan was raised against the hundred, and the return that vindicates or condemns the commitment is the surplus set against the hundred. On the second concept, capital is a stock of productive capacity existing in the present: a going concern whose scale is what it would cost to assemble now, and whose return is meaningful only against that present scale, because the decisions the return informs, expand, contract, exit, borrow, are present decisions comparing present magnitudes. The first concept looks backward to the commitment; the second looks around at the current field of action. Both are intelligible. Both can quote the founding texts, since those texts move between the language of advance and the language of reproduction as the argument requires, never anticipating that a century of national accounting would force a choice between them.

Marx's own treatment of fixed capital feeds both readings, which is why textual arbitration has not closed the question. Fixed capital, in the analysis of Capital's second volume, transfers its value to the product piecemeal across its working life, a treatment that sits naturally with the historical sum actually embodied in the machine. Yet the same analysis introduces moral depreciation: the machine loses value when a better or cheaper machine appears, regardless of its physical state, because its value is governed by the conditions of reproduction in the present. Moral depreciation is a current-cost idea lodged inside the classical text, and the replacement-cost camp is entitled to it. The historical-cost camp replies that moral depreciation in the text concerns the value of the individual capital in the social process, not the bookkeeping of the advance whose return the profit rate measures, and the exchange continues, learned and inconclusive. The reader of this article needs the shape of the stalemate rather than a verdict: the fork is where a genuine ambiguity in the theory meets a genuine necessity in the statistics, and stalemates of that kind are ended, when they are ended, by the theory's development rather than by better data.

A practical consequence follows for how the two families of series should be used in the meantime. They are not best regarded as rival estimates of one magnitude, to be averaged or voted on. They are measurements of two magnitudes, each answering its own question: what return has the capital committed over the era yielded on the sums committed, and what return does the existing apparatus, at the prices of the day, yield now. Both questions are legitimate objects of theory and of history. The mischief enters when a series built for the second question is conscripted as evidence about the first, or the reverse, and much of the polemical literature consists of exactly that conscription, performed silently. A reader trained by the decision tree can detect the conscription in a paragraph: the moment a current-cost recovery is offered as proof that the advance made across the era was well rewarded, or a historical-cost decline as proof that no rational firm could see a return worth expanding for, the concepts have been crossed, and the argument, whatever its other merits, has left the evidence behind.

## Turnover, circulating capital and the annual rate

The published series are annual, and the annual frame conceals a further family of effects that the careful literature handles explicitly. The rate of profit in the theory is stated per circuit of capital, and the number of circuits completed in a year varies with the turnover time of the capital engaged: the weeks or months for which value is locked in production and circulation before returning in money form. Two economies with identical rates per circuit and different turnover speeds register different annual rates, and a single economy whose turnover accelerates, through faster logistics, just-in-time inventories or quicker payment chains, raises its annual measured rate without any change in the surplus extracted per circuit. Empirical constructions differ in how much of this they capture. The treatment of inventories in the denominator, already met on the tree, is the visible part: circulating capital advanced for materials and work in progress belongs to the advance, and series that include a full measure of it show lower and smoother rates than series built on fixed capital alone. The invisible part is turnover itself, which no stock measure records, since the stock shows what is engaged at a moment and not how fast it cycles.

The acceleration of turnover across the disputed decades is one of the era's documented features, in logistics, in retail inventory management and in financial settlement, and it constitutes a genuine counteracting influence on the annual rate that operates independently of the fork. Both families of series register it only insofar as their inventory and circulating components capture it, and constructions differ enough in those components that part of the disagreement about the level of profitability in the later decades may be a disagreement about turnover wearing the clothes of a disagreement about return. The point generalises the article's discipline once more: the ratio delivered by the accounts is a joint product of the surplus, the stock and the speed at which the stock revolves, and a literature that argues about the first two while assuming the third will misattribute a share of every movement it studies. The better reconstructions say what they assume about circulation. The reader's checklist acquires another line.

## Profitability and the timing of crises

A use of the series that attracts wide attention, and correspondingly wide abuse, is the attempt to read the approach of crises off the behaviour of profitability. The thought has a classical pedigree: if the rate of profit is the governor of accumulation, its sustained decline should precede the interruptions of accumulation, and several researchers in this literature have examined the timing formally, asking whether downturns in their preferred series lead the crises of the postwar era with any regularity. The reported results are, predictably by now, construction-dependent: the series that decline persistently offer a long fuse that can be matched to almost any explosion, a convenience critics are quick to name, while the series that recover and fall again offer turning points whose proximity to the crises depends delicately on dating and smoothing. The honest summary is that profitability movements belong to the prehistory of the postwar crises in most serious accounts, as condition and context, while the claim that a particular series times them, still less predicts them, exceeds what any camp has established. Readers will meet the strong claim in movement literature and occasionally in journalism; the decision tree, plus the dating discussion earlier in this article, supplies the audit it requires. Which series, which turning point, dated by which rule, matched against which crisis list drawn up on what independent grounds: a claim that cannot answer those questions in order has not yet reached the starting line of evidence.

## The cycle inside the series

Annual profit rates are strongly cyclical, and the cycle's signature can mislead readers who approach the series as a direct transcript of the tendency. In recession the numerator falls violently, as profits are the most volatile component of national income, while the denominator moves with the slow inertia of an accumulated stock; the measured rate therefore plunges in every downturn and rebounds in every recovery, tracing the cycle with an amplitude far larger than the trend's own movement. Researchers respond with the instruments already surveyed: smoothing across years, averaging peak to peak or trough to trough, correcting toward normal utilisation. Each instrument clarifies the trend at the price of an assumption, and the assumption can be tuned. The extreme illustration is the crisis year itself, in which a financial-inclusive series can record figures so distorted by losses and write-downs that the observation is best treated as belonging to the crisis's own accounting rather than to the trend's. A reader scanning a chart should therefore learn to see two superimposed records, a slow geological movement and a fast weather, and to distrust any argument, from any camp, that exhibits a handful of adjacent annual observations as evidence about the geology. The tendency, if it exists, lives in the geology; the weather is real but it is not the tendency, and the constructions that separate them are making a judgement the reader is entitled to inspect.

## Auditing a chart: the method applied once, in full

The disciplines of this article converge in a single exercise that any reader can perform, and performing it once, slowly, teaches more than the checklist recited. Take a published chart of the rate of profit, of the kind that circulates in books and articles, and refuse to look at the line until the scaffolding has been extracted. Begin with the source note: which accounts, which vintage, whose reconstruction. A chart with no source note fails at the first fence and nothing after it matters. Move to the numerator: find, in the text or the appendix, the definition of profit, its treatment of interest and tax, its sector. Move to the denominator: find the valuation basis, and mark the fork; find the stock's coverage, its gross or net basis, its inventories. Note the endpoints and the smoothing. Only then look at the line, and read it as the answer to the specific question the scaffolding defines, no more and no less. The exercise takes ten minutes with a well-documented study and cannot be completed at all with a poorly documented one, which is itself the audit's verdict on the poorly documented one.

Two habits distinguish the experienced auditor. The first is to locate the study on the spectrum the fork creates before reading its conclusion, since the conclusion's broad shape is then predictable and the interest shifts to the places where the study resists its own construction: the historical-cost study that concedes a sideways decade, the current-cost study that concedes the recovery's incompleteness. Those concessions, made against the grain of the author's own instrument, are the most credible sentences in the literature, and the auditor hunts them deliberately. The second habit is to ask what the study does not plot: the rival branch, the alternative sector, the excluded finance. Absences in a chart are choices with the same status as presences, and the studies that plot their own rivals beside themselves, a small and honourable company in this field, earn a presumption of good faith that no rhetoric can substitute for. A reader who finishes the audit with those two habits owns the literature's method, and can return for any particular study's findings with the confidence that the findings have been weighed rather than merely received.

## The landmarks of the literature, in prose

For readers who intend to go further, the terrain of the primary literature can be sketched by its landmarks, each of which anchors a stretch of the ground this article has surveyed. Gillman's postwar study of the falling rate in the American economy stands at the head of the empirical tradition, valuable now for its pioneering reconstruction and for the clarity with which it exposes the conversion problems every successor inherited. Mage's work on the value categories of the American accounts supplies the conversion apparatus in its most systematic early form. Shaikh and Tonak's volume on measuring the wealth of nations provides the standard mapping between official and Marxian categories and the fullest worked example of the productive and unproductive reconstruction. Moseley's study of the postwar American economy applies that mapping to the falling rate and presses the unproductive labour explanation. Duménil and Lévy's body of work, across several books and many papers, builds the current-cost programme into a general interpretation of the era of neoliberalism. Kliman's book on the failure of capitalist production presents the historical-cost case at full length and in explicit polemic with the recovery thesis. Basu's econometric papers model the trend with a statistician's suspicion of all the camps, his own included. A reader who works through even three of these, with the decision tree open beside the text, will have an education in empirical method that extends well beyond this theory: there are few better schools for learning how economic quantities are made, disputed and, within their stated terms, known.

## The cheapening of capital goods, seen through both ledgers

Among the counteracting influences the classical theory lists against the falling tendency, one has a documented life in the modern data and a peculiar relationship to the fork: the cheapening of the elements of constant capital. Across the postwar era, and with gathering speed in its later decades, the prices of machinery and equipment, adjusted for quality, fell relative to the prices of output in the major economies, a pattern the growth-accounting literature has documented extensively, most sharply for computing and communications equipment, whose power per unit of money multiplied while its price line fell. Cheaper equipment means that a given physical deepening of production requires a smaller advance of value: the composition of capital in physical terms can rise while its value composition rises more slowly, or even falls. In the theory this is a genuine counter-tendency, operating on the magnitude itself. In the statistics it behaves very differently under the two valuations, and the difference illuminates the fork from a new side.

Under replacement cost, the cheapening enters the measured rate twice over. New investment arrives at lower prices, so the stock grows more slowly in value; and the existing stock is revalued downward relative to output prices, shrinking the denominator directly. A current-cost series therefore registers the cheapening as an improvement in profitability with full and immediate force. Under historical cost, the new vintages arrive cheap, but the old vintages remain in the ledger at the prices of their dearer years until they retire, so the denominator digests the cheapening only at the pace of scrapping, and the measured rate responds with a lag of years. Part of the recovery the current-cost literature reports for the disputed decades is, on the historical-cost critique, exactly this repricing effect: the denominator deflated by the falling relative price of equipment, mistaken for a revival of the return on advances actually made. Part of the recovery is also, on the current-cost reply, perfectly real: an economy that can reproduce its apparatus more cheaply genuinely stands in a different relation to its capital than one that cannot, and a measure blind to that fact is blind to something the theory itself names. The exchange cannot be settled here, and this article's discipline forbids settling it. What can be said is that the cheapening of equipment is the point where the fork, the counteracting influences and the interpretation of the disputed decades all intersect, and a reader who locates a study's treatment of it has located the study's centre of gravity.

## The state inside the accounts

Whole-economy constructions must decide what to do with the state, and the decision is less innocent than it looks. Government in the modern accounts is a large holder of produced assets, roads, schools, hospitals, defence installations, and a large payer of incomes, and its treatment can be arranged in several defensible ways, each moving a broad measure. Exclude the state entirely and the series describes the private economy's return, the usual choice and the one most studies make explicit. Include government assets in the denominator while excluding any corresponding surplus, since government does not earn profit in the accounts' sense, and the measured rate is dragged down by a stock that was never advanced for return. Include the state's enterprises, the nationalised industries and utilities of the postwar decades, inside the corporate boundary, as many European series for the earlier period must, and the boundary between the corporate and general government sectors becomes a historical variable in its own right, shifting with privatisations that move assets and earnings across the line without any change in the physical economy. The privatisation waves of the disputed decades therefore register in sector series partly as changes in coverage, a further reason the careful studies fix their boundaries conceptually and rebuild consistently across the whole span, rather than following the legal frontier as it moved.

The fiscal side compounds the matter. The state's take from the surplus arrives as corporate taxes, already treated in the numerator discussion, but also as the interest on public debt, paid substantially out of taxation and received substantially by the financial sector, where it re-enters some numerators as financial income. A construction can therefore count the same surplus twice in its journey, once as the tax that funded the interest and once as the interest received, unless the researcher nets the flows with a care the published aggregates do not supply ready-made. The amounts are large enough in the high-debt decades to matter at the margin of the disputed trends. Here again the field's best practice is disclosure and dual presentation, and here again the citing literature's practice is silence, so that the state's shadow inside the series goes almost entirely unremarked by the audiences most confident about what the series show.

## Housing, households and the imputed corners of the accounts

The household sector hides a similar trap, and it catches whole-economy measures in particular. The national accounts treat owner-occupied housing as a business: the homeowner is deemed to rent the dwelling to herself, an imputed rent enters output and income, and the dwelling enters the stock of assets. The imputation is defensible within the accounts' logic, since it makes tenure changes neutral for measured production, but it injects into any whole-economy profit calculation a large, slow magnitude that has nothing to do with the capital whose return the theory discusses. A researcher building an economy-wide rate must strip the imputed rental sector out, or defend leaving it in, and the stripping is imperfect because the accounts interleave housing with the rest of the household economy. Rented housing raises the same questions in a market form: the landlord's return is a genuine income from an asset, and whether it belongs in the numerator of a Marxian profit rate, and its bricks in the denominator, divides researchers along lines that reproduce, in miniature, the old question of whether rent is a share of surplus or an independent revenue. The decisions matter most for level, less for trend, except where housing booms swell the stock faster than any productive measure, as they did in several economies in the run-up to the financial crisis, bending broad series in ways their authors have had to discuss explicitly ever since.

These corners of the accounts reward attention out of proportion to their size in the textbooks, because they are where the distance between the statistical economy and the theoretical one is greatest. The accounts describe an economy of sectors and imputations arranged for consistency; the theory describes an economy of capitals and classes arranged for explanation. Every bridge between them is a piece of engineering with a designer, and the designer of the housing bridge, whose choices move billions between categories, is a statistical convention committee whose reasoning is published and honourable and entirely innocent of the use to which the reconstruction will put it. The researcher's reconstruction is a second act of design on top of the first, and the reader who grasps the layering has grasped why this literature's appendices run so long and why its conclusions must travel with their scaffolding or not travel at all.

## A neighbouring magnitude that is not this one

Public discussion of the return on capital widened enormously with the success of Thomas Piketty's work on inequality, and the widening brought a confusion this article should explicitly refuse. Piketty's celebrated r, the average rate of return on capital, is not the rate of profit of the present literature, and the two magnitudes can and do move differently. His capital is wealth in the broad sense: housing, land, financial assets, the whole balance sheet of households, valued at market prices, and his return is the flow of capital income, rents, dividends, interest and profits together, set against that wealth. The Marxian rate of the empirical studies is narrower in its stock, restricted to produced assets engaged in production under the researcher's chosen boundary, and narrower or differently composed in its numerator, which the decision tree governs. Confusing the two produces arguments in which a finding about the return on wealth is deployed against a claim about the return on productive capital, or the reverse, with both sides citing real numbers about different objects. The distinction costs one paragraph to state and saves whole debates. It also illustrates the article's master lesson from outside the Marxian tradition altogether: modern economics is populated by ratios that share names in ordinary language and share nothing else, and the first act of literacy with any of them is to demand the numerator, the denominator and the valuation, in that order.

## Open fronts: where the measurement problem is still moving

The survey closes with the fronts on which the measurement problem is actively shifting, since a reader who returns to this literature in later years will find its map redrawn there first. Intangibles, already visited, head the list: as statistical agencies capitalise more categories of knowledge investment and as researchers extend the capitalisation further on their own account, the denominators of the next generation of series will not be the denominators of the canonical studies, and a fresh round of non-comparability between generations is being prepared even now. The multinational organisation of production is the second front. Profit booked where taxes are lowest rather than where production occurs distorts national numerators in ways researchers on profit shifting have documented at increasing scale, and the correction of national series for shifted profits, now being attempted in several quarters, will move the disputed decades' geography: some part of what national series record as the profitability of small, low-tax economies belongs, on any production-based accounting, to the large economies whose series will rise when the correction is made. The third front is the platform and data economy, whose characteristic assets, networks, data holdings, brands built at extraordinary speed, strain both the stock concept and the sector boundaries harder than manufacturing ever did. None of these fronts will abolish the fork; each will give it new material to divide. The reader's equipment for all of them is the equipment this article has built: the tree, the checklist, the demand for the convention before the number, and the knowledge that on the far side of those demands stands a real and partly settled body of evidence, in which a great fall is agreed, a sequel is honestly disputed, and the dispute has been traced to its source.

## Four studies walked down the tree

The decision tree earns its keep when it is applied comparatively, and a short walk down its rows with four canonical constructions in hand will show the reader how the literature's landscape is organised. Take first the historical-cost construction associated with Kliman. Valuation: historical, the defining branch. Profit: broadly the corporate surplus before the distributions he treats as shares of it, with close attention to the difference between the accounts' profit measures and the magnitudes the theory requires. Stock: rebuilt from investment flows at the prices of their years, net of depreciation on stated assumptions, with inventories handled explicitly. Sector: the corporate economy as the accounts bound it, since his quarrel is with the valuation rather than the boundary. Conclusion entailed: a long decline, unreversed. Take second the Duménil and Lévy construction. Valuation: current cost, inherited from the official accounts they deliberately retain. Profit: corporate measures close to the accounts, refined over successive works, with the financial sector's place in the story made a theme rather than an embarrassment. Stock: the agencies' replacement-cost stock, net basis. Conclusion entailed: decline to the early nineteen eighties trough, then recovery substantial enough to anchor an interpretation of the whole following era. The two constructions differ at the first row of the tree and at almost no row do they differ more consequentially; everything downstream is commentary on that first divergence, which is the valuation fork demonstrated on the literature's two most influential bodies of work.

Take third the reconstruction tradition of Shaikh and Tonak as applied by Moseley. Valuation: near the official basis, since the reconstruction's energy goes elsewhere. Profit: rebuilt from the accounts by reclassifying activities, moving the wages and margins of unproductive sectors out of the surplus and into its costs, so the numerator is a theoretical magnitude wearing national dress. Stock: correspondingly rebuilt, with the capital of unproductive activities handled under the same classification. Conclusion entailed: a falling rate across the postwar decades, driven in the decomposition chiefly by the rising weight of unproductive activity, which converts the headline finding into a claim about the changing shape of the economy rather than about mechanisation alone. Take fourth the econometric work in Basu's line. Valuation and sector: inherited from the series under test, with several variants carried through the estimation. Profit: as the tested series define it. Method: the trend is treated as a statistical object, with the econometrician's characteristic finding that the verdict on a downward drift depends on how the trend and the cycle are separated, on the sample, and on the treatment of breaks. Conclusion entailed: qualified support for downward pressure, stated with the sensitivity left in. Four studies, four tempers: the polemicist of the advance, the historians of the current magnitude, the reclassifiers, the econometricians. The tree does not decide between them. It shows a reader exactly where each must be engaged, which is the service a map owes, and it shows that the engagement is possible, row by row, in the open. A literature that can be mapped this cleanly is not in the condition the strongest objection alleges, however far it remains from a verdict.

## Business ratios and the Marxian rate: cousins, not twins

Readers arriving from finance or management often suppose the rate of profit of this literature to be the economist's version of a familiar business ratio, and the supposition breeds errors worth clearing. The return on capital employed of company accounts divides a firm's operating profit by the capital on its balance sheet, where the assets stand substantially at historical cost less depreciation, which makes the business ratio, interestingly, a creature of the historical branch of the fork, with all the vintage effects this article has described, plus the idiosyncrasies of each firm's depreciation policy and acquisition history. The return on equity divides profit by shareholders' funds alone and is therefore levered: two firms with identical operations and different debts print different returns, which disqualifies it as a measure of the return on capital in any economic sense, though it is the ratio shareholders rationally watch. Aggregate studies of business profitability, of the kind central banks and statistical offices publish, build economy-wide cousins of these ratios from the accounts, and their findings on the corporate sector's fortunes belong to the same family as the current or historical series depending on the stock basis each office uses. The Marxian literature's distinctiveness lies not in its arithmetic but in its numerator and its classifications: the surplus concept, the productive boundary, the refusal to treat interest and rent as costs of production rather than as distributions. A business ratio can therefore agree with a Marxian series on direction for a whole era while measuring, strictly, another magnitude, and the reader who cites one as the other adds a category error to whatever measurement errors are already in play. The checklist absorbs the point without strain: before the valuation, before the tax treatment, ask which profit concept, from which intellectual tradition, the number serves.

## The nineteenth century resists the instrument

A natural ambition, to carry the series back to the theory's own century and test the tendency where it was first announced, founders on the record, and the foundering is instructive. Systematic national accounts are a twentieth-century construction; for the nineteenth, the researcher inherits fragmentary censuses, tax records, company archives and the heroic reconstructions of economic historians, among which Charles Feinstein's estimates of the British capital stock stand as a landmark of what can be built. Studies of Victorian profitability exist, built from such materials and from company samples, and they are read with respect by specialists. But every difficulty surveyed in this article is amplified there: the sector boundary is guesswork beside a modern accounts table, depreciation is a matter of the sampled firms' bookkeeping habits, valuation follows whatever basis the surviving ledgers used, and the sample of firms whose records survive is biased toward the successful and the large. The honest verdict of the literature on its own nineteenth-century branch is that it can support narratives and illustrate mechanisms, while the quantitative contest between the tendency and its counteracting influences must be fought on the postwar record, where the instruments exist. Recognising which century can bear which weight of conclusion is part of the same literacy the decision tree teaches, applied to time rather than to convention: the evidence is at its strongest exactly where this article has kept it, in the decades when the statistical state was watching the economy with both eyes open, and it thins rapidly as the gaze is turned backward. Even there, within the strong decades, the fork divides it. Beyond them, the fork is joined by fog.

## What both ledgers agree on, beyond the decline

The split verdict on the disputed decades has monopolised attention, but the two families of series agree on more than the postwar fall, and the further agreements deserve listing because they bound what any future adjudication could conclude. Both families register the squeeze of the nineteen seventies as the deepest profitability crisis of the postwar era, however they date its trough. Both register the recovery, or the failure to collapse further, of the nineteen eighties as bound up with a distributional shift toward capital in the income accounts, visible in the profit share data that neither construction disputes. On most published constructions in both families, the rates of the disputed decades remain below the levels recorded for the high postwar years, so that even the recovery thesis is a thesis about partial restoration within a lower range, not about a return to the golden age. Both families register the financial crisis at the end of the period as a violent interruption whose statistical signature dwarfs ordinary cyclical movement. And both families, read at the level of their authors' prose rather than their polemics, concede the dependence of their conclusions on the conventions this article has catalogued. The agreements do not settle the argument. They define the ground on which the argument is worth having: a shared record of a great fall, a shared distributional story in the national income data, and a single, well-located fork at which the two readings part. Future work that shifted even one of the shared elements, a construction on which the nineteen seventies squeeze failed to appear, say, would revolutionise the field more thoroughly than another decade of fork polemic, and the absence of any such construction, across sixty years of motivated searching by both sides, is among the strongest facts this survey can report.

## The fork in a wider mirror: the capital controversies

The difficulty at the centre of this article will be recognised by historians of economics as a member of a distinguished family. In the nineteen fifties and sixties, the Cambridge capital controversies set economists at Cambridge in England against economists at Cambridge in Massachusetts over a structurally identical problem: whether the value of a capital stock can be measured independently of the prices and distribution it is meant to explain, with the English side arguing that the aggregate capital stock of the textbooks was a construct whose value moved with the very variables under study, sometimes perversely, and the American side defending the construct as a workable approximation. The controversy ended without a surrender document, the defenders conceding the theoretical points in principle while continuing to use the aggregate in practice, and the profession largely walked away from the question rather than answer it. The Marxian empirical literature, arriving at its own version of the problem through the valuation fork, has in one respect handled it better: it cannot walk away, because its central magnitude is a ratio with the contested stock in the denominator, and so it has been obliged to keep the problem open, documented and argued for half a century. The two valuations of the fork are the two horns of the older dilemma made practical: price the stock by its history and the measure is concrete but unit-mixing; price it by the present and the measure is coherent but self-referential, since the prices doing the valuing belong to the system whose performance is on trial.

The kinship carries a consolation and a warning. The consolation is that measurement dominance, the condition named earlier in this article, is not a peculiar vice of Marxian empirics but the local form of a problem that every economics built on aggregate capital must meet, and the Marxian literature meets it with more disclosure than most. The warning is the fate of the older controversy itself: a measurement problem that a field finds inconvenient can be forgotten without being solved, and the forgetting always returns as error when the magnitudes are used by audiences who never heard of the problem. The audiences for profit-rate claims, students, journalists, partisans of every camp, are exactly such audiences. Writing the fork into the citation, every time, is this literature's defence against the forgetting, and it is the habit this article has tried to make reflexive in its reader.

## The summit problem: how high was the postwar peak?

Every decline is measured from a height, and the height from which postwar profitability fell deserves the same scepticism as the fall itself. The rates of the high postwar years stand at the start of nearly every series in this literature, and they were recorded under conditions that flatter them: economies rebuilding war-damaged or deferred capital, so that output ran ahead of a stock still catching up with its own replacement; labour forces expanding; and, in several countries, a stock denominator thinned by the destruction and obsolescence of the war years. A rate computed against an unusually small denominator is unusually high, and part of the subsequent decline, on any construction, is the arithmetic of normalisation as the stock was rebuilt to match the output it supported. The point cuts against no camp in particular, since it qualifies the summit from which both families descend, but it disciplines the rhetoric of the golden age that surrounds this literature. The postwar peak was real as a measurement; as a benchmark of health it needs the same interrogation as any other point on the line, and the studies that begin their series in the reconstruction years without discussing the denominator's condition are making a silent choice at the very first observation. Start dates, like end dates, are conclusions in disguise.

The summit problem also explains a feature of the cross-country record that otherwise looks paradoxical. The economies most devastated by the war display, in several reconstructions, the highest early rates and the steepest subsequent descents, not because their capitalisms were the most dynamic and then the most exhausted, but because the denominator effect was largest where destruction was greatest. Comparative work that ignores the starting condition will read a measurement artefact as a national biography. The lesson is by now familiar in form: every feature of these series, the peak, the slope, the trough, the recovery, is jointly a fact about an economy and a fact about a construction, and the proportions differ by feature. Maturity in this field is knowing, for the feature under discussion, roughly what the proportions are, and saying so.

## A third way that is not a compromise: the stock in constant prices

Between the two great valuations there exists a third construction that readers will meet and should not mistake for a reconciliation. Some studies measure the capital stock in constant prices, valuing every vintage at the prices of a chosen base year, or, in the agencies' chained volume measures, at prices updated year by year in a chain. The intention is to track the physical volume of the stock, its growth as a quantity, purged of the price movements that the fork argues about. The construction has real uses, particularly for the output-to-capital ratio, where a volume denominator matches a volume numerator. As a basis for the rate of profit it faces a conceptual difficulty its users acknowledge: the numerator, profit, is a sum of money in the prices of the year, and dividing a money magnitude by a volume magnitude produces a hybrid whose movements partly reflect the price indices used in the deflation. The chained measures add a technical snare of their own, since chained volume components are not additive, so the stock assembled from them does not equal the sum of its parts in any year but the reference year. None of this disqualifies the third way for the questions it suits. It does mean its series cannot be summoned to break the tie between the two camps, because it does not measure what either camp measures: not the return on the historical advance, and not the return on the apparatus at the prices of the day, but a third magnitude whose interpretation must be built separately. The fork, properly understood, is not a failure to find the middle of two options. It is the discovery that the options answer different questions, and adding options multiplies questions rather than averaging answers. A reader who has absorbed that sentence has absorbed the article.

## The whole span read at once: three eras under both ledgers

Step back from the components and read the postwar span whole, as each ledger narrates it, and the literature's geography becomes a story with three acts whose scripts diverge only in the third. The first act, from the later nineteen forties to the mid nineteen sixties, is the high plateau: profitability at levels neither ledger expects to see again, sustained by reconstruction, by expanding labour forces and, on the summit discussion offered earlier, by denominators still thin from the war. Both constructions record the plateau, and both record its end arriving, in economy after economy, in the later nineteen sixties, as wage pressure built, productivity growth faltered and the stock caught up with the output it served. The second act, from that turning to the trough of the early nineteen eighties, is the agreed decline in its narrative form: the squeeze decade, the energy shocks striking an already weakening position, governments and central banks lurching between expedients, profitability in every major economy compressed to levels that made the era's politics unintelligible without it. On the broad shape of the first two acts the ledgers are one text with two paginations.

The third act is where the texts separate, and it is the act the present inhabits in the literature's telling. On the replacement-cost script, the third act is a restoration drama: disinflation, wage discipline, the restructuring of industry and the rise of finance rebuild the rate from its trough across two decades, until the financial crisis cuts the recovery down and poses the question of its quality in the harshest form. On the historical-cost script, the third act is a long defensive campaign fought on a falling field: the same expedients appear, but as counteracting influences straining against a tendency that never released its grip, their very frenzy testifying to the pressure they answer, and the crisis arrives not as an interruption of health but as the exposure of an unhealed condition. The two scripts share every event. They differ in the meaning the events are given by the line that runs beneath them, and the line is drawn by a valuation. A survey of evidence can go no further than to make that structure unmistakably visible, which this one has tried to do, and to insist that readers who quote either script quote it as a script: authored, constructed, defensible, and rivalled.

## Replication and its discontents

A final workshop matter deserves record, because it explains some of the heat in the exchanges and points to where the field's standards are heading. Profit-rate series are unusually hard to replicate exactly. The construction chains described in this article, from a stated vintage of the accounts through dozens of alignment, bridging and classification decisions, are rarely documented to the last link even by conscientious authors, and the underlying data are themselves revised away beneath the replicator's feet, so that the accounts as they now stand cannot reproduce a series built on the accounts as they stood a decade ago. Attempts to rebuild a rival's series from the published description commonly arrive close in shape and off in detail, and the residual difference becomes a fresh object of dispute: sloppiness, concealment, or the ordinary entropy of a long calculation. The field's response, visible in its stronger recent work, mirrors the response of empirical economics generally: fuller methodological appendices, published code and data where the sources allow, and a growing courtesy of reporting the rival construction's result beside one's own. The direction is welcome and incomplete. For the reader, the lesson is the audit habit once more, applied to oneself as much as to authors: an inability to replicate a number from its stated construction is information about the number's status, and the number should be cited, if at all, with that status attached. Evidence that cannot be rebuilt can still be believed, on the credit of its builder, but it is believed differently, and the difference belongs in the citation.

One institutional development works quietly in replication's favour and deserves to be better known among this literature's readers. Vintage data archives, of which the archival service maintained by the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis for United States series is the best developed example, preserve economic statistics as they stood at each past release date, so a researcher can summon the accounts in the form a predecessor actually faced rather than in their endlessly corrected descendants. Where a profit-rate study's inputs survive in such an archive, its arithmetic can be re-performed on the original ground, and disputes about what a study found can be separated from disputes about what later revisions did to its foundations. The coverage of these archives is incomplete, strongest for headline American series and patchier for the detailed industry and asset tables on which the finer constructions depend, and the European sources are less generously preserved. Even so, their existence changes the moral weather of replication quarrels: a growing share of the canonical results can, in principle, be rebuilt as their builders built them, and the share that cannot increasingly reflects choices about preservation rather than any author's wish to escape checking. Future contributions to this literature will be expected, by reviewers formed in this environment, to deposit their construction where it can be re-walked, and the expectation is among the healthiest the field has generated.

## The disputed decades on the ground: what both scripts must explain

Behind the rival lines lies a shared inventory of events that any reading of the disputed decades must digest, and rehearsing it shows how completely the same material feeds both scripts. The disinflation at the start of the nineteen eighties, engineered by central banks at the cost of deep recessions, broke the price spiral that had driven the two valuations apart and reset the monetary conditions of measurement themselves. Wage bargaining was restructured across the major economies: union density fell, pattern bargaining weakened, and the profit share of national income, the one magnitude neither ledger disputes, climbed back from its squeezed levels in country after country. Corporate taxation was cut and its base rearranged in successive reforms. Finance was deregulated and globalised, multiplying the earnings whose classification this article has shown to be a decision rather than a datum. Production internationalised: supply chains lengthened across borders, manufacturing employment shrank in the old industrial economies, and the boundary of the firm and of the national economy, two more of the tree's quiet rows, blurred together. Computing equipment cheapened at the pace described in the counteracting-influences discussion, transforming both the physical composition of the stock and the prices at which the stock is valued. Each item in the inventory is documented history. None of it arrives with its significance attached.

Watch the two scripts metabolise the same inventory. Wage restraint, in the restoration script, is the mechanism of recovery: the surplus share rises, the numerator fattens, profitability is rebuilt on the only foundation it can have. In the defensive-campaign script, the same restraint is evidence of the pressure it answers: a system whose underlying return was sound would not have required a generation-long offensive against the wage share merely to hold its rate at levels still below the postwar plateau. Financial expansion, in the first script, is a sector earning its place in a diversifying economy; in the second, it is the migration of claims on a surplus whose productive core earns too little, the system paying itself fees for circulation because production alone no longer yields the accustomed return. Globalisation reads, in the first, as the rational extension of accumulation to new fields; in the second, as the search for cheaper labour made urgent by the rate at home. The inventory cannot choose between the scripts, because the scripts differ not over the inventory but over the line that runs beneath it, and the line is drawn in the workshop this article has spent its length describing. That is the final form of the lesson the evidence teaches: facts about the era are plentiful, and every camp commands them; the scarcity is in the measurement, and it is there, at the fork, that the argument must be joined by anyone who wishes to change the conclusion rather than merely repeat it at higher volume.

## The uses of a number that must travel with its scaffolding

One more consequence of everything established here reaches beyond scholarship into the ordinary traffic of argument, and a survey of the evidence should state it before closing the main discussion. Profit-rate figures are among the most conscripted numbers in public life. They are summoned to prove that an economic order is flourishing or failing, that a government's programme has worked or wrecked, that a coming crisis is certain or impossible, usually by speakers who encountered the figure third-hand and could not name a single decision in its construction. The survey's discipline offers those audiences something better than scepticism, which curdles into the belief that all numbers are propaganda. It offers a triage. A figure accompanied by its construction can be engaged, placed on the spectrum the fork creates, and weighed against its rivals; the engagement takes minutes and the weighing is real knowledge. A figure stripped of its construction can be set aside without loss, since nothing has actually been asserted beyond the speaker's allegiance. Between engagement and dismissal there is no third duty, and performing the triage in public, asking the naming questions aloud whenever the number is produced, is how the citation standard this article recommends propagates from a literature that already practises it to the audiences that cite it. The evidence on the rate of profit, handled this way, is neither a weapon nor an embarrassment. It is what this survey found it to be: a partly settled record, an honestly open question, and a standing lesson in how economic knowledge is built.

## One firm, two ledgers: the fork at ground level

The national controversy can be felt at the scale of a single enterprise, and feeling it there dispels any suspicion that the fork is a statisticians' artefact. A firm's statutory accounts carry its plant substantially at what was paid for it, less the depreciation its policies have written off: the historical ledger, kept because the law and the tax authority want the record of actual transactions. The same firm's managers, deciding whether to replace a line, expand a plant or abandon a product, reason in the other ledger entirely. They ask what the capacity would cost to install now, what return the money committed now must earn to clear the firm's hurdle rate, and whether the existing assets, valued at what they would fetch or what they would cost to reproduce, are earning their keep against that present standard. A works manager who discovers that a beloved and fully depreciated line, a star of the statutory accounts because its denominator has been written down to nearly nothing, cannot earn the cost of its own replacement has met the valuation fork in person. On the historical ledger the line is a marvel of profitability. On the replacement ledger it is a candidate for closure, consuming a site, a workforce and a maintenance budget that together represent a present stock far larger than its book value.

This duality inside the firm explains a pattern in the national debate that otherwise puzzles newcomers, namely why practitioners speak past the academic controversy as though it did not exist. Business has always kept both ledgers for different purposes and felt no contradiction, because the purposes are visibly different at ground level: stewardship and taxation look backward to the sums committed, while decisions look forward from the sums that would now be required. The academic controversy becomes sharp only when a single question, did the return on capital fall across forty years, is demanded of both ledgers at once, and the answer is expected to guide judgements about the health of a whole economic order. At that altitude the purposes blur, and the choice of ledger becomes a choice of verdict. The ground-level view does not resolve the choice, but it purifies it: nobody at the works believes one of the ledgers is fraudulent, and nobody should believe it of the national series either. What the ground level also shows, quietly, is that the two questions the ledgers answer are both real, which is this article's closing word on the fork. The advance was made, and its reward can be reckoned. The apparatus stands, and its present yield can be reckoned. A literature, and a reader, large enough to hold both reckonings at once, and to say which one is on the table whenever a number is produced, has nothing to fear from the evidence on the rate of profit, and a great deal to learn from it.

## For students and exam candidates

Your task with this evidence is to state the split verdict exactly, since examiners reward the candidate who knows what is agreed and what is not. Write that measured rates fell from the mid nineteen sixties to the early nineteen eighties on both valuation conventions, and that the sequel depends on whether the stock is valued at historical or at current replacement cost, naming the fork and the decision tree rows that carry your answer. Cite series with their constructions attached, never as bare figures, and when a question invites a verdict on the tendency itself, give the evidence its conditional form and spend the marks on the conditioning. If you use the recovery literature, attribute it to its current-cost basis; if you use the decline literature, attribute it to its historical-cost basis, and show, in one sentence, that you know each would answer differently under the other's convention. That sentence, more than any figure, is what distinguishes the candidate who has studied the evidence from the candidate who has collected its conclusions.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

This literature is a gift to teaching, because it lets students watch a measurement being made and disputed by serious people in full public view. Set the fork before any series: have the seminar compute, from the toy arithmetic in this article, what a decade of inflation does to the two denominators, and only then distribute the rival charts, which the students can now audit rather than admire. Assign the decision tree as an instrument, not a summary: each student walks one published study down its rows and reports the branches, and the class discovers for itself how much of the conclusion the first row carries. Insist on the citation discipline in written work, marking down any profit-rate figure that arrives without its construction, as you would mark down an undated source. The deeper lesson generalises beyond this theory: a cohort that has learned to ask for the convention before the number, on a topic where the convention reverses the answer, has learned the single most transferable skill empirical economics has to teach, and it will apply the skill to every statistic it meets thereafter.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Your layer carries the heaviest duties in this field, because your citations are the ones that harden into common knowledge. Researchers extending these series should treat the reporting standard set out in this article as a floor: branches named on every row of the tree, sensitivity shown against the rival valuation, the financial treatment and the sector boundary, vintages stated, endpoints defended rather than inherited. Where your construction concedes ground against its own grain, print the concession prominently; concessions of that kind are the currency in which this literature's credibility is held. Historians using profitability as evidence for the character of an era should match the era claim to the series that can bear it, remembering that the strongest claims available, the agreed decline and its decomposition, are also the ones with the widest support, while the era-defining recovery or non-recovery rests on a convention that must be argued, not assumed. Journalists and analysts face the sharpest version of the discipline, since your sentences travel furthest with the least scaffolding attached: the sentence you can safely write names the economy, the period, the construction and the direction, and the sentence you cannot write is the one that reports the rate of profit as having simply risen or fallen. A finding logged with its construction, in whatever note system you keep, remains usable years later; a finding logged as a naked number is a liability waiting for a careful reader. The field's own history shows what happens to naked numbers: they are quoted by everyone, believed by no one who checks, and they bring the whole evidence base into a disrepute its careful practitioners never earned.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Has the rate of profit actually fallen?

On the evidence surveyed in this article, measured rates in the major developed economies fell from the mid nineteen sixties to the early nineteen eighties under both main measurement conventions, and that segment commands broad agreement across rival researchers. What happened afterwards is disputed along the valuation fork: series built on a historical-cost capital stock generally report continued decline, while series built at current replacement cost generally report a partial recovery. The accurate short answer is therefore a split verdict, and any answer that keeps only one half of it is quoting a convention as though it were the world.

### Q: How do you measure the rate of profit?

A researcher assembles the measure from national accounts rather than reading it off them. A profit concept is chosen and built, whether the surplus before interest, rent and tax or the profit enterprises retain; a sector is bounded; and the capital stock is constructed by cumulating past investment under the perpetual inventory method, valued at historical cost or at current replacement cost. The ratio of the first magnitude to the second, with decisions also taken on depreciation, inventories and capacity utilisation, is the measured rate. Each decision is recorded in the measurement decision tree set out in this article.

### Q: Why does historical cost versus current cost matter?

Because the two valuations answer to two different concepts of capital and diverge mechanically whenever prices have moved. Historical cost sums what was actually spent on the stock, vintage by vintage, while current cost reprices the whole stock at the prices of the year being studied. A decade of strong inflation drives the two sums far apart, pressing the current-cost rate down while inflation runs and lifting it when inflation subsides, while the historical-cost rate follows the slow digestion of expensive vintages. Across the disputed decades the choice between them is large enough to reverse the reported direction of the rate, which is why this article names it the valuation fork.

### Q: Which profit measure should you use?

The measure that matches the question being asked, stated openly when the result is reported. For questions about the surplus the system generates and struggles over, a pre-distribution measure, before interest, rent and tax, is the coherent choice. For questions about the incentive facing the individual enterprise, the profit it retains after its obligations is the relevant magnitude. For testing the classical tendency, the measure must follow the concept of capital the researcher defends at the valuation fork. There is no universally correct numerator; there are only numerators whose choice has been disclosed and defended, and figures quoted without that disclosure should not be relied on.

### Q: Do the studies agree on the trend?

They agree on the first part of the postwar record and disagree on the sequel. Across constructions, countries and research camps, measured profit rates in the major developed economies fell from the mid nineteen sixties to the early nineteen eighties. After the trough the studies divide, and the division tracks the valuation of the capital stock closely enough that a study's convention predicts its conclusion. The studies also agree on several secondary features, including the depth of the nineteen seventies squeeze and the violence of the financial crisis's mark on the series. Agreement and disagreement in this literature are both informative, and both should be reported when its findings are cited.

### Q: Has anyone measured a world rate of profit?

Yes, world-level series have been constructed by aggregating national accounts across many countries, and their authors have generally reported, on the constructions they defend, a long postwar decline with fluctuations around it. Such series should be handled with more caution than any national one, because they inherit every national measurement decision and add difficulties of their own: incompatible statistical systems, the choice between market exchange rates and purchasing-power parities for conversion, thin data for the earlier decades across much of the world, and the dominance of a few large economies in any aggregate. Breadth of coverage is real in these studies; the precision their single line suggests is not.

### Q: What data sources are used for profit rate studies?

The studies rest on official national accounts and the capital stock statistics built alongside them. For the United States the foundation is the national income and product accounts and fixed assets statistics of the Bureau of Economic Analysis. European work draws on national statistical offices and on harmonised collections such as the European Commission's AMECO database, OECD programmes and the EU KLEMS industry dataset, while world studies adapt international panels such as the Penn World Table. Researchers then reorganise these sources, which were designed for macroeconomic management, into the categories their theory requires. Because the accounts are revised and occasionally redefined, careful studies state which vintage of the data they used.

### Q: Does the choice of sector change the result?

Yes, in level and sometimes in trend, because the boundaries define different objects rather than rival views of one object. Manufacturing, the traditional site of the theory and the sector most exposed to international competition and relocation, has on most constructions fallen harder and behaved differently from the corporate sector as a whole. Whole-economy measures are steadier but must absorb the state, housing and imputed activities of the household sector. A study's conclusion therefore cannot be detached from its boundary, and apparent contradictions between studies frequently dissolve when one is found to be quoting a manufacturing series against another's economy-wide series.

### Q: Why do Marxist economists reach opposite conclusions?

Chiefly because they measure with different conventions, not because they consult different worlds and not, on the documentary record, because of bad faith on either side. The same national accounts feed both families of series; the divergence enters at the decisions catalogued in the measurement decision tree, above all the valuation of the capital stock at historical or current cost. Theoretical commitments do predict which convention a researcher defends, since each convention embodies a concept of capital with roots in the founding texts, and that alignment is the legitimate core of the charge that the field's empirics are theory-laden. The researchers involved publish their procedures and answer one another in detail, which is why the disagreement can be mapped precisely rather than merely lamented.

### Q: What would count as decisive evidence on the profit rate?

No new dataset can be decisive, because the divergence between the rival series is not caused by missing data but by a choice about what the data mean, and the accounts are abundant and shared. Decisive movement would have to come from the theory side: an argued settlement of which concept of capital, the historical advance or the current apparatus, the tendency law quantifies over. A subsidiary route is comparative performance, if one candidate magnitude proved markedly better at explaining investment behaviour and the timing of crises than the other. Until one of those routes delivers, the defensible position is the split verdict this article reports, with the condition named.

### Q: How does the measurement decision tree change a profit-rate series?

The tree does not change any series; it changes what a reader can do with one. By laying out each construction decision, the options at it, the effect of each option on the measured trend and the researchers who take each branch, the tree converts a published figure from a verdict into an argument that can be audited row by row. Walked down the tree, most apparent contradictions between studies resolve into differences of branch, and the disagreements that survive are the genuine ones, concentrated at the valuation fork. A series whose author has not disclosed enough to be walked down the tree cannot be assessed at all, which is itself the most useful verdict the tree delivers.

### Q: What separates historic-cost from current-cost valuation in the data?

In the data, the separation is a repricing. Both valuations start from the same record of investment flows under the perpetual inventory method. Current cost then revalues every surviving vintage at the prices of the measurement year, which is the basis on which statistical agencies publish the stock, so the stock rises with capital-goods inflation even when nothing is added physically. Historic cost keeps each vintage at the prices of its own year, which the agencies do not publish, so the researcher must rebuild the stock from the flows. After an inflationary era the two stocks differ enormously, and the profit rate, computed against each, differs with them.

### Q: Why do pre-tax and post-tax series tell different stories?

Because the tax taken from corporate profit is one of the largest distributions of the surplus and its burden has moved substantially across the period under study. A pre-tax series tracks the surplus production generated before the state's share; a post-tax series tracks what enterprises retained, and where tax burdens were cut across the disputed decades, as they were in several major economies, the post-tax series climbs relative to the pre-tax one and the apparent recovery of profitability strengthens. Neither story is false; they are answers to different questions. Confusion begins only when a figure from one is quoted in a debate about the other, or when the two are compared as rival readings of a single magnitude.

### Q: How should a researcher handle financial sector profits in the measure?

Deliberately, disclosedly, and preferably twice. Financial profits were a small share of the total during the agreed decline, so the decision once mattered little; across the disputed decades the sector's share grew so large that including or excluding its earnings can carry a conclusion. One tradition treats financial earnings as a deduction from surplus produced elsewhere and excludes or nets them; another treats the boundary as untenable in the age of the financialised firm and includes them. The practice this article commends is the one the better literature has adopted: build the series both ways, publish both, and report how much of the headline conclusion survives the switch.

### Q: What makes cross-country profit-rate comparisons so fragile?

The comparisons must reconcile statistical systems that were never designed to agree. Depreciation conventions, sector boundaries, the treatment of self-employment and of the informal economy, and the vintage and revision history of the accounts all differ between countries, so the inputs are commensurable only after harmonisation work that is itself judgement-laden. Conversion and aggregation add further choices for multi-country measures. The comparisons that command confidence in this literature are those among a small set of developed economies whose differences have been studied explicitly, such as the work spanning the United States, the large European economies and Japan; the wider the comparison reaches, the longer the list of conventions that must travel with its numbers.
