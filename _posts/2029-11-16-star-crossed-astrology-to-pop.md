Star-crossed is the most successful astrological term ever coined for a stage, and its success is the measure of a disappearance. When the Chorus of Romeo and Juliet pronounced the lovers star-cross'd in the Prologue, the word was a technical verdict delivered inside a working science: the configurations of the heavens at a birth could favour a life or cross it, and a crossing was not a metaphor but a mechanism, as concrete to that audience as weather. The verdict this article defends is that the phrase's journey from astrology to pop culture is not a story of survival but of amputation. The modern cliché kept the doom and dropped the cosmos. It preserved the conclusion of the astrological judgment, that this couple cannot thrive, while discarding everything that once made the judgment intelligible: the spheres, the aspects, the malefic planets, the cast chart, and the professional reader paid to interpret them. What circulates in headlines, song titles, and valentine prose as star-crossed is therefore a conclusion without its premises, a sentence still being pronounced by a court that no longer sits.

The evidence for the amputation runs through every stage of the word's documented life. Merriam-Webster gives the first known use of star-crossed as 1597, the play's own years, and defines it with a fossil embedded in the definition: not favored by the stars, ill-fated. The first half of that definition is astrology; the second half is what remains when the astrology is forgotten. Reference works divide on the word's exact birthday, the Random House tradition dating the record to 1585-95 and Merriam-Webster to 1597, and the division itself is instructive, because a word whose birth certificate cannot be produced can still have its career documented in full. What the record shows is a term born as doctrine, glossed by editors for centuries with the doctrine's own vocabulary, thwarted by adverse aspects of stars, and then gradually re-employed by a culture that had stopped believing in aspects altogether. By the time a country singer could title a divorce album star-crossed in 2021 and a television network could literalize the stars as visiting aliens in 2014, the word was doing emotional work that its inventors would have found unrecognizable: it had become a compliment to intensity, a way of saying that a love mattered too much for the world, pronounced in a universe with nobody home above the ceiling.

The method of what follows is the method of the record. Every claim about the belief is stated at the precision a named witness supports: a dictionary's dating for the word, an abbey's account for a coronation, a historian's sales figure for the almanacs, a casebook's survival for the consulting room. Where the witnesses divide, as they divide over the word's first record, the division is printed rather than resolved, because a study of how a phrase lost its content should not begin by inventing some. The argument proceeds from the system outward: first the cosmos the word presupposes, then the trade that applied it, then the play that pronounced it, then the long custodianship of glossators, anthologists, schoolrooms, and columnists under whom the content drained away. The verdict announced above will be re-earned at each stage or not at all.

## What Did Star-Crossed Mean When Astrology Was a Science?

To recover the word's weight, the first task is to take the belief seriously as its holders held it, because the condescension of hindsight is the cliché's oldest accomplice. In the England of the 1590s, astrology was not a hobby at the edge of knowledge but a department of knowledge near its centre, taught in the universities as part of astronomy, practised by physicians as part of diagnosis, and consulted by statesmen as part of policy. The historian Keith Thomas, whose Religion and the Decline of Magic (1971) remains the standard map of that world, put the standing of the doctrine in a sentence that should be pinned above every modern use of the word: no one denied the influence of the heavens upon the weather or disputed the relevance of astrology to medicine or agriculture, and before the seventeenth century total scepticism about astrological doctrine was highly exceptional, whether in England or elsewhere. A playgoer hearing the Chorus say star-cross'd was hearing a claim about causation from the period's most prestigious explanatory system, not a decorative flourish from its poetry.

The system's intellectual pedigree was ancient and respectable. Its textbook was Ptolemy's Tetrabiblos, the second-century treatise that organized the art of reading the heavens into a discipline with rules, tables, and a technical vocabulary that endured for fourteen centuries. Around that core the medieval universities built a curriculum in which astronomy, the mathematics of celestial motion, and astrology, the interpretation of celestial influence, were two halves of one study of the sky. The same astronomer who computed a planet's position interpreted its meaning; Johannes Kepler, the architect of the laws of planetary motion, cast horoscopes for the imperial general Wallenstein, and the conjunction was not hypocrisy but job description. Astrology's claim on the educated mind rested on an observed regularity anyone could verify nightly: the heavens moved with a precision no earthly mechanism matched, and they visibly governed the largest facts of terrestrial life, the seasons, the tides, the calendar. A system that predicted the return of spring could plausibly be extended to predict the course of a fever or the fortune of a marriage, and the extension was drawn by the best mathematical minds in Europe.

The doctrine's deepest root in ordinary experience was the body itself. Learned medicine held that the four humours, blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile, composed each person's complexion, and that the planets ruled the humours' balance: Saturn over melancholy, Mars over choler, Jupiter over blood, Venus and the Moon over phlegm and moisture. A temperament was therefore a horoscope made flesh, and the period's everyday psychology, sanguine, choleric, melancholic, phlegmatic, is astrology's vocabulary still walking around in modern mouths with its charts forgotten, a rehearsal in miniature for the fate of star-crossed. The physician who cast a figure for a fever and the astrologer who cast one for a marriage were reading the same sky for the same reason, that the heavens had written the patient's constitution at birth and kept annotating it thereafter. When the Chorus's compound named a marriage's doom, it used the grammar in which its hearers' own bodies had been explained to them since childhood. The word's first authority was not literary and not even, in the first instance, theatrical. It was clinical.

Beneath the prestige stood a social fact that mattered more to the play's first audience: astrology was the period's ordinary technology for managing uncertainty. Thomas's England was a place where crops failed without forecast, children died without diagnosis, and fire, plague, and shipwreck arrived without warning or remedy. In that environment the astrologer offered what no other institution supplied, a dated, specific, actionable account of misfortune: when to bleed a patient, when to sow, when to marry, when to stay home. The art flourished because the demand was real and the alternatives were absent. When the Chorus told a London audience that the lovers were crossed by the stars, every listener had either consulted such a reading, paid for an almanac containing one, or heard one disputed in a sermon that took its power for granted even while condemning its use. The word arrived in the theatre trailing the authority of the consulting room, the sickroom, and the privy council. No pop usage since has carried a fraction of that institutional weight, and the difference between the two hearings is the subject of everything that follows.

### What did the word promise a listener who believed it?

A listener who believed the doctrine heard a diagnosis, not a mood. Star-crossed promised that the lovers' ruin had a cause outside human malice, legible in advance to a trained reader, neither accidental nor anyone's simple fault. It converted a feud's squalid violence into a consequence written into the heavens' structure, sadder than blame and harder to appeal.

The belief was never a monolith, and the article's verdict does not need it to have been one. The Church had long policed a boundary inside astrology itself, tolerating what the schools called natural astrology, the influence of the heavens on bodies, weather, and humours, while condemning judicial astrology, the claim to foretell particular human choices and destinies, as an encroachment on providence and free will. The distinction was scholastic, unstable, and widely ignored in practice, since the same chart that timed a dose of physic could be read for the patient's recovery and the same election that chose a coronation day trespassed on the largest human choices of all. Dissenters spoke from inside the culture rather than outside it. Within a decade of the play, the playwright himself put the classic rebuttal into Edmund's mouth in King Lear, where the bastard mocks the excellent foppery of the world that makes the gods guilty of our disasters and lays human villainy at the door of planetary influence. The mockery lands because the audience knew the doctrine well enough to enjoy its puncturing. Belief in the stars was the default setting of the age, contested at its edges by theologians and satirists, exactly as a reigning science is always contested, and the Prologue's word drew its force from that default. A cliché draws force from repetition; the original drew force from assent, and assent is the first thing the journey spent.

The assent coexisted with a rival determinism, and the rivalry sharpened both. The same culture that consulted astrologers confessed a providence that governed every sparrow's fall, and the two systems competed for the same explanatory territory: was a child's death written in Saturn's aspect or in God's decree, and what room did either leave for prayer, repentance, or blame? John Calvin's Admonition Against Judicial Astrology of 1549 attacked the art from inside the Reformed camp, not as bad astronomy but as stolen property, an attempt to read by the stars what God had reserved to his secret counsel. English preachers repeated the charge from pulpits for a century, and the repetition is itself evidence of the doctrine's grip: sermons do not spend generations refuting what nobody believes. The result was a layered fatalism in the ordinary mind, in which the stars functioned as providence's visible clerks, executing in the heavens what heaven had ordained, and a crossed nativity could be received as a decree's local publication. The Chorus's audience therefore heard the word inside two reinforcing systems of foreknowledge, ecclesiastical and celestial, each certifying the other. Modern usage inherits from neither. It keeps the emotional posture of the congregation and the congregation's certainty that the ending is known in advance, while discarding both the God and the geometry that once made the certainty a doctrine rather than a mood.

## The Cosmos the Word Presupposes: Spheres, Planets, and Aspects

Star-crossed presupposes a machine, and the machine deserves describing, because a word cannot be emptied until its contents are inventoried. The cosmos of Renaissance astrology was the Ptolemaic universe: a spherical Earth at the centre, wrapped in nested transparent spheres that carried the Moon, Mercury, Venus, the Sun, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn, with the fixed stars set in the outermost sphere beyond them. Everything below the Moon was the sublunary world of change, decay, and mixture; everything above moved in perfect circles at unchanging speeds. Influence flowed downward across that architecture. The planets, wandering against the fixed stars along the band of the zodiac, radiated qualities, hot and cold, wet and dry, benign and malign, whose combinations at any moment conditioned the sublunary world the way weather conditions a harvest. A person's birth froze one such combination into a constitution. The chart cast for that moment, the nativity or geniture, was therefore read as a kind of celestial deed to a life: not a script of events but a schedule of strengths, vulnerabilities, and seasons, which a skilled reader could interpret the way a physician interprets a pulse.

The chart's fine structure gave the verdict its texture. The zodiac's twelve signs were distributed among the planets as their houses by day and by night, so that Mars ruled Scorpio and Aries, Saturn ruled Capricorn and Aquarius, and a planet standing in its own house spoke with a landlord's authority while a planet in exile or detriment spoke weakly and askew. Over the whole figure the astrologer laid the twelve mundane houses, counted from the ascendant, the degree rising in the east at the birth moment, and each house governed a province of the life: the first the body and temperament, the seventh marriage and open enemies, the eighth death and the partner's goods, the tenth honour and profession. A verdict was the sum of hundreds of such placements, dignities, receptions, and aspects, weighted by rules that filled the textbooks. When the Chorus compresses the audit into one compound word, the compression is the miracle the first audience savoured: an entire science's paperwork, the ephemeris, the table of houses, the judgment, folded into two syllables that scan. The play trusts its hearers to supply the paperwork from their own acquaintance with the trade, the way a modern audience supplies a laboratory from the words test results. A modern hearer supplies nothing, and the word arrives as pure verdict, paperwork burned.

Within that machine, the operative relations were the aspects, the angular distances between planets as seen from Earth, and the aspects are where the word crossed gets its technical sense. Planets in conjunction occupied the same zodiacal ground and compounded their natures. A sextile of sixty degrees and a trine of one hundred and twenty were the harmonious aspects, easy channels through which benefic influence flowed. A square of ninety degrees and an opposition of one hundred and eighty were the hostile aspects, the configurations in which planetary qualities obstructed, contradicted, and thwarted one another. Editors glossing the Prologue have preserved the vocabulary with precision: the Shakespeare Navigators edition prepared by Philip Weller glosses star-cross'd as thwarted by adverse aspects of stars. The gloss deserves dwelling on, because it shows what the word once compressed. Crossed is the past participle of a geometry. To be crossed was to have one's ruling configurations set at hostile angles, to stand at the intersection where the heavens' influences cancelled rather than compounded. The planets responsible for the worst crossings had names and reputations: Saturn, the greater malefic, cold and dry, governor of melancholy, delay, and death; Mars, the lesser malefic, hot and dry, governor of anger, wounds, and sudden violence. A nativity dominated by the malefics in hard aspect was a life pronounced difficult before it began, and a marriage joining two such nativities doubled the exposure.

The machine also ran at civic scale, and its civic failures are the doctrine's best-documented embarrassments. Mundane astrology read the great conjunctions, the periodic meetings of Jupiter and Saturn that served as the calendar of world history, and the conjunctions of 1524 produced the art's most famous false alarm: with several planets gathered in Pisces, a water sign, astrologers across Europe predicted a second deluge for February of that year, and the prediction ran through more than a hundred pamphlets while the anxious built boats, stocked hilltop refuges, and in places moved households to high ground. February brought rain in parts of Europe and no flood anywhere, and the pamphlets' authors spent years explaining the miss. The episode matters here for what it proves about the system's standing seven decades before the play. Astrology could be wrong in public, at continental scale, in print, and lose none of its authority, because its failures were processed as errors of interpretation inside a framework nobody proposed to discard, exactly as a failed forecast dents a meteorologist and not meteorology. The Moon, meanwhile, did the system's daily work: nearest of the wanderers and swiftest, she gathered the planets' influences and delivered them to the sublunary world, governed the humours and the tides of blood, and timed everything from surgery to sowing in the almanac's monthly pages. A culture that bled patients by her phases and planted by her signs did not experience celestial influence as a theory. It experienced it as the calendar.

### What exactly did an adverse aspect do to a life, in the system's own terms?

An adverse aspect obstructed the flow of influence the way a blocked channel obstructs water. The square and the opposition set planetary qualities against each other, so that what one planet promised, another confiscated: vigour cancelled by melancholy, desire inflamed then denied, timing corrupted until right actions fell on wrong days. The damage was mechanical before it was moral.

The play's own text speaks this machinery fluently, and a handful of its utterances are enough to show the fluency without trespassing on the imagery study that owns the system whole. Romeo, before the Capulet feast, confesses that his mind misgives some consequence yet hanging in the stars, which shall bitterly begin its fearful date with that night's revels (1.4): the vocabulary is calendrical, a consequence suspended in the heavens awaiting its date like a debt awaiting its term. In Mantua, receiving the false report of Juliet's death, he answers the heavens in their own grammar of command and defiance, then I defy you, stars (5.1), a vassal renouncing fealty to the powers that had held his course. The Friar, surveying the tomb, reaches for the same court of appeal and finds it empty: a greater power than we can contradict hath thwarted our intents (5.3). Thwarted is the Prologue's crossed translated into narrative. The word in the Chorus is therefore not an isolated ornament but the thesis of a vocabulary the whole play speaks, the summary judgment of a system whose operations the drama then exhibits scene by scene. The companion study of the play's star imagery maps that system at the length it deserves, in [the analysis of the heavens and astrology imagery across the whole play](/2024/06/14/stars-astrology-imagery-analysis/); the point here is narrower. In 1597, star-crossed named a diagnosis inside a physics. The diagnosis could be wrong, and the physics would prove false, but both were stated in a technical language that its culture could parse, dispute, and apply. A modern listener parses none of it, and the word's long second life begins in that deafness.

## The Astrologer's Trade: Nativities, Elections, and Sickrooms

Doctrine becomes belief when it has practitioners, fees, and instruments, and astrology in the play's century had all three in abundance. The trade divided into recognised branches, each with its own technique and clientele. The nativity, cast for the moment of birth, read a whole life from the heavens' arrangement at its start. The election chose the moment: since influences varied by the hour, an astrologer could select the configuration under which to begin an enterprise, crown a monarch, or sign a treaty, and the election was the branch that touched power most directly. The interrogation, or horary question, answered a specific query from the heavens' arrangement at the moment of asking: where a stolen object had gone, whether a sick child would recover, whether a suitor's intentions were honest. A fourth branch, mundane astrology, read eclipses, comets, and great conjunctions for the fortunes of kingdoms, and filled the almanacs with predictions of plague, dearth, and war. The Chorus's star-crossed belongs to the first branch's vocabulary: it is the language of the nativity, the verdict on a life delivered from its beginning, which is why the Prologue can pronounce it before the lovers have done anything at all.

The practitioners were not marginal fortune-tellers but, at the top of the trade, ornaments of courts and universities. John Dee, mathematician, geographer, and the English Renaissance's most famous consulting astrologer, cast the horoscopes of Queen Mary and the Princess Elizabeth in 1555 and was arrested for the calculation, so seriously did the state take the political content of a royal nativity; a princess's chart implied a date for a queen's death, and reading one was a species of treason by arithmetic. Four years later the same Elizabeth, crowned, employed the same Dee to elect the day of her coronation, and he chose 15 January 1559 as the configuration most favourable to her reign. Westminster Abbey's own account of the coronation records the transaction without embarrassment: the decision on when the coronation would take place was made by the court astrologer. Below the court, the trade served the middling sort at scale. Simon Forman, the London astrologer-physician whose casebooks survive in tens of thousands of entries, cast figures for patients and clients daily: diagnoses, stolen goods, absent husbands, prospects in love. Richard Napier, a clergyman and astrological physician in Buckinghamshire, did the same for a rural practice whose records show villagers consulting the heavens about illness, marriage, and madness with the regularity of patients attending a surgery. These were the readers for whom crossed and favoured were working terms, applied to named lives for stated fees.

The trade ran on paper and brass as much as on doctrine. Behind every figure stood the ephemerides, the printed tables of planetary positions computed for years in advance, without which no chart could be cast in a client's hour of need; behind the tables stood the astrolabe and the quadrant, the instruments that fixed a birth's ascendant against the turning sky; and behind the instruments stood the printers, for whom astrological copy was the steadiest work in the trade. The material chain matters because it explains the doctrine's resilience and its collapse in the same terms. While the tables sold and the instruments were made, the system reproduced itself in every market town; when the new astronomy rewrote the tables' foundations and the instrument-makers retrained on telescopes and chronometers, the system's supply lines were cut at the source. A belief with a manufacturing base does not die when it is refuted. It dies when its tools stop being stocked, and the word that the tools once served was left, like a uniform after an army's disbanding, to be worn by anyone who liked the cut.

The surviving casebooks let the trade be watched at its counter, and the counter's busiest department was love. Lauren Kassell's Medicine and Magic in Elizabethan London (2005), the standard study of Forman's practice, reconstructs a clientele that came with questions about courtship, marriage prospects, unfaithful spouses, and absent lovers in a steady stream alongside the fevers and thefts: whether a suitor intended honestly, whether a marriage would prosper, whether a husband overseas lived. Napier's rural books tell the same story for the villages. The detail matters to the Prologue's first hearers, for whom a crossed or favoured configuration was not a cosmic abstraction but a purchasable finding about exactly the transaction the play stages, two young people and a marriage. Some fraction of every early audience had stood where the Chorus stands, over a cast figure, waiting for a practitioner to pronounce on a match, and had paid for the pronouncement. The theatre's verdict was a quotation of their own errands. When the modern cliché applies the word to lovers as a compliment, it repeats, without knowing it, the posture of clients in a Lambeth consulting room, asking the sky about a wedding.

### Who paid for astrology, and what did the fee buy?

Everyone with a fee paid, from the queen electing a coronation day to the servant asking after a stolen spoon, and the fee bought one commodity at every level: a specific answer, dated and signed by the heavens, to a question prayer, gossip, and guesswork left open. The chart's authority was its particularity: this life, this illness, this marriage.

The trade's print arm carried the doctrine into households that never consulted a practitioner. The almanac, part calendar, part medical handbook, part weather forecast, and part prophecy, was the seventeenth century's mass medium. Bernard Capp's Astrology and the Popular Press (1979), the standard history of the English almanac, established the scale: apart from the Bible, almanacs were the most influential and widely dispersed form of literature in Tudor and Stuart England, and at their zenith in the later seventeenth century they sold at a rate of 400,000 copies a year, read by many people who read little else. Each annual issue carried the year's eclipses, the prognostications drawn from them, the zodiacal man whose diagram mapped the signs onto the body's parts for the timing of treatments, and the calendar of fortunate and unfortunate days. A household that owned one book beyond the Bible owned the heavens' schedule for the year. When the Chorus pronounced two fictional lovers crossed by the stars, it spoke into a culture saturated with this literature's categories, in which the difference between a favoured and a crossed configuration was as familiar as the difference between a fair and a foul wind. The word's authority in the theatre was borrowed from the almanac on the cottage wall, and the borrowing was legible to every rank of listener in the yard and the galleries alike.

The trade's print peak came a generation after the play, in the civil wars of the 1640s, when astrology became the propaganda arm of both camps and its practitioners were public men. William Lilly, whose annual almanac from 1644 made him the Parliament side's licensed prophet and whose Christian Astrology (1647) set out the whole art in English for the first time at textbook length, was consulted by politicians, examined by a parliamentary committee about a prophecy touching the King, and answered by the royalist astrologer George Wharton in rival almanacs that fought the war again in the heavens. Capp's history treats the decade as the almanac's political zenith, and the spectacle is worth holding beside the Prologue: two armies in the field, two astrologers in print, each casting the nation's nativity and finding his own side favoured in it. A word born into that world carried the charge of a state paper. Its pronouncements were the kind of text governments interrogated. Nothing in the phrase's later career, not the newspaper column, not the album sleeve, has restored to it that proximity to power, and the distance between Lilly's examined prophecies and a headline's decorative doom is the distance this article has been measuring all along.

## The Prologue as a Cast Chart: How the Chorus Pronounces the Stars' Judgment

The Prologue of Romeo and Juliet is a sonnet, and it performs the astrologer's office in verse. Its fourteen lines do what a nativity does: they take a life, or two lives, at their origin, from forth the fatal loins of these two foes, and they read the whole course from the configuration at the start. The Chorus names the houses, the city, and the ancient grudge; fixes the subjects, a pair of star-cross'd lovers; states the outcome, take their life; and extends the reading to the survivors, whose children's end buries their parents' strife (Prologue, lines 1 to 14 in the Folger Shakespeare edited by Barbara A. Mowat and Paul Werstine, the edition used for quotation throughout this article). The form is the judgment's guarantee. A sonnet is a closed configuration, its rhymes locking like aspects into a pattern that the volta cannot repeal, and the play's opening therefore does not merely mention astrology. It casts a chart on stage, in public, with the audience cast as fellow practitioners invited to verify the reading as the action unfolds beneath it.

The choice of the sonnet as the chart's vessel is part of the judgment's meaning. The sonnet was the period's form for love under pressure, the fourteen-line room in which a desire argued with its obstacles, and the play will use the form again within the hour, when the lovers' first exchange at the feast composes a shared sonnet between two voices, pilgrim and saint, quatrain answering quatrain. The Prologue's sonnet is that form turned inside out: one voice, no beloved addressed, no desire argued, only the obstacles reported in advance and the desire's owners already buried in its grammar, take their life, the present tense of a fait accompli. A nativity likewise precedes its subject's first act and is likewise composed by another hand. To open a love story in the love form's own metre, with the lovers absent and their deaths declined like nouns, is to tell the audience which document they are reading. They are not reading a story that might turn. They are reading a chart that has already turned, and the pleasure the form promises is the chart-reader's pleasure, watching a configuration verify itself event by event.

The parallels run into the phrasing. A nativity begins from the fatal loins, the inherited configuration of the birth, and the Prologue's lovers are crossed before they are characters, condemned by a sentence pronounced in the play's sixth line over persons the audience has not yet met. Their love is then glossed in the chart's own bookkeeping as death-mark'd (line 9), a second annotation in the same hand, and the remainder of the sonnet reads like the judgment's schedule of consequences: the fearful passage, the continuance of the parents' rage, the two hours' traffic of the stage. Critics divide over how to weigh this sentence against the action that follows it, and the division is the play's central critical quarrel in miniature. Jill L. Levenson, editing the play for the Oxford Shakespeare, summarised the enacted plot in terms that quietly refuse the Prologue's determinism: the lovers, in her account, face impossible obstacles, defy them with secret plans, and fail because of accident or because of misjudgement. Accident and misjudgement are the vocabularies of contingency, not of the stars. On Levenson's showing, the chart the Chorus casts is not the plot's mechanism but its frame, a reading the events keep citing and keep escaping, since an undelivered letter, a friar detained by plague quarantine, and a waking delayed by minutes are the hinges on which the catastrophe actually turns, and none of them is a configuration of Saturn.

### In what form does the Prologue cast its judgment on the lovers?

The Prologue casts its judgment in the form of a nativity compressed into a sonnet. It begins from the birth configuration, the fatal loins and the two houses; pronounces the aspect that rules the subjects, star-cross'd; annotates the outcome, death-mark'd; and schedules the consequences across the remaining lines, as a cast chart schedules a life from its first hour.

The first audience was not of one mind as it listened, and the Prologue can only have gained by the division. A yard full of almanac buyers contained believers of every strength: the convinced, for whom the verdict settled the evening's metaphysics in advance; the conventional, who used the art as their parents had and thought about it as little; and the wits, who had heard the sermons against judicial astrology and Edmund's brief for the defence of human villainy, and who listened to the Chorus the way a courtroom listens to an expert whose science the cross-examination has begun to date. The sonnet's confidence plays differently to each faction, as authority, as custom, and as a position already marked for rebuttal, and the play spends its length paying all three hearings their due: the convinced get their omens and forebodings honoured scene by scene, the conventional get a story shaped like the almanac's morality, and the wits get the undelivered letter. A cliché, by contrast, can be heard only one way, since it asserts nothing that a faction could dispute. The word's modern career is a long process of losing its minorities, until the unanimous, unexamined hearing that remains is indistinguishable from not hearing it at all.

The play itself files a minority report against its own Prologue, and the document is the second Chorus. When the Chorus returns at the head of Act 2, the astrology has vanished from its speech: now old desire doth in his deathbed lie, and young affection gapes to be his heir, and the summary that follows speaks of passion, access, and obstacle, of what Romeo may not meet and Juliet may not enjoy, in the vocabulary of circumstance rather than configuration. No star is consulted; no aspect is named. The shift is quiet and total, and it supports the frame reading against the mechanism reading from inside the text. The heavens preside over the play's entrance like a seal on a document, authenticating what follows without dictating its clauses. Thereafter the characters invoke the stars at their extremities, Romeo in Mantua, the Friar at the tomb, the way litigants invoke a court whose judgments they are no longer certain it delivers. The Chorus of the first Prologue is the only voice in the play that speaks astrology with a practitioner's whole confidence, and it speaks before a single event has occurred, which is the practitioner's professional posture exactly: the verdict precedes the evidence because the verdict was cast from the birth.

The adjudication this article reaches is that both parties to the quarrel are right about different objects, and that star-crossed belongs wholly to one of them. As an account of what kills the lovers, the astrological sentence is the play's weakest explanation: the catastrophe is assembled from a feud's sociology, a sequence of haste, and a chain of accidents so specific that the Friar can enumerate them, and the companion study of [fate and free will in Romeo and Juliet](/2023/12/22/fate-and-free-will-romeo-and-juliet/) works through that machinery at its proper length. As an account of what the first audience believed it was watching, the sentence is the play's strongest frame: a culture that elected coronation days and timed physick by the moon heard the Chorus perform a recognisable professional act, the casting of two nativities, and accepted the verdict with the respect due to a licensed practitioner. The word's tragedy and its later comedy both grow from this double status. Inside the theatre, star-crossed was a frame authoritative enough to organise pity and porous enough to let human error do the killing. Outside the theatre, once the profession that licensed the frame had vanished, the frame is all that remained, and a frame without a profession is a cliché waiting for tenants.

One absence in the plot measures the frame's authority better than any speech. In a culture that elected coronations, timed purges, and consulted figures on marriages as a matter of course, the lovers marry with no consultation at all. No nativity is compared, no election fixes the hour, no practitioner inspects the match that the whole city will die of: the Friar, the one character with a professional's learning, performs the rite on a hope he states in political terms, that the alliance may turn the households' rancour to love, and his pharmacopoeia of herbs and poisons is the play's only applied science on stage. The omission is the tragedy's quietest joke at its audience's expense. Every service the astrologers sold existed to prevent exactly this, an unexamined union at an unchosen hour, and the lovers, who quote the stars in their love talk and defy them in their despair, never once employ them. The Chorus's verdict is thus the bill for a consultation that never happened, presented by the profession's own voice at the theatre's door. When later centuries kept the verdict and dropped the profession, they preserved the punishment and abolished the prevention, which is a fair summary of what a cliché is for.

## Crossed: A Verb of Thwarting With a Heavens Address

The compound's anatomy repays dissection, because its two halves have had separate fortunes. Cross as a verb carried, and carries, the sense of thwarting: to cross a plan is to obstruct it, to cross a person is to oppose them, and cross purposes names the condition of two intentions cancelling each other. The participle crossed therefore arrived in the compound already meaning frustrated and opposed, available to any speaker for any frustration. What the astrological compound added was an address for the frustration: star-crossed assigned the thwarting to a specific office in the universe, the configurations of the heavens, and thereby converted a complaint into a diagnosis. A crossed plan might be anyone's fault and could be uncrossed by effort; a star-crossed life had its obstruction entered in the register of the sky before the life began, appealable to no earthly court. The word's whole subsequent career is the story of the address fading while the frustration remains, until the compound survives as a way of saying thwarted with the thwarter's name worn off.

The documentary record of the compound's birth must be stated at the precision the dictionaries support, and no further. Merriam-Webster gives the first known use of star-crossed as 1597, in the meaning not favored by the stars, and quotes the Prologue as its witness. The early quartos of the play, the First Quarto of 1597 and the Second Quarto of 1599, spell the word starre-crost, a reminder that the compound enters print still warm from the mint of the theatre, its orthography unsettled. The Random House dictionary tradition dates the word's first record to the window 1585-95, which would put paper before the play's first known performances; the variance between the authorities is small in years and large in implication, and it is exactly the kind of variance that the word coined cannot survive. David Crystal, the language scholar whose Shakespeare's Words, written with Ben Crystal, is the standard glossary of the playwright's vocabulary, has made the governing distinction a career's refrain: a first surviving citation records where the paper trail begins, not where speech began, and the plays dominate the paper trail because they were printed, reprinted, and preserved while the spoken language of the 1590s perished unrecorded. Applied here, Crystal's discipline yields the honest sentence: star-crossed is first recorded in this play, in a text of 1597, and the play fixed, launched, and bequeathed it; whether a playwright's pen or a player's or a stranger's tongue compounded it first, the record cannot say, and this article does not say it.

### What can a dictionary date actually prove?

A dictionary date proves a word stood in a surviving text by that year, and nothing more. It cannot prove the author invented it, that no speaker used it earlier, or that the recorded sense was first spoken. For star-crossed, 1597 certifies the play as the word's earliest surviving witness and launching pad, facts large enough without a birth certificate.

The adjudication between the coinage legend and Crystal's correction therefore lands where the series' census of the play's phrases landed it: the legend is wrong in method and partly right in result. For a distinctive compound, unattested before and everywhere after, the inference of decisive popularization is strong, and the compound's afterlife within a generation is documented in the dictionary tradition itself, which cites Philip Massinger's star-crossed destiny as an early successor use: the word had already passed from one playwright's prologue into the common stock of dramatic fate-language while the belief that powered it stood intact. The meaning study that owns the phrase's semantics at full depth, [Star-Crossed Lovers: What the Phrase Really Means](/2022/03/25/star-crossed-lovers-meaning-explained/), traces what the words denote; the concern of this article is what they once did. In their first century they functioned as a citation of authority, the way a legal tag cites a statute: to call lovers star-crossed was to file their case in the court of the heavens and read out its known judgment. The editors who glossed the line for later centuries kept filing the paperwork in the same court, thwarted by adverse aspects of stars, long after the court's other business had drained away, because a gloss must explain what the words meant to their first hearers or explain nothing at all. The gloss tradition is thus the doctrine's longest-lived institution, outlasting the almanac, the election, and the sickroom figure, a point the note below makes visible in tabular form.

Even the word's spelling testifies to its theatrical birth. The apostrophe in star-cross'd is a metrical instrument: the participle crossed, sounded in full, would burden the line, and the elision compresses it to a single beat so the compound lands inside the pentameter's stride, a pair of star-cross'd lovers, with the accent falling where the sense needs it. The quartos' starre-crost, lacking both the settled hyphen and the elision mark, shows a compound still being handled as loose parts, star and crost, before printshop habit fused them into the single token the dictionaries would inherit. The play's own line performs the same compression twice more in nine lines, death-mark'd love carrying the identical elision, as if the Prologue's fate-language had to be filed short to fit its sonnet, the chart squeezed onto one page. Modern typography keeps the apostrophe in scholarly editions and drops it in the wild, and the popular form star-crossed, two words soldered by a hyphen with the elision healed over, is the compound's final disguise: a technical term that has forgotten it was ever pronounced under pressure, in verse, by a man presenting a chart.

The compound's spelling keeps the score of its lexicalization. The quartos' starre-crost is a phrase in transit, two words leaning together; the modern editions' star-cross'd is a token, the elision marking a unit the reader is expected to take whole; the popular star-crossed is the unit naturalized, its seam healed, its hyphen demoted to a courtesy. Dictionaries complete the passport by granting the compound its own headword, the honour English reserves for combinations whose meaning can no longer be computed from their parts by a stranger. The honour is also the obituary of the doctrine, since a headword is precisely a meaning that has stopped needing its derivation. Nobody looks up crossed and consults an aspect table. The entry is consulted instead, and the entry's first clause, not favored by the stars, points backward to a science the entry cannot contain, a signpost at the border of a country the map no longer prints.

The compound also landed in a literary culture already fluent in love's star-language. Philip Sidney's sonnet sequence Astrophil and Stella, published in 1591, six years before the play's first quarto, built its entire fiction from the vocabulary: the lover's name compounds the Greek for star with love, the beloved's name is the Latin for star, and their courtship plays out as an astronomy of glances, influences, and eclipses. A reader formed by the sequence, and the sequence formed a generation, met the Chorus's compound as a dialect word in a known tongue, the star-grammar of desire given a hostile inflection. The play's originality was never the heavens in love poetry. It was the conversion of that ornamental astronomy into a judicial finding, the moment the sonneteers' decorative stars were sworn in as witnesses and asked for a verdict. Pop culture kept the ornament and struck the witnesses, returning the word by a four-century detour to the decorative condition from which the play had conscripted it.

## Among Its Synonyms: Why This Compound Outran the Field

English was not short of words for the lovers' condition, and the compound's victory over its own synonym field is a small election worth auditing. The dictionaries parade the rivals: ill-fated, ill-starred, doomed, hapless, luckless, unfortunate, unhappy, the thesaurus tail that Merriam-Webster assembles around the headword. Ill-starred is the instructive loser, since it shares the astrology and nearly shares the date, and it survives only as a faintly bookish variant while its sibling became a household word. The difference between them is a picture and a plot. Ill-starred assigns a property, a bad star, static as a birthmark. Star-crossed assigns an event, a crossing, something done to the lovers by an agent in motion, and the event implies the before and after that a story needs: configurations approach, cross, and pass, and a narrative can live inside the interval. The compound also carries its genre in its sound, the double stress falling like two blows, and its most famous collocation completes the equipment by supplying the victims, lovers, a plural noun with a plot already inside it. A synonym that names a condition competes with adjectives; a synonym that names a collision competes with nothing, because it is a headline in waiting.

The field test can be run in the other direction, against the play's own alternatives. The Prologue itself offers death-marked, a compound of equal compression and bleaker clarity, and the language declined it: death-marked never left the editions, because its verdict forecloses the story it labels, leaving nothing for hope or irony to do. Star-crossed, by contrast, names an opposition rather than an outcome, and opposition can be fought, mourned, sung about, and merchandised. Fortune's fool, Romeo's cry over Mercutio's body (3.1), had a head start in vividness and stayed a quotation, since it names a person at a moment, not a relation between two people and the sky. The compound that won is the one that describes an arrangement, portable across every pair of lovers, teams, firms, and factions the language would ever need to curse in bulk. Portability is the cliché's first cause, and it was built into the word at the mint: a diagnosis general enough for any nativity is a diagnosis ready to be emptied into any headline.

One further pattern in the rows deserves naming before the narrative resumes. The witnesses thin as they modernize. The doctrine's stages rest on institutions with archives: a court's election recorded by an abbey, a trade measured by a historian of its print runs, casebooks preserved by the thousand, a hoax retold because the pamphlets survive. The cliché's stages rest on broadcasts, brands, and releases, documents manufactured to be consumed and replaced, whose preservation is an accident of databases. The asymmetry is itself a finding about the two regimes of the word. The belief kept records because its claims could be checked against outcomes and its practitioners answered for their findings. The cliché keeps almost none, because its claims are checked against nothing, and a phrase that cannot be wrong leaves no paperwork when it travels. The note's later rows are therefore harder to document than its earlier ones, and the difficulty is not the compiler's failure. It is the phenomenon, caught in the act of being itself.

## How the Belief Died and the Word Did Not Notice

The death of judicial astrology among the educated was a long eviction rather than an execution, and its stages matter because the word survived every one of them unchanged. Thomas's account gives the process its classic shape. Across the seventeenth century the doctrine lost its intellectual credit in stages: the new astronomy dissolved the solid spheres whose machinery had carried the influences; the mechanical philosophy redescribed the heavens as matter in motion, splendid and mute; theologians pressed the old objection that judicial astrology stole from providence and free will with a confidence the doctrine could no longer answer; and the practical arts that had rented astrology's authority, medicine above all, built other foundations. Thomas's verdict on the outcome is the sharpest sentence in the literature: astrology ceased, in all but the most unsophisticated circles, to be regarded as either a science or a crime. The paired loss is the point. A doctrine can survive being prosecuted as a crime, since prosecution concedes its power; it cannot survive being reclassified as a harmless pastime. When the stars' influence shrank from a heresy to a hobby, the vocabulary of the influence should have shrunk with it, into archaism, the way the vocabulary of humoral medicine shrank into metaphors of temperament. Some of it did. Election and interrogation and geniture are museum words. Star-crossed refused the museum.

### What killed the belief: ridicule, or replacement?

Replacement killed it; ridicule signed the certificate. The spheres fell to astronomy, the influences to physics, the sickroom figure to a rebuilding medicine, and the almanac's weather to the barometer. Once each office had a new tenant, laughing at the astrologer became safe, and Swift's hoax of 1708 is the sound of that safety arriving.

The ridicule deserves its paragraph because it marks the exact moment the word's world turned comic. In 1708 Jonathan Swift, writing as Isaac Bickerstaff, published a prediction that the almanac-maker John Partridge would die on 29 March of that year, and followed it with an elegy and an account of the death, gravely circumstantial, while Partridge lived. Partridge protested in print that he was alive, which Swift's persona received as an impostor's effrontery, and the town laughed at the astrologer for needing to prove his own existence. The episode is a set piece in Capp's history of the almanac, and its cultural meaning is precise: in 1555 the state arrested a man for casting a royal horoscope, because the reading was power; in 1708 the public laughed at an astrologer for failing to be dead, because the reading was entertainment. Between those two treatments lies the whole decline, and star-crossed floated across it on the unsinkable raft of the play. The compound had a second anchor the technical vocabulary lacked: it was fixed in the most reprinted, most quoted, most schooled text in the language, and texts preserve words the way amber preserves insects, in perfect form and without life. Quotation dictionaries, school editions, and the gloss tradition carried the word forward as a specimen of the play's language, each generation of editors re-explaining the adverse aspects to readers further from them, until the explanation became a ritual nobody expected to be believed. The word did not survive despite the belief's death. It survived because the belief's death converted it from a term of art into a quotation, and quotations are immortal at the price of being dead.

The almanac itself modelled the afterlife in advance, outliving its doctrine by more than a century as a commercial form. Old Moore's Almanac, founded in 1697 on astrological prognostication, climbed to a peak circulation above 500,000 in the 1830s, deep in the industrial century, selling its mixture of calendar, weather lore, and prophecy to a readership its founder's contemporaries would have found impossibly godless and improbably literate at once. The form kept the furniture of the system, the signs, the eclipses, the prognosticating voice, while the system's warrant drained away beneath it, and nobody involved, publisher, compiler, or reader, needed to decide when the drainage had happened. That is the template the word would follow: the shape retained because the shape sold, the content retained because the shape implied it, the belief retained by neither. Print capitalism is a preservative that works by pickling, and both the almanac and the compound came out of the jar looking fed and tasting of vinegar.

## The Quotation Trade: How Print Kept a Dead Science's Word Alive

Between the doctrine's death and the column's birth, the word lived on a specific industry's life support, and the industry deserves its invoice. The quotation dictionary, the anthology, and the school edition formed the nineteenth century's preservation machinery for famous language, and their method was extraction: a line was lifted from its work, alphabetized under an author's name or a topic, and reissued as a portable unit of culture. John Bartlett's Familiar Quotations, first published in 1855 and swollen through edition after edition into the Anglophone world's default memory, carried the Prologue's sentences into parlours that owned no play at all. Extraction is a preservative with a known side effect, measured across this series in the careers of the play's other exported lines: the extract keeps the wording and sheds the context at a rate set by the format, since an entry can carry a line, a locus, and an attribution, but not a cosmology. Star-crossed in a quotation book is a specimen in spirits, perfectly formed, with its habitat reduced to a parenthesis, Romeo and Juliet, Prologue, and a reader who meets it there has been given the word and the address of its birthplace in place of the world it was born in.

The machinery also explains the word's otherwise puzzling robustness across the very centuries when its parent science was a punchline. A term of art dies with its art unless a second institution adopts it, and the compound was adopted early and often: by editors annotating the play for readers who needed the aspects explained, by anthologists excerpting the Prologue as a freestanding sonnet, by elocution teachers setting the Chorus's speech as a declamation piece, and eventually by the headline and caption writers who inherited the anthologists' stock. Each adoption renewed the word's lease while renegotiating its terms downward, from doctrine to ornament to reflex. The process had no villain and no plan; it is simply what print does to the vocabulary of dead sciences when the vocabulary is beautiful. The humours survive the same way, as temperament and spleen, words whose medical machinery nobody misses because the words were never the front door of a beloved house. Star-crossed was a front door, and the house behind it kept being visited, which is why the word's tenants multiplied even as its meaning thinned.

## The Column That Shrunk the Cosmos: Sun Signs and the New Astrology

The strangest turn in the word's story is that astrology itself returned to mass circulation in the twentieth century, in a form that completed the emptying its absence had begun. The return has a documented birthday. In August 1930 the Sunday Express commissioned a horoscope of the newborn Princess Margaret; the celebrated astrologer Cheiro was unavailable, and his assistant R. H. Naylor wrote it instead, publishing What the Stars Foretell for the New Princess on 24 August 1930 with general forecasts by birth date appended. Weeks later Naylor was credited with foretelling danger to a British aircraft in October, the airship R101 crashed at Beauvais on 5 October, and the paper gave him a weekly column, the first regular astrology column in a British newspaper. By 1937 Naylor had refined the product into Your Stars, forecasts for the twelve sun signs, each computed from a reader's birth date alone. The refinement is the whole story in miniature: a traditional nativity required the hour and place of birth and a practitioner's mathematics; a sun-sign column required a birthday and a printing press. Of the seven wandering stars whose aspects had once crossed or favoured a life, the column retained one, the Sun, and of the chart's geometry it retained nothing at all.

The column's success is not a puzzle once its economics are stated. Twelve forecasts can be written for a nation; a million nativities cannot. The simplified astrology that conquered the newspapers was a publishing technology wearing a cosmology's clothes, and its practitioners said so with varying candour: the proper astrological community of the day largely disowned the columns as a vast oversimplification of an ancient art, a complaint recorded by the columnists' own successors. What matters for this article is the division of labour the reinvention produced. The renewed public astrology took over the future: it promised guidance, compatibility, fortunate days, the daily management of hope. The old word star-crossed was left in undisturbed possession of the past tense of doom. No column told a reader that the week was star-crossed; the phrase did not fit a medium whose business is reassurance in twelve flavours. It stayed where the nineteenth century had put it, in the literary register, attached to lovers in books and headlines, growing more purely a synonym for doomed intensity as its parent discipline grew more purely a synonym for light entertainment. Astrology's two heirs thus divided the estate: the column inherited the stars and made them friendly, and the cliché inherited the crossing and made it beautiful. Neither heir kept the doctrine, in which the stars were neither friendly nor beautiful but simply, terribly, in charge.

The column deserves a closer look as the cliché's finishing school, since it perfected the prose technique the cliché would inherit. A sun-sign forecast must pronounce on millions of lives in a dozen sentences, and it learned to write verdicts engineered to survive every outcome: tendencies instead of events, moods instead of mechanisms, a favourable day for romance that no failed romance can falsify. The technique is the opposite of the nativity's. Forman's figures named names, dates, and dangers, and could be wrong in ways his clients recorded; the column's sentences are built so that wrongness has no purchase on them. Star-crossed underwent the same engineering in its literary register across the same decades, shedding the falsifiable content, the aspects, the malefics, the birth hours, and keeping the unfalsifiable residue, a doom with no date and no agent. The two products of the simplified century resemble each other because they were optimized for the same market condition: a mass audience that wants the feeling of a verdict without the inconvenience of a finding. What the column did to astrology, the cliché did to the word, and the resemblance is not an accident of parallel decay but a shared adaptation to print at scale.

That division explains a feature of the modern phrase that pure literary history cannot: its immunity to the company it keeps. A culture in which millions read sun-sign forecasts without believing a word of them was trained, by the columns themselves, in a new relation to astrological language, the relation of affectionate unbelief. Words from the old system could circulate as charms, jokes, and compliments precisely because the system had been reclassified as harmless. Star-crossed benefited from the training its own doctrine's humiliation provided. Pronounced over a couple in a headline, it asks for no assent and risks no ridicule; it borrows the silhouette of an ancient authority while standing in a queue of readers who have all agreed, without ever discussing it, that the authority is a costume. The forward study of [how the star-crossed lovers phrase works in headlines and news](/2030/01/04/star-crossed-lovers-in-headlines/) examines that queue at its place of work. The point here is the price. Affectionate unbelief is a stable arrangement for a phrase and a fatal one for a meaning, since a word everyone is licensed to use without believing is a word no one is obliged to understand.

The column crossed the Atlantic and completed its conquest of the century's media in forms its inventors could audit against the old trade and find wanting in the same way. Evangeline Adams, the best-known American astrologer of the early twentieth century, had defended the art's professional honour in a New York courtroom in 1914, demonstrating her method to the judge in a fortune-telling prosecution that ended in dismissal, and she carried astrology to radio audiences in the early 1930s, the consulting room dissolved into broadcast. Syndicated columns followed the wire services into hundreds of American papers across the following decades. The sequence has a final term the century held in reserve: the astrology application, of which Co-Star, launched in 2017, became the emblem, delivering the day's configurations as telephone notifications in a gnomic style its users shared as images. Each migration, consultation to column to broadcast to application, preserved the vocabulary and thinned the content by the same ratio. What a nativity had said to one person in an hour, the application says to a million in a sentence, and the sentence, like the cliché it travels beside, is long on verdict and short on sky.

## Star-Crossed in Pop: The Cliché at Full Strength

The pop career of the phrase can be read off three title pages from a single recent decade, each documented, each using the word as a complete argument in two syllables of prefix and one of doom. In February 2014 the American network The CW premiered Star-Crossed, a romance in which a human schoolgirl and an alien schoolboy, one of a group of extraterrestrial teenagers integrated into a Louisiana high school, fall for each other across their species' enmity. The title performs a substitution that the article's whole argument predicts: the stars, which in the doctrine were causes, become in the title a place, the literal heavens the alien arrived from, and the crossing, which in the doctrine was a geometry of hostile aspects, becomes a sociology of hostile communities. The lovers are not thwarted by configurations but by neighbours, which is to say they are thwarted by the Capulet and Montague material alone, with the astrology retained as a label for the feud's antiquity. The series ran thirteen episodes before cancellation, and its title outlived it, because the word no longer needs a successful vehicle. It is a genre in itself, legible on a poster to viewers who have never seen a chart cast.

Three years later the phrase did stranger work. ABC's Still Star-Crossed, produced by Shondaland and premiered in May 2017, adapted Melinda Taub's 2013 novel of the same name, a sequel to the play that marries Rosaline Capulet to Benvolio Montague in the aftermath of the tomb. Here the word's doom is applied to a story whose entire premise is survival: the crossed generation is dead, and the phrase brands the attempt to continue living in its ruins. A title that once pronounced a death sentence serves, in this incarnation, as the promise of a franchise. The inversion could not be completer without ceasing to be visible, and it passed without remark, which is the surest sign of its completeness. Nobody reviewed the series by objecting that its lovers were insufficiently doomed; the word had finished its migration from verdict to atmosphere, and an atmosphere can settle over any plot at all, including a sequel whose commercial proposition is that the story goes on.

The album completes the triptych by turning the verdict inward. Kacey Musgraves released star-crossed in September 2021, a record and companion film drawn from her divorce, which she described as a modern tragedy and shaped in declared debt to the play. The usage is the cliché's most intelligent modern form and its clearest confession. Musgraves does not mean that Saturn stood in hard aspect to Mars at her wedding; she means that the marriage was the kind of story whose ending was present at its beginning, and she borrows the old word because no newer word carries that architecture of doom with that brevity or that glamour. The borrowing is knowing, and knowingness is the cliché's final stage: the speaker signals acquaintance with the source, claims the tragedy's dignity for a private sorrow, and counts on the audience to receive the allusion without auditing it. Audited, the allusion fails in the familiar direction. The play's lovers were crossed by a cosmos that operated regardless of their knowledge; the album's lovers are crossed in retrospect, by narration, the doom conferred backward from the ending like a title conferred on a finished book. Retrospective doom is a literary device. Prospective doom was a physics. The word's career is the journey from the second to the first, and every pop use rows in the same direction, from the heavens that decide to the storyteller who decides to call it the heavens.

### What does the phrase sell when nobody believes it?

The phrase sells inevitability without responsibility. A couple called star-crossed is relieved of every lesser explanation: not incompatibility, not timing, not the families' politics, not their own haste, but the sky. The hidden compliment is that only a love of cosmic scale requires a cosmic opponent, so the doom certifies the love's size.

General usage beyond the title pages shows the same emptied mechanism at work in miniature. The phrase attaches to athletes whose finals keep colliding with one opponent, to companies whose mergers keep failing at the regulator's desk, to politicians whose alliances keep collapsing, and in each case the work it does is identical: it elevates a run of outcomes into a pattern with an author, while naming an author nobody can cross-examine. The dictionaries register the settled result with their usual neutrality. Longman's dictionary for learners defines the adjective as literary and explains star-crossed lovers as people who love each other but cannot be together, a definition in which the stars have vanished entirely and only the togetherness problem remains; Merriam-Webster keeps the fossil first clause, not favored by the stars, as a courtesy to etymology. Between those two definitions lies the whole journey. One dictionary still names the agent of the thwarting; the other has quietly dismissed the agent and kept the thwarting, and the dismissed agent is the subject this article exists to restore.

The play's own afterlife is the phrase's largest employer, and the employment is circular in a way that conceals the drift. Editions, programmes, posters, and précis bill the tragedy by its Prologue's compound so routinely that star-crossed lovers functions as the work's second title, the name under which the story is filed by people who will never open it. The billing is accurate as advertisement and corrosive as criticism, since it instructs each new audience to expect the Chorus's sentence to be the play's meaning, and to read the ensuing five acts as the sentence's illustration rather than its cross-examination. A story introduced as the tale of the star-crossed lovers has been pre-judged in the court this article has been describing, and the pre-judgment is self-sealing: the accidents read as fate because the title promised fate, the haste reads as destiny because the poster said destiny, and the Friar's catalogue of human causes goes unheard beneath the music of the frame. The phrase thus guards the play from its own argument, admitting visitors through a door that opens onto the gift shop of the doctrine rather than the courtroom of the drama.

Grammar keeps the minutes of the dismissal. In the doctrine the construction had an agent with an address: the stars crossed the lovers, an active heaven performing a hostile act at a computable angle, and the passive form star-crossed was a report of that act, open to audit. In the cliché the passive floats free of any agent at all. Couples are star-crossed the way cakes are home-made in a supermarket's chiller cabinet, the participle surviving as pure adjective, a flavour rather than a finding. The drift can be watched in miniature in the phrase's favourite modern neighbour, written in the stars, which pop usage deploys as the benevolent twin: the same heaven, the same handwriting, the outcome reversed into a promise. The doctrine knew no such twin. A configuration could favour, and favouring had its own vocabulary of benefics and trines, but the inscription metaphor belongs to the age of the autograph, not the age of the aspect, and its cheerfulness is the tell. Where the old system's heaven could crush or crown with indifference, the new heaven exists to endorse, a notary for romances. Star-crossed is what the notary's stamp reads on the applications it refuses, and even the refusals are taken as proof of the office's romance.

## The Schoolroom Custody: How the Curriculum Fixed the Cliché

Between the glossators and the headline writers stands the institution that handled the word most often and examined it least: the school. Romeo and Juliet became, across the twentieth century, the play through which the English-speaking world meets its national dramatist in adolescence, a fixture of American secondary classrooms and of the British examination syllabus alike, taught to more unwilling and willing readers than any comparable text of its century. The classroom's method with the word is the vocabulary list. Star-crossed appears in the apparatus of school editions as an item to be defined before the reading begins, glossed in the margin or the glossary in the formula the gloss tradition standardized, thwarted by unfavourable stars, ill-fated, and the definition is then examined, memorized, and carried away. What the method transmits is the word with its explanation attached as a label, and what the method cannot transmit is the explanation's world, since a syllabus with fourteen weeks and an examination in May has no hour to spend rebuilding the Ptolemaic spheres for the sake of one compound in the Prologue's sixth line. The spheres are accordingly taught, where they are taught at all, as background, and background is the curriculum's word for what may safely be forgotten after the test.

### Why does the classroom teach the word before the world that made it?

The classroom teaches the word first because the word is examinable and the world is not. A definition fits a mark scheme; a cosmos does not. Generations met star-crossed as a vocabulary item, a synonym for doomed with a decorative etymology, years before any could have said what an aspect was, and the order became permanent.

The custody has a second effect, subtler than abbreviation. A word learned at fourteen, from a teacher, in a room where the play is the set text and the lovers are the lesson's heroes, is learned inside the romance. The classroom presents the tragedy as the curriculum's great love story, its mature content a warrant of seriousness, and the Prologue's verdict is absorbed as part of the love story's furniture before any student is equipped to hear it as a professional judgment from a dead science. The cliché's great inversion, doom as a compliment to intensity, is thus not a corruption that befalls the word in the marketplace after honest schooling. It is the form in which the schooling itself delivers the word, with the marketplace merely repeating the lesson at scale. Teachers who reach for the machinery, the aspects, the malefics, the cast chart, are working against the grain of the apparatus their editions hand them, in which the heavens occupy a footnote and the footnote occupies a sentence. The note compiled for this article exists because that sentence, expanded back into the system it abbreviates, changes the sound of the play's first six lines from a mood into an argument.

A word's drift would be a lexicographer's private sorrow if the word were not the play's front door. Star-crossed is the phrase through which the largest number of readers, viewers, and headline skimmers enter Romeo and Juliet, and a front door shapes the house behind it. A public taught by the cliché to hear the word as a compliment to intensity enters the play expecting fate to be décor: a purple atmosphere around a romance, licensing tears and excusing everyone. The play it then meets is doing something harder and colder. Its astrology is a working hypothesis argued over by its own characters, endorsed by a Chorus with a professional manner, asserted by Romeo in hope and in despair, and refuted, or at least unassisted, by the event, in which the heavens send no sign that is not better explained by a feud, a plague quarantine, and a boy's haste. The drama lives in the friction between the sentence and the evidence. A reader for whom the sentence is a valentine has been disarmed before the friction starts, and productions, schoolrooms, and adaptations inherit the disarmament. The lovers become the mascots of the very fatalism the play puts on trial, and the trial's other parties, the fathers, the Prince, the city that farmed its hatred to its children, walk out of the theatre acquitted by the stars.

The acquittal falls hardest on the play's most consequential figure. The Juliet of the text is the tragedy's prime mover: she converts a balcony conversation into a marriage contract with a negotiator's precision, dispatches the Nurse, sets the wedding's hour, drinks the potion on a reasoning her soliloquy lays out step by step, and wakes to solve the tomb's horror with a dagger in one speechless minute. The star-crossed frame, absorbed as the story's meaning, reclassifies this agent as fate's passenger, a girl to whom things cosmically happened, and the reclassification flatters every institution that actually constrained her: the father who marketed her to Paris, the mother who declined to know her, the confessor whose plot she had to execute. A criticism that keeps the aspects in view keeps the acquittal visible, because a real astrological verdict at least names a defendant worth appealing, and the play's audience can watch Juliet appealing it with every resource of will she owns. The cliché's verdict names nobody and so acquits everybody, and its Juliet is the price: the most decisive heroine of the early stage, remembered as the sky's most famous victim.

The cost can be itemized in the word's three losses. The first loss is causation: in the doctrine, star-crossed meant that a specific mechanism, adverse aspects among named planets, had produced the obstruction, and a claim with a mechanism can be examined, disputed, and falsified, as Edmund disputed it. In the cliché the word means that obstruction simply belongs to the couple's essence, and a claim without a mechanism can only be repeated. The second loss is scale: the doctrine's doom was cosmic and therefore impersonal, an indictment of the universe's government that comforted nobody and flattered nobody either, since the heavens crossed beggars and kings with the same geometry. The cliché's doom is bespoke, tailored to the couple it decorates, and so functions as flattery, the universe cast as a personal antagonist whose attention certifies importance. The third loss is tense: the doctrine pronounced its verdict in advance, from the birth hour, and the play's horror depends on that priority, on watching the foreknown end assemble itself from free acts. The cliché pronounces its verdict in retrospect, from the ending, and converts foreknowledge into hindsight, dread into nostalgia. A phrase that has lost its mechanism, its scale, and its tense has not been preserved by its journey. It has been pensioned.

The counter-position deserves its hearing before the verdict hardens. Words drift, the linguist's shrug replies, and meanings are nothing but use: if star-crossed serves its speakers as a compact sign for doomed intensity, then that is what it means, and mourning the aspects is antiquarian sentimentality dressed as rigor. The shrug is correct about the mechanism and wrong about the stakes, for two reasons. The first is that drift has a direction as well as a fact, and this drift reversed a valence: a verdict of cosmic hostility became a compliment, which is not the ordinary bleaching by which strong words weaken but a rarer event, a word changing sides. Bleached words bore their hearers; reversed words mislead them, sending audiences into a tragedy armed with the tragedy's own alibi, persuaded that the play endorses the excuse it examines. The second reason is that the word is not free-floating vocabulary but a quotation with an address, and quotations make claims on their sources. Every use of star-crossed cites the play, borrows its prestige, and reports its doctrine, and the report is false: the play does not teach that the stars killed the lovers, it stages a city that would prefer that explanation. A drift that falsifies a source's argument while trading on the source's authority has costs that the shrug cannot wave away, and the costs are paid in the theatre, one mishearing at a time.

The stage has quietly voted in the argument without knowing it held one. A Chorus costumed and lit as a master of ceremonies invites the house to receive the Prologue as a trailer, plot summary with music, and the word star-crossed lands as atmosphere. A Chorus given the practitioner's gravity, a reader with a chart, delivering findings the way Dee delivered an election or Forman a figure, makes the house receive a verdict, and the evening's question becomes the one the first audience brought from the almanac and the consulting room: whether the finding will hold against the evidence of the lives. Directors reach the second effect whenever they resist the trailer instinct, and the resistance costs nothing but a decision about what kind of speaker the Chorus is. The text has already decided. It gave the play's only professional verdict to a voice outside the action, speaking a profession's sentence in a sonnet's sealed form, and then spent five acts letting tradesmen, friars, nurses, and children do the actual causing. Staging that honours the decision does not need astrology restored as scenery. It needs the sentence restored as a sentence, pronounced by an officer of a court the audience is permitted to doubt.

The restoration pays its dividend in a single scene. Hear Romeo's cry in Mantua, then I defy you, stars, with the cliché in the ear, and it registers as romantic emphasis, a young man's shout at bad luck, decorative and forgivable. Hear it with the doctrine restored and the line becomes a legal and theological act: a vassal renouncing fealty to the powers that held his course, a subject declaring war on the government of the universe from a shop in Mantua, with an apothecary's poison already in his mind. The defiance is insane in the second hearing and merely loud in the first, and the insanity is the point, since the play has shown the stars' government failing to govern anything except Romeo's imagination of it. He dies defying an administration that the plot has revealed as understaffed by fortune, plague, and other people's errors. The cliché cannot afford that irony, because the cliché needs the administration real, its opposition the certificate of the love. Every scene of the play that cites the heavens divides the same way under the two hearings, and the division is the practical measure of what the emptying cost: not a nuance, but the play's central irony, traded for a compliment.

The verdict on the phrase's journey is therefore double, and the two halves must be kept apart. As a feat of survival, the career is unmatched in the play's language: a technical compound from a dead science has outlived the science, the spheres, and the profession, and stands in the active vocabulary of people who could not name one of its planets, carried there by the play's prestige and the quotation trade's industry. As a feat of understanding, the career is a long misunderstanding, in which the conclusion of an argument was preserved by being detached from the argument, and the play is widely loved for holding a doctrine it stages as a question. Restoring the astrology does not require believing it, any more than restoring the machinery of the humours requires bleeding a patient. It requires only hearing the Chorus as its first audience heard it, as a practitioner delivering a verdict his culture had licensed him to deliver, over two children whose deaths the audience was about to watch being caused, in scene after scene, by hands entirely human. The stars in the play are the most elegant alibi ever offered to a guilty city. The word's emptying is the alibi's final victory, since a culture that calls the lovers star-crossed as a compliment has stopped noticing that an alibi is what it is repeating.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is an astrological aspect, and which aspects counted as crossing a life?

An aspect is the angular distance between two planets as seen from Earth, measured along the zodiac, and the aspects were the grammar of celestial influence. Conjunction joined two planets in the same ground and compounded their natures. The sextile of sixty degrees and the trine of one hundred and twenty were harmonious, letting influence flow. The square of ninety degrees and the opposition of one hundred and eighty were hostile, setting one planet's quality against another's so that promises were confiscated as fast as they were made. A life was crossed when its ruling configurations stood in the hostile aspects, which is why editors gloss the Prologue's word as thwarted by adverse aspects of stars.

### Q: Which planets did astrologers call the malefics, and what did each one threaten?

The malefics were Saturn and Mars, the two planets whose qualities the system read as hostile to life. Saturn, the greater malefic, was cold and dry, slow and heavy, and governed melancholy, delay, obstruction, chronic illness, and death; a life ruled by Saturn met its obstacles as weather, grinding and prolonged. Mars, the lesser malefic, was hot and dry, and governed anger, fever, wounds, violence, and sudden calamity; a life ruled by Mars met its obstacles as accidents and blows. Benefic Jupiter and Venus promised ease, favour, and fertility. A nativity in which the malefics stood strong and in hard aspect to the lights was the technical substance behind the verdict star-crossed.

### Q: What was a nativity, and how did an astrologer cast one?

A nativity, or geniture, was the chart of the heavens for the moment of a person's birth, and it served as the foundation document of a life's reading. The astrologer first computed the positions of the Sun, Moon, and five visible planets for the birth hour, using printed tables of planetary motion, and placed them in the zodiac's signs. He then divided the chart into twelve houses, the sectors governing life, wealth, marriage, death, and the rest, fixed the ascendant, the sign rising at the birth moment, and judged which planets ruled, assisted, or afflicted each house. From the whole configuration he read the native's constitution, prospects, and seasons of danger.

### Q: What is an election in astrology, and why did a coronation need one?

An election is the branch of astrology that chooses the moment for an action, instead of reading a moment already given. Since the heavens' configurations changed by the hour and carried different qualities, the astrologer computed forward until he found a configuration favourable to the enterprise in hand, and fixed its beginning there. States used elections for acts whose fortunes were meant to endure, and a coronation was the type case: the reign would be read from its first hour for decades. John Dee's election of 15 January 1559 for Elizabeth's coronation is the famous English instance, recorded by Westminster Abbey's own account of the ceremony.

### Q: What did an astrological physician look for in a figure cast for a patient?

The astrological physician cast a figure for the moment the illness began or the question was asked, and read the patient's crisis from the chart's configuration. He looked for the planet ruling the sick body part, since the zodiac's signs governed the body head to foot in the almanacs' zodiacal man; for the Moon's motion and aspects, which timed the disease's turning points, the critical days; and for the malefics' pressure on the lights, which warned of danger. Simon Forman's and Richard Napier's surviving casebooks show the method at work by the thousand: diagnosis, prognosis, and the timing of treatment all read from one cast figure.

### Q: What divided natural astrology from judicial astrology, and why did the Church police the border?

Natural astrology studied the heavens' influence on bodies and weather: tides, seasons, humours, epidemics, harvests. Judicial astrology claimed to judge particular human affairs from the stars: the outcome of a marriage, a theft, a reign, a life. The Church broadly tolerated the first as a branch of natural philosophy, since God governed the world through secondary causes, and condemned the second as a trespass on providence and free will, since a destiny computed from a birth hour left no room for grace, prayer, or choice. The border mattered because the same practitioner crossed it daily, timing a purge in the morning and foretelling a marriage by afternoon.

### Q: What did the annual almanacs carry, and how wide was their readership?

An almanac carried the year's calendar with its feasts and fairs, the dates of eclipses and the prognostications drawn from them, weather expectations, medical guidance keyed to the zodiacal man, fortunate and unfortunate days, and remarks on husbandry, roads, and prices. Bernard Capp's history of the trade gives the scale: apart from the Bible, almanacs were the most influential and widely dispersed form of literature in Tudor and Stuart England, selling at their later seventeenth-century zenith at a rate of 400,000 copies a year, many of them read by people who read little else. The heavens' timetable hung in households that owned almost no other print.

### Q: Why did an astronomer of Kepler's standing cast horoscopes?

Kepler cast horoscopes because his science had not yet divided into the two disciplines that later ages separated. Astronomy supplied the mathematics of the heavens; astrology supplied the interpretation of the same motions, and one practitioner was trained, paid, and expected to perform both. Kepler reformed the heavens' geometry, discarding the perfect circles on which the old cosmos rested, while continuing to read configurations for patrons, including the imperial general Wallenstein, whose horoscopes Kepler cast and later revised. His private writings grumble at the trade's vulgar forms and defend a reformed core. The conjunction looks contradictory only from a century in which the disciplines have different names, buildings, and degrees.

### Q: What stood at the centre of the cosmos the word presupposes, and what moved around it?

The Earth stood at the centre, motionless, wrapped in the nested spheres of the Ptolemaic universe. Around it turned the spheres carrying the Moon, Mercury, Venus, the Sun, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn, each planet fixed in its transparent sphere and circling at its own speed, and beyond Saturn turned the sphere of the fixed stars. Influence descended through the spheres from the perfect, unchanging heavens into the sublunary world of change and decay where human lives ran. The arrangement made astrology a physics: if the heavens governed the seasons and the tides from their stations, their configurations plausibly governed the smaller weather of a constitution, a marriage, or a reign.

### Q: What happened when Swift predicted the almanac-maker Partridge's death in 1708?

Swift, writing under the name Isaac Bickerstaff, published a prediction that John Partridge, a prominent almanac astrologer, would die on 29 March 1708, and then published a circumstantial account of the death on schedule. Partridge responded in print that he was alive, and Swift's persona treated the protest as the work of an impostor trading on a dead man's name. The town took the joke's side, and the episode became a set piece in the almanac's history because it measured a cultural reversal: a century and a half after the state arrested Dee for the power of a royal horoscope, the public laughed at an astrologer reduced to proving his own existence.

### Q: What does Keith Thomas's history say ended astrology's reign among the educated?

Thomas's Religion and the Decline of Magic (1971) describes a loss of credit in stages rather than a single refutation. The new astronomy dissolved the spheres that carried the influences; the mechanical philosophy redescribed the heavens as matter in mute motion; medicine and meteorology built foundations of their own; and theologians pressed the free-will objection with growing confidence. His verdict on the result is that astrology ceased, in all but the most unsophisticated circles, to be regarded as either a science or a crime. Demotion to pastime, not prosecution, was the fatal sentence, since a doctrine persecuted is a doctrine feared, and a doctrine indulged is a doctrine finished.

### Q: Who was R. H. Naylor, and what did his column change?

R. H. Naylor was the British astrologer whose horoscope of the newborn Princess Margaret, written for the Sunday Express of 24 August 1930 when the celebrated Cheiro proved unavailable, began the newspaper horoscope column. Credited weeks later with foretelling danger to a British aircraft in the month the airship R101 crashed, he received a weekly column, the first of its kind in a British newspaper, and by 1937 had settled it into Your Stars, forecasts for the twelve sun signs. The change he embodies is the transfer of astrology from the consulting room to the printing press: from individual charts cast for fees to general forecasts manufactured for millions.

### Q: Why did the sun-sign column have to discard the birth chart to reach a newspaper's audience?

A birth chart is unmanufacturable at newspaper scale. A nativity needs a client's exact birth hour and place, computed positions for seven wandering stars, a division into houses, and a practitioner's judgment, all for one reader. A newspaper needs twelve paragraphs that sort its entire circulation by birthday. The sun-sign system solved the production problem by keeping one planet of the seven, the Sun, whose sign changes monthly and can be assigned from a birth date alone, and by discarding aspects, houses, and the Moon altogether. What the reader received was therefore not a simplified chart but a different instrument, built from the old system's cheapest part.

### Q: Why did Kacey Musgraves title a divorce album star-crossed?

Musgraves titled the 2021 album star-crossed because she had built the record as a tragedy, and the word names tragedy's architecture in a single compound. Drawn from her divorce from Ruston Kelly and released with a companion film in September 2021, the album was described by its maker as a modern tragedy shaped in declared debt to Romeo and Juliet, the story of a love whose ending was present at its beginning. Its title track had been released ahead of the album in August 2021, so the word reached its audience first as a single song's name and only later as the whole work's frame, the verdict preceding the evidence in the release schedule as faithfully as it precedes it in the Prologue. The word's work in the title is retrospective: it confers the lovers' doom backward over a finished marriage, claiming the play's dignity for a private sorrow. No configuration of Saturn is alleged; the borrowing is of the verdict, not the doctrine.

### Q: What did the 2014 television series Star-Crossed put in the astrology's place?

The CW's Star-Crossed, premiered in February 2014, replaced the astrology with astronomy's raw material: its lovers are a human schoolgirl and an alien schoolboy among extraterrestrial teenagers integrated into a Louisiana high school, so the stars in the title are the literal heavens the boy arrived from. The crossing, which in the doctrine was a geometry of hostile aspects, becomes a sociology of hostile communities, two peoples whose enmity thwarts the pair. Nothing in the series requires a chart, an aspect, or a belief; the feud does all the work the cosmos once did, and the title keeps the old word as a genre label legible on a poster.

### Q: What happened to the rest of the astrologer's vocabulary, election, interrogation, and geniture?

The rest of the vocabulary retired into the museum of dead technique. Election survives only in a political sense borrowed from a different root of choosing; interrogation kept its word and changed its subject entirely, from the heavens to the police cell; geniture is a dictionary ghost, known to historians of the art and to nobody else. The words died because their practices died and no beloved text carried them. Star-crossed escaped their fate because it had emigrated before the collapse, into a play that every century reprints, where the gloss tradition maintained it as a specimen long after the profession that minted it had been laughed into retirement.

### Q: How did a term for cosmic hostility become a compliment couples apply to themselves?

The compliment grew inside the doom as its consolation. To be crossed by the heavens was to be opposed by the largest power available to the imagination, and opposition at that scale certifies the importance of what is opposed: only a love worth the universe's attention requires the universe's veto. As belief faded, the certification survived the veto, because couples kept the flattering half of the transaction, the cosmic scale, and discarded the terrifying half, the mechanism. Self-application completed the turn. A verdict once pronounced over strangers by a Chorus is chosen, in the phrase's late career, by lovers as a description of their own intensity, worn the way a borrowed title is worn, for the dignity.

### Q: What would a practitioner of 1597 have needed before pronouncing a couple star-crossed?

A practitioner of 1597 would have needed the couple's two nativities, cast from their birth hours and places, before pronouncing any verdict at all. From the charts he would have read each native's ruling planets, the condition of the lights, and the malefics' placement, and then compared the two configurations for the aspects between them, since a marriage joined two celestial deeds into one household. A verdict of crossed required the technical finding: hostile aspects, Saturn or Mars dominant and afflicting, the houses of marriage and life under pressure. The Chorus performs the whole audit in a compound word, and the compression is what the play's first audience, raised on almanacs, was trained to unpack.
