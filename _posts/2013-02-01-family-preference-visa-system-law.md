## Why these three sections decide family immigration outcomes

Most explanations of family immigration begin with policy arguments and arrive at the statute eventually, if at all. This article reverses that order. The Immigration and Nationality Act devotes three consecutive sections to the mechanics of who may immigrate through a family relationship, how many may do so in a given year, and how the available visa numbers are divided among countries of birth. Section 201 sets the worldwide level. Section 202 imposes the per-country ceiling. Section 203 allocates immigrant visas among the family preference categories. A reader who can state what each of those three sections does, who can name every family category and say who qualifies for each, who can explain the difference between an unlimited immediate relative and a numerically capped preference relative, and who can work the single arithmetic fact that the per-country ceiling is a flat percentage applied identically to every country regardless of its population or demand, possesses the working knowledge that practitioners, journalists, and families search for constantly. Everything else in this article is scaffolding around that core.

![Diagram showing the family preference categories and per-country ceiling](/assets/images/blog/blog-86.webp)

The thesis that organizes the pages below is that the operative text is the thing that decides outcomes. Immigration debates tend to be argued in the language of values, and values matter to legislators, but the family that waits twelve years for a visa number is waiting because of arithmetic written into the United States Code, not because of anyone's rhetoric. What follows is a section-by-section account of that arithmetic: the worldwide level, the country ceiling, the preference allocations, and the procedures that turn those numbers into waiting lists.

That claim about the text deserves a precise formulation, stated once and then demonstrated across the sections that follow rather than repeated. The flat-cap arithmetic: the entire pattern of decades-long waiting lists follows from applying an identical per-country ceiling to countries with vastly different populations and demand, and no explanation of immigration backlogs that omits this fact is complete.

### Which three sections of the Immigration and Nationality Act control family immigration?

Section 201 (8 U.S.C. 1151) sets the worldwide level and immediate relative exemption from numerical limits. Section 202 (8 U.S.C. 1152) imposes the ceiling of seven percent for states and two percent for dependent areas. Section 203 (8 U.S.C. 1153) allocates visas among family preference and employment-based categories.

The numbering deserves a short note because it confuses first-time readers of the statute. The Immigration and Nationality Act is the public law as Congress wrote and amended it; the United States Code is the topical arrangement of federal law maintained by the Office of the Law Revision Counsel. INA section 201 appears in the Code as 8 U.S.C. 1151, INA section 202 as 8 U.S.C. 1152, and INA section 203 as 8 U.S.C. 1153, and the Code headings track the INA section titles closely enough that either citation leads a researcher to the same text. INA section 204, codified at 8 U.S.C. 1154, governs the petition procedure by which a citizen or permanent resident asks the government to recognize a qualifying relationship, and it appears throughout this article as the procedural companion to the three allocation sections. Readers unfamiliar with the relationship between a public law and its Code placement can consult a general guide on [how to read a federal statute](/2022/05/01/how-to-read-a-federal-statute/) before continuing, since the distinction between an INA section number and a U.S.C. citation recurs on every page of immigration practice. The primer walks through how subsections, paragraphs, and subparagraphs nest inside these sections, so that allocation language referring to paragraph (1) or subparagraph (A) resolves to the correct category.

Section 201 does two jobs at once. Its subsection (a) states the worldwide levels, the annual totals within which the system operates. Its subsection (b) then carves out the aliens who are not subject to those levels or to the numerical limitations that follow: immediate relatives of United States citizens, certain returning residents, and a small set of other exempt classes. The structure matters because it creates the first great divide in family immigration, the divide between relatives who are counted and relatives who are not. A spouse, an unmarried child under twenty-one, or a parent of a United States citizen who is at least twenty-one falls into the uncounted class. Every other family relationship recognized by the statute falls into the counted class and competes for a finite annual allocation. The rest of the article walks that divide in detail.

Section 202 does one job with outsized consequences. It provides that the total number of immigrant visas made available to natives of any single foreign state under the family and employment preference subsections of section 203 may not exceed seven percent of the total made available under those subsections in a fiscal year, with a two percent ceiling for natives of a dependent area. The percentage looks modest and evenhanded on the page. Its interaction with unequal demand across countries is what generates the waiting lists that define the lived experience of the system. The section also contains the refinements a careful reader needs: the combined family-plus-employment base against which the percentage is measured, the partial exemption for most of the F2A allocation, and the quarterly lifting rule that lets unused numbers flow past the ceiling when supply outruns demand.

Section 203 does the detailed allocation work. Its subsection (a) divides the family preference visas among four numbered preferences, with the second preference split into 2A and 2B, and sets the base allocation for each. Its subsection (b) does the same for the employment-based preferences, and its subsection (c) creates the diversity program. The family half of subsection (a) is the heart of this article: five categories, each with a defined petitioner, a defined beneficiary, a base number, and a set of roll-down rules that move unused numbers from lower-numbered to higher-numbered preferences. The employment and diversity provisions appear here only insofar as they share the per-country ceiling or the worldwide level with the family categories, since each has its own dedicated treatment elsewhere in this series.

## Immediate relatives: the class without a numerical limit

The single most consequential sentence in the family immigration provisions is the definition tucked into 8 U.S.C. 1151(b)(2)(A)(i). It provides that the term immediate relatives means the children, spouses, and parents of a citizen of the United States, with the qualification that in the case of parents, the citizen must be at least twenty-one years of age. Three relationships, one age condition, and a petitioner class limited to citizens. From that definition flows the exemption in section 1151(b), whose heading reads "Aliens not subject to direct numerical limitations," and whose operative language provides that such aliens are not subject to the worldwide levels or numerical limitations of subsection (a). An immediate relative does not take a number from the 226,000 floor or from the 480,000 family level. An immediate relative does not wait for a visa number to become available in the monthly Visa Bulletin. An immediate relative's case moves at the speed of adjudication, not at the speed of the queue.

### Who counts as an immediate relative under the statute?

A spouse of a United States citizen, an unmarried child under 21 of a citizen, or a parent of a citizen who is at least 21 years old. The petitioner must be a citizen; a permanent resident cannot confer this status. A widow or widower of a citizen may also qualify where the marriage existed at death.

The word "child" in that definition carries a technical meaning that the statute supplies in INA section 101(b)(1). A child is a person who is unmarried and under twenty-one years of age. The two conditions are conjunctive. A nineteen-year-old who marries is no longer a child for immigration purposes, and a twenty-two-year-old who has never married is not a child either. The distinction between a child and a son or daughter runs through the entire preference system: in the vocabulary of the Act, sons and daughters are the adult offspring, twenty-one or older, or married, who have aged or married out of the child definition but remain related by blood or adoption. Every preference category below uses "sons or daughters" rather than "children" for exactly this reason, and the F2A category is the lone place where the child definition reappears, because it covers the minor children of permanent residents.

The exemption from numerical limitation is what makes the immediate relative class the engine of total family immigration. Because immediate relatives are admitted outside every cap, the actual number of family-based admissions in a year routinely exceeds the nominal family levels that dominate public discussion. The Congressional Research Service, in its 2018 survey of family-based policy, called the 480,000 family level a "permeable cap," a phrase that captures the design: the level bends upward under the weight of uncapped immediate relatives and cannot be held to its stated figure. A concrete illustration comes from fiscal year 2016, when the United States admitted 566,706 immediate relatives alongside 238,087 family preference immigrants, for a family-based total of 804,793 lawful permanent residents. The immediate relative figure alone exceeded the entire 480,000 nominal level. The preference figure, by contrast, sat near the 226,000 floor that the statute guarantees as a minimum, a floor discussed in the arithmetic section below.

One misunderstanding recurs so often that it deserves explicit correction here. Immediate relatives are exempt from the worldwide levels and the numerical limitations of section 201(a), but they are not exempt from the substantive requirements of immigration law. The relationship must be genuine and documented. The petitioner must file the petition and, where required, the affidavit of support. The beneficiary must be admissible, must complete medical examination and background screening, and must either adjust status inside the United States or complete consular processing abroad. Numerical exemption removes the wait for a visa number. It removes nothing else. Families who assume that an immediate relative petition guarantees admission discover, sometimes painfully, that the inadmissibility grounds, from prior immigration violations to certain criminal history to health-related grounds, apply with full force to the uncapped class.

## The five family preference categories, in statutory order

Section 203(a) of the Act, codified at 8 U.S.C. 1153(a), divides the family preference allocation among four numbered preferences, with the second preference divided by regulation and practice into 2A and 2B. The order is statutory, not editorial: first preference, second preference, third preference, fourth preference, each defined by the relationship between petitioner and beneficiary and each carrying a base allocation plus roll-down rights to unused numbers from specified other preferences. The walk below follows that order, giving for each category the petitioner who may file, the beneficiary who qualifies, the base number, and the way unused numbers cascade.

### F1: unmarried sons and daughters of United States citizens

The first preference covers the unmarried sons or daughters of citizens of the United States. The petitioner must be a citizen; a permanent resident cannot file in this category. The beneficiary must be a son or daughter, which in the Act's vocabulary means an offspring who is twenty-one or older, or who is married and therefore outside the child definition regardless of age, with the additional condition that the beneficiary be unmarried at the time of admission. The category thus serves the adult children of citizens who have not married: the twenty-five-year-old daughter of a citizen, the forty-year-old son of a citizen, each unmarried, each chargeable to the first preference.

The base allocation is 23,400 visa numbers, plus any numbers not required for the fourth preference. The roll-down runs from F4 to F1, which means that unused sibling-category numbers flow upward to the unmarried adult children of citizens rather than disappearing. The "plus any visas not required" language appears in each preference definition and creates a cascade: numbers unused by F4 augment F1, numbers unused by F1 augment F2, numbers unused by F1 and F2 augment F3, numbers unused by F1 through F3 augment F4. The cascade runs in both directions across the preference list, with the first preference drawing from the fourth and the fourth drawing from the first three, a circularity that ensures no family preference number goes unused for want of a recipient in its nominal category before it is offered elsewhere in the family system.

Queue arithmetic for F1 is straightforward at the base level: 23,400 numbers, augmented only by unused F4 numbers. Where the fourth preference absorbs its full allocation for a given country of birth, the roll-down contributes nothing and F1 operates on its base alone. The derivative structure adds a wrinkle: the unmarried son or daughter who is the principal beneficiary may have a spouse and minor children of their own who immigrate as derivatives, so a single F1 approval can carry a small household even though the category is defined by one relationship. Because derivatives count against the same allocation and the same per-country ceiling as principals, the number of petitions the F1 base serves in a year is lower than the visa-number figure suggests wherever the average derivative unit is large.

### F2A: spouses and minor children of lawful permanent residents

The second preference, subcategory A, covers the spouses or children of aliens lawfully admitted for permanent residence. The petitioner is a permanent resident, not a citizen. The beneficiary is either a spouse or a child in the technical sense: unmarried and under twenty-one. This is the only preference category that uses the child definition rather than the son-or-daughter definition, and the choice is deliberate. It gives permanent residents a path for the nuclear family they most urgently seek to reunite with: the husband or wife, and the young children.

The second preference as a whole carries a base allocation of 114,200, plus the amount by which the worldwide family level exceeds 226,000, plus unused first preference numbers, with the proviso that not less than seventy-seven percent of the second preference numbers be allocated to F2A. The seventy-seven percent floor is the statute's way of prioritizing the nuclear family of permanent residents over their adult offspring. When the worldwide family level sits at its 226,000 floor, the F2 base is 114,200 and the F2A share is at least 87,934. When immediate relative admissions fall and the computed family level rises above the floor, the F2 base rises with it, and the F2A floor rises proportionally. The F2A subcategory is thus the most elastic of the family preferences, expanding in years when the uncapped class contracts.

F2A carries two special features found nowhere else in the family preferences. First, seventy-five percent of the F2A allocation is exempt from the per-country ceiling under INA 202(a)(4), a carve-out that Congress added to keep the nuclear families of permanent residents from being trapped behind the country caps that bind every other family category. The exemption applies to three-quarters of the F2A numbers; the remaining quarter is subject to the ceiling like any other preference allocation. Second, the F2A queue has historically been the shortest of the family preference queues, and in some periods the category has had visa numbers available for all countries, meaning no waiting list at all, because the allocation plus the exemption has been sufficient to absorb demand. Those periods do not last, and the category has also experienced backlogs, but the structural advantages, the large base, the seventy-seven percent floor, and the seventy-five percent country-cap exemption, make F2A the least queue-bound of the counted family categories.

The marriage condition in F2A operates differently from the unmarried conditions of F1 and F2B. The spouse beneficiary is married by definition, and the petition depends on the marriage's continued existence: divorce before the beneficiary immigrates ends the qualifying relationship and revokes the petition, with no surviving priority date. The child beneficiary must remain unmarried, and marriage reclassifies the child out of the child definition, ending F2A eligibility. Naturalization of the petitioner while the case is pending converts the petition instead of ending it: the spouse becomes an immediate relative of a citizen and the queue disappears, while a qualifying child is judged under the Child Status Protection Act's naturalization provision. The marriage condition is thus both a vulnerability, through divorce, and a pathway, through the petitioner's naturalization.

### F2B: unmarried adult sons and daughters of lawful permanent residents

The second preference, subcategory B, covers the unmarried sons or unmarried daughters, but not children, of permanent residents. The parenthetical exclusion is the statute's own: "but not children" means the beneficiary must be twenty-one or older, the adult offspring of a permanent resident who has not married. The petitioner is a permanent resident. The beneficiary is the grown child. The category exists because Congress recognized that permanent residents, like citizens, have adult children, but it placed those adult children behind the permanent resident's spouse and minor children in priority, giving F2B the remainder of the second preference allocation after the seventy-seven percent F2A floor is satisfied.

A distinctive hazard attaches to F2B petitions: the marriage of the beneficiary. If the unmarried son or daughter of a permanent resident marries before immigrating, the petition is revoked by operation of law, because no category exists for the married son or daughter of a permanent resident. The statute provides no F3 equivalent for permanent residents. The married adult child of a permanent resident has no family preference category at all unless and until the petitioner naturalizes, at which point the beneficiary becomes the married son or daughter of a citizen and the petition converts to F3. Families navigating F2B must therefore treat the beneficiary's marital status as a live condition of the case, not a background fact, and practitioners routinely counsel F2B beneficiaries to defer marriage until after admission, a counsel that carries obvious human costs and that the statute's structure imposes without exception.

The F2B allocation follows by subtraction. The second preference total, at the 226,000 floor, is 114,200, and the statute reserves at least seventy-seven percent of that total for F2A, which is 87,934. The remainder available to F2B is therefore at most 26,266, before any roll-down from unused F1 numbers. That arithmetic makes F2B the smallest allocation in the preference system in most years: the other bases are fixed at 23,400, 23,400, and 65,000, and only the F2A floor and the F4 base exceed the F2B remainder. Unlike F2A, the F2B numbers carry no exemption from the per-country ceiling, so the full country throttle applies to the smallest base. The demand, drawn from permanent residents with adult unmarried children abroad, is substantial against that supply, which is why the F2B line for oversubscribed countries of birth moves slowly.

### F3: married sons and daughters of United States citizens

The third preference covers the married sons or married daughters of citizens of the United States. The petitioner must be a citizen. The beneficiary is a son or daughter who is married, regardless of age. The category is the citizen analogue of the missing permanent-resident category: where a permanent resident cannot petition for a married adult child at all, a citizen can, and the petition falls into F3. The spouse and children of the married son or daughter accompany as derivatives, so an F3 petition typically carries an entire household: the principal beneficiary, the beneficiary's spouse, and their minor children.

The F3 base allocation of 23,400 matches F1's, but the roll-down that feeds F3 is broader: unused numbers from both the first and second preferences augment F3, so the category's effective supply rises in years when F1 or F2 leave numbers unused. The beneficiary unit is typically larger than in F1 or F2, since a married son or daughter commonly immigrates with a spouse and minor children as derivatives, and each derivative consumes a number against the same base and ceiling. F3 also demonstrates the statute's use of marriage as a sorting mechanism across the petitioner divide. An unmarried adult son of a citizen is F1; upon marrying, the same son becomes F3 with the priority date retained. A married adult son of a permanent resident has no category at all; upon the parent's naturalization, the same son becomes F3. The category is thus the destination for two different conversions, one that lengthens the wait and one that creates the case.

### F4: siblings of adult United States citizens

The fourth preference covers the brothers or sisters of citizens of the United States, provided the citizen petitioner is at least twenty-one years of age. The petitioner must be a citizen and must have reached twenty-one. The beneficiary is a sibling: a brother or sister sharing at least one parent, including half-siblings and, under the Act's definitions, certain step-siblings and adoptive siblings who meet the statutory criteria. The twenty-one-year threshold mirrors the parent-petition rule and serves the same pacing function, preventing minor citizens from initiating sibling chains.

The base allocation is 65,000, plus unused numbers from the first three preferences, making F4 the largest of the family preference allocations. The size of the allocation reflects the category's history: when Congress built the preference system, the sibling category absorbed demand that the earlier national-origins system had distributed differently, and the 65,000 figure has survived every subsequent rewrite. The demand, however, dwarfs the allocation. Every adult citizen with a foreign-born sibling is a potential petitioner, the beneficiary unit often includes a spouse and children as derivatives, and the per-country ceiling applies in full. The result is the longest queue in the family immigration system for oversubscribed countries of birth, the queue most often cited in public discussion of backlogs, and the category most frequently targeted by proposals to narrow family immigration, including the 1995 recommendation of the Jordan Commission to eliminate it, discussed in the final section.

The fourth preference is the terminus of the roll-down cascade, and the terminus position cuts both ways. Unused numbers from the first, second, and third preferences flow into F4, so in years when the higher preferences leave numbers on the table, the F4 allocation swells beyond its 65,000 base and the line advances faster. At the same time, F4 is the donor of last resort upward: unused F4 numbers flow back to F1, completing the circle. In years when demand absorbs the full allocation in every preference, the cascade moves nothing, because there is nothing unused to move, and F4 operates on its base alone against the heaviest demand in the system. The circularity means the preference system wastes almost no numbers internally; the binding constraints are the worldwide floor and the per-country ceiling, not the distribution among the preferences.

## Relationships the statute does not recognize

A map of the family preference system is incomplete without its blank spaces. The Act recognizes a closed set of relationships, and the closed set excludes some of the kinship ties that matter most in ordinary family life. Stating the exclusions plainly prevents the most common and most painful misunderstandings in family immigration practice.

### Can a lawful permanent resident petition for a parent or a sibling?

No. A permanent resident may petition only for a spouse, a child who is unmarried and under twenty-one, or an unmarried son or daughter who is twenty-one or older. Parents and siblings of permanent residents have no family preference category, and no other provision of the Act admits them on the basis of the relationship alone.

The list of nonexistent categories is short and worth memorizing. A permanent resident cannot petition for a parent. A permanent resident cannot petition for a sibling. No petitioner, citizen or permanent resident, can sponsor a cousin, an aunt, an uncle, a grandparent, a grandchild, a niece, a nephew, a parent-in-law, or a sibling-in-law through the family preference system. The Congressional Research Service states the point without qualification: the Act does not permit either citizens or permanent residents to sponsor grandparents, grandchildren, cousins, aunts, or uncles. Citizens may petition for parents and siblings, provided the citizen has reached twenty-one; permanent residents may not petition for either.

The parent exclusion for permanent residents surprises many families because it inverts the intuitive hierarchy of closeness. A permanent resident's spouse and minor children may immigrate through F2A, often with little or no wait, while the permanent resident's own mother or father has no path at all through the family provisions. The asymmetry is a deliberate feature of the statutory design, not an oversight. Congress reserved parent and sibling sponsorship for citizens, on the theory, expressed across decades of legislative history, that the fullest family sponsorship rights should attach to the fullest membership in the political community. A permanent resident who wishes to petition for a parent must first naturalize; upon taking the oath, the former permanent resident becomes a citizen petitioner, the parent becomes an immediate relative, and the bar that seemed absolute dissolves. Naturalization is the bridge across every petitioner-class divide in the system, and its strategic importance cannot be overstated.

One further exclusion deserves mention because it traps the unwary. There is no family preference category for the fiance or fiancee of a citizen or permanent resident. The K nonimmigrant visa classifications allow a fiance to enter the United States to marry and then adjust status, but that is a nonimmigrant bridge to an immediate relative or preference petition after marriage, not a family preference category in itself. Families who assume that an engagement confers immigration rights discover that the statute recognizes marriages, not engagements, and the distinction has ended more than one planned wedding timeline.

## The arithmetic of the ceiling

The family preference system runs on three numbers and one percentage. The numbers are the 480,000 nominal family level, the 226,000 floor below which the computed preference level may not fall, and the per-category base allocations walked in the preceding section. The percentage is seven, with a two percent companion for dependent areas. This section works through how those figures combine, because the combination is the mechanism that produces the queues.

Begin with the worldwide level. Section 201(c) computes the family-sponsored preference level for a fiscal year by a formula: 480,000, minus the number of immediate relatives admitted in the prior fiscal year, minus certain parolees, plus the number of unused employment-based preference numbers from the prior year. The result may not fall below 226,000. The formula's moving parts deserve individual attention. The 480,000 starting point is the nominal level, the figure most often quoted and least often realized. The subtraction of prior-year immediate relative admissions is the hydraulic mechanism described earlier: uncapped admissions press the counted allocation downward. The addition of unused employment-based numbers is the spillover mechanism: employment numbers that go unused in one year augment the family allocation in the next, and the symmetric provision in the employment section returns the favor. The 226,000 floor is the guarantee: no matter how many immediate relatives were admitted last year, the family preference allocation cannot be computed below 226,000.

The floor binds in practice. In fiscal year 2012, the family preference limit was exactly 226,000, the floor itself, because prior-year immediate relative admissions had pressed the computed level down to the minimum. The per-country limit that year was 25,967, a figure derived from the seven percent rate applied to the combined family and employment preference totals. The 2012 instance is useful because it shows the system at its tightest: the family allocation at the statutory minimum, the country ceiling computed against minimized totals, and every preference category competing for shares of a pool that could not lawfully shrink further.

Turn to the per-country ceiling, the provision that does the most work in the shortest text. INA section 202(a)(2), codified at 8 U.S.C. 1152(a)(2), provides that the total number of immigrant visas made available to natives of any single foreign state or dependent area under the family and employment preference subsections of section 203 in any fiscal year may not exceed seven percent, for a single foreign state, or two percent, for a dependent area, of the total number of such visas made available under those subsections in that fiscal year. Three features of that sentence require emphasis, because each is a recurring source of error.

First, the base against which the percentage is measured is the combined total of family-sponsored and employment-based preference visas, not the family total alone and not any single category. The seven percent is seven percent of the whole preference pool. A country's family preference usage and its employment-based usage draw against the same country ceiling. This combined base is the correction the fact record for this article emphasizes, and it matters for queue analysis: a country with heavy employment-based demand has less room under its ceiling for family preference admissions, and the Visa Bulletin's country-specific cut-off dates reflect the combined pressure.

Second, the ceiling applies per country of birth, technically per country of chargeability, not per country of citizenship or residence. A person born in one country who holds the passport of another is charged to the country of birth, with limited exceptions for the children and spouses of principal beneficiaries, who may be charged to the principal's country under the cross-chargeability rules. The chargeability detail matters because it determines which queue an applicant joins, and families with members born in different countries sometimes find their cases split across different cut-off dates.

Third, the ceiling is subject to two statutory refinements. Under section 202(a)(4), seventy-five percent of the F2A allocation is exempt from the per-country limit, the carve-out that keeps the spouses and minor children of permanent residents moving. Under section 202(a)(3), the per-country ceiling lifts for the remainder of a calendar quarter when the total number of available visa numbers exceeds the number of qualified applicants, allowing unused numbers to flow to oversubscribed countries rather than going to waste. The quarterly lift is the system's safety valve: the ceiling binds only when demand exceeds supply, and when supply exceeds demand the ceiling steps aside.

### Why do beneficiaries in the same preference category wait different lengths of time?

The worldwide category allotment and the per-country ceiling act together. A country whose demand stays beneath its seven percent share draws numbers as fast as the worldwide allotment allows. A country whose demand exceeds that share can use no more than the ceiling, so its qualified applicants pile up while others advance.

## The priority date: how a place in line is created and kept

Every counted family immigration case has a priority date, and the priority date is the case's place in line. The concept is simple, its consequences are not, and misunderstandings about it account for a large share of the anxiety families bring to the process.

### How does a priority date decide who receives a visa?

The priority date is the date the immigrant petition, Form I-130, was properly filed. The State Department publishes monthly cut-off dates in the Visa Bulletin for each oversubscribed preference category and country of birth. A beneficiary may proceed to a visa number only when the priority date falls earlier than the applicable cut-off date.

The filing date, not the approval date, establishes the place in line. A petition filed on March 14 of a given year and approved eighteen months later carries a March 14 priority date for the entire life of the case. The eighteen months of adjudication do not cost the beneficiary eighteen months of queue position, because the queue position was fixed at filing. This is why practitioners urge prompt filing: every day of delay in filing is a day of queue position permanently lost, while delay in adjudication costs nothing in queue terms. The Child Status Protection Act, discussed in the next section, extends the same logic to the aging-out problem by subtracting petition processing time from the beneficiary's age, but the priority date itself needs no such correction. It is immune to processing delay by definition.

The Visa Bulletin is the instrument that translates the statutory arithmetic into monthly reality. Published by the State Department, it lists for each preference category and each oversubscribed country the cut-off date: applicants with priority dates earlier than the cut-off may be issued visa numbers; applicants with later priority dates must wait. The Bulletin also distinguishes, in its two-chart format, between dates for filing and final action dates, a refinement that allows applicants to begin assembling their cases before a number is actually available. The cut-off dates move as numbers are used and become available: forward when demand within the available numbers permits, backward in the phenomenon known as retrogression when demand has exceeded the authorized usage and the Department must pull the date back to stay within the statutory limits. Retrogression is the queue's cruelest feature, the month when a family whose priority date nearly reached the cut-off finds itself years away again, and it follows directly from the arithmetic of fixed allocations against variable demand.

## What a decades-long queue looks like in numbers

Abstract arithmetic becomes concrete through worked illustrations. The illustrations below use two kinds of numbers, and the distinction between them is essential. Dated figures drawn from official sources describe the system at a specific point in time. Hypothetical figures, each one labeled as such, demonstrate how the statutory mechanics produce queue lengths from assumed demand levels. Neither kind describes the wait facing any pending case, and no illustration below should be read as guidance about how long any particular case will take.

The dated figure that anchors this section comes from the Congressional Research Service: as of November 1, 2017, approximately 3.95 million approved family preference petitioners were waiting for visa numbers. That figure counts approved petitions, not individuals, since each petition may carry derivative family members, and it counts only the preference categories, since immediate relatives hold no queue position. The 3.95 million is a stock, not a flow: it is the accumulated backlog of approved cases against annual preference allocations in the low hundreds of thousands. The arithmetic of stocks and flows does the rest. When the annual inflow of new approvals exceeds the annual outflow of visa issuances, the stock grows; when a category-country combination is oversubscribed, the stock for that combination grows fastest.

Consider a labeled hypothetical that shows how the per-country ceiling converts unequal demand into unequal waits. Hypothetical illustration one: suppose the F4 category in a given fiscal year has an effective allocation of 70,000 visa numbers after roll-down, and suppose the per-country ceiling for the combined preference pool yields an F4 country share of roughly 4,900 numbers for each oversubscribed country. Country Alpha has 2,000 F4 applicants with pending priority dates; Country Beta has 60,000. In Country Alpha, every applicant is accommodated within the year's allocation, the category carries no cut-off date for Alpha, and new Alpha petitioners face only processing time. In Country Beta, 4,900 numbers serve 60,000 applicants, the cut-off date advances by roughly one-twelfth of the queue each year, and a newly filed Beta petition joins a line measured in decades. The category is the same, the allocation formula is the same, the ceiling percentage is the same. The demand differs, and the demand difference is the entire explanation of the wait difference. This hypothetical uses round numbers for clarity; actual allocations and country shares vary by year with the worldwide level, the cascade, and the combined family-employment base.

Hypothetical illustration two demonstrates the F2A exemption's effect. Suppose the F2A effective allocation is 90,000 numbers, of which seventy-five percent, or 67,500, is exempt from the per-country ceiling. For the exempt portion, no country-specific cut-off applies; applicants from every country of birth draw from the common pool in priority date order. For the remaining 22,500, the ceiling applies normally. The exemption thus converts most of F2A into a worldwide queue, where a petitioner from a high-demand country waits behind only the worldwide line rather than a country-specific line. The illustration shows why F2A has cleared its queue faster than its sibling categories in past periods: three-quarters of its numbers ignore the country cap that binds the other preferences in full.

Hypothetical illustration three shows retrogression. Suppose a preference category for a particular country has a cut-off date of June 2015, meaning applicants with priority dates before June 2015 may proceed. Suppose 8,000 such applicants exist but only 5,000 numbers remain available to that country-category combination for the fiscal year. The State Department must prevent usage beyond the allocation, so it retrogresses the cut-off date, perhaps to June 2012, cutting off the 3,000 applicants whose priority dates fall between the old and new cut-offs. Those applicants retain their priority dates and their places in line; they simply cannot be issued numbers until the date advances past them again, which requires a new fiscal year's allocation. Retrogression is not a loss of place. It is a pause imposed by the arithmetic, and it is the mechanism by which the Department keeps total issuances within the statutory limits when demand forecasting proves optimistic.

A final dated illustration returns to the fiscal year 2012 figures introduced earlier. With the family preference limit at its 226,000 floor and the per-country limit at 25,967 across the combined preference pool, each oversubscribed country's total preference usage could not exceed 25,967 for the year. The F4 base allocation alone was 65,000 worldwide. The comparison shows the scale mismatch that defines the system: a single preference category's worldwide base is more than twice any single country's total preference ceiling, so the worldwide allocation must be divided among all countries and the oversubscribed countries' shares are exhausted long before their queues clear. The numbers are from 2012 and describe 2012. The structural relationship they illustrate, worldwide category allocations divided by a flat country percentage against concentrated demand, is permanent.

## The Child Status Protection Act: two rules for aging out

The Act's definition of a child, unmarried and under twenty-one, creates a boundary that time crosses without permission. A beneficiary who is twenty when the petition is filed may be twenty-three when the visa number becomes available, and under the pre-2002 reading of the statute, that beneficiary had aged out: no longer a child, no longer eligible in the category for which the petition was filed, the years of waiting converted into a nullity. Congress addressed part of this problem in the Child Status Protection Act of 2002, enacted August 6, 2002 as Public Law 107-208, which added subsection (f) to 8 U.S.C. 1151. The Act's mechanism is precise, limited, and widely misunderstood, and the misunderstanding usually takes the form of believing the protection is broader than it is.

### What did the Child Status Protection Act of 2002 change?

It created two different age-protection rules. For immediate relatives of citizens, the child's age freezes on the date the I-130 petition is filed. For family preference and other capped categories, the protected age equals the beneficiary's age when a visa number becomes available minus the number of days the petition was pending with immigration authorities.

The two rules reflect two different problems. The immediate relative rule, codified at 8 U.S.C. 1151(f)(1), addresses adjudication delay for the uncapped class. An immediate relative has no queue; the only waiting is processing time. If a citizen files for a nineteen-year-old child and the agency takes two years to approve the petition, the beneficiary is twenty-one at approval and would have aged out under the old reading. The statute freezes the age at filing: a beneficiary who was under twenty-one when the I-130 was filed remains a child for immediate relative purposes regardless of how long adjudication takes. The protection is complete for processing delay and irrelevant to queue delay, because the uncapped class has no queue.

The preference rule addresses a harder problem. A preference beneficiary waits twice: once for petition adjudication and once for a visa number. The statute protects against the first wait but not the second. The formula takes the beneficiary's biological age on the date a visa number becomes available and subtracts the number of days the petition was pending, filing to approval. If the resulting CSPA age is under twenty-one, the beneficiary retains child status; if it is twenty-one or older, the beneficiary has aged out despite the protection. A worked example shows the rule's limits. Suppose a permanent resident files an F2A petition for a nineteen-year-old child, the petition pends for one year, and a visa number becomes available four years after filing, when the beneficiary is twenty-three. The CSPA age is twenty-three minus one year of pending time: twenty-two. The beneficiary has aged out. The one year of agency processing was forgiven; the three years of queue waiting were not. The statute forgives the government's delay. It does not forgive the queue.

The Act contains a second protection of great practical importance: the naturalization provision at 8 U.S.C. 1151(f)(2). When a permanent resident petitioner naturalizes, a pending petition for a child is treated under the immediate relative rules, with the child's age judged as of the naturalization date. A nineteen-year-old beneficiary of an F2A petition whose parent naturalizes when the beneficiary is twenty becomes an immediate relative whose age froze, in effect, at twenty, young enough to qualify. The provision converts the parent's naturalization into the child's salvation, and it is one more reason practitioners treat the petitioner's naturalization as the highest-leverage event in a family case.

The statute's limits are as important as its protections. The CSPA age must still be under twenty-one after the subtraction; the formula helps only where the pending time bridges the gap. The beneficiary must remain unmarried; marriage ends child status under the Act's definitions and no CSPA provision restores it. The beneficiary must have sought lawful permanent resident status within one year of visa availability, absent extraordinary circumstances, a diligence requirement that punishes families who do not act promptly when their number arrives. And the Act does nothing for the beneficiary who ages out during the queue wait itself, the largest source of aging-out in the family preference system, because the queue wait is a creature of the numerical limits that the CSPA left intact. Families who assume the 2002 Act solved aging out discover its boundaries only when a consular officer applies the formula and the result comes back twenty-one.

### The four limitations, applied to derivative beneficiaries

The four limitations the statute places on the CSPA protection apply with equal force to derivatives, and each one has ended real cases. First, the adjusted age must still be under twenty-one when the number becomes available; the subtraction of petition processing time helps only where the pending time bridges the gap between the biological age and twenty-one, and the years of queue waiting are never subtracted. Second, the derivative must remain unmarried; marriage ends child status under the Act's definitions and no CSPA provision restores it, so a derivative who marries while waiting loses the classification regardless of the age calculation. Third, the derivative must have sought lawful permanent resident status within one year of visa availability, absent extraordinary circumstances, and the requirement is per person rather than per petition: a principal who files on time but neglects to file for an accompanying child can forfeit the child's protection while preserving their own. Fourth, the Act does nothing for the beneficiary who ages out during the queue wait itself, which is the largest source of aging out in the preference system, because the queue wait is a creature of the numerical limits the 2002 Act left intact.

## Four amendments that built the enacted text

Sections 201, 202, and 203 did not emerge in their present form from a single enactment. They are the product of four amendments across twenty-five years, each responding to the perceived failures of its predecessor, each layering new mechanics onto old foundations. The history matters to a provisions explainer because the enacted text's oddities, the hemispheric echoes, the roll-down cascade, the permeable cap, are fossils of these revisions.

The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952, the McCarran-Walter Act, already contained a family preference hierarchy, a fact that corrects the common impression that family-based immigration began in 1965. What the 1965 amendments created was the modern system: the abolition of the national-origins quotas, the seven-category preference system emphasizing family reunification and occupational skills, and the uncapped immediate relative class. Public Law 89-236, enacted October 3, 1965 upon H.R. 2580, set the Eastern Hemisphere limit at 170,000 with a 20,000 per-country cap, left the Western Hemisphere under a separate 120,000 limit without preference categories, and admitted immediate relatives without numerical restriction. President Lyndon Johnson signed the bill at the foot of the Statue of Liberty and declared that from that day forward admission would rest on skills and close relationship to those already in America. The 1965 Act's family emphasis was a deliberate repudiation of the national-origins system's ethnic ranking, and its demographic consequences, the reshaping of immigration flows toward Asia, Latin America, and Africa, are traced in this series' companion account of the [demographic impact of the 1965 amendments](/2013/02/15/immigration-act-1965-demographic-impact/). The companion piece measures those consequences through admission and census data across the decades after enactment. Readers seeking the full legislative narrative of the 1965 enactment will find it in the series guide to the [Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965](/2012/12/01/immigration-nationality-act-1965-guide/). The guide carries the full preference ranking as enacted and the legislative record behind the uncapped immediate relative class.

The 1965 structure contained an asymmetry that became untenable within a decade. The Eastern Hemisphere operated under preferences and a per-country cap; the Western Hemisphere operated under an overall ceiling without preferences, on a first-come, first-served basis. The asymmetry produced its own distortions, and Congress corrected it in Public Law 94-571, enacted October 20, 1976, which extended the preference system and the 20,000 per-country limit to the Western Hemisphere. The 1976 amendment is the less-remembered of the four, but its structural importance is considerable: it made the preference categories and the country cap worldwide in application, ending the hemisphere-by-hemisphere administration that had characterized the first decade of the modern system.

The dual-hemisphere architecture survived the 1976 repair for only two more years. Public Law 95-412, enacted October 5, 1978 upon H.R. 12443, eliminated the separate hemispheric quota systems and established a single worldwide ceiling of 290,000. The 1978 consolidation simplified administration and removed the last structural trace of hemispheric differentiation from the numerical limits. The per-country ceiling, carried forward from the 1965 and 1976 enactments, applied against a single worldwide total rather than two hemispheric totals, a change that altered the arithmetic for oversubscribed countries without changing the seven percent rate's successor figure of 20,000 per country.

The Immigration Act of 1990, Public Law 101-649, enacted November 29, 1990, rewrote section 201 and set the numerical architecture that remains in force. The 1990 Act established the 480,000 family-sponsored level with its 226,000 floor, the 140,000 employment-based allocation, the 55,000 diversity visas, and the 675,000 worldwide cap that the Congressional Research Service describes as permeable, regularly exceeded by the uncapped immediate relatives and by other exempt classes. The Act also restructured the preference categories into the form described in this article, set the base allocations walked earlier in this article, and carried forward the seven percent per-country ceiling of section 202 with its two percent dependent-area companion. The full legislative history of the 1990 rewrite, its employment-based innovations, its diversity lottery, and its political compromises, is told in the series guide to the [Immigration Act of 1990](/2013/03/01/immigration-act-1990-guide/). The companion guide treats the employment-based innovations and the diversity program in full, which is the other half of the shared ceiling the family categories draw against. For the family provisions, the 1990 Act's significance is that it wrote the text this article explains: the immediate relative definition, the five preference categories, the floor, the permeable level, and the flat country percentage, all in substantially their present form.

## From petition to green card: the procedural path

The statutory categories allocate visa numbers. A separate procedural machinery moves a family from a qualifying relationship to an issued immigrant visa. The machinery has five stages, and a failure at any stage ends the case regardless of how strong the underlying relationship is.

The first stage is the petition. A United States citizen or lawful permanent resident files Form I-130, Petition for Alien Relative, with United States Citizenship and Immigration Services, asking the government to recognize the claimed family relationship. The petitioner submits evidence of the petitioner's status, citizen or permanent resident, and evidence of the relationship: a marriage certificate for a spouse, a birth certificate for a child or parent, birth certificates showing a common parent for a sibling. The agency adjudicates the petition on the relationship's validity, not on visa availability; an approvable petition is approved even if the preference queue stretches for decades. Approval establishes the priority date, the filing date, and classifies the beneficiary into the applicable preference category or the immediate relative class. Denial, for failure to prove the relationship or the petitioner's status, ends the case before any queue is joined.

The third stage is consular processing or adjustment of status. Beneficiaries outside the United States proceed through the National Visa Center, which collects the affidavit of support, civil documents, and fees, and then schedules a consular interview at a United States embassy or consulate. Beneficiaries inside the United States who entered lawfully and maintained status may instead file Form I-485 to adjust status without departing, provided a visa number is available and no bar applies. The two paths converge at adjudication: medical examination, background screening, and a determination of admissibility.

The fifth stage is admission and the derivative structure. The principal beneficiary immigrates in the petitioned category. The principal's spouse and unmarried minor children, if any, immigrate as derivatives in the same category on the same priority date, without separate petitions. Derivatives count against the category allocation and the country ceiling alongside principals, which is why per-petition visa consumption varies with family size and why the effective queue for a category depends on the average derivative count as well as the petition count. After admission, the new permanent resident may, within the limits of the recognized categories, file petitions for qualifying relatives of their own, the mechanism the next section describes in neutral terms. Readers working through a family case against the statutory text may find a structured [legislation study notebook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html) useful for tracking the category, priority date, and Bulletin movement of each petition in the family.

## How the family, employment, and diversity shares fit together

The family preference categories do not operate in isolation. They share the worldwide level and the per-country ceiling with the employment-based preferences, and they sit alongside the diversity program within the overall immigration framework. The interactions are mechanical, but they affect family queues in ways that surprise readers who study the family provisions alone.

The per-country ceiling's combined base is the principal interaction. Section 202(a)(2) measures the seven percent against the total of family-sponsored and employment-based preference visas made available under section 203(a) and (b). A country's family preference admissions and its employment-based admissions draw against one ceiling, not two. In practice this means that countries with large employment-based usage have less room under the ceiling for family preference admissions, and the State Department's country-specific cut-off dates for family categories reflect the combined demand. The family practitioner who ignores the employment-based demand from the same country of birth misunderstands the queue, because the queue is set by the total, not by the family share alone.

The diversity program stands apart. Section 203(c) allocates 55,000 diversity immigrant visas annually to natives of countries with historically low immigration to the United States, selected by lottery among qualified entrants. The diversity allocation is outside the family-employment preference pool for per-country ceiling purposes under section 202(a)(2), which by its terms covers only subsections (a) and (b) of section 203. Diversity has its own regional distribution and its own country limitations within subsection (c). The family provisions interact with diversity only at the margins: a diversity selectee's spouse and children immigrate as derivatives, and a family preference beneficiary may also enter the diversity lottery as an independent path, the two systems operating in parallel without interfering. The essential point for the family reader is negative: the diversity numbers do not augment the family allocation, the diversity demand does not consume family numbers, and the per-country ceiling that shapes family queues is computed without regard to diversity admissions.

### The employment preferences at a glance

Because the per-country ceiling pools family and employment numbers, a family practitioner needs a working sketch of the employment side. Section 203(b) allocates 140,000 visas across five employment preferences: EB-1 for priority workers, including persons of extraordinary ability, outstanding professors and researchers, and multinational executives, at 28.6 percent; EB-2 for professionals with advanced degrees or exceptional ability, at 28.6 percent; EB-3 for skilled workers, professionals, and other workers, at 28.6 percent; EB-4 for special immigrants, including certain religious workers and broadcasters, at 7.1 percent; and EB-5 for employment-creating investors, at 7.1 percent. Unused numbers cascade downward through the employment preferences much as they do on the family side, and the two systems exchange leftovers across fiscal years under Section 201's carryover rules.

The employment system's demand profile differs sharply from the family system's, and the difference matters for the shared ceiling. Employment demand concentrates in a small number of countries whose nationals dominate the skilled-worker pipelines, while family demand spreads across the countries with the largest diasporas. When employment demand from a particular country surges, it consumes that country's seven percent share and leaves less room under the same ceiling for the country's family applicants, even though the two queues serve entirely different populations. Conversely, when employment demand falls short worldwide, the unused numbers roll to the family side the next year and ease the family queues at the margin. The ceiling makes the two systems roommates, and roommates affect each other's comfort whether they interact or not.

One asymmetry deserves explicit notice. The employment preferences have no equivalent of the immediate-relative category: every employment-based immigrant counts against the numbers. The family system's uncapped category therefore has no employment parallel, and the permeable-cap phenomenon, where total admissions exceed the announced level, is driven on the family side by immediate relatives and on the employment side by nothing at all. Analysts who add the two systems' totals together without noting this asymmetry misattribute the overflow.

## The neutral mechanism behind a loaded term

Public discussion of family immigration employs a term that the statute never uses. "Chain migration" appears nowhere in the Immigration and Nationality Act. The Congressional Research Service describes it as "a term some have used to characterize the process by which family-based immigration can create self-perpetuating and expanding migration flows, as foreign nationals who obtain lawful permanent resident status and citizenship then sponsor other relatives under the family-based immigration provisions." The mechanism the term denotes is real, it operates entirely through the categories this article has walked, and it can be described without adopting either the term's pejorative charge or its celebratory inversion.

### How does one admission make later petitions possible?

A citizen or permanent resident files Form I-130 asking the government to recognize a qualifying family relationship. Approval places the relative in line by priority date within the applicable preference category, subject to annual caps and the per-country ceiling. After immigrating, and for some categories after naturalizing, the new immigrant may petition for qualifying relatives.

The sequence is lawful at every step because each step uses a category Congress wrote for the purpose. A citizen petitions for a sibling under F4. The sibling immigrates, and after five years of permanent residence, naturalizes. The new citizen petitions for a spouse as an immediate relative and for minor children as immediate relatives, and for a married son or daughter under F3. Each petition is adjudicated on its own relationship evidence. Each beneficiary waits in the applicable queue or, for immediate relatives, proceeds without one. The chain is not a loophole or a circumvention. It is the petition process of INA section 204 operating as designed, one recognized relationship at a time.

Both terms in public use refer to this same mechanism. Critics of the pattern call it chain migration and illustrate its multiplicative potential: the 1981 Select Commission on Immigration and Refugee Policy, chaired by Theodore Hesburgh, illustrated how a single admitted couple could, through successive petitions across generations, make as many as eighty-four persons eligible for admission. Supporters of family immigration reject the term as pejorative and speak instead of family reunification, emphasizing the humanitarian value of keeping families intact across borders. Empirical measurement exists: researchers Carr and Tienda estimated an immigration multiplier of 3.46 for the 1996 to 2000 period, meaning one hundred initial lawful permanent residents subsequently sponsored three hundred forty-six new immigrants through the family provisions, a figure far below the hypothetical maximums used in advocacy illustrations. This article's own voice uses neither label as a characterization. The mechanism is family-based sponsorship through the petition process Congress wrote into INA 204, operating within the categories of INA 203(a), subject to the numerical limits of INA 201 and the per-country ceiling of INA 202. Whether the resulting flows are described as chains or reunifications is a question of vocabulary that the statute leaves to its commentators, and this article does not adjudicate it.

The sequence has natural brakes that the phrase "chain" tends to obscure. Every link requires a qualifying relationship from the statute's closed list, so the sequence cannot wander to cousins or in-laws. Every preference link requires a visa number, so the sequence moves at the pace of the allotments and the country ceiling. Naturalization, which opens the citizen-only categories, takes years and is never guaranteed. And the immediate-relative category, which moves fastest, is available only to the spouses, minor children, and parents of citizens. The petition sequence is real, it is lawful, and its pace and reach are set by the same numbers that govern everything else in this article.

## Myths about the queue

The family preference system generates a durable folklore, much of it wrong in ways that cost families time and money. The myths below are the ones practitioners report encountering most often, each followed by the statutory correction. The corrections rest on the same text the article has walked: the closed petitioner lists, the section 1151(b) exemption, the filing date rule, the two CSPA rules, the marriage reclassification rules, and the combined ceiling base. A companion treatment of [common myths in immigration law](/2013/04/15/us-immigration-law-myths/) collects these and related misconceptions in one place.

The first myth holds that a permanent resident can sponsor any close relative. The correction is the closed set: spouses, children, and unmarried sons and daughters, and nothing else. Parents, siblings, married children, and the extended family are outside the permanent resident's petitioning power until naturalization.

The second myth holds that immediate relatives count against the annual caps and therefore face queues. The correction is section 1151(b): immediate relatives are not subject to the worldwide levels or numerical limitations, they hold no Visa Bulletin queue position, and their cases move at adjudication speed.

The third myth holds that the priority date is the date the petition was approved. The correction is the filing date rule: the I-130 filing date fixes the place in line, adjudication delay costs no queue position, and prompt filing is the only way to secure an early date.

The fourth myth holds that the Child Status Protection Act freezes a child's age for the entire wait. The correction is the two-rule structure: the filing-date freeze applies to immediate relatives, the subtraction formula applies to preference beneficiaries, and the years spent waiting for a visa number are never subtracted.

The fifth myth holds that marrying has no effect on a pending petition. The correction is the category reclassification rules: an F1 beneficiary who marries becomes F3, an F2B beneficiary who marries loses the petition entirely, and marital status is a live condition of every family case.

The sixth myth holds that each preference category has its own per-country limit. The correction is the combined base, and it is important enough to carry its own question.

### Does the per-country ceiling apply separately to each preference category?

No. INA 202(a)(2) sets a single ceiling of seven percent for each foreign state, measured against the combined total of family-sponsored and employment-based preference visas. A country's usage across all family categories and all employment categories counts against the same limit.

## Chargeability: the country of birth rule

The per-country ceiling is measured against natives of a foreign state, and "native" means, with limited exceptions, a person born in that state. The governing concept is chargeability: every immigrant visa applicant is charged to a country, and the charge determines which country-specific cut-off date, if any, applies to the case. The default rule is birthplace. A person born in Country Beta who holds citizenship in Country Alpha and has lived for decades in Country Gamma is charged to Country Beta. Citizenship does not control. Residence does not control. The birth certificate controls.

The exceptions matter because they reunite families that the default rule would split across queues. The principal beneficiary's spouse and children may be charged to the principal's country of birth under the cross-chargeability rules, even when the spouse or child was born elsewhere. A principal born in an oversubscribed country whose spouse was born in an undersubscribed country may have the entire family unit charged to the spouse's country, moving all of them from a decades-long queue to a category with no cut-off date. The election runs in one direction: it allows charging to the less oversubscribed country, not the more. A second exception covers children born in a country where neither parent was born and where neither parent had residence at the time, who may be charged to either parent's country of birth. A third covers persons born in the United States, who are not subject to chargeability at all because birth on American soil confers citizenship.

The dependent-area rule extends the same logic at two percent. Natives of dependent areas, territories and possessions that are not independent states, face the lower ceiling under the same statutory sentence that sets seven percent for foreign states. The base for the two percent is the same combined family and employment preference total, so the dependent-area ceiling is two percent of the whole preference pool, not two percent of a smaller number. In practice the dependent-area ceiling binds rarely, because few dependent areas generate petition volume approaching even two percent of the combined total, but the provision confirms that the ceiling's unit is the political entity of birth: independent states receive the full seven percent and dependent areas the reduced two percent, and the individual applicant's queue is set by whichever entity the birth certificate assigns.

## Reading the Visa Bulletin's family chart

The Visa Bulletin's family-sponsored chart is the monthly publication where the statutory arithmetic becomes a set of dates, and learning to read it is a practical skill this article can teach directly. The chart lists the five preference categories as rows and chargeability areas as columns: a worldwide column for all countries not separately listed, plus separate columns for the oversubscribed countries of birth. Each cell contains either the letter C, meaning visa numbers are available, the letter U, meaning unauthorized or unavailable, or a cut-off date.

A C means visa numbers are available to all qualified applicants in that category and chargeability area, regardless of priority date. A U means no numbers are available, usually because the annual limit for that category has been reached. A cut-off date means only applicants with priority dates earlier than the date may proceed. The worldwide column shows C for a category when total worldwide demand stays within the allocation; the oversubscribed-country columns carry earlier cut-off dates reflecting the country ceiling's bite. A reader comparing the worldwide F4 cell with an oversubscribed country's F4 cell sees the ceiling's effect in a single glance: the same category, two different dates, the gap between them the visible measure of concentrated demand.

The Bulletin's two-chart format adds a refinement. The Dates for Filing chart tells applicants when they may submit their documents to the National Visa Center and begin case assembly. The Final Action Dates chart tells them when a visa number may actually be allocated and the case approved. The filing date is typically later, more generous, than the final action date, allowing the pipeline to fill ahead of number availability. An applicant whose priority date has passed the filing date but not the final action date may prepare but not complete the case. The distinction prevents the administrative pipeline from becoming the bottleneck behind the numerical bottleneck.

Someone must do the arithmetic and publish it. Each fall, the State Department's Visa Office announces the numerical limits for the coming fiscal year: the family preference level after the Section 201(c) computation, the per-category allotments after the cascade, and the per-country ceiling derived from the combined family-employment total. The monthly Visa Bulletin is the same office's running application of those figures: each month the Visa Office compares the remaining supply against the known demand and sets the cutoff dates that ration the supply across the fiscal year's remaining months. When the office sees demand outpacing supply, it holds cutoffs steady or retrogresses them; when it sees supply outpacing demand, it advances cutoffs rapidly and may invoke the quarterly exemption that lifts the country ceiling.

Three reading cautions complete the lesson. First, cut-off dates move in both directions. They advance when numbers free up and retrogress when usage threatens the limits, so a date that moved forward for six months may jump backward in the seventh. Second, the Bulletin reflects allocations, not processing capacity; a category with available numbers still requires months of adjudication, and families who confuse numerical availability with administrative speed misplan their timelines. Third, the Bulletin is prospective and monthly. It states the position for the coming month based on reported demand and remaining supply. It does not forecast, and the State Department cautions against treating a trend of advancing dates as a promise of continued movement. The chart is a snapshot of the arithmetic, taken monthly, and the arithmetic recomputes with every visa issued.

## Marriage, naturalization, and category strategy

The family preference system rewards certain life decisions and punishes others, mechanically and without regard to intent. Families who understand the reward structure can time their decisions to it. Families who do not discover the structure only when it operates against them. This section states the strategic considerations plainly, as practitioner guidance grounded in the statutory mechanics.

Naturalization of the petitioner is the highest-leverage event in the system. A permanent resident who naturalizes converts a spouse's F2A petition into an immediate relative case, eliminating the queue. The same naturalization converts an unmarried adult son or daughter's F2B petition into F1, usually shortening the wait, and it opens categories that did not exist before: parents become immediate relatives, siblings become F4 beneficiaries, married sons and daughters become F3 beneficiaries. The petitioner who naturalizes early in a case's life confers years of advantage; the petitioner who delays naturalization leaves those years on the table. Practitioners routinely advise eligible permanent resident petitioners to naturalize as quickly as the residence and physical presence requirements allow, not for civic reasons but for the arithmetic consequences to pending family cases.

Marriage of the beneficiary is the highest-risk event. An F1 beneficiary who marries is reclassified to F3, retaining the priority date but joining a longer queue. An F2B beneficiary who marries loses the petition entirely, because no category covers the married son or daughter of a permanent resident. The asymmetry produces difficult counsel: the F2B beneficiary approaching the front of a decade-long queue must choose between marrying and immigrating, a choice the statute forces without softening. The only escape is the petitioner's naturalization before the marriage, which converts the case to F1, where marriage merely reclassifies rather than revokes. Families in F2B therefore watch two clocks: the Bulletin's movement toward their priority date and the petitioner's progress toward citizenship.

Marriage of the petitioner follows different rules. A permanent resident who marries may petition for the new spouse in F2A, with a fresh priority date at filing. A citizen who marries may petition for the new spouse as an immediate relative, with no queue at all. Divorce ends a spousal petition: the qualifying relationship ceases, the petition is revoked, and any accrued priority date is lost. The death of the petitioner generally ends the petition as well, though the widow and widower provisions and certain humanitarian reinstatement authorities create narrow exceptions at the margins.

## Derivatives and the arithmetic of visa consumption

Each approved petition consumes visa numbers not only for the principal beneficiary but for every derivative who immigrates alongside. The derivative count varies by category and by the demography of the beneficiary pool, and it affects queue length as directly as the petition count does, because the allocation is measured in visa numbers, not in petitions.

The F3 category illustrates the effect most directly. A married son or daughter of a citizen typically immigrates with a spouse and one or more children, so a single F3 petition may consume three, four, or five visa numbers against the 23,400 base allocation. An F4 petition for a sibling often carries a similar unit. By contrast, an F1 petition for an unmarried adult son or daughter frequently carries no derivatives at all. The effective capacity of a category, measured in families served per year, is therefore lower than its visa-number allocation suggests wherever the average derivative count is high. Two categories with identical allocations can clear very different numbers of petitions per year if their derivative profiles differ.

The per-country ceiling counts derivatives against the country's share alongside principals. An oversubscribed country's 25,967 total preference numbers, to use the fiscal year 2012 figure, must cover principals and derivatives together across all family and employment categories. A country whose family petitioners have large derivative units exhausts its share on fewer petitions than a country with the same petition count and smaller units. The derivative effect thus compounds the demand concentration that the ceiling already rations, and it explains part of why queue lengths differ even among countries with similar petition volumes.

The Child Status Protection Act's derivative protections interact with this arithmetic in a way that can shrink a petition's visa consumption. A derivative who ages out during the queue is lost to the case, reducing the petition's visa consumption but at the cost of family separation. The CSPA subtraction of petition processing time rescues some derivatives, but the queue years remain unprotected, and in the longest lines the protection is mathematically insufficient. Families in F3 and F4, the categories with the longest waits and the largest derivative units, face the highest aging-out risk, and the statute offers them the least relief. The derivative structure is generous in design, extending the principal's queue position to the whole household, and unforgiving in its time limits, withdrawing the extension the moment a child crosses twenty-one under the adjusted age calculation.

## The Western Hemisphere before 1976

The 1976 amendment that extended the preference system to the Western Hemisphere deserves a closer look, because the pre-1976 arrangement explains features of the enacted text that otherwise seem arbitrary. From 1965 through 1976, the two hemispheres operated under different immigration regimes. The Eastern Hemisphere had the seven-category preference system, the 170,000 limit, and the 20,000 per-country cap. The Western Hemisphere had a 120,000 overall limit with no preference categories and no per-country cap, administered on a first-come, first-served basis within the hemisphere total. The change mattered most for Mexico, whose migrants had faced no per-country ceiling before 1976 and faced the same 20,000 ceiling as every other country afterward.

The first-come, first-served Western Hemisphere system produced its own pathologies. Without preferences, a skilled worker and a sibling of a citizen competed for the same undifferentiated numbers in order of application. Without a per-country cap, high-demand countries in the hemisphere could absorb large shares of the 120,000 total, leaving little for the rest. The system was simple to administer and difficult to defend as policy, and by the mid-1970s Congress concluded that the hemisphere distinction served no purpose. Public Law 94-571, enacted October 20, 1976, imposed the preference categories and the 20,000 per-country limit on the Western Hemisphere, unifying the substantive rules while retaining separate hemispheric totals for two more years.

The 1978 consolidation into a single 290,000 worldwide ceiling completed the unification, and the 1990 rewrite preserved it. The modern reader who wonders why the statute speaks of a single worldwide level rather than hemispheric quotas is looking at the settlement of the 1976 to 1978 reforms. The per-country ceiling's worldwide application, the feature that generates the queues this article analyzes, dates in the form analyzed here to those amendments: one percentage, measured against one worldwide total, applied to every country of birth without hemispheric distinction.

## Widows, widowers, and the edge of the immediate relative class

The immediate relative definition contains a humanitarian exception that tests the definition's boundaries. A widow or widower of a United States citizen may qualify as an immediate relative even though the citizen petitioner is deceased and cannot file or maintain a petition. The statute allows the surviving spouse to self-petition, provided the marriage was intact at the time of death, converting the spousal relationship into an immediate relative case without a living petitioner.

The provision's conditions are strict because its departure from the petitioner model is extraordinary. The marriage must have existed at death; a divorced surviving former spouse does not qualify. The self-petition must demonstrate the bona fides of the marriage, the same genuineness inquiry that governs living-petitioner cases, conducted without the petitioner's testimony. The provision reflects a legislative judgment that death should not punish the surviving spouse of a qualifying marriage by extinguishing the immigration benefit the marriage would otherwise have conferred.

Family petitions rest on living relationships, and the statute anticipates that relationships change during the long waits the system creates. When a petitioner dies, the petition does not automatically survive, but Congress has provided several forms of relief. The widow or widower of a citizen may self-petition as an immediate relative when the marriage existed at the citizen's death, a protection noted earlier. For preference petitions, the regulations and later statutory amendments allow reinstatement or humanitarian reinstatement in the agency's discretion when the beneficiary meets the statutory criteria, and a substitute sponsor may sign the affidavit of support when the original petitioner is gone. The relief is discretionary and fact-specific, not automatic, which means families in this position need individual legal analysis rather than general reassurance.

## Self-petition after death: the I-360 and the surviving spouse

The widow and widower provision works through a self-petition filed on Form I-360, the form the agency designates for widow and widower petitions among other special immigrant categories. The surviving spouse files as both petitioner and beneficiary, establishing that the marriage to the citizen existed at the time of death and that it was bona fide. The classification, once approved, is the immediate relative classification: no annual allocation, no per-country ceiling, and no priority date queue. The self-petition thus converts a case that would otherwise die with the petitioner into an uncapped case that proceeds on its merits, operating inside the immediate relative class that the 1965 amendments created.

The contrast with preference petitions is sharp. When the petitioner in an F1, F2A, F2B, F3, or F4 case dies before the beneficiary immigrates, the petition does not convert; the qualifying relationship ran between the petitioner and the beneficiary, and with the petitioner gone the statutory predicate fails. The regulations and later statutory amendments provide narrow discretionary paths, including humanitarian reinstatement and the surviving-relative provision that allows a substitute sponsor to sign the affidavit of support, but none of these operates automatically and each requires the beneficiary to meet the statutory criteria in the agency's discretion. The widow and widower self-petition is the exception that proves the rule: Congress preserved the spousal case past death through an affirmative statutory mechanism, and left preference cases to discretionary relief, a difference that follows from the immediate relative class's humanitarian logic rather than from any distinction in the genuineness of the underlying relationships.

## The affidavit of support as an enforceable obligation

The procedural path section introduced the distinction between the petitioner, who files Form I-130, and the sponsor, who signs Form I-864. The distinction deserves fuller treatment because the affidavit of support is the most underestimated legal instrument in family immigration, an enforceable contract whose obligations outlast the immigration case that required it.

The affidavit's function is to satisfy the public charge ground of inadmissibility. Immigration law excludes applicants likely to become primarily dependent on the government for subsistence, and the affidavit answers that concern with a private guarantee: the sponsor promises to maintain the sponsored immigrant at no less than 125 percent of the federal poverty guidelines and to reimburse any agency that provides the immigrant with means-tested public benefits. The promise is not aspirational. It is a contract between the sponsor and the United States, enforceable in federal or state court by the sponsored immigrant and by benefit-granting agencies. Sponsors have been ordered to pay support years after the immigration case closed, in amounts computed from the poverty guidelines rather than from any actual need the sponsor perceived.

The obligation's durability surprises even sophisticated petitioners. Divorce between the sponsor and the sponsored immigrant does not terminate it. The sponsor's bankruptcy does not discharge it. Only a narrow set of terminating events ends the obligation, and the passage of time alone is not among them. The practical consequence is that signing Form I-864 is the most financially consequential act in the family immigration process, more so than paying the filing fees or even than the years invested in the queue. Practitioners counsel prospective sponsors to evaluate the commitment with the seriousness of a long-term debt, because in legal effect that is what it is.

The joint sponsor mechanism exists for petitioners whose income falls short. A joint sponsor must be a United States citizen or permanent resident, at least eighteen years old, domiciled in the United States, and willing to accept the same enforceable obligation for the immigrant. The joint sponsor need not be related to the petitioner or the beneficiary; any qualifying person may serve. The petitioner's own obligation remains alongside the joint sponsor's, so the immigrant gains two obligors rather than trading one for another. Household members of the sponsor may also combine income with the sponsor's under specified conditions, a pooling arrangement that helps sponsors whose individual earnings fall below the threshold but whose households clear it.

## How the employment-based spillover moves family numbers

The interaction section described the spillover between the family and employment systems in outline. The mechanics repay a closer look, because the spillover is the largest source of year-to-year variation in the family preference allocation after the immediate relative subtraction.

The statute provides that immigrant visa numbers not used in the employment-based preferences in one fiscal year are added to the family-sponsored preference allocation in the next fiscal year, and symmetrically that unused family preference numbers augment the following year's employment-based allocation. The transfer operates on a one-year lag and in whole numbers: the State Department tallies each system's usage against its allocation at year end, computes the remainder, and adds the remainder to the sibling system's next-year allocation. The family preference level for a given year is therefore the computed figure, 480,000 minus prior-year immediate relatives plus prior-year unused employment numbers, floored at 226,000, and the employment-based level is 140,000 plus prior-year unused family numbers.

The spillover's direction and magnitude depend on demand patterns in both systems. In years when employment-based demand falls short of the 140,000 allocation, whether because of adjudication backlogs, per-country constraints within the employment categories, or economic conditions reducing employer sponsorship, the unused employment numbers flow into the family allocation and every family preference queue benefits. In years when employment-based demand absorbs the full allocation, nothing spills, and the family allocation rests on the 480,000-minus-immediate-relatives computation alone. The symmetric flow means family undersubscription would augment employment, but family undersubscription is rare: the family queues are deep enough in every category to absorb any plausible spillover, and the binding constraint on the family side is nearly always the allocation rather than the demand.

A labeled hypothetical shows the scale and the floor's interaction with the spillover. Suppose in fiscal year Alpha the employment-based system uses 120,000 of its 140,000 numbers, leaving 20,000 unused. Suppose prior-year immediate relative admissions were 300,000. The family preference computation for fiscal year Beta is 480,000 minus 300,000 plus 20,000, equaling 200,000, which the floor raises to 226,000. The spillover added nothing, because the floor already bound. Suppose instead that immediate relative admissions were 200,000. The computation is 480,000 minus 200,000 plus 20,000, equaling 300,000, and the preferences gain 20,000 numbers beyond what the uncapped flow alone would have allowed. The hypothetical demonstrates that the spillover matters only when the computed level sits above the floor; in high immediate relative years the floor absorbs the spillover's effect entirely.

## The quotas the 1965 Act replaced

The 1965 amendments are often described as creating family-based immigration, but the more precise description is that they replaced an ethnic ranking with a family ranking. The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952, the McCarran-Walter Act, distributed immigration through the national-origins quota system, which allocated visa numbers among countries according to the national origins of the American population as measured in the 1920 census. The formula favored northern and western Europe, restricted southern and eastern Europe, and all but excluded Asia and Africa. The 1952 Act did contain a family preference hierarchy within the quota structure, a fact worth preserving against the oversimplified narrative, but the hierarchy operated inside country quotas that were themselves the system's defining feature.

The national-origins system's defect, as its critics framed it across the 1950s and early 1960s, was that it judged immigrants by ancestry rather than by relationship or merit. The 1965 Act's answer was the preference system: within an Eastern Hemisphere limit of 170,000 and a 20,000 per-country cap, visas would go first to the close relatives of citizens and permanent residents and to workers with needed skills, without regard to national origin. The per-country cap survived the transition, transformed from an instrument of ethnic restriction into an instrument of formal equality: every country would receive the same maximum share, a principle the 1990 Act carried forward as the seven percent ceiling. The continuity of the country cap across the 1965 rupture is one of the statute's great ironies. The provision most responsible for the modern queues was inherited from the system the 1965 Act repudiated, repurposed from hierarchy to evenhandedness, and left to interact with demand patterns its drafters did not foresee.

The nuance worth preserving is that the 1952 Act's hierarchy gave family ties a real but subordinate role. Within each country's quota, relatives in the preferred classes moved ahead of non-preference applicants, so family sponsorship shortened the wait inside the quota without enlarging the quota itself. The national origins formula remained the system's defining feature: a country's total determined everything, and the family hierarchy only ordered the line within that total. The 1965 amendments inverted that relationship, making family reunification and occupational skill the organizing principles of admission and reducing the country cap to a uniform ceiling applied after the preferences had done their sorting. The family preference hierarchy therefore predates the modern system by thirteen years; what the 1965 Act created was not the idea of preferring relatives but a system in which preferring relatives, rather than ranking ancestries, decided who immigrated.

## What unmarried means across the categories

The word unmarried appears in the definitions of F1, F2B, and the child definition that underlies F2A and the immediate relative class, and it operates as a sorting mechanism with sharp edges. Its meaning is consistent: not married at the relevant time. Its consequences vary by category, and the variance is worth cataloging.

For the immediate relative child of a citizen, marriage ends the classification immediately, as the myths section noted. For F1, the unmarried son or daughter of a citizen, marriage reclassifies the petition to F3, with the priority date retained. For F2B, the unmarried son or daughter of a permanent resident, marriage revokes the petition outright. For F2A, the spouse beneficiary is married by definition, and divorce before immigration ends the petition; the child beneficiary must remain unmarried, and marriage reclassifies the child out of the child definition with no alternative category unless the petitioner has naturalized. The pattern is that marriage is neutral or fatal depending on the petitioner's status and the beneficiary's category, and the statute provides no grace period for marriages undertaken in ignorance of the consequences.

## Priority date retention across conversions

The priority date section stated the retention principle: the date survives reclassification between existing categories and perishes when the relationship ceases to be recognized. The conversions deserve an itemized walk, because each follows a different statutory path and practitioners plan around the differences.

Conversion by the petitioner's naturalization is the most consequential. An F2A petition for a spouse becomes an immediate relative petition, and the queue disappears. An F2A petition for a child becomes an immediate relative petition if the child qualifies under the age-freeze rules, or remains a preference petition reclassified to F1 if the child has aged into son-or-daughter status. An F2B petition becomes F1, retaining the priority date. In each case the beneficiary keeps the original filing date, carrying the accrued wait into the new category. The naturalization must occur before the beneficiary immigrates for the conversion to matter; a petitioner who naturalizes after the beneficiary's admission has converted nothing.

Conversion by the beneficiary's marriage runs the other direction. F1 to F3 retains the priority date but lengthens the queue. F2B upon marriage is revoked, with no retention, because the new relationship has no category under a permanent resident petitioner. The asymmetry between the F1 and F2B marriage consequences is the starkest illustration of the petitioner-class divide: the same life event, marriage, demotes one beneficiary and destroys another's case, depending entirely on whether the petitioner is a citizen or a permanent resident.

Following to join is the derivative's version of retention. A principal beneficiary who immigrates may have a spouse or child follow later in the same category on the same priority date, provided the derivative relationship existed at the time of the principal's admission and continues to exist. The following-to-join derivative does not need to have been listed on the original petition, but the relationship must be documented and the derivative must remain unmarried and, for children, within the CSPA-adjusted age limit. The provision allows families to stagger their migration, the principal establishing the household while derivatives complete schooling or other obligations, without losing the queue position the family earned together.

## Two readings of the same provisions

The family preference categories have been read in two opposed ways since their creation, and the opposition predates the text analyzed here. Both readings accept the statutory mechanics described in this article. They differ on what the mechanics are for. This section presents each reading with the care its proponents gave it, attributes every characterization, and draws no conclusion about what the mix of categories should be.

The family-unity reading holds that reunifying families is the primary purpose of immigration policy and the humanitarian core of the preference system. Its classic expression came at the signing of the 1965 amendments, when President Lyndon Johnson, at the foot of the Statue of Liberty on October 3, 1965, declared: "This bill says simply that from this day forth those wishing to immigrate to America shall be admitted on the basis of their skills and their close relationship with those already here." The sentence made family relationship a coequal basis of admission with occupational skill, and it has been quoted ever since as the 1965 Act's statement of intent. The reading received its most authoritative commission-level endorsement from the Select Commission on Immigration and Refugee Policy, chaired by Theodore Hesburgh, which reported in 1981 that family reunification should be the primary goal of immigration policy, citing its humanitarian character and the benefits to the United States through the stability, health, and productivity of individual family members reunited with their immediate family. The Congressional Research Service, surveying the commissions, reports that family reunification was cited by both the Hesburgh and the Jordan commissions as the primary goal of United States immigration policy, a convergence across bodies that otherwise disagreed. In this reading, the preference categories are the instruments of a humanitarian commitment, the queues are the regrettable cost of honoring that commitment within numerical limits, and the proper response to backlogs is to expand the numbers rather than narrow the relationships.

The numerical-limits reading holds that the family provisions extend well beyond any defensible conception of reunification and that the resulting volumes exceed what the country should accommodate. Its classic expression came from the United States Commission on Immigration Reform, chaired by Barbara Jordan, which recommended in 1995 that family-based preference immigration be limited to the nuclear family, spouses and minor unmarried children, and which recommended eliminating the fourth family preference, the sibling category, altogether. The Jordan Commission's rationale, as summarized by the Congressional Research Service, was that advocates of lower immigration levels take issue with broadening family reunification beyond immediate relatives, arguing that the United States has neither the responsibility nor the obligation to effectively reconstitute immigrants' families beyond the nuclear unit. The Hesburgh Commission, despite its endorsement of family reunification as the primary goal, likewise recommended eliminating the fourth preference and warned that the existing structure raised false hopes among millions with no prospect of immigration, hopes that would foster unauthorized immigration when the legal queues proved illusory. In this reading, the preference categories are an overextension of a sound principle, the sibling and married-children categories are the clearest cases of overextension, and the proper response to backlogs is to narrow the recognized relationships rather than expand the numbers.

## The category bases as shares of the floor

The four preference bases conceal an elegant property: added together, they equal exactly the statutory floor. Twenty-three thousand four hundred for the first preference, plus one hundred fourteen thousand two hundred for the second, plus twenty-three thousand four hundred for the third, plus sixty-five thousand for the fourth, sums to two hundred twenty-six thousand, the minimum family preference level guaranteed by Section 201(c). The correspondence is not a coincidence. Congress sized the categories so that their bases exactly fill the floor, which means the allocation clauses that add roll-down numbers, employment carryover, and the excess above 226,000 describe everything beyond a perfectly tiled foundation.

Expressed as shares of that foundation, the second preference dominates: its 114,200 base is just over half the floor, reflecting the judgment that the spouses and children of residents deserve the system's largest capped commitment. The fourth preference's 65,000 base is under three-tenths, the largest single-category commitment to any citizen-petitioned group. The first and third preferences, at 23,400 each, are about one-tenth apiece, the smallest bases in the system. These shares describe the statute's priorities in proportional terms: residents' closest relatives first by volume, siblings second, and the adult children of citizens in the smaller categories.

The tiling also explains why the second preference's growth clause is written the way it is. When the worldwide family level exceeds 226,000, the statute adds the excess to the second preference's base, so the F2 category absorbs one hundred percent of the above-floor growth while the other categories' bases stay fixed. In a year when the computed level reaches 280,000, the second preference base becomes 168,200 while the other three bases remain 23,400, 23,400, and 65,000, and the four still sum exactly to the computed level before the cascade. The floor tiles the foundation; the growth clause pours the surplus into a single category. A reader who checks any year's published per-category limits against this arithmetic will find the statute's fingerprints on every figure.

## Three hypothetical families, worked through the formulas

Abstract formulas become concrete fastest through worked examples. The three families below are hypothetical, with invented countries, invented dates, and rounded numbers chosen for clarity. They illustrate how the same statutory text produces different outcomes from different facts. No figure below represents any country's actual waiting time.

The Alvarez family: a citizen petitions for a spouse. Maria, a United States citizen, marries Jorge, who lives abroad, and files Form I-130 in March of Year 1. Jorge is classified as an immediate relative. No annual allotment applies, no per-country ceiling applies, and no priority date queue forms. The petition is adjudicated on its merits, the National Visa Center processes the case, the consulate interviews Jorge, and he immigrates in Year 2. The total elapsed time reflects processing and document-gathering, not visa availability. Maria's petition also has a systemic side effect she never sees: Jorge's admission counts in the prior-year immediate-relative subtraction that sets next year's preference level, pressing it slightly toward the 226,000 floor. The uncapped category's invisibility to its own beneficiaries is complete; the pressure it exerts on the capped categories is equally complete.

The Okafor family: a permanent resident petitions for an unmarried adult son. Chidi, a lawful permanent resident, files Form I-130 for his twenty-four-year-old unmarried son Emeka, born in Country Gamma, in June of Year 1. The petition establishes a priority date in June of Year 1 and classifies Emeka in F2B. Suppose the F2B base for the relevant years runs near its statutory figure and Country Gamma's demand exceeds its seven percent share, so the Visa Bulletin's F2B cutoff for Gamma advances roughly one year per calendar year. Emeka's June Year 1 priority date becomes reachable in roughly Year 8 or Year 9, depending on the cascade and the quarterly exemption mechanics. During the wait Emeka must remain unmarried; marriage would destroy the F2B classification, and no Child Status Protection Act formula would restore it, because the Act protects children, not adult sons. If Chidi naturalizes in Year 4, the petition converts to F1, keeping the June Year 1 priority date, and Emeka's line changes to the first preference's cutoff for Gamma, which may be longer or shorter than F2B's. The family's outcome thus depends on four variables the statute supplies: the category, the allotment, the ceiling, and the petitioner's citizenship status.

The Haddad family: a citizen petitions for a sibling. Layla, a thirty-year-old United States citizen, files Form I-130 for her brother Karim, born in Country Delta, in September of Year 1. The petition classifies Karim in F4 with a September Year 1 priority date. Suppose Country Delta's F4 demand vastly exceeds its seven percent share of the combined family-employment total. The F4 base is 65,000 plus roll-down, and Delta's annual F4 supply under the ceiling might be roughly 4,500 numbers. With tens of thousands of Delta-born F4 beneficiaries ahead of Karim, the bulletin cutoff for Delta F4 advances slowly, and Karim's wait stretches well beyond a decade in this hypothetical. Karim's wife and two minor children are derivatives on the same petition; when the number finally becomes available, all four immigrate together, consuming four F4 numbers against Delta's ceiling. The single petition thus illustrates three mechanics at once: the fourth category's large base, the country ceiling's throttling of high-demand countries, and the derivative multiplier that makes petition counts understate visa demand.

## Why reform arguments map onto the three sections

Public proposals to change family immigration, whatever their direction, always land on one of the three sections, and identifying the landing site clarifies what the proposal would actually do. A proposal to raise or lower total family immigration targets Section 201's worldwide level and its 226,000 floor. A proposal to exempt a category from the country ceiling, to raise the seven percent figure, or to compute the ceiling per category rather than across the combined total targets Section 202. A proposal to add a category, remove the sibling category, shift numbers among categories, or change the seventy-seven percent F2A rule targets Section 203. Proposals that do not specify a section are not yet proposals; they are sentiments awaiting a statutory address.

This mapping also explains why some reforms would disappoint their supporters. A proposal that raises the worldwide level without touching the country ceiling would admit more immigrants worldwide while leaving the longest country-specific lines nearly unchanged, because the seven percent throttle, not the worldwide total, binds those lines. A proposal that eliminates a category without reallocating its numbers would shrink total family immigration rather than shorten other lines, unless the numbers were explicitly redirected. A proposal that recaptures unused numbers from prior years would produce a one-time infusion followed by a return to the same arithmetic, because the annual formulas would continue generating the same supply afterward. Each of these outcomes follows from the interaction of the three sections, not from anyone's intentions, and the mapping lets a reader test any proposal against the machinery before forming a view of it.

The article takes no position on any of these proposals, in keeping with this article's evenhanded approach. The point of the mapping is analytical, not persuasive: whatever change a reader favors or opposes, the change must be written into one of the three sections, and its effects will flow through all three. The statute is the terrain on which every argument about family immigration is fought, and this article has tried to draw the map accurately enough that readers can locate any argument on it.

## A note on numbers, dates, and verification

This article states the statute's formulas rather than the figures those formulas produce in any particular year, and the distinction is deliberate. The allotments, the country ceiling's visa equivalent, and the Visa Bulletin cutoffs all change annually or monthly, while the text that generates them changes only when Congress acts. A reader who memorizes this year's per-country visa figure will hold stale information within twelve months; a reader who memorizes the seven percent rule and the Section 201(c) formula will hold working knowledge for as long as the statute stands. The article's dated figures, the fiscal year 2012 limit of 226,000, the 25,967 country figure for that year, the 3.95 million waiting petitioners as of November 1, 2017, and the fiscal year 2016 admission totals, are labeled with their dates and should be treated as illustrations of the structure, not as descriptions of any other year.

Verification follows the same principle. The State Department's annual numerical limits announcement gives the coming year's family preference level, per-category allotments, and country ceiling. The monthly Visa Bulletin gives the cutoff dates that ration those numbers. The Congressional Research Service's reports on family-based immigration policy compile the admission totals and the queue figures with source citations. The statute itself, at 8 U.S.C. 1151 through 1154, is the final authority when any summary and the text appear to disagree, and the text wins every such contest. A reader who checks a claim against those four sources, in that order, will rarely be misled.

## Putting the pieces together: a reader's checklist

A reader facing a real family case can reduce this article's machinery to a sequence of questions. First, who is the petitioner, a citizen or a resident, and how old is the petitioner if a parent or sibling is the beneficiary. Second, what is the exact relationship, tested against the statutory definitions rather than everyday language, including the marital status and age of the beneficiary on the legally significant dates. Third, does the relationship fall in the uncapped category or in one of the five preference categories, because that answer decides whether a queue exists at all. Fourth, for a preference case, what are the category's allotment, the country's demand relative to the seven percent ceiling, and the priority date's position against the Visa Bulletin's two charts. Fifth, what events during the wait could convert, preserve, or destroy the petition: naturalization, marriage, divorce, death, or a birthday. Sixth, when the number arrives, does the beneficiary clear the admissibility screens and does the affidavit of support hold. Six questions, each answered by a different section of the statute, and the green card follows only when all six answers align.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What are the family preference categories in the Immigration and Nationality Act?

The Act recognizes five family preference categories under INA 203(a), 8 U.S.C. 1153(a). F1 covers unmarried sons and daughters of United States citizens. F2A covers spouses and minor children of lawful permanent residents. F2B covers unmarried adult sons and daughters of permanent residents. F3 covers married sons and daughters of citizens. F4 covers siblings of citizens who are at least twenty-one. Each category has a defined petitioner, a defined beneficiary, and a base allocation, with unused numbers cascading between categories under statutory roll-down rules. Immediate relatives of citizens sit outside the preference system entirely and are admitted without numerical limitation.

### Q: Who counts as an immediate relative under the Immigration and Nationality Act?

An immediate relative is the spouse of a United States citizen, the unmarried child under twenty-one of a citizen, or the parent of a citizen who is at least twenty-one years old, as defined in 8 U.S.C. 1151(b)(2)(A)(i). The petitioner must be a citizen; a permanent resident cannot confer immediate relative status. A widow or widower of a citizen may also qualify where the marriage existed at the time of death. The word child carries its technical meaning from INA 101(b)(1): unmarried and under twenty-one, both conditions required. Immediate relatives are exempt from the worldwide levels and numerical limitations, so they hold no Visa Bulletin queue position and their cases move at adjudication speed.

### Q: What is the per country limit in the Immigration and Nationality Act?

INA 202(a)(2), 8 U.S.C. 1152(a)(2), provides that natives of any single foreign state may receive no more than seven percent of the immigrant visas made available under the family and employment preference subsections of INA 203 in a fiscal year, with a two percent ceiling for natives of a dependent area. The percentage is measured against the combined family-plus-employment preference total, not against any single category. Seventy-five percent of the F2A allocation is exempt from the ceiling, and the ceiling lifts for the remainder of a calendar quarter when visa supply exceeds qualified demand. Chargeability follows country of birth, with limited cross-chargeability exceptions for spouses and children.

### Q: Why are Immigration and Nationality Act green card backlogs decades long?

Backlogs arise from the layered arithmetic of fixed supply against uncapped demand. Each preference category has a base annual allocation, petitions are approved faster than visa numbers become available, and each approved case holds a place ordered by priority date. INA 202(a)(2) then caps each country's share of the combined family and employment preference pool, so the State Department sets separate, slower-moving cut-off dates for oversubscribed countries of birth in the monthly Visa Bulletin. The accumulated result was visible in the dated count of approximately 3.95 million approved family preference petitioners waiting as of November 1, 2017. Categories with the smallest allocations relative to demand clear slowest.

### Q: Can a green card holder sponsor a sibling under the Immigration and Nationality Act?

No. A lawful permanent resident may petition only for a spouse, a child who is unmarried and under twenty-one, or an unmarried son or daughter who is twenty-one or older. Siblings and parents of permanent residents have no family preference category, and the Act provides no alternative family path for those relationships. Only a United States citizen who is at least twenty-one may petition for a sibling, under the F4 fourth preference, or for a parent, as an immediate relative. A permanent resident who wishes to petition for a parent or sibling must first naturalize; upon taking the oath, the petitioner becomes a citizen and the previously unavailable categories open.

### Q: What is chain migration under the Immigration and Nationality Act?

The statute never uses the term. The Congressional Research Service describes chain migration as a term some have used for the process by which family-based immigration creates self-perpetuating flows, as foreign nationals who obtain permanent residence and citizenship then sponsor other relatives under the family provisions. The underlying mechanism is the petition process of INA 204: a citizen or permanent resident files Form I-130, approval places the relative in line by priority date, and after immigrating, and for some categories after naturalizing, the new immigrant may petition for qualifying relatives of their own. Supporters of family immigration reject the term as pejorative and say family reunification instead. Both labels describe the same statutory provisions.

### Q: What is a priority date under the Immigration and Nationality Act?

The priority date is the date the immigrant petition, Form I-130, was properly filed, and it fixes the beneficiary's place in line within the applicable preference category. The State Department publishes monthly cut-off dates in the Visa Bulletin for each oversubscribed category and country of birth; a beneficiary may proceed to a visa number only when the priority date falls earlier than the cut-off. Filing date controls, not approval date, so adjudication delay costs no queue position while filing delay permanently loses it. Priority dates are retained across reclassifications between existing categories but perish when the underlying relationship ceases to be recognized, as when an F2B beneficiary marries.

### Q: How many family visas does the Immigration and Nationality Act allow each year?

The family-sponsored preference level starts at 480,000, minus prior-year immediate relative admissions, plus prior-year unused employment-based numbers, and may not fall below 226,000 under 8 U.S.C. 1151(c). Because immediate relatives are admitted outside every numerical limit, total family immigration routinely exceeds both figures; in fiscal year 2016, for example, 566,706 immediate relatives plus 238,087 preference immigrants totaled 804,793. Within the preference level, base allocations run 23,400 for F1, 114,200 for the second preference with at least seventy-seven percent to F2A, 23,400 for F3, and 65,000 for F4, with unused numbers cascading between categories.

### Q: What is the difference between F2A and F2B?

Both are filed by permanent resident petitioners, but they serve different beneficiaries under different rules. F2A covers spouses and minor children, meaning unmarried and under twenty-one, while F2B covers unmarried sons and daughters who are twenty-one or older. F2A receives at least seventy-seven percent of the 114,200 second preference base, and seventy-five percent of the F2A allocation is exempt from the per-country ceiling, advantages F2B does not share. If an F2B beneficiary marries before immigrating, the petition is revoked because no category exists for a married child of a permanent resident. Naturalization of the petitioner reclassifies both: F2A spouses become immediate relatives and F2B beneficiaries become F1.

### Q: How does the Child Status Protection Act protect a child who turns twenty-one while waiting?

The 2002 Act, Public Law 107-208, created two rules. For immediate relatives of citizens, the child's age freezes on the date the I-130 is filed, so processing delay cannot cause aging out. For preference beneficiaries, the protected age equals the beneficiary's age when a visa number becomes available minus the days the petition was pending; only agency processing time is subtracted, never the years spent waiting in the queue. A separate provision judges the child's age as of a permanent resident parent's naturalization date, often rescuing F2A beneficiaries. The beneficiary must remain unmarried and must seek permanent residence within one year of visa availability absent extraordinary circumstances.

### Q: What happens to unused family preference visa numbers at the end of a fiscal year?

They do not go to waste. Within the family system, unused numbers cascade between preferences under the roll-down rules of INA 203(a): unused F4 numbers augment F1, unused F1 numbers augment F2, unused F1 and F2 numbers augment F3, and unused F1 through F3 numbers augment F4. Across systems, unused family preference numbers spill over to the employment-based allocation for the following fiscal year, and unused employment-based numbers spill symmetrically into the family allocation. The per-country ceiling also lifts for the remainder of a calendar quarter when supply exceeds qualified demand, letting numbers flow to oversubscribed countries. These mechanisms keep the system close to full utilization of the authorized totals.

### Q: Can a United States citizen sponsor a married son or daughter, and what category applies?

Yes. The married sons and daughters of citizens fall into the F3 third preference, with a base allocation of 23,400 plus unused numbers from the first and second preferences. The beneficiary's spouse and minor children accompany as derivatives in the same category on the same priority date. An unmarried adult son or daughter of a citizen starts in F1 and is reclassified to F3 upon marrying, retaining the original priority date but joining a longer queue. By contrast, a permanent resident has no category for a married son or daughter at all; that petition becomes possible only after the petitioner naturalizes, at which point it is filed or converted as F3.

### Q: What is a derivative beneficiary in family immigration?

A derivative beneficiary is the spouse or unmarried minor child of a preference principal beneficiary, immigrating in the principal's category on the principal's priority date without a separate petition. Derivatives count against the category's annual allocation and the per-country ceiling alongside principals, so petitions with large derivative units consume more visa numbers than single-beneficiary petitions. A derivative's eligibility depends on the principal's: if the underlying relationship between petitioner and principal ends, the derivatives fall with it. The Child Status Protection Act extends limited age protection to derivatives by subtracting petition processing time from the derivative's age when a visa number becomes available. Immediate relatives have no derivatives because each relationship requires its own citizen petitioner.

### Q: How does the per-country ceiling treat F2A differently from other family categories?

F2A is the only family preference with a country-cap exemption: under INA 202(a)(4), seventy-five percent of the F2A allocation is not subject to the per-country ceiling. The exempt numbers form a worldwide pool distributed in priority date order regardless of country of birth, while the remaining twenty-five percent is subject to the seven percent ceiling like every other preference allocation. Combined with F2A's large base, at least seventy-seven percent of the 114,200 second preference allocation, the exemption has historically made F2A the shortest family queue and at times eliminated its backlog entirely. No other family category enjoys any exemption; F1, F2B, F3, and F4 are fully subject to the ceiling on every visa number.

### Q: What is the difference between a petitioner and a sponsor?

The petitioner is the United States citizen or permanent resident who files Form I-130 asking the government to recognize the qualifying family relationship. The sponsor is the person who signs Form I-864, the affidavit of support, promising to maintain the immigrant at no less than 125 percent of the federal poverty guidelines. Usually the petitioner serves as sponsor, but the roles can diverge when a joint sponsor with sufficient income signs a second affidavit. The distinction carries legal weight because the affidavit is an enforceable contract: the immigrant and agencies providing means-tested benefits can sue to enforce it, and the obligation survives well beyond admission. Proving the relationship and underwriting the support are independent requirements, and both must be satisfied.

### Q: Can an immediate relative lose that status by marrying before immigrating?

Marriage affects the child subcategory but not the others. A citizen's unmarried child under twenty-one who marries before immigrating is no longer a child under INA 101(b)(1), which requires both unmarried status and age under twenty-one, and therefore loses immediate relative classification. The petition would need reclassification into a preference category if one fits. A spouse beneficiary is already married by definition, so marriage changes nothing there. A parent beneficiary's status does not depend on the parent's marital state. The practical lesson is that marital status is a live condition of the child definition throughout the case, and families should confirm the immigration consequences before a beneficiary marries.

### Q: If a permanent resident petitioner naturalizes, does the petition category change?

Yes, and the change is usually the most favorable event in a family case. A pending F2A petition for a spouse converts to an immediate relative petition, eliminating the queue entirely because the uncapped class has no queue. A pending F2B petition for an unmarried adult son or daughter converts to F1, with the original priority date retained. Under the Child Status Protection Act, a child's age is judged as of the naturalization date for petitions covered by that provision, which can rescue beneficiaries approaching twenty-one. Petitions for beneficiaries with no citizen-petitioner category do not benefit, but for spouses, children, and unmarried sons and daughters, the petitioner's naturalization accelerates or uncaps the case by operation of law.

### Q: Is there a limit on how many relatives one person may file for?

The statute sets no numerical cap on petitions: a qualifying petitioner may file for every relative who fits a listed category, and each petition is adjudicated on its own merits. The practical constraint is financial rather than numerical. The affidavit of support obligates the sponsor to maintain each sponsored immigrant at 125 percent of the poverty guideline, and the household size used in that calculation grows with every immigrant sponsored, including those from earlier petitions whose obligations remain in force. A sponsor with modest income may qualify to support one immigrant but not three, and the National Visa Center will hold the later cases until adequate sponsorship is shown. The petitioning power is unlimited; the sponsoring power is bounded by income and assets.

### Q: Can a lawful permanent resident petition for a parent?

No. A permanent resident's petitioning power covers a spouse, an unmarried child under twenty-one, and an unmarried son or daughter aged twenty-one or older, and nothing else. Parents appear nowhere in the resident's closed list. The path runs through citizenship: after naturalizing, the former resident may petition for a parent, provided the petitioner is at least twenty-one years old, and the parent is then classified as an immediate relative with no numerical limit, no country ceiling, and no queue. The years spent as a resident do not create a shortcut; only the change of status to citizen opens the parent category. Families planning around an aging parent should factor the naturalization timeline into their expectations from the start.

### Q: What did the Immigration Act of 1990 change for family categories?

The 1990 Act repealed the old Section 201 and wrote the numerical architecture in force since: a 480,000 family-sponsored level with a 226,000 floor, computed by subtracting prior-year immediate-relative admissions. It kept the five family preference categories, their relative order, and the seven percent per-country ceiling, while separating employment immigration into its own subsection with 140,000 visas and adding the 55,000-visa diversity program. Within the family categories it added the two F2A protections: at least seventy-seven percent of the second preference for spouses and minor children, and the seventy-five percent exemption of F2A numbers from the country ceiling. The Act thus preserved the 1965 category structure while resetting every number around it and building the ceiling, floor, and cascade mechanics this article describes.
