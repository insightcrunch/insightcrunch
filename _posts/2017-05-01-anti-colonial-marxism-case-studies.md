Marxism was adopted, adapted, and argued over by many of the movements that dismantled the European empires in the twentieth century, and it was nowhere adopted unchanged. The doctrine had been built for industrial societies, with their large factory workforces, their organised labour movements, and their long experience of parliamentary politics. The societies in which anti-colonial movements operated were mostly agrarian, their industrial working classes small or embryonic, their populations bound together less by shared employment than by shared subjection to a foreign power. Taking Marxism into such societies therefore meant rebuilding it: deciding who the revolutionary agent could be where the industrial proletariat barely existed, deciding how class demands related to the prior demand for national independence, and deciding what a party should look like when it had to be constructed in villages, jungles, and exile rather than in union halls.

![Anti-colonial Marxism in practice, the case comparison and the analysis and mobilisation division - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-15.webp)

This article compares those rebuildings rather than narrating them one country at a time, because the comparative question is the one the existing coverage rarely asks. The country-by-country literature is rich; a reader can find detailed histories of Vietnamese communism, of the Algerian war, of the Indian left, of the liberation movements in the Portuguese colonies. What the reader will struggle to find is a disciplined answer to a simpler and more useful question. When Marxism and nationalism entered the same movement, as they did almost everywhere, which of them did what? The finding this article defends, and which it names the analysis and mobilisation division, is that Marxism characteristically supplied the analysis of imperialism and the organisational form of the disciplined party, while nationalism supplied the mass force that filled the party's ranks and marched behind its banners. The post-independence trajectory of each state then tracked which of the two had prevailed inside the movement that inherited power. That finding is developed through five principal cases, a comparison table that states it in findable form, and a record section that refuses to soften what happened after the flags changed.

Three cautions belong at the front. First, anti-colonial Marxism is not a synonym for the anti-colonial movement as a whole. Some of the century's largest independence struggles were led by movements that were not Marxist, and their communist parties were marginal or worse. Second, the adoption was an act of intellectual agency by colonised thinkers, not an export mechanically received: the interesting history lies precisely in the modifications. Third, the record after independence includes one-party states, severe economic difficulty, and in several instances mass violence, and this article reports that record in the same register as it reports the movements' achievements, because a comparison that flinches at its outcomes is worth nothing. The framework against which the cases are read, the Marxist theory of empire in its classical and later forms, is set out in the companion [Marxism and imperialism guide](/2017/02/01/marxism-and-imperialism-guide/), and is linked rather than rehearsed here.

## Anti-colonial Marxism and the comparative question

Anti-colonial Marxism denotes the adoption and adaptation of Marxist analysis and organisational forms by twentieth-century movements against colonial rule, characteristically fusing class analysis with national liberation and modifying Marxist categories for predominantly agrarian societies. Each element of that definition does work. Adoption marks the fact that the doctrine was chosen, translated, and fought for by colonised intellectuals and organisers, often against the indifference or hostility of metropolitan communist parties. Adaptation marks the modifications that followed, above all the substitution of the peasantry for the industrial working class as the principal revolutionary mass. Fusion marks the distinctive political product: movements that pursued national independence and social transformation as a single struggle, and that therefore argued, in a way European social democracy rarely had to, about the order in which the two could be won and the price of postponing either.

The doctrine these movements adopted had itself been assembled for a different theatre. Its account of why empires exist, of how value moves between centre and periphery, and of why colonial rule must generate resistance, travelled extremely well, because it explained the colonised reader's own experience with a precision no rival framework matched. Its sociology travelled badly. A theory whose central actor is an industrial working class confronts obvious embarrassment in a territory where wage labourers form a small minority, where the typical producer is a peasant working family land under customary tenure, and where the sharpest line of domination runs not between employer and employee but between the colonial state and everyone it governs. The movements handled that embarrassment in different ways, and the differences are the substance of the cases below. Some stretched the category of the proletariat until it covered the rural poor in general. Some demoted class to a secondary contradiction, to be addressed after independence. One, the most theoretically ambitious, rebuilt the theory of historical agency itself.

### Were anti-colonial movements actually Marxist?

Some were led by communist parties, notably in Vietnam and China. Others, including the movement that led the Algerian war, were not, and their countries' communist parties were marginal. Marxism more often supplied analysis and organisational form than leadership, with nationalism supplying the mass force.

The honest answer to the question therefore has three parts, and collapsing them is the commonest error in popular coverage. At one pole stand movements in which a communist party, self-consciously Marxist in doctrine and organisation, led the independence struggle and took power at its end: Vietnam is the cleanest case in this article, and China, whose revolution is treated in its own companion piece and deliberately left aside here, is the largest. At the opposite pole stand movements in which the leading organisation was nationalist in self-definition, drew selectively on Marxist texts, and kept its country's communists at the margin or suppressed them: Algeria is the case examined below. Between the poles lies the crowded middle ground of movements that borrowed the analysis, the vocabulary, and the party form while reserving their ultimate loyalty to the nation, a category that covers much of Africa's liberation politics and the various African socialisms. Any sentence that begins by treating anti-colonialism and communism as interchangeable has already failed to describe this landscape.

### What did a colonised reader find in the Marxist account of empire?

A colonised reader found an explanation of foreign rule as a system rather than an insult: empire appeared in the Marxist account as the outward expression of an economic order, maintained for identifiable interests, and therefore vulnerable to organised resistance rather than fated to last forever.

The appeal deserves to be specified rather than gestured at, because it explains the pattern of adoption better than any story about Moscow's gold or Comintern instruction, both of which existed and neither of which accounts for the doctrine's hold on people who quarrelled with Moscow freely. The Marxist account of empire did at least four things for a colonised intellectual that rival accounts did not. It explained why the railway ran from the mine to the port and not between the country's own towns, why the colony exported what it did not consume and imported what it did not make, why the administration that preached free trade at home practised monopoly abroad. It converted humiliation into structure: the official who sneered was no longer the point, since the system that posted him would post another. It predicted that the order generating colonialism was itself historical and perishable, which turned endurance into a strategy rather than a sentence. And it offered the colonised a universal history in which they were not latecomers begging admission but participants in the central conflict of the age, allied in principle with workers and movements on every continent. A doctrine that explains your poverty, absolves your grandfather, promises your victory, and introduces you to allies is a formidable instrument, and it was taken up accordingly.

### How were Marxist categories rebuilt for agrarian societies?

The rebuilding concentrated on agency: where the industrial working class was small, movements elevated the peasantry into the principal revolutionary force, treated the national bourgeoisie as an unreliable ally rather than the automatic leader, and reassigned the party the task of supplying politically what the economy had not yet produced socially.

Each of those moves carried a theoretical price, and the movements' internal debates were largely arguments about whether the price had to be paid. Elevating the peasantry sat awkwardly with a tradition that had treated peasants as a class dissolved by history's progress and politically inert between revolutions; the movements answered from experience, pointing to villages that sustained guerrilla armies for decades. Treating the national bourgeoisie as unreliable reversed the expectation that a colony's own capitalists would lead the fight against foreign capital, an expectation the record of collaboration kept refuting. The theoretical question underneath all of it, whether revolution in an agrarian society must wait for capitalism to mature the classes that would make it, is the question of [permanent revolution explained](/2014/09/15/permanent-revolution-explained/), and the anti-colonial movements mostly settled it in practice before they settled it on paper: they made their revolutions where they stood, and adjusted the theory to fit the fact. The Chinese case, reserved for separate treatment, and the Cuban case, treated in [the Cuban revolution's Marxist analysis](/2018/11/01/cuban-revolution-marxist-analysis/), belong to the same family of practical settlements.

A last framing distinction will be needed throughout, and it is worth engraving before the cases begin. A movement's doctrine and its organisational form are separate variables, and a movement can score high on one and low on the other. The vanguard party, centralised, disciplined, organised in cells, led by a committee claiming scientific knowledge of history's direction, was Marxism's most successful export of all, adopted by movements whose official doctrine was nationalist, by movements whose doctrine was a local socialism, and by at least one movement whose doctrine was primarily religious in idiom. Conversely, parties with impeccably Marxist programmes sometimes remained debating societies of students and exiles, incapable of moving a crowd. When the cases below are graded, they are graded on both variables separately, because the analysis and mobilisation division is precisely a claim about how the two variables combined: doctrine travelling as analysis, organisation travelling as form, and mobilisation arriving from a different source altogether. With that distinction in hand, the cases can be read in sequence, from the fullest fusion of the Marxist and the national to the cases in which the fusion failed or was never attempted.

The selection of cases follows the comparison's logic rather than the map's. Vietnam, Algeria, India, Guinea-Bissau, and Ghana with its Tanzanian annex were chosen because between them they exhaust the combinations the finding distinguishes: the party-led fusion, the front-led borrowing, the party that subordinated itself out of the succession, the movement whose analysis was made at home by its own leader-theorist, and the family of governments that borrowed concepts under doctrines of their own. Cases excluded by the brief's disambiguation, China and Cuba above all, are excluded because the series treats them at length elsewhere and because their scale would swamp a comparison built to make mechanisms visible; the control cases admitted later, Malaya and the Philippines, Kenya and Indonesia and the Congo, enter as tests rather than as rows, chosen because each isolates a variable the principal cases entangle. A reader suspicious of the selection is invited to perform the exercise the article's method makes possible: take any anti-colonial movement the article omits, grade it on the three functions, and observe whether its successor state's character surprises the finding. The article's confidence in its pattern rests less on the six rows it prints than on the pattern's behaviour when readers run it, unassisted, over the cases it left out.

## How Marxism travelled: the circuits of transmission

Before the cases, the mechanics of adoption deserve a section of their own, because the modernising objection examined later draws much of its plausibility from a lazy picture of transmission, doctrine radiating outward from a metropolitan centre to passive recipients. The actual circuits were stranger, more personal, and more reciprocal than that picture allows. Marxism reached the colonised world through seamen and students, through exile presses and prison libraries, through the reading circles of port cities where a single translated pamphlet might be worn to fragments by a hundred hands. Ho Chi Minh encountered Lenin's theses on the national and colonial questions as a kitchen worker and photo retoucher moving between Paris, London, and New York, and carried them to Canton and Siam in the pockets of a professional revolutionary. Cabral absorbed his Marxism in Lisbon lecture halls and in the company of Portuguese and African students who would staff half the liberation movements of the Portuguese world. Indian communism was nursed in London and Tashkent and Bombay simultaneously, its founders arguing across three continents by censored post. The doctrine arrived, in other words, already creolised by the journeys it had taken, and the movements that received it were staffed by men and women who had themselves been formed by the empire's own circuits of labour migration, military service, and education.

Institutional channels existed and mattered: the Communist International and its successor networks trained cadres, funded presses, and attempted, with diminishing success over the decades, to dictate lines, as the Indian party's wartime reversal painfully demonstrates. But the Comintern's role is routinely overstated in both directions, by celebrants who credit it with creating movements it merely advised, and by critics who treat every colonial communist as a ventriloquist's dummy. The archival record shows something more interesting: colonial parties bargaining with the centre, concealing local initiatives from it, translating its instructions into local idioms the centre would not have recognised, and, after the International's dissolution in 1943, improvising freely. The Vietnamese party's strategy of protracted rural war, the PAIGC's reconstruction of historical materialism, the Indian party's eventual parliamentary turn were none of them dictated from abroad, whatever foreign encouragement or discouragement attended them. Transmission was a negotiation conducted across inequality, and the inequality diminished as the century proceeded and the students became the teachers: by the nineteen sixties it was the Cuban, Vietnamese, and Guinean experiences that European and American radicals studied, in a reversal of the original flow that the phrase third world Marxism was coined to describe.

Print did much of the work that institutions could not. The colonial world was governed through censorship regimes that banned Marxist literature outright in territory after territory, which conferred on the banned texts the prestige of contraband and on their possessors the solidarity of conspiracy. Study circles formed around single copies; translations were made by prisoners and exiles, often from secondhand versions, a French rendering of a Russian original read aloud in a Canton lodging house or a Dakar courtyard, accumulating local glosses at every remove. The consequences for the doctrine were not corrupting but creative. Concepts arrived detached from the scholastic apparatus that policed their meaning in Europe and were tested immediately against experience their authors had never contemplated: what a Vietnamese reader made of the Asiatic mode of production, or an African organiser of the peasantry's revolutionary capacity, was determined by the rice field and the groundnut basin rather than by the exegetical tradition. Anti-colonial Marxism's characteristic intellectual virtues, its concreteness, its impatience with scholasticism, its readiness to discard categories that failed, were forged in those transmission conditions, as were its characteristic vices: a certain contempt for theoretical caution, and a habit of treating each season's strategic improvisation as a discovery of universal law.

The circuits had a geography, and mapping it explains the doctrine's regional accents. Paris was the first great node, because the French empire's own machinery concentrated its colonial subjects there: students at the Sorbonne and the law faculties, workers in the war industries, soldiers demobilised into a metropole that denied them the equality their service had been promised. In Paris the Vietnamese, Algerian, Senegalese, and Malagasy organisers of the interwar generation met one another, and met the French left, in the reading rooms and police-watched cafes where the intercolonial union and its successors taught a generation that their separate colonies were instances of one system; Ho Chi Minh was a founding figure of that milieu before he was anything else, and Messali Hadj's movement was born among Algerian workers in France rather than in Algeria. London played the corresponding role for the anglophone world, with the difference that its colonial students encountered there a labour movement and a publishing industry as well as a communist party; the West African Students' Union and, after the war, Padmore's circle made the city a clearing house where the future leaders of Ghana, Kenya, and Nigeria argued out the doctrines they would carry home. Lisbon, improbably, became the third node, because Portugal's African students, Cabral and Mondlane and Neto among them, were gathered by the regime's own scholarships into a handful of faculties and student houses where they discovered one another and the literature the censor had banned at home. Moscow and Canton and, after 1949, Beijing supplied the training schools; Accra, after 1957, became the first node on African soil with a state behind it, its conferences and its bureau of African affairs functioning as the movements' diplomatic university. The doctrine a movement received depended on which nodes its founders had passed through, which is why French-speaking Africa's Marxism carries a Parisian accent of Jacobin centralism, why the anglophone movements argue in a more parliamentary cadence, and why the Portuguese territories produced, in Cabral's generation, the most insistently empirical variant of all: their node had been a faculty of agronomy as much as a party school.

## The raw materials: the societies that received the doctrine

What the doctrine landed on mattered as much as what it said, and a brief survey of the receiving societies explains why the same texts produced such different movements. Colonial rule had, by the middle of the twentieth century, restructured every society in this article along lines the Marxist analysis could describe with uncomfortable precision. Land had been concentrated, by concession, by settler expropriation, by the conversion of customary tenure into state property leased to companies, until in territory after territory a peasantry that remembered communal access confronted landlords, plantations, and tax collectors as a single apparatus. Labour had been set in motion on an imperial scale: Vietnamese coolies on Michelin rubber plantations dying in numbers their employers recorded and historians still contest, Algerian workers commuting between Kabyle villages and French factories, Guinean peasants walking to the groundnut basins of Senegal, Indian weavers ruined by Lancashire's competition and Indian soldiers fighting Britain's wars on every continent. Taxation payable only in cash forced subsistence producers into the money economy on terms set by the buyers of their crops and labour. Education, provided grudgingly and in the coloniser's language, manufactured a stratum literate enough to administer the system and excluded enough to hate it, the classic recruitment pool of every movement in this article.

These were the raw materials, and they explain both halves of the analysis and mobilisation division. They explain the analysis's appeal, because the Marxist account had been built by generalising exactly such processes, primitive accumulation, the creation of labour markets by coercion, the state's role in securing them, from European and American history; a reader in Hanoi or Bissau did not need to be persuaded that the description fitted, only to be shown that it was a description rather than a fate. They explain nationalism's monopoly of mobilisation, because the injuries colonialism inflicted were experienced nationally before they were experienced as class injuries: the tax, the pass law, the colour bar, the governor's contempt fell on the colonised as colonised, on the merchant and the professor as on the coolie, and a movement that proposed to answer those injuries in class vocabulary alone asked its audience to subdivide a wound they felt as one. The movements that prospered were those that grasped both facts simultaneously, organising the class grievance inside the national one and letting each lend the other its force, and the movements that failed at the fusion, the Indian party in 1942 subordinating the nation to a foreign war, the later party-states subordinating every grievance to the nation's supposed unity, failed by violating one of the two facts. The cases that follow should be read with the raw materials in view: they are not interchangeable backdrops but distinct configurations of land, labour, and literacy, and the comparison's discipline consists in holding the doctrine constant, as nearly as it can be held, while the configurations vary.

One historical accelerant acted on all the configurations at once, and its omission would leave the timing of everything that follows unexplained. The Second World War broke the imperial order's material and moral defences simultaneously. The European empires emerged victorious and exhausted, their treasuries indebted, their conscript armies demobilising into electorates unwilling to fund colonial reconquest indefinitely; in Asia the Japanese occupation had already destroyed, in a matter of months in 1941 and 1942, the prestige of European invincibility on which a century of rule by small garrisons had rested, and the nationalist forces that had fought the Japanese, in Vietnam, in Malaya, in the Philippines, in Burma, possessed at the surrender the arms, the organisation, and the legitimacy of resistance that the returning empires confronted as accomplished facts. The Atlantic Charter's language of self-determination, however cynically its authors had intended its scope, furnished the movements with the empires' own promissory note, and the founding of the United Nations gave the note a forum in which it could be presented annually. Every case in this article sits downstream of that conjuncture. The movements of the nineteen twenties and thirties, the Indian communists among them, had possessed the analysis without the opportunity; the war supplied the opportunity to a generation that had the analysis ready, and the speed of the subsequent collapses, empires that had taken a century to assemble dissolving within twenty years, is unintelligible unless the preparation and the conjuncture are held together, which is to say, unless the comparison's division of intellectual labour is extended to history itself: the movements supplied the organisation and the force, and the world war, no one's instrument and everyone's catastrophe, supplied the moment.

## Vietnam: the fusion of national and social revolution

Vietnam is the case in which the fusion the definition describes came closest to completion, and it is therefore the case against which the others in this comparison are measured. A communist party, founded in exile in 1930 and led by Ho Chi Minh, placed itself at the head of the independence struggle, built the broad national front through which that struggle was fought, survived two long wars against Western powers, and took power over a unified country in 1975. At every stage the party presented the national and social revolutions as a single process: the landlord and the colonial official were analysed as parts of one structure of domination, and the peasant who fought the second was promised liberation from the first. No other movement in this article sustained that fusion so completely for so long, and none paid for it in quite the same coin.

The fusion was constructed, not given, and its construction is the theoretically instructive part. Ho Chi Minh came to Marxism through nationalism and never pretended otherwise: his own account of his conversion records that what first drew him to Lenin's writings was their treatment of colonial peoples, and that his allegiance began as a patriot's before it became a theorist's. The party he built institutionalised that ordering. The Viet Minh front, formed in 1941 to unite all Vietnamese willing to fight the French and the Japanese occupation alike, subordinated the social programme to the national one without abandoning it: rents were pressed down and communal lands redistributed in the zones the front controlled, but expropriation waited, and landlords willing to support the resistance were recruited rather than expropriated. The August Revolution of 1945, in which the front seized power in the vacuum left by Japan's collapse, and Ho's declaration of independence that September, quoted the American and French declarations against their own empires, a drafting choice that announced the movement's double character to the world: national in its claim, universalist in its authorities.

The party's prehistory contains the comparison's neatest illustration of fusion learned by failure. Founded in 1930, the Indochinese Communist Party threw its young strength into the uprisings of that year and the next, the Nghe-Tinh soviets, in which peasants of two central provinces seized local power, abolished taxes and rents, and governed themselves for months before French aircraft, legionaries, and famine destroyed them. The soviets were heroic and premature, a social revolution attempted in a country whose national movement had not yet been built, and the party, hunted nearly to extinction in the repression that followed, drew the conclusion its subsequent strategy embodied: social demands raised before the national front existed would isolate the party and deliver its cadres to the executioner. The famine of 1945, in which a million or more northern Vietnamese are generally believed to have starved under the joint exactions of French administration and Japanese occupation, though every figure in that catastrophe remains contested, completed the education. It was against the famine's background that the Viet Minh's seizure of Japanese grain stocks in 1945 converted a clandestine front into a national government in waiting: the movement that fed the people in the spring was proclaimed by them in the autumn. Analysis, organisation, and mass force were all present in that sequence, supplied respectively by the party's doctrine, its cells, and a starving nation's gratitude, and the order in which they combined, national deliverance carrying social promise inside it, became the template the party never abandoned.

### What held the Vietnamese fusion together across two wars?

The fusion held because the party kept the two revolutions visibly connected in the lives of its base: the peasant who received land, rent relief, and literacy through the movement experienced the national cause and the social cause as one deliverance, and the party's organisation reached into villages no rival could penetrate.

That village presence was the decisive organisational fact of the Vietnamese case, and it illustrates the comparative argument of this article in miniature. The party form, cells, committees, mass associations for peasants, women, and youth, was the Marxist contribution, refined in exile and in the maquis. The mass force that made the form formidable was national: the determination, widespread far beyond any doctrine, that foreign rule should end. The party's achievement was to weld the two so thoroughly that the distinction became academic in the liberated zones. Land was the weld's material. In a country where tenancy and usury structured the peasant's experience of oppression as directly as the colonial tax, a movement that attacked the landlord's rent while fighting the landlord's foreign protector did not need to persuade its followers that two struggles were one. The followers could see it at the threshing floor.

The wars tested the fusion in opposite directions. The war against France, ended at Dien Bien Phu in 1954, rewarded the strategy: a peasant army, supplied by porters through jungle the French staff had dismissed as impassable, destroyed the colonial expeditionary corps' strongest position, and the Geneva settlement that followed gave the party the northern half of the country. The war against the United States and the Saigon government then demanded the national front be rebuilt in the south under conditions of far greater violence, and the party rebuilt it, in the National Liberation Front, on the same template of subordinated but genuine social programme. When Saigon fell in 1975, the fusion had outlasted two empires. It is easy, reading backward, to make that outcome look inevitable. It was not: it rested on an organisation's forty-five years of continuous adaptation, on catastrophic errors by its opponents, and on a peasantry's willingness to absorb suffering on a scale that no article should pass over without marking, though the contested state of every numerical account of that suffering obliges this one to mark it without figures.

### Why did land reform in Vietnam both build and damage the revolution?

Land reform built the revolution by giving its peasant base a material stake in victory, and damaged it because the campaign of the mid nineteen fifties was conducted through mass accusation and quotas of classified enemies, producing wrongful persecution on a scale the party itself was forced publicly to acknowledge and correct.

The episode belongs in this article not as an embarrassment to be footnoted but as evidence about the fusion's mechanics, because it shows the social revolution operating with its own momentum inside the national one. After Geneva, the northern government moved from rent reduction to thoroughgoing land reform, classifying rural households by wealth and redistributing holdings. The campaign's methods, struggle meetings, denunciations, classifications fixed by quota, escaped the control of the cadres sent to conduct it, and people were persecuted, imprisoned, and in a number that remains contested executed for membership of a landlord class they did not belong to. The party's response was unusual in the annals of ruling communist parties: an official rectification of errors, public apology by senior figures, release and rehabilitation of the wrongly classified, and the demotion of the campaign's overseers. None of that undid the harm, and the episode entered the country's memory as a wound. For the comparison, the instructive point is the direction of the pressure. The excess arose not from nationalism overruling social analysis but from the social programme pursued with a ferocity the leadership had licensed; the fusion's dangers, Vietnam shows, could come from either of its elements.

The period after unification displays the same doubleness in economic form. The unified state attempted to extend the northern model southward at speed: collectivisation of southern agriculture, nationalisation of southern commerce, a planned economy imposed on a region that had lived by the market and by American subsidy. The result, compounded by war with Cambodia's Khmer Rouge regime, by a border war with China in 1979, and by embargo, was severe economic difficulty, shortages, inflation, and the departure by sea of hundreds of thousands of people, many of them ethnic Chinese Vietnamese driven out in the crisis with Beijing. The state that had won two wars against foreign armies came close to losing the peace to its own economic model, and said so: the renovation adopted from 1986, market mechanisms under continued one-party rule, was an admission drafted by the party itself. Vietnam therefore supplies the comparison with its most complete instance of a movement in which Marxism supplied both analysis and organisation, nationalism supplied the force, and the post-independence difficulties arose less from one element defeating the other than from the victorious doctrine being applied beyond the conditions it understood. The one-party state endured through all of it, which is also part of the record: no competitive election has ever tested the party's mandate, and the fusion's heirs governed, as they had fought, alone.

Two further features of the Vietnamese case distinguish it within the comparison and will recur as criteria when the finding is graded. The first is the party's treatment of time. Where the Indian party allowed an external event to reverse its strategy in a season, the Vietnamese leadership thought in decades and subordinated every campaign to a sequence it had mapped in advance: build the front, accumulate forces in the countryside, contest the towns politically, and fight the decisive battle only when the correlation of forces had matured. That patience, which owed as much to the Chinese Communists' hard schooling as to any text, was itself a theoretical position, a refusal of the insurrectionary shortcut that European communism had romanticised and that kept destroying smaller parties elsewhere. The second feature is the party's handling of its own errors, of which the land reform rectification is the type specimen. A movement whose doctrine claims scientific status acquires, if it takes the claim seriously, an obligation to correct its record in public; the Vietnamese party discharged that obligation more often than most ruling parties of its family, apologising for the land reform, reversing the collectivisation of the south, and eventually renaming its economic model rather than defend the indefensible. Self-correction does not cancel the errors, and this article's record section does not ask the reader to forget them. It does mark the institutional difference between a doctrine functioning as a discipline, capable of indicting its own application, and a doctrine functioning as a flag.

The southern struggle adds a final precision. The National Liberation Front of South Vietnam, founded in 1960, was formally an alliance in which non-communist nationalists held visible office, and historians continue to argue over how completely the party directed it. For the comparison, the argument matters less than the structure both sides of it concede: the front's programme was national and democratic rather than socialist, its appeal was to all southerners who rejected the Saigon government and its American patron, and the party's cadres supplied its organisation at every level. Here, inside the comparison's most Marxist case, the analysis and mobilisation division is visible in cross-section. Even where the party led, it led a national movement, on a national programme, and postponed the social transformation until the national question was settled by arms. The Vietnamese fusion was real, but it was a fusion of sequence as much as of substance: first the nation, the party promised, and then, on the nation's foundations, the social order the doctrine envisioned. That the second stage proved the harder, and that its difficulties filled the decades after victory, is the burden of the record this article has already entered against the case.

Vietnamese diplomacy deserves separate notice as the case's fourth instrument, because the party treated negotiation as a theatre of the struggle equal to the battlefield, and the skill compounds the comparison's interest. At Geneva in 1954 the delegation arrived victorious from Dien Bien Phu and left with half a country, pressed by its own allies, Moscow and Beijing, to accept partition as the price of a wider settlement; the party swallowed the instruction, filed the grievance, and spent twenty years reversing the outcome by arms, a sequence that taught it the limits of fraternal gratitude more durably than any polemic. At Paris two decades later its negotiators sustained a public diplomacy, through a chief delegate whose performance in the world's press became a legend of the period, while the real bargaining proceeded in secret channels, and the settlement of 1973 extracted the American withdrawal the battlefield had been arranged to compel. The pattern is consistent with the fusion this section has described: diplomacy was never an alternative to the fusion's other elements but their continuation, the analysis identifying which contradictions in the enemy's camp, Washington's domestic exhaustion, Paris's colonial politics, the allies' diverging interests, could be widened by negotiation as by ambush. A movement that could fight, organise, analyse, and negotiate at the same pitch for forty years had integrated its instruments as no other case in this article integrated them, and the comparison's grading of Vietnam at the head of its order rests on the integration as much as on the victory.

Two episodes from the northern state's first decade belong in any honest grading of the fusion, because they mark the points at which the party's discipline turned on the society that had carried it. The regroupment of 1954 and 1955 moved the party's southern military and political cadres north under the Geneva settlement, leaving behind stay-behind networks instructed to prepare, within a settlement that promised reunification elections, for a resumption that the party's leadership already suspected would be necessary; the suspicion was vindicated, the elections were never held, and the stay-behind cadres became the skeleton of the southern insurgency, a continuity that explains the second war's speed of organisation and that the southern population paid for in the repression visited on anyone the skeleton touched. The second episode cuts the other way. In 1956, in the brief thaw that followed the land reform's rectification, northern intellectuals and writers, many of them veterans of the resistance, used the journals Nhan Van and Giai Pham to demand the freedoms the revolution had promised its artists, and the party, after months of toleration, suppressed the journals, subjected their contributors to public criticism and worse, and reasserted a cultural discipline from which northern letters did not recover for a generation. The episode is small beside the wars and it is diagnostic beyond its size: the movement that had built its strength by listening to peasants could not extend the same discipline of listening to its own writers, and the analysis that indicted landlords and colonels stopped, as it stopped in so many of the comparison's states, at the door of the party's own house. Vietnam's exceptionalism, which this article has credited in the matter of self-correction, has this boundary, and the boundary is entered here so that the record of the fusion's best case contains its shadow in the right proportion.

The southern theatre of the second war adds a complication the comparison should not suppress, because it shows the fusion competing in a crowded market of mobilisations. The south's countryside in the nineteen fifties contained, alongside the party's surviving networks, the armed religious polities of the Cao Dai and Hoa Hao, mass movements with their own heavens, hierarchies, and private armies, and a Binh Xuyen syndicate that held Saigon's vice trades and its police. Ngo Dinh Diem's government broke the sects' armies in 1955 with American backing, buying some commanders and crushing others, and the party, watching a rival nationalism dismantle the sects it had itself courted, drew the lesson its southern strategy embodied: the countryside's allegiances were plural, armed, and available, and the front it built in 1960 was designed as an umbrella under which sect remnants, disaffected landlords' sons, Buddhist activists, and its own cadres could all stand without signing its doctrine. The umbrella is the analysis and mobilisation division in its most deliberate form, a structure planned by men who knew exactly which element they were supplying and which they were borrowing. That the umbrella's non-communist members discovered, after 1975, how completely the borrowed force had been spent and how completely the supplying party intended to rule is part of the record of the unification already entered above, and it closes the Vietnamese case with the comparison's characteristic doubleness: the fusion's architects were sincere in their nationalism and systematic in their party's supremacy, and the two qualities were not in tension for them, which is precisely what their partners, in the end, could not forgive.

## Algeria: the war that Marxism informed but did not lead

Algeria is the comparison's necessary counterweight, the case that prevents the article's finding from hardening into a slogan. The war that ended French rule between 1954 and 1962 was one of the century's defining anti-colonial struggles, saturated with Marxist language, advised and celebrated by Marxist intellectuals across three continents, and it was not led by a Marxist party. The movement that launched the insurrection, the National Liberation Front, was a nationalist coalition formed by activists who had concluded that every existing organisation, the communist party emphatically included, was too slow, too legalistic, or too compromised to begin the fight. The Algerian Communist Party, which had a genuine history in the colony's labour movement, was marginal to the war the front waged, was banned by the French in 1955, and saw its members join the struggle largely as individuals under front discipline after an agreement that dissolved their independent role. Marxism's presence in the Algerian revolution was real but oblique: it informed the revolution's analysis of its enemy, staffed parts of its diplomacy and its press, and supplied its most famous diagnostician, while the command of the struggle remained nationalist from first to last.

The communists' marginality had structural causes that repay attention, because they recur across the comparison. The Algerian party had been formed within a colonial labour movement whose settler working class occupied a privileged position in the colony's hierarchy, and the party's record on the national question carried that inheritance: for decades its line had privileged the anti-fascist and class struggles of the metropole, had treated Algerian independence as a premature demand, and had looked at the settler population as a constituency rather than an occupying class. A nationalist movement whose founding act was armed insurrection could not be led by an organisation with that biography. The earlier nationalist current associated with Messali Hadj, organised among Algerian workers in France, had built the mass base and the independence demand over decades, and the front's founders came out of its radical wing. When the insurrection came, the constituency existed, the programme was national, and the communists were offered a place inside the tent on terms that ended their party as an independent actor. They took it, many of them honourably and at great personal risk, but the terms themselves are the datum.

### Why was the Algerian communist party marginal to its own country's war?

The party was marginal because its history made it unusable as the war's leader: it had subordinated Algerian independence to metropolitan priorities for decades, carried the inheritance of a settler labour movement, and was overtaken in 1954 by nationalist militants who founded the front precisely to bypass organisations like it.

The war the front then fought was waged with an organisational discipline that owed much to the Marxist repertoire while owing nothing to Marxist doctrine: cells, political commissars, a provisional government conducting diplomacy as a weapon, an army structured in wilayas with political officers alongside commanders. The front's own theorisation of its struggle remained nationalist in grammar, independence, dignity, the recovery of a stolen sovereignty, even where its economic programme used socialist vocabulary. Frantz Fanon, the movement's most penetrating analyst and the subject of a companion article to which this one defers for his doctrine, served the front as writer and diplomat while his masterpiece diagnosed the very class that would inherit the new state: the national bourgeoisie of the colonised world, which he foresaw stepping into the coloniser's shoes as intermediary rather than transformer. Fanon's diagnosis matters here as evidence of the division this article is tracking. The sharpest Marxist analysis available to the Algerian revolution was produced inside a movement that Marxism did not lead, by a thinker the movement honoured and did not follow.

The Soummam platform of 1956, the front's nearest approach to a social programme, repays attention precisely because it shows the borrowing's limits from inside. Drafted at a congress held in the bled at the war's height, the platform subordinated the military to the political, the exterior delegation to the interior leadership, and sketched an independent Algeria of land reform, nationalisation of commanding resources, and secular citizenship open to the European minority on equal terms. It was a serious document, more systematic than anything the party of Algerian communism had produced for the country, and its social clauses were real. Yet its premises were national throughout: classes appear in it as constituencies of the nation, never as antagonists whose conflict the revolution existed to settle, and the land reform it promised was a reform of ownership between Algerians and departing settlers rather than a transformation of relations among Algerians themselves. When independence came, the platform's social clauses were implemented exactly to the extent that they did not disturb the coalition the front had become, and abandoned where they did, in the taming of the workers' self-management of abandoned estates into a state sector administered by appointees. The sequence, platform's promise, coalition's veto, army's inheritance, is the Algerian case in miniature, and it generalises: in movements where doctrine was borrowed rather than embodied in a party of its own, social programmes were hostages to the coalition's least radical indispensable member.

### What did the Algerian front take from Marxism without becoming Marxist?

The front took the organisational repertoire, cell discipline, the political officer, the provisional government as diplomatic instrument, and a vocabulary of imperialism for naming its enemy; it declined the class analysis of Algerian society, the atheism, and any subordination of the national goal to a social programme.

Independence, when it arrived through the Evian Accords in 1962, therefore installed a state whose founding legitimacy was national and military, and whose socialism, proclaimed under Ben Bella and systematised under Boumediene after the 1965 coup, was a state doctrine administered from above rather than the programme of a ruling Marxist party answerable to a doctrine it had carried through the struggle. Land was redistributed, first through the self-management of estates abandoned by departing settlers, then through an agrarian revolution decreed by the state; hydrocarbons were nationalised; industry was built by decree on the rents of oil and gas. The results were substantial in infrastructure, education, and health, and the political form was constant: a one-party state, front and army fused, in which elections ratified and never decided. When the system's oil income collapsed in the nineteen eighties and riots shook the cities in 1988, the regime conceded a pluralism it had never intended, elections were held, an Islamist movement won them, the army annulled them, and the country descended into a decade of civil conflict whose violence, massacres of villagers, assassinations, a state apparatus fighting an insurgency that also massacred, stands among the worst internal catastrophes of the post-independence world. Every estimate of its dead is contested, and this article states the catastrophe qualitatively and plainly: a state born of a liberation war consumed a generation in a war against its own people. Algeria's lesson for the comparison is exact. Where nationalism supplied both the analysis and the force, and Marxism attended as a guest, the post-independence state reproduced the pattern Fanon had predicted from within the movement's own ranks: an intermediary stratum governing through a party converted into an instrument of rule, its legitimacy drawn from a liberation it monopolised and could not renew.

The Algerian war's internal history sharpens the case's value to the comparison, because the front's nationalism was never the untroubled unity its communiques proclaimed. Authority inside the revolution was contested throughout between the provisional government abroad, the wilaya commanders in the interior, and, increasingly, the regular army massed on the Tunisian and Moroccan frontiers under Houari Boumediene. The army of the frontiers, better fed, better armed, and least damaged by the fighting, was the institution that inherited the state in 1962, marching its preferred leader into power over the provisional government's objections and, three years later, dispensing with him too. A Marxist reading of that outcome writes itself, and several of the front's own veterans wrote it in exile: the revolution's social content had been left unexamined for so long, in the name of national unity, that the question of who would rule the new Algeria was settled by the only organised force the unity had produced, an army. Nationalism had supplied the movement's force and its legitimacy; organisation, in the absence of a party with a doctrine and a social base of its own, defaulted to the officer corps. The comparison's other cases distribute those functions differently, and the differences are precisely what the table exists to display.

Settler colonialism gave the Algerian case its ferocity and distinguishes it from the Indian and Vietnamese theatres. More than a million Europeans lived in Algeria, farmed its best land, staffed its administration, and regarded the territory not as a colony but as France itself, a conviction the French state ratified by administering the north as departments. A national movement confronting that structure faced not a governor who could be withdrawn but a population that would fight to stay, backed by an army that mutinied against its own government when withdrawal loomed. The war's conduct, torture institutionalised by the French command, the herding of rural Algerians into regroupment camps, the front's own massacres of rivals and of villagers judged collaborationist, belongs to the record this article keeps, and it is entered here in general terms because the contested-figures protocol governs the detail: the violence was systematic on the French side and episodic but real on the front's, and the independence settlement triggered an exodus of the settler population within months, together with reprisals against Algerians who had served the French, whose abandonment and killing stand among the settlement's permanent stains. A movement formed in such a war, against such an enemy, will privilege unity over doctrine with something approaching necessity; the comparison's point is not that the Algerians chose wrongly in 1954, but that the choice's consequences were still governing the country when the army annulled the elections of the nineteen nineties.

The front's French federation, sometimes called its eighth wilaya, adds the case's most paradoxical chapter, since it shows a national movement mobilising a diaspora inside the enemy's capital with an organisational sophistication its doctrine never theorised. Several hundred thousand Algerians worked in France, in the factories of Paris and Lyon and the mines of the north, and the front taxed them, organised them, policed them, and fought for their allegiance against the rival movement of Messali Hadj in a war within the war conducted in cafes and lodging houses, whose killings, Algerian against Algerian on French streets, the French press recorded with horror and the French state with comparative indifference. The federation's levies financed a large share of the struggle; its networks sheltered the front's diplomats and moved its documents; its demonstrations, above all the great peaceful march of October 1961 that the Paris police answered with massacre, carried the war's meaning into French politics as no communique could. For the comparison, the federation is evidence that mobilisation, the function this article assigns to nationalism, was itself an organisational achievement of the first order, planned and administered by cadres whose skills the party-form analysis would predict and whom the front, a nationalist coalition, had trained in its own schools. The boundary between the comparison's variables leaks here in the most instructive direction: the front's nationalism, at its most effective, behaved exactly like the disciplined party it had declined to become, and the lesson generalises uncomfortably for tidy typologies. Movements converge on the forms their conditions demand, whatever their banners say, and the division between analysis, organisation, and mobilisation describes a distribution of emphases inside real movements, not a set of boxes into which movements can be sorted without remainder.

The French Communist Party's conduct during the war supplies the metropolitan counterpart to the Algerian party's marginality, and the comparison enters it because it completes the pattern of subordination first met in India. The French party, the largest in the country, possessed the analysis, the organisation, and a membership that included the conscripts shipped to Algeria; it used them, for most of the war, to call for peace in Algeria rather than for Algerian independence, and in 1956 its deputies voted the special powers with which the Mollet government intensified the war it claimed to be ending. The party's reasoning reproduced Moscow's and the Indian comrades' in a metropolitan key: the French working class was not ready, the alliance against the right came first, the colonial demand must wait upon the metropolitan sequence. Algerian militants in France, organised in the front's French federation, drew the conclusion the pattern teaches and built their struggle with the help of individual French supporters, the teachers, priests, students, and party dissidents of the Jeanson network who carried the front's funds and its fugitives, rather than with the blessing of the great party of the French left. When independence came, it came over the French party's caution as over the settlers' resistance, and the Algerian state's subsequent refusal to treat metropolitan communism as its tutor dates from those years. The comparison files the episode beside 1942: two theatres, one structure, in which the analysis arrived with instructions attached and the instructions were written elsewhere.

The Algerian war's boomerang struck the metropole almost as hard as Portugal's wars struck Lisbon, and the comparison should register it before leaving the case. The Fourth Republic died of Algeria in 1958, brought down by an army and settler revolt that installed de Gaulle rather than surrender the territory; the Fifth Republic he founded then fought the war for four more years before conceding at Evian what the revolt had been staged to prevent. Within France the war produced a crisis of the republic's own values conducted in the vocabulary this article has been tracking: the systematic use of torture by French forces, documented by the French press and by the victims' French advocates while the government censored the documentation, forced a generation of French intellectuals, many of them Marxists, to decide what their doctrines meant when their own state held the electrodes. The police massacre of Algerian demonstrators in Paris in October 1961, in which peaceful marchers were beaten and drowned in the Seine by the forces of the republic in its capital, was for decades officially minimised, and stands in this article's record without figures, its scale contested and its occurrence established, as the metropole's share of the war's violence. The front understood the boomerang as strategy: its diplomacy aimed from the first at making the war ungovernable in France as well as unwinnable in Algeria, and the strategy worked, which is a final Algerian lesson about the analysis and mobilisation division. The front's analysis of its enemy included the enemy's domestic contradictions, parliament against army, republic against settlers, and its mobilisation extended, by design, into the enemy's own capital; a nationalist movement with a borrowed analysis deployed both with a sophistication the comparison has found nowhere else outside Vietnam.

## India: the wartime line and the price of loyalty

India supplies the comparison with its most uncomfortable episode, and the brief for this article is right to insist that it be stated rather than summarised away. The Communist Party of India did not lead the independence struggle; that struggle's mass leadership belonged to the Indian National Congress and to Gandhi. The communist party, founded in the nineteen twenties, built a real presence in the trade unions, among students, and in the peasant movements of several regions, and it carried the Marxist analysis of British rule into Indian politics with considerable intellectual distinction. Its historical reputation, however, turns on a decision taken in 1941 and 1942, when the party aligned itself with the British war effort at the very moment the national movement launched its greatest campaign of resistance. The decision was not corrupt and it was not cowardly; it followed from an analysis, sincerely held and internationally derived, of what the Second World War was. Its costs were also real, they were paid in the currency the party could least afford, nationalist credibility, and the tradition has been arguing about the bill ever since. The full history of Indian communism is treated in the companion [Marxism in India complete guide](/2017/12/01/marxism-in-india-complete-guide/), and its organisational history in [the communist movement in India's history](/2017/12/15/communist-movement-india-history/); what follows is the episode and its consequences for the comparison.

The sequence matters, because the party's position reversed itself in step with events in Europe rather than in India. When the war began in 1939, the international communist line, and the Indian party's with it, characterised the conflict as an imperialist war between rival blocs, in which Indian support for Britain was support for India's own oppressor; the party opposed the war effort and its activists were imprisoned for it. Germany's invasion of the Soviet Union in June 1941 transformed the war's character overnight, in Moscow's analysis and therefore in the Indian party's: the conflict was now a people's war against fascism, the defence of the Soviet Union was the overriding duty of communists everywhere, and in India that duty required supporting, or at least not obstructing, the Allied effort conducted from Indian soil. The party adopted the people's war line in the winter of 1941 and 1942, was legalised by a grateful Raj in 1942, and found itself, by August of that year, in opposition to the Quit India movement, the Congress campaign demanding immediate British withdrawal, whose leaders were imprisoned and whose underground activists the legalised communists were denounced for betraying, in some accounts to the police.

### Why did the Indian party choose the war effort over the national movement?

The party chose as it did because its analysis subordinated the Indian struggle to the war's global character: once the Soviet Union was invaded, defeating fascism became the precondition of every other liberation, and a mass uprising against Britain was judged, on that premise, an obstacle to the precondition.

The premise was arguable; the party's leading theorists argued it with learning, pointing to the difference between a war for colonial spoils and a war whose outcome would decide whether fascism or its enemies inherited Europe. What could not be argued away was the optics and the experience: at the moment of the Raj's greatest illegitimacy, when repression of the Quit India campaign killed demonstrators in numbers that remain contested and filled the prisons with the national movement's cadres, the communist party was legal, publishing, organising, and explaining why the campaign had to fail. The party used its legality constructively by its own lights, building unions, peasant organisations, cultural fronts, and relief work during the Bengal famine, and its membership grew severalfold during the war years. Growth and disgrace proceeded together. To the generation that filled the prisons in 1942, the party had stood with the jailer at the decisive hour, and that memory was available to every rival for the rest of the century.

### How long did the wartime decision shadow the Indian party afterwards?

The shadow lasted for decades: the wartime line became the standing charge in every subsequent contest for the nationalist inheritance, available to Congress governments prosecuting communists, to rivals on the left, and to historians, and the party's own later self-criticisms never fully discharged it.

Independence in 1947, arriving with partition and its mass violence, found the party poorly placed to claim the nationalist succession, and its subsequent history reads as a series of attempts to recover a standing its wartime choice had spent. An insurrectionary turn at the decade's end, peasant armed struggle in Telangana, adventurist general strikes, was abandoned for a parliamentary path; the party contested elections, and in 1957 won power in the state of Kerala, forming one of the first democratically elected communist governments anywhere, an achievement that announced the Indian party's distinctive adaptation: Marxism pursued through the ballot box in a poor and largely agrarian country, within a constitutional order the party had not made. The Kerala government was dismissed by the centre within two years, the party split in 1964 over doctrine and the border war with China, and its successor parties settled into their lasting pattern, formidable in particular states, marginal in the nation, their mass force regional and linguistic rather than all-Indian. For the comparison, India's datum is double. Marxism supplied India with a distinguished analysis of colonialism and a durable party form, as the finding predicts. But the movement that supplied the mass force of independence was Congress nationalism, and the communist party's one great chance to share that force was declined, on instructions derived from an analysis made in another continent. The analysis and mobilisation division appears here in its negative form: where the analysis was imported rather than made, it could command the party to act against the mobilisation, and the party obeyed.

The Indian case repays one more excavation, because the party's wartime decision is sometimes excused as an aberration forced on an otherwise national movement, and the record supports a colder and more instructive reading. The party's analysis of India was, by the standards of the colonial world, exceptionally sophisticated. Its historians and economists had absorbed the drain-of-wealth critique pioneered by Indian liberals and rebuilt it on Marxist foundations, tracing the mechanisms by which the Raj transferred the country's surplus abroad and arrested its development; its organisers had built the subcontinent's strongest trade union centres and its most disciplined peasant movements; its cultural fronts set the terms of progressive literature in several languages. None of that sophistication protected the party at the decisive moment, because the party's strategy was not, in the last instance, made in India. The two reversals of 1939 and 1941 were executed in obedience to an analysis of the war formulated in Moscow, by a leadership that had internalised the discipline of a world movement as a virtue higher than local judgement. Indian communism's tragedy, for this comparison, is that its analysis of its own country was indigenous and excellent while its strategy was imported and, at the critical juncture, ruinous: the analysis and mobilisation division appears within a single party, its components at war.

The postwar years displayed both the party's resilience and the ceiling its reputation imposed. In Telangana, its peasant guerrillas held liberated villages against the Nizam's forces and then against the Indian army, in one of the largest armed agrarian movements of the century; in the naval mutiny of 1946 and the strike waves that accompanied the transfer of power, its unions were at the centre of the action. Yet when the Constituent Assembly wrote the republic's founding document and Congress distributed the nationalist inheritance, the communists were creditors no one intended to pay. The party's own retrospective literature, unusually self-critical by the standards of its family, eventually described the wartime years as a period in which it had grown in organisation while losing the nation's ear, a formulation this article adopts because it concedes exactly what the comparison needs: growth and disgrace together, and the disgrace outlasting the growth. Later generations of the Indian left, including the Maoist currents that broke from the parliamentary parties denouncing their caution, defined themselves against 1942 as much as against the Raj, which is a measure of how long a single decision, taken on imported premises, can govern a tradition's imagination.

The party's compensating achievement, and the reason its history cannot be written off as the tale of an error, was cultural and intellectual penetration out of all proportion to its political ceiling. Through its writers' associations, its theatre movement, its publishing houses, and its historians, Indian communism set a large part of the agenda of the country's intellectual life for two generations: the progressive writers redefined the literatures of Urdu, Hindi, Bengali, and Malayalam; the people's theatre carried political modernism into towns no party organiser reached; the party's scholars produced the economic histories of the Raj against which all later scholarship argues. The comparison recognises the pattern: it is the analysis functioning at maximum strength in the one arena where the mobilisation deficit did not matter, the republic of letters, where a doctrine's quality is tested by argument rather than by crowds. India's communists lost the succession to their country and won a large share of its mind, and the division between those outcomes, the same division this article has tracked through war and statecraft, is visible nowhere more cleanly than in the contrast between the party's thin parliamentary benches and its thick presence in the footnotes of everyone who has written about India since.

## Cabral and Guinea-Bissau: the theory rebuilt from the liberated zones

The theoretically most sophisticated contribution to anti-colonial Marxism came neither from the largest movement nor from the oldest party, but from a small liberation struggle in Portuguese West Africa and from the agronomist who led it. Amilcar Cabral founded the African Party for the Independence of Guinea and Cape Verde in 1956, built an armed struggle from 1963 that liberated much of Guinea-Bissau's countryside while Portugal still held the towns, and was assassinated in January 1973, months before his movement declared the independence he did not live to see. His writings, speeches to his own cadres, an address delivered in Havana in 1966, analyses of the social structure of the colony he was fighting, are read wherever the theory of national liberation is studied, and they earn that attention because Cabral did what the comparison needs most: he treated Marxism as an instrument to be recalibrated against a society it had not been designed for, and he wrote down the recalibration. The movement he led also matters to the comparison for a second reason. In Guinea-Bissau the analysis and the mobilisation were joined with unusual honesty about their difference, and the post-independence record, which includes a coup against his own brother, a split between the two halves of his movement, and a civil war at the century's end, tests his prediction about his own class with a cruelty he foresaw in the abstract.

Cabral's starting point was empirical in a way few revolutionary texts are. Trained as an agronomist in Lisbon, employed by the colonial agricultural service, he had conducted the colony's agricultural census and knew its peoples, crops, and social forms village by village. The party he built in the liberated zones was designed from that knowledge: people's assemblies, village committees, schools, clinics, and people's stores that broke the Portuguese trading monopoly's grip on peasant produce. His instruction to his cadres, to tell no lies and claim no easy victories, is sometimes quoted as a moral ornament; in context it was an organisational doctrine, a directive that the movement's intelligence about itself must be accurate because a peasantry asked to sustain a guerrilla war would detect falsehood faster than any censor. Analysis, in the PAIGC, was not an import consulted on feast days. It was the movement's operating system, revised continuously against reports from the zones.

The movement's road to arms illustrates the same empirical temper. The PAIGC began in legality, organising dockers and urban workers in Bissau, and turned to armed struggle only after Portuguese troops massacred striking dockworkers at the Pidjiguiti quay in 1959, an event Cabral treated in the movement's memory as the demonstration that the colonial state would answer organisation with killing. The years that followed were spent not in immediate insurrection but in political preparation of the countryside, cadre by cadre and village by village, a slowness for which impatient allies criticised him and which he defended as the difference between a rebellion and a revolution: a rebellion could be lit anywhere, but a revolution had to be organised among people who would have to sustain it for a decade. When the armed struggle opened in 1963 it therefore opened inside a political architecture already built, and the liberated zones that architecture governed, with their elected village committees, their schools teaching in a country the Portuguese had left almost wholly illiterate, their courts and clinics and people's stores, were governed as a rehearsal for the state the movement intended to found. Visitors who travelled to the zones in the war's last years, journalists, solidarity workers, a United Nations mission that recognised the PAIGC as the territory's effective authority over much of its claimed area, returned with accounts that embarrassed Lisbon's claim to be fighting barbarism. The zones are the case's strongest evidence that analysis, in Cabral's movement, was not a commentary on practice but its design discipline.

### What did Cabral change in the theory of history?

Cabral argued, in the Havana address of 1966, that the motive force of history is the development of the productive forces, the mode of production, rather than class struggle as such, so that societies without classes are not placed outside history but move by their own contradictions.

The argument's stakes were immediate and political, which is why the address, titled by its author as a theoretical weapon, deserves the attention this article gives it. Orthodox doctrine held that class struggle is the engine of historical change; applied mechanically, that doctrine implied that the largely classless or incipiently classed societies of Guinea-Bissau stood somehow before history, waiting for classes to form before their struggles could count as historical action. Cabral inverted the relationship: class struggle is itself a product of a stage in the development of the productive forces, one motor among others that that development makes possible, and peoples without classes possess histories driven by the contradictions of their own modes of production. The correction sounds scholastic and was anything but. It licensed the movement he led to treat the Balanta or Manjaco village not as raw material awaiting a history made elsewhere but as a society whose internal dynamics the revolution had to understand and could ally with, and it removed the theoretical warrant for waiting, for tutelage by more developed movements, and for the metropolitan parties' habit of grading colonial struggles by their distance from a European script. Societies without classes are not outside history: the sentence is the address's hinge, and this article paraphrases rather than quotes it because its force here is structural, and because a paraphrase cannot be mistaken for a slogan torn from its argument.

### What did Cabral demand of the revolutionary petty bourgeoisie?

Cabral demanded that the educated stratum leading the movement commit what he called class suicide: that the revolutionary petty bourgeoisie renounce its aspiration to become a new possessing class, merge its fate with the peasantry's, and accept being remade by the struggle it led.

Class suicide is the brief's second great concept, and it is aimed with precision at the stratum Cabral knew from inside, the colonial-educated clerks, teachers, nurses, and minor officials, himself among them, from whom every anti-colonial movement drew its leadership. His analysis held that this stratum faced a fork at independence. It could use its monopoly of literacy, administration, and foreign connection to constitute itself as a national bourgeoisie, governing in the interest of its own accumulation and serving, in the older vocabulary, as the intermediary of foreign capital. Or it could abdicate that future, bind its conditions of life to the producers', and continue after independence the self-transformation the struggle had begun. Cabral was under no illusion that exhortation settled the matter; he treated the choice as a structural temptation to be countered by organisation, by the party's roots in the villages, and by the cadres' material dependence on the peasantry during the war. The concept connects this case directly to the comparison's finding, because it names, from within a movement where Marxism supplied the analysis, the exact mechanism by which nationalism's victory would otherwise convert the analysis into decoration: a leadership stratum that stops analysing its own position the moment its position becomes comfortable.

### Why was Cabral killed before independence arrived?

Cabral was assassinated in Conakry in January 1973 by dissident members of his own movement acting with Portuguese encouragement and assistance, at the moment when the liberated zones had made Guinea-Bissau's independence a question of timing rather than of outcome.

The aftermath belongs to the record this article has undertaken to state plainly. The movement held together, declared independence in September 1973, and won Portuguese recognition after Portugal's own dictatorship fell in 1974. Cabral's brother Luis led the new state; in 1980 he was overthrown by the movement's military commander, Joao Bernardo Vieira, in a coup whose justifications included grievances between Guineans and Cape Verdeans that shattered the two territories' union and expelled Cabral's party from the islands his strategy had joined to the mainland cause. Guinea-Bissau then lived the sequence the comparison has come to expect where the analysis lost its grip: economic deterioration, a one-party state decaying into a military one, structural adjustment imposed from outside, and in 1998 and 1999 a civil war between the president and his dismissed army chief that wrecked the capital and required foreign intervention to end. Cape Verde, separating from the catastrophe, moved to competitive politics in 1991 and built the region's most stable order on a fraction of the resources. One movement, two inheritances: the divergence is among the cleanest natural experiments the comparison possesses, and it supports the finding that the analysis's survival inside the state, not the fact of independence, governed what followed.

Cabral's analysis of his own society's structure, worked out in the movement's internal documents, shows the recalibration operating at ground level and deserves summary before the comparison moves on. Guinea-Bissau's peoples differed, he argued, in their modes of organisation: some, like the Fula and Mandinga, possessed stratified societies with chiefs, states, and embryonic class divisions the movement could exploit or confront; others, like the Balanta, whose rice-growing society recognised no chiefs, presented a horizontal order in which authority had to be constructed by persuasion alone. The party's tactics were differentiated accordingly, village by village, in a manner that inverted the metropolitan habit of applying a uniform class template. His treatment of the urban strata was equally unsentimental: the petty bourgeoisie of Bissau and the ports was at once the movement's indispensable recruiting ground and its principal long-term danger, the dockers and transport workers were its most reliable militants, and the peasantry, neither the revolutionary essence of romantic populism nor the inert mass of orthodox dismissal, would support the struggle in proportion as the struggle visibly served its interests, a proportion the liberated zones' schools, clinics, and people's stores existed to raise. This is what the analysis looked like when a Marxist movement made it at home: empirical, differentiated, and permanently open to correction by the people it described.

Cabral's writings on culture extend the recalibration into the territory where this article's religion section will later tread, and they deserve a summary here because they show the analysis refusing its own orthodoxies on a second front. Orthodox doctrine treated the cultures of colonised peoples ambivalently, as survivals to be respected tactically and superseded historically; Cabral, lecturing on national liberation and culture in his movement's last years, argued instead that a people's culture is the fruit of its history and the strongest single form of its resistance, since the colonial project aimed precisely at a people's cultural paralysis, its reduction to a museum of itself, and since the liberation struggle was in consequence an act of culture before it was a programme. The argument had a cutting edge aimed at his own stratum: the assimilated intellectual's temptation, which he named the return to the source, the theatrical rediscovery of a tradition the intellectual had been educated out of, was in his judgement a dead end unless it passed over into the people's actual struggle, because a culture worn as a costume by the educated is as alienated as the colonial culture it replaces. Here again the recalibration is empirical at root. A movement fighting village by village cannot afford to despise the moral world of the villages; it must either work through that world or work against the population it claims to free. Cabral chose to theorise the necessity rather than merely practise it, and the theory, liberation as the recovery of a people's historical agency in its cultural as in its economic life, is the point at which anti-colonial Marxism came closest to answering, from within, the nationalist counter-objection this article has already recorded: that the doctrine treated the nation's inner life as decoration. In Cabral's hands it did not, and the difference between his movement's village practice and the Algerian settlement's cultural decrees from above measures, once more, the distance between an analysis owned and an analysis recited.

The reception of Cabral's work completes the case. His writings circulated in translation through the liberation movements of southern Africa, whose own theorists acknowledged the debt; they were taken up in European and American universities as the most serious attempt to restate historical materialism from the standpoint of the colonised; and they were, inevitably, simplified in transit into slogans their author had hedged with conditions. Class suicide detached from the address's argument becomes a purity test; quoted whole, the address is a sterner and more useful document, an instruction to a leadership class about the institutional arrangements, dependence on the peasantry, answerability to assemblies, refusal of privilege, by which its self-abolition might be made practical rather than rhetorical. The gap between the slogan and the document is itself a datum for the comparison: analysis travels worst precisely where it is most needed, because the stratum charged with applying it controls its interpretation. Guinea-Bissau's post-independence rulers quoted Cabral constantly and governed in defiance of his central demand, a combination that the modernising objection, to which this article now turns by way of the remaining cases, predicts with uncomfortable accuracy.

## Neo-colonialism and the African socialisms

Between the communist-led movements and the purely nationalist ones stood a broad middle territory of leaders and parties that adopted Marxist concepts, Marxist vocabulary, and sometimes Marxist organisation while declining the doctrine's name and discipline, and it was from this territory that the period's most influential single concept emerged. Kwame Nkrumah, who led Ghana to independence in 1957 as the first sub-Saharan colony to break from European rule in the postwar wave, built his Convention People's Party into a mass machine, proclaimed a philosophy he called consciencism, and in 1965 published the analysis that fixed a word in the world's vocabulary: neo-colonialism, defined in his book of that year as the survival of economic domination after its political shell has been discarded. The concept addressed the precise experience of the new states. The flag had changed, the anthem had changed, the governor had sailed home, and the cocoa price was still set in London, the mines were still owned abroad, the currency still answered to a foreign treasury, and an American or European ambassador's displeasure could still unmake a budget. Nkrumah's book, whose full title made the claim explicit by calling neo-colonialism the last stage of imperialism, catalogued the mechanisms: aid with conditions, trade structured by inherited specialisation, military agreements, the cultivation of client elites. Publication cost him measurably; the United States protested and cut aid to Ghana, an episode that did the concept's circulation more good than any review.

### What did Nkrumah mean by neo-colonialism?

Nkrumah meant a condition in which a state possesses every outward attribute of sovereignty while its economy, and therefore its real policy, remains directed from outside; the term named domination that had shed direct rule and no longer needed it.

The correction of a standard misreading belongs here, because the term is constantly used as a loose synonym for imperialism and Nkrumah meant something more specific and more useful. Imperialism in the classical literature names a stage or system of the dominant economies themselves; neo-colonialism names the condition of the formally independent state on the receiving end, and the analytical point of coining it was that the receiving end's formal independence had become compatible with, even instrumental to, the sender's continued extraction. A neo-colonial state is not a colony with better public relations; it is a state whose sovereignty has been hollowed at the level where sovereignty is exercised, in decisions about investment, prices, borrowing, and war. The distinction matters to this article because the concept was the middle territory's characteristic intellectual product: not Marxist doctrine, which Ghana's ruling party never adopted as such, but a Marxist-derived analysis, sharpened on the experience of governing, that Marxist parties themselves took up and still use. Ghana's own trajectory then enacted the concept's warning upon its author. Nkrumah's state grew steadily more authoritarian, preventive detention from 1958, a one-party republic by 1964, a personality cult that embarrassed his own philosophy's materialism; the economy, squeezed by collapsing cocoa prices and ambitious industrial debts, deteriorated; and in 1966 the army overthrew him while he was abroad on a peace mission. The analyst of neo-colonialism ended as an exile in Guinea, honoured there with a ceremonial co-presidency by a fellow theorist of African socialism, while Ghana entered its own long alternation of soldiers and civilians.

Ghana's party deserves a paragraph in its own right, because the Convention People's Party was the most successful mass machine in the comparison that was not communist, and its methods show the organisational borrowing operating at full sophistication. Nkrumah, returned from a decade in America and Britain steeped in Marxist and Pan-African literature, broke with the respectable nationalist leadership of the Gold Coast over pace, founded his party in 1949 on the slogan of self-government now, and built it through the market women, the ex-servicemen, the elementary school leavers his rivals dismissed as veranda boys, organised in branches that reached towns the older politicians had never visited. His campaign of positive action, strikes, boycotts, and civil disobedience consciously adapted to local use, won the election of 1951 while he sat in prison, an achievement of mobilisation that no analysis alone performs. The party in power then exhibited, in accelerated form, the trajectory this article has tracked elsewhere: the machine built to win independence became the instrument for disciplining the society it governed, its youth wings and its women's wing converted from mobilisers into transmitters, its doctrine hardening from a living syncretism into the founder's collected speeches, cited as scripture. Nkrumah's continental ambitions, the union proclaimed with Guinea after France's punitive withdrawal, the All-African People's Conference hosted at Accra, the training and funding extended to liberation movements across the continent, were the foreign policy of the analysis and mobilisation division's middle category: a state that had borrowed its analysis generously, lending it onward to movements still in the bush, while its own practice at home converged on the intermediary pattern its finest concept had named.

The Ghanaian record's internal detail matters to the comparison because Ghana is the case where the middle category's degeneration can be dated almost to the year, and dating it disciplines the analysis. The Preventive Detention Act of 1958, directed first against a regional opposition the government accused of tribalism and violence, was renewed and widened until detention without trial became the ordinary instrument against critics of every kind, including the movement's own founding generation: J.B. Danquah, the doyen of Gold Coast nationalism and Nkrumah's former patron, died in detention in 1965, an event that did more than any opposition pamphlet to instruct Ghanaians in what the party-state had become. The republican referendum of 1960 and the constitutional amendment of 1964 that made the CPP the sole legal party were conducted as mobilisations rather than as counts, and the distinction was understood on all sides. Inside the party, a cadre of genuine socialists, formed in the unions and in the party's own ideological institute at Winneba, watched the doctrine they taught converted into the founder's liturgy and the institute itself purged when its teaching threatened to become a standard against which the practice could be measured. Ghana therefore supplies the modernising objection with its best single case and simultaneously with its limiting datum: the objection explains the trajectory completely except for the residue of cadres for whom the analysis remained real enough to be dangerous to the state that recited it. A doctrine that is pure decoration does not need its teachers purged. The purging is the trace of the analysis's survival inside the machine that was digesting it, and the comparison, which grades functions rather than essences, records the trace as carefully as the degeneration it accompanied.

### How did African socialism differ from the Marxism it borrowed from?

African socialism differed in its subject: where Marxism located socialism's agent in classes produced by capitalism, the African socialisms located it in a precolonial communal order they proposed to restore and modernise, treating class itself as a foreign import their societies could decline to develop.

The variants deserve separate handling, because the label covered opposed projects. Julius Nyerere's Tanzania gave the idea its most serious institutional form: the Arusha Declaration of 1967 committed his party to public ownership, self-reliance, and leadership codes against accumulation by officeholders, and ujamaa, familyhood, proposed to build socialism on the communal practices of the African village rather than on a proletariat Tanzania did not possess. The experiment's coercive turn is part of this article's record: in the nineteen seventies the state moved millions of peasants into planned villages by compulsion, disrupted the agriculture it meant to develop, and deepened the economic difficulty it was trying to escape, a failure Nyerere himself later acknowledged with a candour rare in the period's leadership. Senegal under Leopold Senghor took the opposite fork: an African socialism of cultural assertion and negotiated continuity, wedded to the doctrine of negritude, retaining close ties to France, and explicitly rejecting Marxist materialism in favour of a spiritualised communalism; Guinea under Sekou Toure took a third, proclaiming a revolutionary socialism of mass mobilisation while constructing one of the continent's most repressive party-states. What unites the variants is the analytical move the H3 answer names: all of them declined the Marxist sequence, in which socialism succeeds a capitalism that has first to be built, and proposed instead to leap from a communal past to a socialist future, using the party, Marxism's organisational gift, as the vehicle. The comparison files them accordingly: doctrine, national and communal; analysis, partly borrowed; organisation, borrowed wholesale; mass force, national. They are the analysis and mobilisation division's most populous category, the movements Marxism informed without leading, and their records, paternalist development, one-party rule, economic disappointment relieved by no one's complete catastrophe, are the division's median outcome.

Guinea under Sekou Toure deserves extraction from the variants paragraph, because it is the middle category's extreme case and extremes test definitions. Toure, a trade unionist formed in the French CGT's African federations, led Guinea to the independence referendum of 1958 in which his was the only territory to vote no to de Gaulle's community, accepting immediate independence and France's punitive withdrawal, down to the removal of equipment and archives, as the price of dignity. The dignity was real and the defiance heroic, and the state built on it became, over twenty-six years, a byword for the party-state's pathology: the Democratic Party of Guinea organised the population into a hierarchy of committees reaching every village and workplace, its women's and youth wings mobilising the society in continuous campaign, while its security apparatus filled the Camp Boiro in the capital with the regime's real and imagined enemies, in waves of arrests that consumed ministers, officers, teachers, and eventually much of the founding generation itself. Estimates of the camp's dead are contested in the manner this article has recorded throughout, and the article states the fact without the figures: a liberation government devoured its own cadres and terrorised its own people on a scale that made the country, rich in bauxite and water and agricultural promise, a net exporter of refugees. Guinea matters to the comparison because nothing in its catastrophe can be blamed on Marxist doctrine's presence or absence in any simple way. Toure's regime recited Marxist vocabulary, borrowed the party form entire, and subordinated the analysis, from the first year, to the leader's security; it is the modernising objection's case and the party-form section's case and the record's case simultaneously, the purest demonstration in the article that the form, unruled by the analysis, becomes an engine for converting a whole society into an instrument of rule.

## What the movements read: a canon in transit

A movement is partly the list of texts it treats as authoritative, and the anti-colonial movements' canon had a distinctive composition that shaped the doctrine they practised. At its head stood Lenin's writings on the national and colonial questions, read less for their letter than for their licence: the founder of the first socialist state had declared the colonial revolt an ally of the world revolution and had revised the movement's expectations accordingly, and that declaration authorised generations of colonial communists to put the national struggle first without feeling themselves renegades. Behind Lenin stood the Chinese texts, above all the writings on protracted war and on the peasantry, which circulated in translation through every guerrilla movement of the postwar decades and functioned as an operating manual where Lenin was a charter. Marx himself was read selectively and often at second hand: the volumes on India, the analysis of primitive accumulation, the chapters on the working day travelled; the full architecture of the critique of political economy, with its abstractions, largely did not, a fact the university Marxists of the metropoles held against the movements and the movements held against the universities.

The canon grew by annexation as the century proceeded. Fanon's final book became, within a decade of its publication, a text movements quoted who had no other Marxist literature, which is one measure of the analysis and mobilisation division: the most widely carried Marxist analysis of colonialism was written by a man whose movement was not Marxist-led and whose book is as much an anatomy of nationalism as a summons to it. Nkrumah's neo-colonialism treatise entered the reading lists of parties that regarded its author as a deviationist in other respects. Cabral's addresses circulated in mimeograph through the southern African movements and arrived, in the way of such texts, with their hedges sanded off. What the canon never contained is as telling as what it did: the democratic and libertarian strands of the Marxist tradition, the council communists, the critics of the vanguard party from within, travelled badly or not at all, because the movements that selected the canon were at war and selected for war. Anti-colonial Marxism's authoritarian temptations were therefore not accidents of personality alone, though the record supplies personalities in abundance; they were present in the movement's library, in the systematic absence of the tradition's own antidotes, and the post-independence single-party states had been prefigured in the reading lists long before they were proclaimed in the constitutions.

Alongside the doctrinal canon ran a literary one, and the comparison notes it because the movements' imagination of themselves was formed as much by poems and novels as by programmes. The negritude poets, Cesaire above all, whose Discourse on Colonialism prosecuted Europe in a prose that Marxists recognised as kin and claimed with reservations, gave the francophone generation a language for the colonial wound that no party text had supplied; Cesaire's own passage through and out of the French Communist Party, resigning in 1956 in a letter that charged the metropolitan party with subordinating the colonial question exactly as Padmore had charged the International, is the literary wing's version of this article's recurring structure. In the Portuguese world, the poets of the Lisbon student houses became the movements' founders and diplomats, and the PAIGC's and FRELIMO's publications carried verse as a matter of course; in Vietnam, the party's cultural policy manufactured a literature of resistance whose best work outlasted the policy; in India, the progressive writers' movement performed the same office for three generations of the left. The literary canon mattered to the comparison for a precise reason. Doctrine could be borrowed without being believed, and organisation without being understood, but the poems and the prose of the wound were the means by which the analysis entered the feelings of people who would never attend a study circle, the mobilisation's inner life rendered in the first person. When the successor states decayed, it was characteristically their writers who first recorded the decay in a form the censors recognised too late, which is the final evidence that the canon, like the analysis it carried, could not be wholly nationalised by the states it had helped to found.

## The vocabulary the movements made

The movements also manufactured the language in which their century discussed them, and the comparison pauses over the vocabulary because the words carried the analysis into institutions that would never open a Marxist text. National liberation itself, as a compound noun naming a war as legitimate by definition, was the movements' coinage in its modern force: it converted insurgency from a crime into a claim of right, and it was carried, by the diplomatic offensives the Algerian provisional government pioneered and the Vietnamese perfected, into the United Nations, whose 1960 declaration on the granting of independence to colonial countries and peoples ratified in the assembly's language what the movements had asserted in the field. Self-determination travelled the same road, from a Wilsonian slogan the empires had intended for Europe into a universal right the colonial powers were made to answer for annually. The third world, a phrase coined in Paris in 1952 by analogy with the third estate, gave the new states a collective name that was neither an alliance nor an ideology but a location in the world's hierarchy, and its career, through Bandung, through the non-aligned movement, through the demands for a new international economic order in the nineteen seventies, records the middle category's attempt to convert the analysis into a diplomacy after the movements had become governments.

The vocabulary's success had the usual price of success. National liberation, stretched to cover every armed movement with a flag, lost the analytical content the cases in this article gave it, in which liberation named a specific fusion of national and social programmes and could therefore be distinguished from a change of rulers. Neo-colonialism, coined for a precise mechanism, decayed in journalistic use into a general term of disapproval for foreign influence of any kind, which is why this article has taken care to restore its narrower meaning wherever the word does work. Even class suicide survives mostly as an exotic phrase, its institutional content forgotten. The decay of the vocabulary is not a merely literary loss. These words were the analysis in its portable form, the shape in which it entered law, diplomacy, and the schoolbooks of the successor states; as the words thinned, the analysis thinned with them, until the successor states retained the slogans of a doctrine whose arguments their ruling parties no longer taught. A reader who wants the analysis at full strength must therefore go behind the vocabulary to the cases, which is the journey this article has been conducting in reverse.

## Havana and the Tricontinental: the movements convene themselves

In January 1966 the movements held a summit of their own, and the comparison pauses over it because the gathering was the analysis and mobilisation division rendered as a conference. The Tricontinental Conference at Havana assembled delegates from Asia, Africa, and Latin America, movements in power and movements in the bush, parties and fronts and governments in exile, under a presiding spirit, the Moroccan organiser Mehdi Ben Barka, whose abduction and disappearance in Paris months earlier, in an affair reaching into French officialdom, had already announced the price the old order set on such convening. Cabral delivered there the address this article has treated as the period's most sophisticated theoretical statement, and the setting is part of the statement's meaning: the correction of Marxist orthodoxy was pronounced not in a metropolitan seminar but before the assembled movements of three continents, as one item of business among resolutions on solidarity, on Vietnam, on the coordination of struggles that proceeded, in the conference's daily work, to be exactly as fractious as the movements' relations everywhere else. The organisation the conference founded survived as a secretariat and a magazine; its practical effects were modest beside its symbolic one. For the first time the colonised world's movements possessed an institution that owed nothing to Moscow or to any metropolitan party, in which the traffic of analysis ran horizontally among the movements themselves. The horizontal traffic is the conference's true significance for this article. The doctrine's second and third waves were educated substantially by one another, Guineans reading Vietnamese manuals, Algerians advising Mozambicans, the whole network comparing notes on land reform and party building in a literature of solidarity that the metropolitan left then consumed at second hand. Whatever verdict the comparison reaches on the movements' states, their achievement in building, for one historical moment, a republic of letters and of arms that treated the colonised as the authors of theory rather than its audience stands as the transmission story's completion, and as the standard against which the successor states' own sealed archives and foundation myths measure their fall.

## The road not taken: movements that won without the doctrine

The comparison gains rigour from the cases at its edge, the movements that achieved independence with little or no Marxist component, because they show what the doctrine's presence and absence each explain. Kenya's struggle is the sharpest instance. The grievance there was land in its most literal form: the alienation of the Kikuyu highlands to settler farmers, the confinement of the dispossessed to reserves and to wage labour on the farms their grandfathers had owned. The movement that fought the British in the nineteen fifties, remembered by its enemies' name for it and by its fighters as the Land and Freedom Army, was built from oathing networks, ex-servicemen, the Nairobi unions' radical wing, and the forest fighters' astonishing endurance, and its ideological equipment was correspondingly local: land, freedom, and the restoration of a people's dignity, articulated in Kikuyu idiom, with Christianity's vocabulary contested between mission and movement. Marxist texts circulated at its edges, its most famous prisoner, Jomo Kenyatta, had studied in Moscow among much else and had written an ethnography of his own people that remains a classic, but the movement's analysis of its enemy was the settler and the state that protected him, stated in the language of stolen land rather than of a world system. Kenya won independence in 1963, under Kenyatta's nationalist party, and proceeded to build a capitalist state in which the land question was settled substantially in favour of those with the means to buy, a trajectory the comparison cannot file under Fanon's prediction, since no borrowed analysis had promised anything else, nor under the modernising objection, since no modernising doctrine had been adopted to betray. Kenya shows the null case: nationalism unaccompanied, mobilising magnificently, analysing narrowly, and governing thereafter within the horizon of its analysis.

Indonesia supplies the edge case from the other side, a movement that encountered Marxism early, absorbed much of it, and deliberately synthesised it away. The Dutch East Indies had one of Asia's oldest communist parties, founded in 1920 from a fusion of Dutch radicals and Indonesian organisers, and its premature revolts of 1926 and 1927 were crushed so thoroughly that the national movement's leadership passed to a generation that had watched communism's local catastrophe. Sukarno, the movement's dominant figure and the republic's founding president, built his doctrine of Marhaenism as an explicit synthesis: Marxist in its analysis of imperialism and of the little man's exploitation, nationalist in its subject, and hospitable to religious sentiment in a manner orthodox doctrine forbade. The republic proclaimed in 1945 survived its war against the returning Dutch, and the Indonesian Communist Party rebuilt itself into the largest communist party outside the socialist camp, operating legally under Sukarno's patronage as one pillar of his guided democracy, until the catastrophe of 1965 destroyed it in massacres whose scale remains contested and whose perpetrators included the army and religious organisations the party had coexisted with for two decades. Indonesia's lesson for the comparison is written in that ending. A movement Marxism informed at the level of the presidency, and a party Marxism embodied at the level of millions of members, coexisted without fusion, neither leading the other, until a crisis arrived in which the party discovered that its millions had been mobilised on sufferance and the state it had not sought to command disposed of it in months. The analysis and mobilisation division, inverted: here the party supplied the mobilisation, the presidency supplied the analysis's diluted national form, and the army, which neither analysis had reckoned with, supplied the verdict.

## The wars come home: Portugal and the boomerang of empire

The Portuguese case, which runs through Cabral's story like a ground bass, deserves a section of its own, because it is the instance in which anti-colonial Marxism changed the colonising country as dramatically as the colonised. Portugal, the poorest of the imperial powers and governed by Europe's most durable authoritarian regime, fought three simultaneous colonial wars from 1961, in Angola, Mozambique, and Guinea, rather than concede the independence every other empire had begun to grant, and conscripted a generation of its young men to fight them. The wars radicalised both ends of the empire. In Africa they produced the comparison's second wave of Marxist movements: the MPLA in Angola and FRELIMO in Mozambique, founded by intellectuals of Cabral's generation and milieu, Eduardo Mondlane of FRELIMO having been formed in the same Lisbon student circles, which adopted Marxist analysis and one-party organisation with Cabral's example before them and Soviet and Chinese assistance behind them. In Portugal they produced a conscript officer corps that read the enemy's literature to understand its defeats, concluded that the wars were unwinnable and the regime that demanded them indefensible, and in April 1974 overthrew the dictatorship in the name, among others, of the liberation movements' own vocabulary. The Carnation Revolution is the comparison's most startling single datum: an anti-colonial analysis, developed for the villages of Guinea, circulating through the Portuguese army's captains until the empire's own soldiers dissolved the empire and democratised the metropole. No finding about these movements is adequate that treats influence as flowing one way, from doctrine to colony to history's margin; in Lisbon in 1974 the margin abolished the centre's regime.

The second wave's post-independence records then extend the comparison's table downward into the century's bleakest entries, and honesty requires them. Angola and Mozambique won independence in 1975 into civil wars that the Cold War's rival patrons armed and prolonged for a generation: the MPLA government in Luanda, sustained by Cuban troops against South African invasion, governing an oil enclave surrounded by insurgency; FRELIMO's Mozambique subjected to a destabilisation campaign by Rhodesia and then South Africa that wrecked its economy and killed its citizens in numbers that remain, like all the figures in this article's territory, bitterly contested. Both movements had adopted the analysis in its most rigorous available form; both built parties of genuine discipline; both were then fed into a geopolitical mincer that no analysis of their own societies could have governed. Their records therefore qualify the finding in a direction opposite to the modernising objection. Where the objection holds that doctrine was a decoration on elite ambition, the Portuguese-African cases show doctrine and organisation alike overwhelmed by an international order that treated the new states as terrain. The analysis and mobilisation division explains what the movements brought to independence; it does not pretend that what they brought decided everything that independence would meet.

## The socialist camp and the movements: aid, training, and friction

No account of these movements is complete without the international order that armed, funded, and sometimes frustrated them, and the comparison requires that order to be described with the same refusal of romance applied to the movements themselves. The Soviet Union and, after 1949, China supplied the anti-colonial world with weapons, training, scholarships, printing presses, and diplomatic cover, on a scale that materially altered what small movements could attempt: Vietnamese air defence, Algerian diplomacy, Guinean logistics all bear the mark of that assistance. The assistance was neither charity nor simple manipulation. It was state policy pursued by governments with interests of their own, extended generously to movements that served those interests, withdrawn or hedged when they did not, and accompanied by expectations of alignment that the more independent movements learned to discount. Ho Chi Minh manoeuvred between Moscow and Beijing through their split, extracting support from both while surrendering strategy to neither; the Algerian front accepted arms from every available source, including governments hostile to communism, and gave no ally a voice in its councils; India, whose communists had obeyed the international line at ruinous cost, stood in the comparison as the warning of what subordination could do.

The Sino-Soviet split, which split the world's communist parties in the nineteen sixties, reached the anti-colonial movements as both burden and opportunity. Burden, because parties from India to South Africa fractured along the fault line, expending on fraternal polemic energies their enemies could only applaud; the Indian split of 1964, one of the comparison's recorded facts, cannot be understood apart from it. Opportunity, because two competing patrons bid up the price of allegiance and widened the space in which a movement could define its own doctrine, and because China's example, a peasant revolution victorious in the largest agrarian society on earth, offered the colonised world a model closer to its conditions than the Soviet original. Bandung and the non-aligned movement supplied the rival frame: a diplomacy of the new states that declined both camps and treated Marxism as one resource among others. The movements in this article navigated all of it pragmatically, and the pragmatism is itself evidence for the finding. Analysis that could be detached from its state sponsors, translated, and put to work in a Guinean village or an Algerian wilaya had a life independent of the camp that claimed ownership of it; where the movements prospered intellectually, they treated the socialist camp as a supplier and an audience, never as a headquarters.

## Neo-colonialism in practice: cocoa, copper, and oil

The concept of neo-colonialism earns its place in the comparison only if its mechanisms can be shown operating, and the cases supply the demonstration in commodity after commodity. Ghana's cocoa is the type specimen. The colonial marketing board, retained and expanded by the independence government, bought the crop from farmers at a price fixed by the state and sold it at the world price, appropriating the difference for development funds and, increasingly, for the party-state's general purposes; when the world price collapsed in the mid nineteen sixties, the state's revenues collapsed with it, while the farmers, who had been taxed by the board in good years without sharing its proceeds, deserted the ruling party whose overthrow they did not mourn. Every element of Nkrumah's analysis is visible in the episode, including the element he had not exempted: the extraction ran through an independent African state, administered by its own officials, and the line between neo-colonial mechanism and domestic class formation runs exactly where Cabral's class suicide said it would. The Zambian copper belt told the same story in a harder currency: a nationalist government inheriting a mining economy owned abroad, its budget a function of a metal price set in London, its sovereignty over the subsoil asserted by nationalisation only after the price cycle and the companies' investment strike had demonstrated where effective sovereignty resided.

Guinea's bauxite and Algeria's hydrocarbons complete the demonstration from opposite directions. Guinea possessed a substantial share of the world's bauxite reserves and a government, Toure's, that proclaimed the continent's most uncompromising revolutionary socialism; the ore was nonetheless mined and shipped for decades by consortia of foreign companies on terms negotiated from weakness, because the capital, technology, shipping, and markets for alumina all lay outside Guinean control, and the state's revolutionary vocabulary altered none of those coordinates. Algeria, richer in the commodity that mattered most, used hydrocarbon nationalisation to seize a commanding share of its oil and gas rents, and the feat, genuinely an assertion of sovereignty against the companies, financed the state so completely that the state ceased to need its society: a government funded by rents rather than taxes could dispense with the consent taxation purchases, and the rentier logic Fanon's generation had not foreseen became the material base of the front's later decay. The comparison draws the generalisation the cases support. Neo-colonialism named a real structure, visible in the accounts of every state in this article; but the structure's victims included governments that wielded its vocabulary most fluently, and its mechanisms ran as readily through a liberation party's marketing board as through a colonial governor's, which is why the concept survives best as an analytical instrument rather than as any government's alibi.

The Congo crisis of 1960 and 1961, though the Congo appears in no row of the comparison's table, stands as the mechanism's paradigm demonstration, and the article enters it here because Nkrumah's concept was in part generalised from it. The Congo won independence from Belgium in June 1960 with a nationalist government under Patrice Lumumba, a leader whose doctrine was a Congolese nationalism of dignity and unity rather than any Marxism, and within weeks the country's richest province, Katanga, seceded under a leader sustained by Belgian mining interests and Belgian officers, the army mutinied, Belgian troops intervened, and the United Nations operation summoned to restore order became the arena in which the young state's sovereignty was negotiated away. Lumumba, dismissed by his president under Western encouragement, was arrested, transferred to Katanga, and murdered there in January 1961, in circumstances in which Belgian officials participated and American intelligence had separately plotted against his life, as later official inquiries in both countries established. The sequence is the concept's anatomy lesson: a formally sovereign state, an elected nationalist government, a resource province detached by the former power's companies, a leader eliminated with foreign complicity, and a successor order, Mobutu's three decades of kleptocratic alignment, installed as the mechanism's long-term tenant. No Marxist party led anything in the Congo; the analysis the episode vindicated had been written by a Ghanaian nationalist generalising his own experience, and the comparison cites the case to close the question of whether neo-colonialism named something real. It did, and its reality did not depend on the ideology of its victims, which is why the concept outlasted the movements that coined it and remains the middle category's most durable gift to the world's political vocabulary.

## Who filled the movements: workers, veterans, students, and women

The comparison's categories sharpen further when the movements' social composition is examined, because the analysis and mobilisation division turns out to describe not only functions but people. The cadres who carried the analysis were drawn overwhelmingly from the colonial order's narrow educated stratum: teachers, clerks, medical orderlies, journalists, railwaymen, the products of mission schools and metropolitan universities, a stratum small enough that its members across a territory often knew one another personally. The mass force came from elsewhere: from peasants defending land and subsistence, from migrant labourers radicalised by the compounds and the colour bar, from market women whose trade the colonial economy taxed and harassed, from ex-servicemen who had fought the empire's wars and returned with skills, grievances, and a refusal of the deference they had been promised equality for abandoning. The war veterans recur in the founding generation of movement after movement, in Algeria, in Kenya, in Guinea-Bissau, in Vietnam, and their prominence corrects a purely intellectual history of the doctrine's spread: for many of the men who built the mass organisations, the analysis arrived after the radicalisation, as an explanation of experiences, the army's racial hierarchy, the veteran's discarded uniform, that had already made them rebels.

Two constituencies deserve separate mention because the movements' treatment of them exposes the fusion's limits from within. Organised workers, the class in whose name the doctrine spoke, were real but unevenly placed: strongest where ports, mines, and railways concentrated them, in the docks of Bissau and Dakar, the mines of the Copperbelt and the Rand, the railways and mills of Bombay and Saigon, and their unions supplied several movements with their earliest mass organisations and their most disciplined strikes. Yet in most territories they were a minority of the population and sometimes, as in Algeria's settler labour movement, an ambivalent one, and the movements learned to treat the proletariat as a valued detachment rather than the main body, which is the social fact beneath the theoretical revisions catalogued earlier. Women, who sustained every movement's logistics, fed its armies, carried its intelligence, and in the liberated zones attended its schools in numbers that transformed their societies' futures, were promised by most movements an emancipation the post-independence states largely postponed or revoked; the Algerian women who had carried bombs and messages were returned to domesticity by the family code of the state they had made, and the pattern, with local variations, repeats from Vietnam's honoured but subordinated heroines to the PAIGC's partially honoured commitments. A comparative history that reports the movements' achievements and failures must enter this one in the same column as the annulled elections and the detention acts: the constituencies whose mobilisation was indispensable were not reliably the constituencies whose claims survived victory, and the division between what a movement uses and what it rewards is the analysis and mobilisation division's shadow, visible inside the movements as well as between doctrine and nation.

One institution of recruitment deserves naming because every case in the article passed through it: the colonial prison. The empires imprisoned their opponents in cohorts, for years at a stretch, in establishments where books were smuggled, languages learned, and the comparative study of empire conducted by its most attentive students. Indian communists and Congressmen alike emerged from the Raj's prisons with educations their captors had not intended; the Vietnamese party's cadres were tempered in the island prison of Poulo Condore, whose alumni staffed the revolution for two generations; Algerian militants of every tendency shared cells in which the front's future ministers argued doctrine with the communists their movement would marginalise; Kenyatta, Nkrumah's lieutenants, and a long roll of the century's founders carried prison numbers as credentials. The prison functioned, in the comparison's terms, as the one place where analysis and mobilisation were forcibly fused: men and women thrown together by repression, with time as their only resource, converted the time into study and the study into organisation. Historians of the movements neglect the institution because it leaves few records of its own; the memoirs do not neglect it, and a reader following the memoirs will notice how often the decisive intellectual encounters of the period, the meeting of future rivals, the conversion of nationalists to Marxism and of communists to national patience, are set in cells. The empires, seeking to decapitate the movements, repeatedly convened their general staffs at state expense.

## Schools of the struggle: how the movements reproduced themselves

A movement that lasts decades must manufacture its own successors, and the anti-colonial movements' educational machinery, improvised under fire, is among their most consequential and least examined achievements. The Viet Minh and its successor organisations treated literacy as a weapon system: the resistance zones ran mass campaigns in which the newly literate taught the illiterate in turn, so that a peasant army could read its own orders, its own newspapers, and eventually its own government's decrees, and the party's cadre schools layered political education onto the literacy in graded courses that reproduced, in jungle clearings, the functions of a university. The PAIGC's schools in the liberated zones served a double purpose Cabral stated with characteristic plainness: to teach children the colony had abandoned, and to train the administrators of a state that did not yet exist, with the movement's boarding school in Conakry educating a generation of Guinean cadres abroad when the war made schooling at home impossible. The FLN's educational effort was necessarily thinner, its war more purely military, and the difference shows in the successor states: Algeria at independence possessed a liberation army and a devastated school system, and had to import teachers from Egypt and France to staff the classrooms of its own revolution.

The cadre school is also where the comparison's central tension reproduced itself in miniature. What the schools taught was the analysis, and the analysis, taught as a curriculum with correct answers, tended toward the catechism: generations of cadres learned the vocabulary of stages, contradictions, and class forces as a liturgy whose mastery certified loyalty, and the line between a movement educating its members to think and a movement certifying its members for thinking correctly was crossed in school after school, usually without anyone deciding to cross it. The exceptions illuminate the rule. Cabral's instructions to his political workers insist, in document after document, that the cadre's first duty is to learn from the population, that the people's knowledge of their own conditions is the curriculum's foundation, and that a political worker who arrives with finished answers has failed before he begins; the PAIGC's schools were designed as exchanges, however imperfectly the design survived wartime. The contrast between the two pedagogies, catechism and exchange, predicts more about the successor states than many a constitutional provision: states founded by movements that had taught catechisms governed by them, and the single-party regimes' later inability to hear unwelcome information, about harvests, about prices, about their own popularity, was a habit acquired in the cadre schools of the struggle, where the correct answer had so often been a condition of survival.

## The movements and the sacred: religion as rival, idiom, and ally

No variable exposes the difference between doctrine and mobilisation more cleanly than religion, which the orthodox doctrine treated as an obstacle and the successful movements treated as a medium. Colonial societies were not secularised populations awaiting a materialist gospel; they were worlds in which Islam, Buddhism, Hinduism, Catholicism, and a hundred local cosmologies organised time, community, and moral judgement, and a movement that addressed those worlds in the doctrine's atheist register addressed almost no one. The Algerian front understood this in its bones: its proclamations spoke of a national personality that was Muslim in substance, its fighters were blessed and buried as martyrs in the religious sense, and the Association of Algerian Muslim Ulama, whose reformist Islam had kept national consciousness alive through the decades of assimilationist pressure, supplied the movement with a genealogy no communist pamphlet could have furnished. The front's socialism, when it came, was careful to declare itself compatible with Islam, and the independent state's eventual family code, patriarchal and religious in its provisions, recorded the price of the alliance: the constituency whose idiom had mobilised the nation collected its fee in the settlement's terms, and Algerian women, whose war service the front had celebrated, paid it.

Vietnam's communists, formed in a Confucian and Buddhist civilisation, practised a comparable negotiation under a doctrine that permitted less of it. The party's villages built their solidarity partly through the communal house and the ancestor cult's moral economy, the party's own rituals of commemoration absorbed the forms of the cult of the dead, and the regime's later cult of its founder reproduced, in socialist dress, structures of veneration its doctrine officially disowned. India's communists, facing the most religiously articulated national movement of the century, never solved the problem Gandhi solved instinctively: his idiom of prayer, fasting, and renunciation mobilised millions in a vocabulary the communists could only translate, and translation always arrived late and sounded foreign. The comparison generalises the point into the finding's vocabulary. Mobilisation is made of a people's actual moral materials, and where those materials are religious, the movement that wins is the one that speaks the sacred fluently, whether or not its doctrine permits fluency; the movements in this article that spoke it as a second language, learned for the purpose, prospered in proportion to their accent. Marxism supplied the analysis of why the temple's lands were taxed; the temple supplied the crowd.

## City and countryside: the geography of the struggle

The comparison's cases divide by terrain as instructively as by doctrine, and the division maps onto the theory's central revision. The classical script located revolution in the city, among the concentrated workers of great factories, with the countryside as a reservoir of reaction to be neutralised; the anti-colonial movements inverted the geography by necessity and then by doctrine. In Vietnam, in Guinea-Bissau, in China's excluded but exemplary case, the countryside encircled the cities in the strategy's literal cartography: base areas governed as proto-states, supply lines through villages organised cell by cell, the towns left to the enemy until the correlation of forces ripened, sometimes for decades. The inversion was forced by arithmetic, the colonial state held the cities with its garrisons and its police, and by sociology, the peasantry was the overwhelming population; but the movements theorised the necessity into a principle, arguing that in the colonised world the countryside was not the revolution's rear but its principal theatre, because that was where the colonial order's extraction was most naked and its administration thinnest. Algeria complicated the principle with its most famous exception: the Battle of Algiers, in which the front carried the war into the capital's casbah and European quarters, was a military defeat and a political victory of the first rank, demonstrating to France and to the world's press that the empire could not hold even its showcase city in tranquillity, and the front's strategists absorbed the lesson that urban action's value was measured in newspapers and parliaments as much as in territory.

India and Ghana ran the geography the other way, and their trajectories mark the difference. The Indian movement's decisive theatres were urban and electoral, its general strikes and its salt marches and its parliamentary manoeuvres conducted before newspaper cameras in a largely literate political nation; the communists, whose strongest cards were the unions of Bombay and Calcutta and the peasant leagues of the deltas, never resolved whether their revolution was to be made in the mill districts or the villages, and the indecision, visible in the oscillation between insurrectionary and parliamentary lines, was one material cause of the marginality the comparison has recorded. Ghana's positive action was an urban art, of markets, lorry parks, newspapers, and the crowd in the capital's streets, and it won power with a speed the rural wars never approached; but the CPP's geography bequeathed its state a corresponding weakness, a party whose roots in the countryside ran through chiefs and brokers rather than through the village committees the PAIGC had built, so that when the cocoa price fell and the brokers' loyalty was repriced, the machine discovered how shallow its rural foundations were. Terrain, in the comparison's austere summary, is a variable like doctrine: the movements that organised where their people actually lived, at whatever cost in slowness, built the states that lasted; the movements that seized the capital's squares inherited the capital's illusions along with its palaces.

## Land: the master grievance

Beneath the comparison's categories runs a single material grievance that connects cases otherwise separated by continent and doctrine, and a section is owed to it. The colonial order was, in its daily operation, a machine for taking land: for settlers in Algeria and Kenya, for plantations in Vietnam and Malaya, for mines and for the reserves into which the dispossessed were compressed, and the taking was experienced not as an economic policy but as the world's overturning, the ancestors' ground fenced, the grazing closed, the forest made the state's. Land was therefore the one demand that every constituency in the colonised world understood identically, the peasant whose plot was seized, the herder whose range was enclosed, the veteran promised a farm, the chief whose authority had rested on allocating it, and the movements' agrarian programmes were the point at which analysis and mobilisation fused most completely, because the analysis of land, its concentration, its rents, its alienation, was simultaneously the doctrine's most concrete chapter and the crowd's most intimate knowledge.

The cases distribute themselves by what they did with the grievance. Vietnam's party made land its battering ram twice, in the rent reductions of the resistance and the reform of the nineteen fifties, and the second use demonstrated the grievance's danger to its own wielders: the campaign's violence against the rural order it had mobilised left scars the party spent decades acknowledging. Algeria's front promised the recovery of settler land and delivered it, in the self-managed estates and then the agrarian revolution, into the hands of a state whose beneficiaries were its own appointees as often as the landless. Kenya's fighters made land and freedom a single word and received, at independence, a settlement that transferred the highlands by purchase to those with capital and connections, the grievance's most literal betrayal in the comparison and the seed of grievances that Kenyan politics still cultivates. India's communists built their strongest movements where the land question was sharpest, in Telangana and Tebhaga and later in the Naxalbari rising that split their tradition again, and lost the nation partly because Congress, with its promise of zamindari abolition delivered as legislation rather than revolution, harvested the grievance at the centre. Land explains why the peasantry could be a revolutionary class in these societies without waiting for the doctrine's permission: the peasant's demand was already a demand for the overturn of the existing order of property, stated in the idiom of restitution, and the movements that learned to hear a revolutionary programme inside a demand for the ancestors' fields back had grasped the century's central political fact about the colonised world.

## Why the party form outlasted the doctrine

One of the comparison's strangest findings, visible across the whole period, is that Marxism's organisational export proved more durable than its doctrine, surviving the doctrine's abandonment by the very movements that had adopted both. The disciplined vanguard party, with its hierarchy of committees, its cells in workplace and village, its political education and its claim to embody a science of society, was taken up not only by communist parties but by nationalist movements of every complexion, by the front in Algeria, by Nkrumah's machine in Ghana, by TANU in Tanzania, and in adapted forms by movements whose official creeds were religious or ethnic. Its attractions under colonial conditions are not mysterious. A movement facing a police state needs compartmentation; a movement claiming to act for a whole people needs a structure that can transmit decisions across a territory and generations of members; a movement led by the educated needs a device that converts their literacy into authority while presenting that authority as the people's own. The party form solved all three problems at once, and it did so independently of the truth of the doctrine inscribed on its banner, which is why a historian can trace its diffusion across movements that agreed on nothing else.

The form's afterlife in power is the darker half of the finding. A structure designed for conspiracy against a colonial state converts readily into a structure for administering a post-colonial one: the cell becomes the neighbourhood committee that reports as much as it organises, the centre's discipline becomes the president's instrument, political education becomes the recitation of the leader's speeches, and the claim to embody science becomes the claim that opposition is not merely mistaken but objectively counter-revolutionary. Not every party-state travelled the full distance, and the comparison's cases distribute themselves along the road: Vietnam's party retained internal procedures robust enough to change course at intervals; Tanzania's retained a leader willing to retire; Algeria's was absorbed by the army; Guinea's became the personal property of its founder; Ghana's did not survive its founder's fall. The doctrine, where it survived as anything more than liturgy, survived as the standard against which members could indict the party itself, as Vietnamese rectifications and Tanzanian leadership codes intermittently show. Where the doctrine died first, nothing remained to indict with, and the form governed alone. This is perhaps the comparison's most portable lesson for readers of later movements anywhere: audit the organisation before the programme, because the organisation is what will still be there, exercising power, when the programme has been revised beyond recognition.

## The case comparison

The cases can now be set side by side. The case comparison below states, for each movement examined, whether its leading organisation was Marxist, what Marxism supplied to the struggle, what nationalism supplied, what the post-independence trajectory was, and which of the two factors prevailed in the resulting state. The table is the article's findable core: a reader who absorbs nothing else should leave with its pattern, and a reader who disputes the article's argument should dispute it row by row, since the finding is an aggregate of the rows and stands or falls with their individual accuracy.

Reading down the columns is more instructive than reading across any single row. The first column answers the snippet question statistically: one clear yes in six, one substantial, four noes, which is the quantitative form of the claim that anti-colonialism and Marxism were not coextensive. The second column shows what travelled even where leadership did not: in every row, including the four noes, Marxism supplied something the movement used, an analysis, a concept, a repertoire, and in no row did it supply nothing. The third column is the column the movements' own memoirs underplay and the comparison insists on: nationalism supplied the people. No doctrine in any of these cases mobilised a village, a dockyard, or a student generation by itself; the nation did that, the promise that foreign rule would end and the humiliated would govern themselves. The fourth column is deliberately written without adjectives of praise or blame, and its uniformity is the sobering datum of the whole inquiry: one-party states in five rows of six, severe economic difficulty in all six, mass violence in at least three. The fifth column then does the article's analytical work, because the trajectories, uniform in form, differed in their internal logic in ways that track the fifth column's entries: where the analysis remained a living discipline inside the state, policy corrected itself, late and at cost, but observably; where nationalism had consumed the movement entire, the state reproduced the colonial structure with new personnel, as the movement's own diagnostician had warned it would.

### What pattern appears when the cases are set side by side?

The pattern is a division of labour with a consequence: Marxism supplied analysis and organisational form in every case and leadership in few, nationalism supplied mass force in every case, and the post-independence state reproduced whichever element had dominated the movement that founded it.

Two qualifications keep the pattern honest, and both are visible in the table rather than imposed on it. The first is that the boundary between the columns leaks. Vietnamese nationalism was not an external fuel poured into a Marxist engine; it was constitutive of the party's own identity from Ho's conversion onward, and Algerian nationalism absorbed so much Marxist analysis that grading the front's doctrine requires the separation of variables this article performed at the outset rather than any verdict on its essence. The division is an instrument for comparison, not a chemistry of pure substances. The second qualification is that the table's cases were chosen because the comparison's literature treats them as the type specimens of their categories, and a different selection, adding Cuba, adding China, adding the Portuguese colonies' other movements, would enrich the rows without, in this article's judgement, reversing the pattern; the companion treatments of the Chinese and Cuban revolutions exist precisely so that readers can test that judgement against fuller evidence.

Each row also argues back against the table that contains it, and the qualifications deserve stating before the finding is built on the rows. Vietnam's row credits Marxism with the analysis, but the party's founder arrived at the doctrine as a patriot and said so; the analysis was nationalist in its motive force from the first day, and a reader who files Ho Chi Minh under imported theory has not read his account of his own conversion. India's row records a party marginal to the national movement, yet in 1946, in the naval ratings' mutiny and the strike waves of the transfer of power years, the communists stood near the centre of the subcontinent's most dramatic confrontations with the Raj, and their marginality in the succession was a political outcome, not a measure of their absence from the struggle. Ghana's row files Nkrumah's party under nationalism armed with borrowed analysis, but the party contained a genuine socialist cadre formed in the unions and the study circles, whose members experienced the founder's later cult as a betrayal of something they had actually believed; the row's verdict describes the state's destination, not the whole of the movement's life. Guinea-Bissau's row, finally, records a divergence between the mainland territory and Cape Verde that no single verdict column can hold, a reminder that the table's unit, the case, sometimes conceals two histories wearing one movement's name. These qualifications do not dissolve the pattern; they are what distinguishes a comparative finding from a typology imposed on the evidence, and the article records them here so that the finding, when it comes, arrives already discounted by its own exceptions.

## Three waves of decolonisation, and the doctrine's changing role in each

The cases distribute themselves across three waves of imperial retreat, and the doctrine's role shifted measurably from wave to wave, a variation the table's rows conceal and the finding should absorb. The first wave broke over Asia in the decade after 1945: Indonesia, Vietnam, India, Burma, the Philippines, and China at its edge, in a theatre where communist parties were already substantial organisations with wartime resistance records, and where the doctrine therefore appeared at its maximum strength, supplying leadership in Vietnam and China, a mass party in Indonesia, and in India the paradox this article has examined. The second wave broke over Africa between 1956 and the mid nineteen sixties, Ghana to Algeria to Guinea, in a theatre where communist parties were embryonic or settler-compromised and the initiative lay with nationalist mass movements, charismatic founders, and the borrowed-analysis pattern that dominates this article's table; the doctrine's role contracted from leadership to supply, of concepts, of cadres trained abroad, of diplomatic method. The third wave, the Portuguese collapse of 1974 and 1975, was the first two waves' pupil: its movements had studied in the earlier theatres' schools, quoted Cabral as scripture and Vietnam as proof, and attempted, consciously, to avoid the second wave's errors by fusing doctrine and nation from the founding document onward, an attempt whose frustration by Cold War intervention the Portuguese section has already entered in the record.

Periodisation disciplines the finding in a useful way. Read synchronically, the analysis and mobilisation division can look like a timeless law of movements; read across the waves, it is a distribution that changed as the century learned. In the first wave the doctrine could still plausibly present itself as the general staff of world revolution, and parties behaved accordingly, with India's obedience and Vietnam's apprenticeship as the twin consequences. By the second wave the doctrine had become one resource among several in a market that included Bandung diplomacy, American anti-communist patronage, and the new states' own accumulated examples, and the movements behaved as customers rather than as branches. By the third wave the customers had become critics and improvers, selecting from the doctrine with a confidence born of watching its earlier applications succeed and fail. The finding survives the periodisation, because the distribution of functions recurs in all three waves; what the waves add is the direction of travel, from a doctrine that commanded, through a doctrine that supplied, to a doctrine that was used, criticised, and in the using transformed.

The waves also differed in the diplomatic instruments at their disposal, and the difference reinforced the doctrinal one. The first wave's movements fought in a world whose institutions had been designed by the empires and offered no forum but the battlefield and the metropolitan parliament; the Vietnamese and Indonesian delegations of the nineteen forties were supplicants at conferences whose outcomes the great powers arranged. The second wave inherited the United Nations enlarged by its own victories, the Afro-Asian bloc's voting weight, and the solidarity organisations headquartered in Cairo and Accra and Algiers, and its leaders, Nkrumah and Ben Bella and Toure among them, were consequently statesmen of a world stage while their parties at home were still young; the analysis they carried into those forums was sharpened by the audience, since a doctrine that must persuade neutrals and adversaries in public learns economy and loses cant. The third wave, arriving when the forums were crowded and the Cold War's patrons were bidding openly for clients, enjoyed the instruments and suffered the attention in the highest degree: its movements were recognised, funded, and armed by rival camps before they had won their wars, and several of them discovered at independence that their foreign friendships had acquired mortgages over their domestic choices. The comparison notes the progression because it completes the periodisation's lesson. Each wave's movements were more knowing than the last, and knowledge, in this history, was not cumulative advantage but a change in the terms of trade between the movements and the world: the doctrine commanded least where it was needed most, and arrived most plentifully where the need for it had already been compromised by the suppliers' conditions. A reader who grasps that trajectory possesses the article's subject in its historical motion, which is the form in which the movements themselves, at their best, insisted on grasping everything.

## The analysis and mobilisation division

The comparative finding can now be stated in its full form. Across the movements examined, Marxism characteristically supplied the analysis and the party form while nationalism supplied the mass force, and the post-independence outcome tracked which of the two dominated the resulting state. This is the analysis and mobilisation division, and it is offered as a comparative finding rather than a judgement on either contributor: nationalism's force was not a regrettable adulterant of a purer doctrine, and Marxism's analysis was not a foreign gloss on an indigenous impulse. The movements were fusions, and the division names the internal distribution of functions within the fusion, the way an account of a firm might distinguish its engineering from its sales force without implying that either could have incorporated alone.

Why should the division have taken this shape so consistently? The components answer the question. Analysis is portable in a way mobilisation is not: a text can be translated in a prison cell, carried across an ocean in a coat lining, and taught in a study circle of five, and the Marxist analysis of empire was supremely translatable because it had been composed, from its classical statements onward, as an account of a world system rather than of any one country's arrangements. Organisation is portable for the same reason: the disciplined party is a technology, specifiable in rules, adaptable to jungle and city alike, and it solved the standing problem of movements that had to survive illegality, infiltration, and the arrest of their leaders. Mobilisation is not portable at all. It is made of the grievances, languages, memories, and shames of a particular people in a particular place, and the idiom that reliably converted those materials into collective action at scale was the nation: the promise that the foreigners would leave and the despised would rule. A movement shopping for its components in the twentieth century's market of ideas therefore assembled the same package again and again, analysis from the Marxist shelf, organisation from the Marxist shelf, and mass force from the national one, whether or not its leaders described the transaction honestly.

### Which theorist predicted what nationalism's victory would produce?

Frantz Fanon predicted it from inside the Algerian struggle: a national bourgeoisie stepping into the departed coloniser's place as intermediary for foreign capital, a liberation party decaying into an instrument of rule, and ethnic or regional appeals replacing the national project it could no longer animate.

Fanon's forecast, set out in the closing movement of his final book and examined in [the companion article on Fanon and Marxism](/2017/02/15/frantz-fanon-and-marxism/), was not a lucky guess but a derivation: he argued that a bourgeoisie in a colonised economy, lacking capital, technology, and a productive base of its own, could not perform the historical role its European counterpart had performed, and would therefore live by brokerage, skimming the transactions between its country and foreign firms, while the party that had led the liberation, emptied of a programme beyond the leader's person, would govern by patronage and by appeals to region and descent when the national idea's credit ran out. The comparison's fourth and fifth columns read, in case after case, like the forecast's verification file, most exactly where nationalism had prevailed unaccompanied. Ghana's intermediary stratum and party-state, Guinea's repressive mobilisation regime, Algeria's front decaying into a property of the army, Tanzania's paternalism, each instance differs in cruelty and in competence, and the structure recurs through the differences. Even the exceptions argue for the derivation's force: Vietnam, where the analysis prevailed, escaped the intermediary pattern and met a different set of failures, economic and military, arising from its doctrine's strengths applied too confidently. A prediction that sorts the failures correctly has earned its place in the finding, and the division accordingly treats Fanon's warning as the finding's corollary: the state inherits the movement's internal balance, and where the balance was all mobilisation, the state will be run by brokers until the brokers are displaced.

The division also explains a feature of the movements that purely doctrinal histories leave mysterious: their unequal longevity under repression. Movements survived defeat in proportion to the independence of their components. The Vietnamese party survived the destruction of the Nghe-Tinh soviets, the partition of its country, and the loss of a generation of southern cadres, because its analysis could be revised, its organisation rebuilt from surviving cells, and its national claim renewed for each new audience; all three elements regenerated because none depended wholly on another. The Algerian front, by contrast, did not survive its victory as a living organisation, because its unity had been the mobilisation's unity, a coalition held together by the war; with the war won, the coalition's components, army, government, wilayas, fell to disputing the inheritance with no shared doctrine to arbitrate them, and the front subsisted as a name the army governed behind. The Indian party survived everything except its reputation: repression, splits, electoral humiliations, and the defection of generations, borne by an organisation whose analysis and discipline were genuine and whose mass connection, after 1942, was never again national. Longevity, the comparison concludes, belongs to movements that own all three functions in working order; movements strong in one or two survive as sects, as memories, or as the decorative presidencies of states other forces run.

A counter-objection from the nationalist side must be answered before the finding can be left standing, because it charges the division with a condescension built into its terms. To say that Marxism supplied the analysis, the objector argues, is to repeat the colonial distribution of intellectual labour in reverse: theory from the Marxist shelf, mere energy from the nation, as though the national movements had not thought for themselves. The charge deserves respect, and the record concedes part of it. The national traditions generated analyses of their own, some of them profound: the Gandhian critique of industrial civilisation, whatever a Marxist makes of its conclusions, was an analysis, worked out over decades and tested against the same colonial economy the communists studied; Nkrumah's consciencism attempted a systematic philosophy; Cabral, the comparison's own hero of analysis, always insisted that his movement's theory arose from its people's practice and would have rejected the suggestion that it was imported with footnotes. What the division claims is narrower than the objector supposes. It claims that, in the cases examined, the specific analytical tasks of explaining imperialism as a system and of designing an organisation to fight it were in fact performed, in the main, with instruments the Marxist tradition had forged, whoever wielded them and whatever else the wielders believed. Nationalism supplied not raw feeling but the movements' sovereign purposes and, in the best cases, moral insights of its own about dignity and self-rule that the Marxist texts of the period conspicuously lacked. The division is between functions within a fusion, not between a thinking head and an unthinking body, and the finding survives its correction: the head, where it analysed empire, was usually working with tools from one particular workshop, and the purposes it served were set by the nation it had joined.

## A worked application: South Africa and the alliance of nation and class

The finding's portability, promised in the lessons, can be tested against the one major case the table omits for reasons of length, and the test is worth performing in the open. South Africa's liberation was conducted by an alliance between a national movement, the African National Congress, founded in 1912 and older than most of the parties in this article, and a communist party that, after its refoundation in 1953, worked inside the alliance as its analytical and organisational cadre. The division of functions the comparison predicts is visible in the alliance's charter documents with unusual nakedness. The communists supplied the alliance's characteristic theoretical product, the analysis of South Africa as colonialism of a special type, a formula holding that the country combined colony and metropole in one territory, the Black population standing to the white state as a colonised people while sharing its borders and its economy; the national movement supplied the mass force, the townships, the unions' rank and file in the alliance's later decades, the exiles and the prisoners whose names became the struggle's banner. Organisation was shared and contested, the armed struggle after 1961 run jointly, the unions rebuilt in the nineteen seventies by a generation in which the two traditions were personally inseparable.

The case also refines the finding at its point of greatest delicacy, the question of who leads. In the alliance's own doctrine, leadership belonged to the national movement, and the communist party formally renounced any competing claim, a subordination its members defended as the lesson of India's error and the colonial world's experience: the party that outruns the nation loses it. When the transition came in the nineteen nineties, negotiated rather than conquered, the alliance's balance produced a state that broke the comparison's table in one significant respect: it did not become a one-party state, its founding election was genuinely contested, and its constitution entrenched the pluralism the other founders had deferred. The comparison can offer partial explanations without claiming the case as a trophy. The movement had never held a state to defend before it held power nationally; its exile had been spent inside plural host societies and international institutions; its mass force, uniquely in this article, included an industrial working class of metropolitan scale whose unions retained an independence no party had digested. The exception therefore grades the finding rather than refuting it: where the balance of elements differed, above all where mobilisation had an organised industrial base with institutions of its own, the successor state differed in precisely the direction the finding's variables predict. A method that can locate its own exception's causes has earned more trust than one that must deny the exception exists.

Zimbabwe, the third wave's other great southern African case, tests the finding from the opposite side and can be run through it briefly. The movement that took power in 1980, ZANU under Robert Mugabe, described itself without embarrassment as Marxist-Leninist, had been armed and trained within the socialist camp's networks, and had fought a long guerrilla war whose political education of the peasantry, conducted in all-night pungwes in the operational zones, was as thorough an exercise in fusing analysis with mobilisation as the period produced anywhere. The finding, applied mechanically, would predict from that fusion a successor state in Vietnam's family, doctrine disciplined and self-correcting. The record delivered something closer to the table's median and below: a negotiated settlement at Lancaster House that deferred the land question, the grievance on which the war had been fought, for a decade of willing-buyer purchase; a one-party state in all but name constructed within years; a campaign of mass violence against the rival movement's heartland in Matabeleland in the nineteen eighties, conducted by a brigade trained abroad and entered here, like all the article's episodes of its kind, without figures and without euphemism; and, a generation later, the land seizures by which an ageing party-state, its analysis long since decayed into liturgy, attempted to renew its mobilisation by executing at last, chaotically and corruptly, the promise on which it had been founded. The case is the comparison's sternest warning against reading the finding as a charm. ZANU possessed the analysis in its own estimation, possessed the organisation, possessed the mass force, and the successor state still travelled the road to the intermediary pattern, because the protections this article identified in the objection's answer, institutional self-correction and a leadership answerable to its base, were dismantled early and deliberately. Doctrine professed is not doctrine operative; the finding grades functions, and Zimbabwe's functions, audited decade by decade rather than at the founding, tell the table's familiar story in a southern African accent.

## What the movements gave back to the theory

The traffic in ideas along the circuits described earlier ran, by the period's second half, at least as strongly from colony to metropole as the reverse, and the comparison should record the return flow, because it is among the strongest evidence that the analysis in the successful cases was a living thing rather than a recitation. Fanon's reworking of the master and slave dialectic through the colonial relation, his insistence that in the colony the economic and the racial are a single infrastructure, was taken up in Paris by the very intellectuals who had imagined themselves the theory's custodians, and quarrelled over for a generation. The Chinese demonstration that a peasant war could take power in a vast agrarian country reorganised strategic debate in every communist party on earth, splitting several. Cabral's reassignment of history's motive force was debated in Havana, in Lisbon, and in the liberation movements' own schools as a correction to be answered, not a slogan to be repeated. The tricontinental idea, that the colonised and formerly colonised world constituted a single theatre with its own priorities, passed into the vocabulary of the metropolitan new lefts of the nineteen sixties, whose activists learned their anti-imperialism from Algiers and Hanoi more than from any European text, and carried the movements' portraits into demonstrations in the imperial capitals themselves.

The return flow had a distorting side the record should also carry. Metropolitan admirers, encountering the movements at the moment of their heroism, frequently imported the romance and left the analysis, celebrating a Vietnamese patience or a Guinean honesty they had no intention of practising, and treating the movements' internal debates, over land reform's methods, over the party's proper limits, as treasonous static interfering with a pure signal. The movements noticed, and resented, the condescension of supporters who required them to remain symbols. That resentment is part of the archive, and it supports this article's method from an unexpected direction: the movements' own best writers insisted on being read as analysts of their own societies, arguing with evidence, correcting themselves in public, and entitled to the same unsentimental examination they applied to their enemies. The comparison has tried to honour that insistence, which is why its judgements have fallen where they have, on friends and on the tradition's heroes as readily as on its embarrassments.

## The post-independence record, stated plainly

The record section of this article exists because the comparative finding could be misread as a celebration, and the record forbids celebration. Across the cases examined, independence was followed in most instances by the conversion of the liberation movement into a one-party state, in all instances by severe economic difficulty, and in several instances by mass violence inflicted by the new order on its own people or arising from its collapse. Vietnam's unified state fought two further wars within four years of unification, drove a substantial part of its southern population to flight by sea, and imprisoned large numbers of the defeated side's personnel in re-education camps. Algeria's post-independence order, after a quarter century of one-party rule, annulled an election and fought a civil conflict in which villagers were massacred by insurgents and by forces of the state in numbers no honest account will fix, every figure being contested and the catastrophe being beyond serious dispute. Guinea-Bissau's liberation state decayed through coup and counter-coup into a civil war that devastated its capital. India's independence arrived with partition's massacres and migrations, violence on a scale that dwarfs most of the century's episodes, though there the new state was not the liberation movement's party-state but a pluralist republic whose communists played no governing part. Ghana and Tanzania escaped mass violence of that order and recorded the milder entries in the ledger: detention without trial, compelled resettlement, economic decline administered by governments that had promised transformation.

### Where does the post-independence record resist both celebration and dismissal?

The record resists celebration because the party-states reproduced too much of the domination they had fought, and resists dismissal because the same states delivered literacy, health, and sovereignty itself to populations the colonial order had kept illiterate, sick, and governed from abroad.

Both halves of that answer are the article's considered position, and each half disciplines the other. The achievements were not incidental decorations on a record of failure: the Vietnamese state's literacy and public health, Algeria's mass schooling of girls the colonial system had left unlettered, Tanzania's primary education and Swahili nation-building, Ghana's universities and dam, were transformations in the lives of millions, purchased by the same organisations whose prisons and decrees this article has recorded. Colonial rule had, in most of these territories, educated a small clerical stratum and governed the rest through chiefs and companies; the post-independence states, whatever else they did, made the whole population their business. Equally, the failures were not the unavoidable price of those achievements, charged by history at a fixed rate. The annulled election, the preventive detention act, the compulsory village, the quota of class enemies were choices, made by identifiable leaderships against available alternatives, sometimes against the explicit warnings of their own traditions' best analysts, Cabral's class suicide and Fanon's pitfalls being warnings filed in advance by men the movements claimed to honour. A comparative history that cannot hold both halves will be conscripted by one of the two mythologies the subject attracts, the romance in which liberation excuses everything done in its name, or the dismissal in which the whole anti-colonial project figures as a swindle practised on the colonised by their own elites. The strongest form of that second mythology is a serious argument and deserves a section of its own, at full strength, before this article answers it.

The achievements, since the record section has invoked them, should be specified with the same care as the failures, because vague credit is as much a failure of method as vague blame. Vietnam's northern state, between 1954 and 1975 and under bombardment for much of it, carried literacy to a population the French had left overwhelmingly unlettered and built a primary health system whose barefoot practitioners became a byword in the international public health of the period; these were not by-products of the war effort but among its stated purposes, pursued by the party's mass organisations in the villages. Algeria's post-independence state schooled a generation of girls at a rate the colonial administration had never attempted and staffed the country's first indigenous cadres of doctors, engineers, and teachers, even as its Arabicisation policies stored up grievances of their own. Tanzania's adult literacy campaigns and universal primary education drive made its population among the most literate in Africa within two decades of independence, a transformation no balance sheet of villagisation's costs should be allowed to erase, and none of the costs should be allowed to excuse. Ghana's independence government founded universities and a research establishment out of proportion to the country's size, and the generation it educated staffed half the continent's institutions. These achievements share a signature the comparison can use: each was delivered by the mobilisation capacities of the founding movements, the party networks, the mass organisations, the habits of collective effort formed in the struggle, converted to civilian purposes. The same organisational inheritance produced the detention acts and the compulsory villages. The record does not permit the reader to keep one and discard the other as inauthentic; it permits only the harder judgement that a single instrument served both, and that what differed, case by case and decade by decade, was whether an analysis still lived inside the instrument capable of asking what it was for.

The wars' aftermaths constitute a slower stratum of the record, less visible in the table's columns and no less real in the successor societies' lives. Vietnam's unified state inherited battlefields saturated with unexploded ordnance and defoliants whose effects on the land and on the bodies of those exposed, Vietnamese and American alike, remained subjects of medical and diplomatic contention for decades; the country that had won its independence as completely as any in this article spent its first peaceful generation clearing its own fields of its liberation's munitions. Algeria's settlement left behind it populations in suspension: the harkis, Algerians who had served as auxiliaries of the French army, abandoned at the ceasefire to reprisals in Algeria or to camps and marginality in the France that had recruited them, their fate unassimilated by either state's official memory for half a century. Guinea-Bissau's war had the unusual distinction of improving some measures of its people's welfare during the fighting, through the liberated zones' clinics and schools, so that independence there meant, in part, the extension of a wartime welfare the peace then failed to fund. The comparison draws no single moral from the aftermaths, but it notes their common property: each successor state's founding generation governed in the presence of wounds the founding had inflicted or received, and the states' authoritarian habits, their suspicion of pluralism, their treatment of veterans' claims and widows' pensions and maimed men's places in the queue, were formed in that presence. A record section that ended with the flags and the constitutions would mistake the movements' story for finished at the moment their difficulties changed form.

The economic strand of the record deserves its own statement, because severe economic difficulty appears in every row of the comparison and a reader is entitled to ask how much of it the movements chose. The inherited structures were genuinely disabling: economies specialised, by colonial design, in the export of one or two commodities whose prices the new states did not control; treasuries empty or indebted; skilled personnel numbered in the hundreds in territories of millions; infrastructure running from mine to port rather than between the country's own regions. Against that inheritance, the new states' choices diverged and the divergence is informative. Vietnam's attempt to collectivise a market society at speed, Tanzania's compulsory villagisation, Algeria's wager that hydrocarbon rents could purchase an industrial base administered by decree, Ghana's prestige projects financed against a falling cocoa price: each was a decision, taken against available alternatives and in several cases against warnings from within the governments' own advisers, and each deepened difficulties that inheritance alone would have made serious. The nineteen eighties then administered a common sentence to nearly all of them, as commodity prices fell, debts contracted in easier years came due, and the international financial institutions imposed structural adjustment programmes that dismantled, in the name of the market, much of what the liberation states had built in the name of the plan. The comparison draws from the economic record the same moral as from the political: the movements' analyses had explained their enemies' economies better than their own. A doctrine formed to anatomise the extraction of surplus by foreign capital proved markedly less reliable as a manual for running a treasury, and the states that fared least badly were those, like Vietnam in its renovation, that permitted the analysis to be corrected by the results.

One summary judgement, against the movements' own stated aims rather than against this article's preferences, may be permitted to close the record. The movements promised three things, in varying orders and proportions: independence, development, and the dignity of self-government, with socialism, where it was promised, as the name for the three fused. Independence was achieved, completely and irreversibly, in every case in this article; the empires that were to last a thousand years are gone, and no serious account can describe as failure a generation that removed them. Development was achieved partially and unevenly, in literacy and health and infrastructure more than in prosperity, and the gap between the promise and the delivery is the economic record already entered. Self-government, the aim the founders ranked first in their rhetoric and last in their practice, was achieved as sovereignty and deferred as democracy, and the deferral, renewed by each successor generation of rulers in the founders' vocabulary, hardened from an expedient into a tradition. A comparative history is not a court, and this article's method has been designed to resist sentencing; but the aims were the movements' own, stated in their own documents, and a record that declined to measure the outcomes against them would be a subtler form of the condescension the nationalist counter-objection warned against. Measured so, the movements succeeded at the aim that required mobilisation, struggled with the aim that required analysis corrected by experience, and failed, in most of their states, at the aim that required the analysis to be turned, permanently and institutionally, upon the rulers themselves. The division with which this article began predicts even that distribution, and the record section rests on it.

## The movements' own defence of the one-party state

Fairness to the record requires one argument to be stated that the record's critics often skip, because it was the argument the founders themselves actually made, in public and in earnest, for the political form this article has entered against them. The case for the single party, as Nkrumah, Nyerere, Toure, and the Vietnamese and Algerian leaderships variously presented it, ran as follows. A poor country, ethnically and regionally divided by boundaries a colonial office had drawn without reference to its peoples, facing tasks of construction that required the mobilisation of its whole population and the deferral of consumption for a generation, could not afford the luxury of organised opposition, which in such conditions would organise itself, inevitably, as the party of a region, a tribe, or a foreign patron. The liberation movement, which alone had united the nation in the struggle, was the nation's trustee for the construction as it had been for the war; competitive politics could resume when the nation had been built, as the movement's theorists were fond of adding, on the far side of development. The argument was not offered cynically by most of those who made it. Nyerere defended the one-party form in essays of genuine philosophical care, arguing that within a single national movement a Tanzanian could enjoy more real deliberation, in village and party meeting, than in the theatrical alternation of two machines in a poor country, and his Tanzania held internal party elections of a competitiveness several formally pluralist states might have envied.

The comparison grades the argument as it grades everything else here, by the record it produced. Its diagnostic half was often right: opposition parties in the successor states did organise regionally and ethnically, did accept foreign patronage, and did in several cases become the vehicles of the fragmentation the founders feared, as Ghana's and Nigeria's early multiparty experiments, and later the warlord politics of states whose parties collapsed entirely, attest. Its prescriptive half failed on the mechanism the founders had not designed for: a trustee that cannot be dismissed ceases to distinguish between the nation's interest and its own, and the movement's internal deliberation, deprived of any external check, decayed in case after case into the ratification of decisions taken elsewhere, in the leader's office or the army's mess. The founders had assumed that the analysis would discipline the trustees from within, that a party educated in the critique of power would police its own. The record's cruellest entries are those in which the assumption was most sincerely made. A comparative history owes the founders the courtesy of stating their argument as they made it, and owes the record the discourtesy of observing that they were wrong about the one thing they could not afford to be wrong about: themselves.

## The modernising objection at full strength

The objection this article must now face holds that everything said so far about analysis mistakes the doctrine's function. On this account, Marxism in the anti-colonial movements functioned principally as a modernising ideology for new elites: a creed whose real work was to legitimate the rule of an educated stratum, to mobilise populations for state-led development, and to dress the building of national economies in the language of liberation. Its adoption, the objection continues, is therefore better explained by its utility for state-building than by the truth or power of its analysis of exploitation, which the elites in question neither needed nor, in most cases, seriously applied to the societies they came to govern, least of all to their own position in them.

The objection in this form descends from an argument made about an earlier theatre by the economic historian Alexander Gerschenkron, whose essays collected as Economic Backwardness in Historical Perspective, published in 1962, remain its most rigorous statement. Gerschenkron argued that industrialisation in countries arriving late to it differed systematically from the pioneer case: the more backward the economy, the more the process depended on institutional substitutes for the prerequisites it lacked, banks doing the work of accumulated capital, then the state doing the work of both, and the more it required an ideology of industrialisation, a systematic faith that could summon the sacrifices development demanded and sanctify the authority directing them. Saint-Simonianism had served France in that office, nationalism Germany, and Marxism Russia, where, on his account, the doctrine's hold on the stratum that made the revolution is unintelligible unless its function is seen: it focused a backward country's educated class on industrial development as history's command, converting a programme of forced modernisation into a world-historical vocation. Transposed to the colonial world after 1945, the argument requires little adjustment. The new states' elites, clerks, teachers, officers, lawyers, the only stratum the colonial order had educated, needed a doctrine that promised their societies could skip the queue of development, that vested direction of the skipping in a party they would staff, that explained their countries' poverty by external theft rather than internal deficiency, and that cast their own ascent to office as the people's liberation. Marxism, and its diluted African and Asian variants, fitted every clause of the specification. On the objection's reading, that fit, and not the analysis of exploitation, which would have indicted the new stratum's own accumulations soon enough, is what the adoption records. The doctrines' subsequent career is then exactly what the objection predicts: retained as legitimation, celebrated in textbooks and party schools, and quietly set aside wherever its categories threatened the stratum that recited them.

The objection has a second, sharper form that comes not from the academy but from inside the movement's own history, and it should be stated with its proponent named, since he paid for the statement with his career in the movement he accused. George Padmore, the Trinidadian organiser who had run the Communist International's work among Black workers and colonial peoples from Hamburg and Moscow, broke with the International in the early nineteen thirties over its subordination of anti-colonial struggle to Soviet diplomacy, and spent the remaining decades of his life building, from London, the Pan-African network that educated Nkrumah and half the anglophone African leadership. Padmore's charge was precise: the world movement's centre had treated the colonies as counters in a European game, restraining colonial militancy when Moscow courted London and Paris, licensing it when the alliances shifted, and disciplining colonial communists who objected. The Indian party's reversals of 1939 and 1941 are the charge's documentary proof, and Padmore's own alternative, an anti-colonialism answerable to the colonised alone, staffed by the network he gathered around himself in London, is the historical ancestor of the very movements this article files in its middle category. The objection from Padmore cuts deeper than Gerschenkron's, because it does not dispute the analysis's power; it disputes the analysis's ownership, arguing that the instruments the movements used had been forged for purposes not their own and bore the marks. The comparison's answer is the one the cases have already given. Instruments can be seized. The movements that made the analysis at home, in Guinea-Bissau, in Vietnam's long apprenticeship, in the Algerian front's borrowed repertoire reshaped by its own congresses, used the instruments for their own purposes and corrected them in use; the movements that held them on loan, as India did in 1942, discovered the loan's terms at the worst moment. Padmore's break is therefore less a refutation of the finding than its earliest formulation by a participant: the division that mattered was between movements that owned their analysis and movements that rented it.

### Did the movements' own analyses ever run against their elites?

They did, in documented instances this comparison has already recorded: land reform pursued against the landlord class in Vietnam at real cost to the party's comfort, Cabral's demand of class suicide addressed to his own stratum, leadership codes against accumulation imposed in Tanzania, the self-management seizure of estates in Algeria before the state tamed it.

The answer to the objection begins by granting its territory and then contesting its completeness. What the objection cannot explain is the residue of cases in which the analysis bit the hand that held it. A purely legitimating creed is not normally turned, by its own priests, against the priesthood's material interests; yet the Vietnamese party expropriated the class through which rural administration had run and publicly rectified its own campaign's crimes at the height of its prestige, the PAIGC organised its war so that cadres ate what peasants ate and answered to village assemblies, and Nyerere, borrowing the analysis without the doctrine, used it to forbid his own officials the accumulation their offices invited. These episodes do not refute the objection; the same decades supply it with ample confirmations, and this article concedes explicitly that it describes a substantial part of the record, arguably the larger part of the post-independence record taken as a whole. Ghana's party-state reciting socialism while detaining its critics, Guinea's mobilisation regime, the Algerian front's decay into a property of its officer corps, belong to the objection's evidence, and the analysis and mobilisation division predicts them: where nationalism prevailed, the borrowed analysis had no institutional defender against the stratum it should have indicted. The objection and the finding therefore divide the record between them rather than contesting all of it. Where the analysis was carried by a party that kept it as a discipline, it sometimes acted against elite interest, which utility alone cannot explain. Where it arrived as vocabulary in a nationalist movement, it behaved as the objection says, as the modernising faith of the stratum that recited it. Gerschenkron's argument, forged for Russia's industrialisation, thus survives its transposition in a qualified form: it explains why the doctrine was available and attractive to new elites everywhere, and it does not explain why, in a minority of movements, the doctrine was allowed to rule its carriers.

The concession should be made with the same precision as the answer, since a vague concession is a way of conceding nothing. Counting the table's rows by their destinations, the analysis ended as the legitimation of a ruling stratum, recited and inert, in the majority of the successor states the article has examined: in Ghana after the founder's consolidation, in Guinea under its mobilisation regime, in Algeria after the army's inheritance, in Tanzania in the milder key of a paternalism its founder half-recognised in himself. The exceptions are two, and their protections were different and both fragile. Vietnam's party was protected by its institutional continuity, a leadership renewed through war, procedures of criticism and rectification old enough to be habits, and a doctrine internalised by cadres who had joined it when it promised prison rather than office; the protection held imperfectly, failed over the collectivisation of the south, and was renewed, at real political cost, by the renovation. Guinea-Bissau was protected by a person: by Cabral's authority and by the village answerability he had institutionalised while he lived to enforce it. The protection did not survive his assassination by seven years, and the coup of 1980, carried out by the movement's own soldiers against the movement's own president, is the comparison's bleakest single confirmation of the objection's mechanism, arriving in the case where the analysis had been most self-consciously defended against it. The division of the record therefore stands as follows. The modernising objection describes the default outcome, what happened wherever no specific protection intervened; the analysis's capacity to rule its carriers was the achievement, always local, always maintained against entropy, and in one case extinguished with the man who embodied it. A reader who retains only that sentence from this section will have retained the objection's due and its limit in their correct proportions.

## The control cases: insurgencies that failed

A comparative finding assembled only from movements that reached power invites the suspicion of selection, and the suspicion is answered by the cases in which Marxist-led insurgencies did not. Malaya is the most instructive failure. The Malayan Communist Party, forged in the anti-Japanese resistance, emerged from the war with arms, prestige, and the strongest union movement in the peninsula, and in 1948 launched an armed struggle against the returning British that lasted twelve years and ended in defeat. The party's analysis of Malaya's colonial economy was competent; its organisation was formidable; its jungle army survived a decade against a major counterinsurgency. What it never possessed was the national mass force, because the party was identified, in its membership and its language, overwhelmingly with the peninsula's Chinese minority, while the Malay majority, whose nationalism the British cultivated and eventually satisfied with an independence settlement under conservative Malay leadership, regarded the insurgents as a foreign body. The British, for their part, prosecuted the war with a political intelligence the French in Algeria never matched, conceding the national demand to moderates while isolating the party that had fused its cause to a fraction of the nation. Malaya is the analysis and mobilisation division's cleanest negative proof: analysis and organisation at full strength, mobilisation confined to one community, and the outcome decided by the missing element exactly as the finding predicts.

The Malayan defeat repays a closer look at its mechanics, because the counterinsurgency that won is itself part of the comparison's evidence about mobilisation. The British response, under the Briggs Plan and its successors, resettled half a million rural Chinese into wired new villages, severing the guerrillas from the food, money, and intelligence of the squatter settlements on the jungle's edge, while simultaneously promising and then delivering independence to a Malay-led government that could claim the nationalist succession the party had forfeited. The strategy's genius lay in fighting on the comparison's own terrain: it conceded the national demand to a rival supplier while starving the party of the population on which its organisation fed. The guerrilla army, unbeaten in the purely military sense for years, withered into a border existence in southern Thailand, signing its peace only in 1989, four decades after the rising. Chin Peng, the party's wartime resistance hero and postwar leader, ended as an exile petitioner asking to die in a country whose national story had been written without him. The case therefore contributes more than a negative instance. It demonstrates that the colonial power, too, could learn to grade a movement by its functions, and that the final colonial wars were lost and won as much on the mobilisation variable as on any question of doctrine or firepower, which is the finding's claim stated from the other side of the hill.

The Philippine Huk rebellion tells the same story in a different register. The Hukbalahap, the anti-Japanese army in which communists and socialists held leading positions, emerged from the occupation as the strongest force in Central Luzon's countryside, with a programme of land reform and a record of resistance no rival could match. The restored republic, backed by the United States, refused its elected representatives their seats, outlawed the movement, and fought it to defeat by the mid nineteen fifties, combining military pressure with a reform programme designed to drain its grievances. The Huks' analysis of Philippine landlordism was vindicated by nearly every subsequent study; their organisation survived years of repression; what they could not do was convert a regional peasant movement into a national one in an archipelago of languages and islands, against a state that held the cities, the church's allegiance, and the patronage of the departing and returning superpower. The control cases discipline the comparison's pride in both directions. They show the finding operating in defeat as in victory, and they remind the reader that the successful cases in the table were selected by history's brutality as well as by this article's design: for every movement that fused its elements into a state, another fused them no less sincerely and was destroyed, and the difference lay less in doctrine than in whether the nation, whole and mobilisable, was available to be fused with.

## What the tradition learned from its own cases

The tradition that emerged from these cases was not the tradition that had entered them, and the lessons it drew, in its journals, its party schools, and its subsequent practice, can be enumerated without pretending they were drawn unanimously. The first lesson concerned the peasantry. A movement family that had begun with a doctrine in which the rural population figured as a sack of potatoes, politically formless, ended by treating the peasantry as the principal revolutionary class of the colonised world and by revising its theory of agency accordingly; Cabral's Havana correction is the lesson's most rigorous statement, and the Chinese experience, whose full treatment belongs to the companion [Marxism in China complete guide](/2018/05/01/marxism-in-china-complete-guide/), is its largest demonstration. The second lesson concerned sequence: the national question could not be postponed until after a social revolution that, in a colony, had no social base from which to start, and later movements, in southern Africa and in Latin America, built the fusion into their founding documents rather than discovering it under fire. The third lesson ran from politics into economics. The experience of formal independence without economic sovereignty, which Nkrumah named, was developed by the tradition's economists into the research programme of [dependency theory explained](/2016/07/01/dependency-theory-explained/), the analysis of underdevelopment as something produced by the world economy's structure rather than merely inherited from a pre-modern past, and that programme carried the anti-colonial analysis into universities on every continent long after the movements themselves had faded. The fourth lesson was the one the movements were slowest to learn, because it concerned their own form. Fanon's pitfalls and Cabral's class suicide had both warned that the party of liberation becomes, unexamined, the state of the brokers; the post-independence record taught the warning to everyone else, and the tradition's later internal literature, its debates over the party, over one-party rule, over the national bourgeoisie, is unintelligible without the cases in this article standing behind it as the evidence no one could argue away.

The afterlife of the cases in later movements confirms that the lessons were received as this article has stated them. The liberation movements of southern Africa in the nineteen seventies, in Angola, Mozambique, and Zimbabwe, were founded by men and women who had studied the earlier record as a syllabus of warnings: they built the fusion of national and social programmes into their founding documents, they theorised the peasantry's role from the first day rather than discovering it under fire, and they quoted Cabral, their near contemporary and fellow Portuguese-colonial subject, more than any metropolitan authority. Their post-independence records, civil wars fuelled by the Cold War's proxies, one-party states, economic collapse and eventual accommodation with the market order they had fought, extend the comparison's table rather than overturning it, and the South African movement's long internal argument about the ordering of national and social demands, conducted in explicit dialogue with the Vietnamese and Indian experiences, shows the cases functioning as the tradition's case law. In the universities, meanwhile, the anti-colonial archive became foundational for whole disciplines: the dependency school's economics, the historiography of empire written from the colonised side, and the later postcolonial studies that defined themselves partly against Marxism while standing on ground its analysts had cleared. A tradition may be judged by the arguments it makes possible even among its opponents, and by that measure the anti-colonial cases remain generative long after the states they founded disappointed their founders.

One further lesson belongs to the record because the movements' own historians drew it with unusual unanimity, and it concerns the writing of history itself. The anti-colonial movements were, almost without exception, betrayed by their official memories: foundation myths that airbrushed the coalition's compromises, martyrologies that converted contested decisions into sacred ones, archives sealed by the party-states that inherited them. The comparative method of this article, grading movements by function, separating doctrine from organisation, refusing both romance and dismissal, was developed by scholars working against those official memories, often at personal cost, from within the societies concerned. It is owed to them to end the enumeration of lessons with theirs: that a liberation movement's own account of itself is evidence about its legitimation needs before it is evidence about its history, and that the analysis and mobilisation division is worth the reader's trust only insofar as it was built, as it has been here, from the records the movements could not retrospectively edit.

### What survives of the comparison for a reader meeting new movements?

The separation of variables survives: a reader who asks of any movement, separately, what supplies its analysis, what supplies its organisation, and what supplies its mass force, and who then asks which of the three is positioned to inherit the state, possesses the comparison's whole method in portable form.

That portability is the article's final claim for its finding, and it is a claim about method rather than about allegiance. The cases are closed; the empires they fought are gone; the parties they founded are transformed, dissolved, or embalmed. What is not closed is the analytical situation the cases first displayed, in which a doctrine, an organisation, and a mass sentiment combine in a movement whose subsequent history will be governed by their internal balance. Learning to see the three components separately, to notice when the analysis is a discipline and when it is a decoration, and to predict the state from the movement's balance rather than from its proclamations, is the use to which the anti-colonial cases can still be put by readers who owe the movements nothing and fear them not at all. It is also, in the end, the only tribute the comparison pays that the movements' own best analysts would have recognised: Cabral insisted that a people in struggle is owed the truth about itself, told without lies and without easy victories, and this article has tried to conduct its comparison in that spirit throughout.

## Reading the cases against each other

Three controlled comparisons, in which two cases resemble each other in most respects and differ in the one the finding cares about, let a sceptical reader test the analysis and mobilisation division rather than merely receive it. Vietnam against Algeria isolates the variable of leadership. Both movements fought long wars against France, both drew on peasantries radicalised by colonial land policy, both operated in the same decades under the same international conditions. One was led by a communist party that had spent fifteen years preparing its fusion of programmes; the other was founded in a night by nationalists determined that no existing party would lead it. The states that emerged differ as the finding predicts: Vietnam's party, whatever its errors, retained an institutional life of its own and a doctrine against which its performance could be measured and, at intervals, corrected; Algeria's state belonged from birth to the armed institution that had won the frontier war, and no doctrine existed inside it with standing to call the rulers to account. Leadership, the comparison concludes, is not a label but a mechanism: it determines whether the movement carries into power an instrument of self-criticism or only a memory of struggle.

India against Guinea-Bissau isolates the variable of where the analysis was made. The Indian party possessed the colonial world's most developed Marxist scholarship and subordinated it, at the decisive moment, to a strategy decided abroad; the Guinean movement possessed a fraction of the scholarship and made its strategy at home, in continuous consultation with the villages it organised. The outcomes invert the obvious expectation. The brilliantly analysed case produced the tradition's most damaging single decision; the modestly resourced case produced the period's most original theory and a decade of liberated-zone governance that visitors of every political colour described as the most impressive in Africa. Scale explains part of the difference, a subcontinent is not a small coastal territory, and India's communists faced a national movement of genius in Gandhi's Congress, for which no Guinean equivalent existed. But the residue supports the finding's quietest implication: analysis functions as a discipline only where the movement owns it, and an analysis held on loan from a foreign centre can be recalled at the moment it is needed most.

Ghana against Tanzania isolates the variable of sincerity. Both governments were led by intellectuals of genuine conviction, both proclaimed socialisms rooted in national traditions, both built one-party states, and both ended in economic difficulty. They differ in what the proclaimed doctrine was permitted to do to the proclaimers. Nyerere's leadership code forbade his officials the accumulation their offices invited, his party's declaration bound the leader as publicly as the led, and his eventual retirement, voluntary and on schedule, was an act almost without precedent among the comparison's founders. Nkrumah's Ghana moved the other way, from a mass party of real vitality toward a cult of the founder in which the doctrine's function narrowed to acclamation. The comparison does not sentimentalise the difference: Tanzania's order was coercive in its villagisation and poor in its results, and honesty about that belongs to the record already entered. What the paired cases show is that even within the category of movements Marxism merely informed, the borrowed analysis could be turned, by a leadership willing to be bound by it, into a restraint on power, and that where no such willingness existed, the same vocabulary decorated power's expansion. Sincerity, in the comparison's austere vocabulary, names the willingness of a leadership to let its doctrine cost it something.

## The struggle over memory in the successor states

The last theatre in which the comparison's tensions played out was the successor states' own teaching of the struggle that had founded them, and it deserves a section because a movement's historiography is its analysis turned upon itself, with the same temptations and the same escapes. Vietnam's official history institutionalised the fusion: the party's museum and textbook narrative presents the national and social revolutions as a single river, and the land reform's victims, the southern front's non-communist patriots, and the postwar exodus occupy in it the margins their counterparts occupy in this article's fuller record. Algeria's state built its legitimacy on a foundation myth of a million and a half martyrs and a united people in arms, a narrative whose figure is contested in exactly the manner this article's protocol describes, and whose function was precise: to vest the front, and behind the front the army, with the whole nation's sacrifice as its inheritance, and to render the revolution's internal plurality, its Messalists and communists, its Berber particularities and its women with their revoked emancipation, invisible in the founding picture. Tanzania taught ujamaa's history as a national epic whose failures belonged to external forces and falling prices; Ghana, having overthrown its founder, oscillated for decades between his demonisation and his rehabilitation, the state unable to decide whether its founding father was its glory or its warning.

Against the official memories stands the counter-archive the movements themselves inadvertently created: the memoirs of the sidelined, the exile presses, the veterans' associations arguing over pensions and precedence, the women who began, in the decades after independence, to write the chapters the foundation myths had omitted. The comparison has drawn on that counter-archive throughout, and its findings, the separation of doctrine from organisation, the refusal to grade movements by their proclamations, are in large part the counter-archive's own methods formalised. There is a fitting circularity in ending the substantive history there. The analysis and mobilisation division was not invented in a library; it was learned by participants whom the fusion had disappointed, written down by the sidelined and the self-critical, by Padmore breaking with his International and by the veterans of fronts who discovered which of their movement's elements had inherited the state. This article has systematised their lesson and tested it against the cases. The test, as the next section states plainly, has limits, and the lesson survives them.

## Comparison as method: what the cases have and have not shown

A closing word on method is owed, because the article's finding is a comparative instrument and its limits are part of its content. The comparison has shown, across six principal cases and a penumbra of control cases, edge cases, and second-wave repetitions, that the distribution of functions inside anti-colonial movements followed a pattern regular enough to name: analysis and organisation travelling from the Marxist tradition, mass force arising from the nation, and the successor state reproducing the balance. It has shown the pattern operating in defeat as well as victory, in Malaya's jungle as in Hanoi's ministries, and it has shown the pattern's two great internal warnings, Fanon's on the intermediary bourgeoisie and Cabral's on the leadership stratum, to have been filed in advance by participants and verified by the record they warned against. Against the modernising objection it has held a divided verdict: the doctrine plainly served new elites as an ideology of state-building across much of the record, and plainly, in a minority of movements, disciplined those elites against their own interests, a residue the objection cannot digest. Against the romantic counter-claim it has held the record: one-party states, economic failures, annulled elections, mass violence, the subordination of the women and workers who had carried the struggle, entered without euphemism and weighed against achievements entered without irony.

What the comparison has not shown should be stated with equal clarity. It has not shown that the pattern was inevitable, since its own control cases include movements that refused the doctrine and won, and parties that held the doctrine and lost. It has not distributed moral verdicts on the movements as wholes, because its method, grading functions rather than souls, is designed to prevent exactly that distribution, and readers who want a ranking of heroes and villains will have to construct it from the materials supplied rather than receive it. It has not treated the cases' internal complexities exhaustively: each of its sections could be, and in the series' companion articles several have been, expanded into a study of its own, and the compression a comparison requires is a cost the article has paid openly, marking with links the places where fuller treatments exist. Above all, it has not claimed that the pattern governs movements outside its period and conditions. The empires whose fall it narrates are gone; the party form it anatomises has mutated; whether the division between analysis, organisation, and mobilisation still sorts the movements of a different century is a question the method equips a reader to ask, not one this article presumes to answer in advance of the evidence.

A note on sources and confidence belongs to the same accounting. The article's factual spine, the characters of the movements, the dates of the addresses, congresses, and reversals, the wartime lines and their timing, the assassination of 1973, rests on the standard scholarly record of the period, and where that record is contested the article has said so in terms, most strictly under its rule that no casualty figure for any anti-colonial war or post-independence episode is stated anywhere in its pages. Colonial-era terminology in the sources consulted has been characterised rather than reproduced, in accordance with the policy governing this cluster of the series, and accounts of violence have been kept accurate and specific without gratuitous detail. The interpretive claims, by contrast, are the article's own and are labelled as such at the points where they are made: the analysis and mobilisation division is a finding argued from the cases, not a datum extracted from them, and the grading of the modernising objection, divided between the finding and the objection as the evidence divides, is a judgement for which the article accepts responsibility rather than a consensus it reports. Readers who dispute the judgement now possess, in the case sections, the materials from which to dispute it on the evidence, which is the most a comparative article can offer and the least this one intended.

## For students and exam candidates

Your examination risk on this material is the blended answer: a narrative that marches from country to country retelling independence stories without ever comparing them. Examiners reward the comparative move, and this article has built its answer in that shape for you. Learn to state the analysis and mobilisation division in one breath, to support it with one row of the case comparison stated precisely, Vietnam for the fusion, Algeria for the absent leadership, India for the imported analysis, and to complicate it with Fanon's prediction and Cabral's correction before you conclude. ## For teachers and seminar leaders

The case comparison is designed to be lifted whole into a seminar exercise. Give each group one row with the final column removed, and require them to argue the verdict from the movement's founding documents and wartime record before the completed table is revealed; the disagreements that surface, most reliably over Guinea-Bissau and Ghana, are the lesson, since they force students to say what evidence would distinguish an analysis that disciplined a movement from a vocabulary that decorated one. A productive second session sets the two great warnings against each other, Fanon on the national bourgeoisie beside Cabral on class suicide, and asks which of them better predicted the Algerian as against the Vietnamese outcome, and why the same writer could be right about a movement he served and a movement he never saw. Close by returning to the definition: students who can explain why the doctrine had to be rebuilt for agrarian societies have understood the topic's engine, and those who cannot have merely memorised its itinerary.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The single strongest recommendation this batch of articles has to make is short enough to read in an evening: Cabral's Havana address of 1966, the text in which the motive force of history is reassigned from class struggle to the development of the productive forces and the revolutionary petty bourgeoisie is instructed in its own abolition. Read it against the grain of its usual citation, not as a period piece of liberation rhetoric but as a working recalibration of a theory against fieldwork its author had personally conducted, and then test the recalibration against the PAIGC's liberated-zone institutions, which are documented in the movement's own reports. Researchers coming from the separate national histories will find the comparative frame most useful as a generator of anomalies: the Indian party's wartime legality, the Algerian front's organisational borrowing without doctrinal borrowing, and Cape Verde's divergence from Guinea-Bissau after a shared founding are the points where the frame creaks, and therefore where new archival work will bite hardest.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Why did Marxism appeal to anti-colonial movements?

It explained the colonial condition as a system with causes rather than as a natural hierarchy of peoples. A doctrine that traced the colony's poverty to the structure of its insertion into a world economy, that identified the interests served by foreign rule, and that treated empire as a historical arrangement certain to pass gave colonised intellectuals both an account of their situation and a ground for expecting victory. It also arrived with practical assets no rival framework bundled together: a theory of the party capable of surviving illegality, a network of allies and publishers across continents, and a vocabulary in which a Vietnamese, Algerian, or Guinean struggle could be stated as an instance of a universal conflict rather than a local grievance begging for a hearing.

### Q: Were anti-colonial movements actually Marxist?

Only a minority were Marxist in the full sense of being led by parties committed to the doctrine. Vietnam's struggle was directed by a communist party from foundation to victory, and China's followed a comparable path. The Algerian war, by contrast, was commanded by a nationalist front inside which communists served as individuals, and Africa's best-known liberation governments mostly worked with borrowed concepts under doctrines of their own coinage. The accurate general statement is therefore about functions rather than labels: Marxist analysis and Marxist organisation appear in nearly every major case, Marxist leadership in few, and the mass energy of the movements came overwhelmingly from the national desire to end foreign rule.

### Q: Who was Amilcar Cabral?

Amilcar Cabral was the founder and leader of the African Party for the Independence of Guinea and Cape Verde, the movement that fought Portuguese rule in Guinea-Bissau from 1963. Trained as an agronomist and employed in the colonial agricultural service, he knew the colony's peoples and crops at first hand, and he built the liberated zones' assemblies, schools, clinics, and stores on that knowledge. His theoretical writings, above all an address delivered in Havana in 1966, recalibrated Marxist theory for societies without developed classes, and his demand that the revolutionary petty bourgeoisie commit class suicide became a classic statement of the leadership problem in liberation movements. He was assassinated in January 1973, months before his movement declared independence.

### Q: What is class suicide?

Class suicide is Amilcar Cabral's term for the act he demanded of the educated stratum that led his movement. The revolutionary petty bourgeoisie, clerks, teachers, officials, and professionals formed under colonialism, stood to inherit the colonial state at independence and to constitute itself as a new possessing class living by brokerage between its country and foreign capital. Cabral argued that this stratum must instead renounce that future, merge its conditions of life with those of the peasantry it claimed to serve, and allow the struggle to remake it. The demand was not primarily moral. It identified the precise mechanism by which a liberation movement's leadership becomes the thing it fought, and proposed organised self-abolition as the countermeasure.

### Q: What did the Indian communists do during Quit India?

When the Congress movement launched the Quit India campaign in August 1942, demanding immediate British withdrawal, the Communist Party of India opposed the campaign and remained legal while Congress leaders were imprisoned and the uprising was suppressed. The party had adopted a people's war line after Germany invaded the Soviet Union in 1941, reasoning that the conflict had become a war against fascism whose outcome outweighed every other struggle, and that resistance to Britain obstructed the Allied effort. It used its legality to build trade unions, peasant organisations, and famine relief, and its membership grew. The price was a durable reputation, among the generation that had filled the prisons, for having stood aside from the nation's decisive confrontation with its rulers.

### Q: Was the Algerian independence movement Marxist?

No. The National Liberation Front that launched the insurrection of 1954 and led the war to the Evian Accords was a nationalist coalition, formed by militants who considered the existing organisations, the communists included, unfit to begin the fight. The Algerian Communist Party had a real history in the colony's labour movement, but it had subordinated independence to metropolitan priorities for decades, was banned by France in 1955, and entered the struggle only by dissolving its independent role, its members serving under front discipline. Marxist ideas circulated powerfully inside the revolution, in its diplomacy, its press, and the writings of Frantz Fanon, and its organisational methods owed much to the communist repertoire. Leadership, programme, and legitimacy, however, were nationalist throughout.

### Q: What happened after independence in these countries?

The trajectories shared a discouraging family resemblance beneath real differences. Most of the movements converted themselves into one-party states in which elections ratified rather than decided. All of the new states encountered severe economic difficulty, whether from inherited structures, collapsing commodity prices, war debts, or the costs of the development models they imposed. Several descended into mass violence: wars on Vietnam's borders and repression at home, an annulled election and civil conflict in Algeria, coups and civil war in Guinea-Bissau. Against that ledger stand achievements the same states delivered, mass literacy, public health, universities, and sovereignty itself, which is why the record in this article is presented as a subject for analysis rather than for either celebration or dismissal.

### Q: Did nationalism or Marxism prevail in these movements?

Both prevailed, at different functions, and the interesting question is which prevailed in the state. Nationalism prevailed as the mobilising force almost everywhere, since the promise of ending foreign rule moved populations as no doctrine could. Marxism prevailed as analysis and as organisational form, shaping how the movements understood their enemy and how they structured their parties. In the post-independence states the balance varied. Where a party kept the analysis as a working discipline, as in Vietnam, policy retained a capacity for self-correction, however costly its errors. Where nationalism had absorbed the movement entire, the state tended to reproduce the pattern Fanon predicted, an intermediary elite governing through a party that had become an instrument of rule.

### Q: What is African socialism?

African socialism was the family of doctrines under which several of the continent's first independence governments, in Tanzania, Ghana, Senegal, Guinea, and elsewhere, proposed to build socialism without passing through the Marxist sequence. Its shared premise was that African societies possessed a communal tradition, located in the village and the extended family, which could be modernised directly into a socialist order, and that class division was a foreign import their nations could refuse to develop. The variants differed sharply in practice: Nyerere's ujamaa in Tanzania was earnest, developmental, and eventually coercive in its villagisation campaigns; Senghor's Senegalese version was cultural and pro-French; Toure's Guinea was mobilisational and repressive. All borrowed Marxist organisation while declining Marxist class analysis.

### Q: What lessons did the tradition draw from these cases?

Four lessons recur in the tradition's later literature. The peasantry, dismissed in the classical texts as politically formless, had to be recognised as the principal revolutionary force of the colonised world, and the theory of agency revised to match. The national question could not be postponed behind a social revolution that had no base from which to begin. Formal independence without economic transformation reproduced domination in a new form, a lesson developed into dependency theory. And the liberation party itself had to be treated as a problem, since the record showed it decaying into an instrument of rule wherever the warnings issued by Fanon and Cabral, from inside the movements, went unheeded.

### Q: How does the analysis and mobilisation division grade a movement's Marxism?

It grades functions rather than professions of faith. A movement is asked three separate questions. What supplies its understanding of the enemy and of the society it hopes to build? What supplies its organisational skeleton, its discipline, recruitment, and survival under repression? And what actually moves masses of people to risk themselves in its cause? A movement may answer Marxism to the first two and the nation to the third, which is the most common pattern in the cases, or Marxism to all three, or to none. Grading by function prevents the two standard errors: crediting a movement as Marxist because its pamphlets are, and dismissing the doctrine's role because the crowds marched under a national flag.

### Q: What should a student separate between a movement's doctrine and its organisational form?

A student should separate what a movement believed from the machine it built to act on the belief, because the two travelled independently throughout the anti-colonial world. The centralised, disciplined, cell-based party was adopted by movements whose doctrine was Marxist, by movements whose doctrine was a national socialism, and by movements whose public idiom was primarily cultural or religious, since the form solved problems of illegality and coordination that every underground faces. Doctrine, conversely, circulated as texts and slogans far beyond any party's control. An examination answer that infers doctrine from organisation, or organisation from doctrine, will misdescribe most of the century's movements; the separation is the first analytical habit this topic requires.

### Q: Why did nationalism supply the mass force more often than doctrine did?

Because mobilisation runs on materials doctrine cannot manufacture: language, memory, humiliation, kinship, and the promise of a specific people's deliverance from specific foreign rulers. Marxist analysis could explain to a peasant why the tax collector and the landlord belonged to one structure, and in the strongest movements it did exactly that. But the willingness to endure a war, to feed guerrillas at the price of reprisals, to send sons and daughters into the maquis, was drawn from the conviction that the nation itself was at stake and that its subjection was an intolerable wrong. Doctrine is portable across borders because it is abstract; mass force is local because it is made of lives, and in the twentieth century the nation was the form in which local lives could be assembled at the necessary scale.

### Q: How does the case comparison order the movements?

It orders them by the internal balance of the elements each movement combined rather than by geography or chronology. At one end stands Vietnam, where a Marxist party led the struggle and fused the national and social programmes most completely. Next stands Guinea-Bissau, where Marxist analysis guided a movement whose leader subjected the analysis itself to revision. India and Algeria occupy the middle in opposite ways, a Marxist party marginal to a national movement it misjudged in the first, a nationalist front borrowing Marxist methods in the second. Ghana and Tanzania close the order, governments armed with borrowed concepts under doctrines of their own invention. The ordering is an analytical instrument: it lets a reader predict a state's character from the balance its founding movement struck.

### Q: What separates a Marxist-led movement from a movement Marxism informed?

Leadership is the separator, and it shows in who sets the programme and whose categories govern decisions when goals conflict. In a Marxist-led movement, a party holding the doctrine commanded the struggle, subordinated alliances to its strategy, and carried its programme into the state it founded, as the Vietnamese party did through two wars and into unification. In a movement Marxism informed, the analysis, the vocabulary, and often the organisational repertoire were taken up by a leadership answerable to a different loyalty, usually the nation, which retained the right to set doctrine aside whenever the two pulled apart, as the Algerian front did throughout its war. The distinction predicts a great deal: informed movements produced states in which socialism was a policy of the government, revisable at will, while led movements produced states in which it was the regime's constitution.
