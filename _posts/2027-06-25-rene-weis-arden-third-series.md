Every generation of readers meets *Romeo and Juliet* inside an edition, and the edition decides far more than spelling. It decides which of the rival early texts supplies the words, how much of the lost first quarto is allowed back into the play, where the notes push the eye, and whose story the introduction says the tragedy is. Since 2012 the scholarly meeting place for the play has been the Arden Third Series volume edited by Rene Weis, Professor of English at University College London, and the volume earns that standing by two acts of restitution that no predecessor attempted together. Weis treats the first quarto of 1597, for two centuries dismissed as a stolen and garbled text, as a document with authority of its own, the record of the play in performance, and he builds from it freely. At the same time his introduction argues, in the publisher's summary of his case, that the play is ultimately Juliet's, and it reorganizes the whole account of the tragedy around her. Those two restitutions, of a text and of a heroine, are the edition's signature, and they survive the sharpest review the volume received. Emma Smith, writing in *The Review of English Studies*, called the book Janus-faced, modern in its feeling for performance and afterlife, old-fashioned in its eclectic text. The charge is accurate as description and wrong as verdict. Weis's eclecticism is not nostalgia. It is the working method of an editor who had already spent a career refusing to let one witness silence another, and its inconsistencies, which are real, sit at the level of single lines rather than at the level of the edition's design.

![The Arden Third Series Romeo and Juliet edited by Rene Weis - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-55.webp)

The verdict argued through this article is therefore double. As a reading text and a teaching instrument, Weis's edition is the most complete single volume the play has had, because it joins a Q2-based text saturated with the first quarto's stage life to an introduction that gives students the play's poetry, its four-day clock, its stage and screen afterlife, and a facsimile of the 1597 quarto itself in an appendix. As a piece of bibliographical reasoning, it is less systematic than its own learning deserves, and at three or four famous places its editor intervenes in the words without printing the argument that would justify the intervention. Both halves of that verdict matter. An edition is the form in which scholarship reaches readers who will never open a textual monograph, and Weis understood the responsibility in those terms. What follows examines the editor he was before this play, the textual policy he brought to it, the decisions where his judgment is most exposed, the introduction's Juliet, the clock he builds under the action, his handling of sources and commentary, the afterlife he records, and the disagreement among his reviewers, before the evidence is gathered into the InsightCrunch Weis profile.

## Rene Weis Before Verona: The Lear Editor and the Biographer

Weis came to the Veronese lovers from the most textually divided play in the canon. His *King Lear: A Parallel Text Edition*, published by Longman in 1993 and reissued in a second edition in 2009, prints the 1608 quarto *History of King Lear* and the 1623 Folio *Tragedy of King Lear* on facing pages in modern spelling, each fully annotated, and its notes, by the edition's own declared policy, grant quarto and Folio equal authority. The introduction re-examines the revision hypothesis, the claim that the Folio text records Shakespeare's own reworking of the quarto, and concludes that the case for full-scale revision remains controversial, that some differences between the texts look like varying states of the author's working drafts and others like politically motivated interference from outside, and that on balance the editor declares cautiously for the quarto. A young editor who spends years inside that problem learns a permanent lesson: the early texts of a Shakespeare play are not a ladder with a sound copy at the top and corruption below, but a set of witnesses whose disagreements are themselves evidence about how the play was written, staged, and transmitted. The whole of Weis's later practice on *Romeo and Juliet* grows out of that lesson. Where the orthodox twentieth-century editor of the lovers' tragedy quarantined the first quarto as a reported text, Weis was formed by a play on which the shorter, rougher quarto had turned out to deserve a hearing on every page.

The *Lear* edition also supplied the mechanical problem Weis's Arden volume had to solve. A parallel text can refuse to choose: two versions face each other across the gutter, and the reader performs the editorship. A series volume cannot refuse. The Arden Shakespeare prints one text, and an editor formed by the parallel method who is required to print one text must find a single-page equivalent of the facing-page honesty he had practised for two decades. Weis's equivalent has three parts, and each part of the finished edition corresponds to one element of the *Lear* solution translated into a single text. The patches from the first quarto translate the parallel page's equality into hierarchy: the rival witness is heard inside the chosen text rather than beside it. The collations at the foot of the page translate the parallel page's visibility: the rejected readings remain present, demoted to small type but never suppressed. And the facsimile appendix translates the parallel page's ultimate appeal, the evidence itself, placed before the reader unmediated. Read in this light, the edition's much-discussed eccentricity is a *Lear* editor's fidelity to his own formation under new constraints, and the critics who found the volume old-fashioned in manner were detecting the manner of 1993 carried into 2012, the parallel-text conscience still at work inside a format that forbids parallel texts. The formation explains the failures as neatly as the successes: an editor whose instinct is to keep both witnesses in the room will sometimes keep a word from each in a single line, and the Queen Mab composite is what the facing-page habit looks like when it is forced onto one page.

The biographer in Weis matters as much as the bibliographer. His *Shakespeare Revealed: A Biography* appeared in 2007, five years before the Arden volume, and it works the documentary record of the life, the Stratford and London archives, with the same habit of letting stubborn paper witnesses argue against inherited story. An editor who writes biography learns to distrust the smooth tradition and to ask what the surviving documents, read without the varnish of retelling, will actually support. Readers of the Arden introduction meet that habit in an unexpected place, in the attention Weis pays to the ages of the characters and to the calendar of the action, matters a more purely literary editor might leave vague. Juliet is not yet fourteen, because the Nurse counts the years from the earthquake and fixes her birthday at Lammas Eve (1.3). Romeo's age no text states, and Weis's willingness to treat the undocumented boy as a problem in evidence rather than an occasion for romance is the biographer's instinct at work inside a critical introduction. The edition's realism, which Smith noticed and partly resisted, begins here: Weis reads the play the way he reads a life, asking what the record fixes, what it leaves open, and what readers have filled in from sentiment.

There is a third formation to name, the teacher's. The Arden Third Series addresses students and scholars in one volume, and Weis's introduction is built to be taught from, with its illustrated account of the play's language, its schedule of the action, its excursions into performance and film. The *Times Literary Supplement*, in a verdict quoted by the publisher, found introduction and commentary alike sensitive, sharp-eyed and clear, and predicted that the volume would serve well. Teachers' praise gathered around the same qualities, a clear and spacious text and a wealth of critical and contextual material. An editor who has spent a career in lecture rooms knows which cruces students actually trip on, and Weis's commentary spends its length accordingly. The edition's populism is deliberate and disciplined: nothing in it is dumbed down, but everything in it is explained at the point where a reader meets it, on the page, rather than in a separate apparatus of forbidding aspect.

## What the Arden Third Series Asked of Weis

The Arden Shakespeare has carried the scholarly text of the plays through three series across more than a century, and the Third Series reformatted the inheritance for a new scholarly settlement. Its volumes keep the series' traditional strengths, a long critical introduction, a text established from the early editions, collations recording where the printed words depart from the copy-text, and commentary notes facing the reader on the same page as the lines they explain. Around those strengths the Third Series rebuilt the introduction's obligations: the play's stage history and screen afterlife became standard matter, illustration became normal, and the editor was expected to argue a position rather than merely survey the field. Weis's volume is a characteristic product of that settlement, and readers who want the comparative ground can consult [a comparison of the modern editions from Arden, Oxford, Cambridge and Folger](/2026/06/12/modern-editions-compared-arden-oxford/), which sets the rival houses side by side. Within the Arden line itself, Weis had a specific predecessor to succeed, and the succession defines his task. Brian Gibbons edited the play for the Arden Second Series in 1980, and his volume, examined in [Brian Gibbons and the Arden Second Series](/2027/06/11/brian-gibbons-arden-second-series/), set the terms Weis inherited: a text based on the second quarto of 1599, an introduction alive to the play's rhetoric and its Italian sources, and a first quarto held at the distance the New Bibliography had prescribed.

The Gibbons settlement on the text rested on a theory of the documents. The second quarto, printed in 1599 with the title page boast that it was newly corrected, augmented, and amended, was taken to descend from Shakespeare's own draft papers, while the first quarto of 1597, shorter by some eight hundred lines and rougher in its verse, was explained as a reported text, reconstructed from memory by actors or auditors and printed without authority. On that theory an editor's duty was clear: print the second quarto, emend it sparingly where it fails to make sense, and let the first quarto testify only occasionally, chiefly where its stage directions preserve business the fuller text omits. Gibbons executed that duty with distinction, and for thirty years his text was the scholarly standard. But the theory underneath it had already begun to move. Through the 1980s and 1990s the bad quarto category itself came under audit, and the first quarto of this play in particular attracted defenders who read its differences not as the smudges of faulty memory but as the marks of the play cut and shaped for performance, a text with the authority of the stage if not of the study. Weis, arriving with the *Lear* parallel texts behind him, was the editor formed by exactly that shift. His Arden Third does not overturn the Gibbons settlement at its base: the words on his page still descend from the second quarto. What he changes is the first quarto's standing in the court. It enters his edition not as a suspect to be cross-examined on rare occasions but as a standing witness whose testimony, above all about staging, is presumed worth hearing. That change of standing, more than any single emendation, is what the Third Series volume revises in the Second.

### Which quarto supplies most of the words in Weis's text?

The second quarto of 1599 supplies the copy-text, the base from which Weis's page is built and against which his collations record every departure. The first quarto of 1597 patches that base throughout, contributing stage directions wholesale and readings wherever Weis judges the performed text superior, so the play is Q2 in substance and Q1 in its stage life.

The house style of the Third Series also asked its editor to modernize. Weis's text is printed in modern spelling, with punctuation lightly regularized for a reader who meets the lines silently, and with speech headings and stage directions set out in the clear typography the series had made familiar. Modernization is never innocent, since every respelling chooses among the early texts' ambiguities, but it belongs to the Arden contract with its readers, and Weis honours it while keeping the record of what he has changed in the collations at the foot of the page. A reader who wants to audit the editor can do so line by line without leaving the volume, and the appendix, which reproduces the British Library copy of the 1597 quarto in facsimile, extends the audit to the edition's second witness entire. Few editions in the play's history have put so much of their own evidence on display, and the display is itself an editorial argument: Weis's text asks to be trusted because it can be checked.

The manner of his text, though, departed from what the third series had led its readers to expect, and Smith's phrase for the departure, eclectic in the manner of the older Ardens rather than the third series, identifies the norm by contrast. The third series built its reputation on editions whose textual introductions argue like bibliographers, establishing the copy-text's authority from the history of the print shop and deriving emendation policy from that argument, so that a reader can predict an editor's decisions from stated principles before meeting them. Weis's volume reverses the expected order of authority. Its introduction argues like a critic, from language, structure, and performance, and its text follows by judgment at the point of need, with the bibliographic accounting kept in the collations rather than erected into a system at the front. Nothing in the series' commission forbade the reversal, and the volume's defenders can fairly note that the play it edits, surviving in a good text and a performed one, invites judgment more frankly than most. But the reversal is the fact about the edition from which its whole reception follows: readers who came for the third series' usual contract, principle first and text second, found the contract rewritten, and the argument about the book has been an argument about that rewriting ever since.

## The First Quarto in Weis's Hands: A Text With Its Own Authority

The first quarto announces itself on its title page as the play *as it hath been often (with great applause) plaid publiquely*, and for most of its editorial history that claim was read as a pirate's alibi. Weis reads it as a description. The 1597 text is shorter than the 1599 text by roughly a fifth, its verse is sometimes compressed to the edge of paraphrase, and whole transitions vanish, yet its stage directions are fuller, more physical, and more specific than anything the second quarto offers, and its shape is the shape of a playing version: swift, actable, and organized around what a company could perform. The reassessment Weis brings to the Arden Third treats that shape as evidence. A text that records the play in performance has authority of a different kind from a text that records the play on paper, not a lesser kind, and an editor constructing a reading text for an age of performance criticism is entitled, even obliged, to draw on it. Smith's review concedes the modernity of this stance while questioning its discipline, and the questioning is fair; but the stance itself aligns the edition with the strongest movement in the play's textual scholarship of the preceding generation, the movement that dissolved the old bad quarto verdict into a set of separate questions about how each early text was made.

The clearest measure of the reassessment is the stage directions. Smith counts some two dozen directions that the first quarto supplies and the second lacks or leaves poorer, and reports that of those two dozen Weis declines only two, adopting the rest wholesale into his text. Directions are where a performed text speaks most directly, since they encode the movement, grouping, and properties of an actual staging rather than the literary shape of a speech. When the second quarto's balcony scene is content with a bare entrance, the first quarto gives the staging its physical grammar in the direction that brings Romeo and Juliet on at the window, and Weis prints a composite that keeps the first quarto's window while retaining the second quarto's word aloft, so that the direction records both the performed placing of the lovers and the vertical architecture of the early stage. The composite is typical of the edition's habits, and it shows the habit at its most defensible: two witnesses, each strong where the other is silent, are allowed to complete one another, and the collation at the foot of the page tells the reader exactly what has been done. Directors rehearsing from this text inherit a staging memory that reaches back to the 1590s playhouse, and that inheritance is not decoration. It changes what the printed play is: less a poem with directions attached, more a score whose earliest performance is one of its documents.

### How many of the first quarto's stage directions does Weis adopt?

Nearly all of them. Of the two dozen or so directions that the 1597 quarto supplies beyond the second quarto's sparer record, Emma Smith's count finds only two that Weis refuses, and the remaining twenty-two and more enter his text wholesale, so that the staging of his printed play is in large part the staging the first quarto remembers.

The boldest expression of the reassessment is not in the text at all but behind it, in the appendix, where Weis prints a facsimile reproduction of the British Library copy of the 1597 quarto. An appendix of this kind converts the edition's argument into an exhibit. Readers who have been told that the first quarto is a performed text with its own authority can turn to the back of the volume and inspect the witness directly, its compressed speeches, its present-tense directions, its very different feel on the page from the stately second quarto. Smith calls the facsimile a welcome addition, and the welcome is deserved, because the appendix quietly redefines what an Arden edition is for. The older Arden volume asked to be believed; this one asks to be verified, and it hands its readers the means of verification. Students who work through even a few pages of the facsimile learn more about the play's textual condition than any summary can teach, since the difference between a text made for readers and a text made from performance stops being a formula and becomes a visible fact.

The facsimile also changes the experience of reading the edition's own text, in a way no reviewer quite isolated. A reader who has spent an hour inside the 1597 pages, where scenes run shorter, entrances arrive with a performer's economy, and the verse sits on the page like a script rather than a monument, returns to Weis's modernized text with altered eyes. The patches stop looking like intrusions and start looking like negotiations between two real objects, both of them present in the same binding. That is the appendix's deepest service: it converts the edition's textual policy from a claim the editor makes into an experience the reader has. Editions of other plays in the series reproduce documents selectively, a title page, a portrait, a page of a source. Weis reproduces the whole rival witness, all of it, in a gesture whose completeness is the argument. Half a facsimile would have illustrated the first quarto; the whole facsimile installs it, and a reader who finishes the volume has in a strict sense read both early texts of the play, one in the editor's modern dress and one standing in the print shop dress of 1597. The Arden Third volume is therefore the first edition of the play that contains the play's textual problem entire between its covers, and every future editor inherits not only Weis's solutions but his standard of display.

None of this requires Weis to pretend that the first quarto is the better text line by line, and he does not pretend it. His copy-text remains the second quarto precisely because its readings are fuller and, in the great majority of contested places, more likely to preserve what Shakespeare wrote for the page. The reassessment is about standing, not supremacy: Q1 is promoted from convicted pirate to competent witness, believed on staging, consulted on readings, and exhibited in facsimile, while Q2 keeps the bench. Critics who want a sterner formulation, one witness enthroned and the other excluded, will find Weis's arrangement untidy. The untidiness is the point. The play's early transmission was untidy, and an edition that irons the untidiness away in the name of system has answered a tidier question than the one the documents ask.

Weis's trust in the first quarto is discriminating rather than general, and the discrimination runs by kind of evidence. Where the 1597 text remembers the stage, in directions, groupings, and the physical business of scenes, he follows it almost without exception. Where it offers a rival version of the dialogue, he treats it as one witness among others, to be believed when its reading explains the copy-text's difficulty or sharpens the performed sense, and to be set aside when its compression merely thins the verse. The distinction explains the shape of the whole text: the last act, where the first quarto's abbreviation bites hardest and its dialogue contracts toward summary, stands in Weis's edition at the second quarto's fullness, while the staging directions that frame that act's solemn procession to the tomb come substantially from the shorter text. An editor who trusted Q1 uniformly would have printed a shorter tragedy with better directions. An editor who distrusted it uniformly would have printed the Gibbons text again. Weis prints neither, and the resulting volume has to be described scene by scene rather than by formula, which is why reviewers reach for metaphors of two faces. The description this article prefers is jurisdictional. Weis assigns each witness a jurisdiction, the performed text ruling on staging, the fuller text ruling on dialogue, the editor reserving a discretion at the border that he exercises, as the prosecution showed, sometimes without leaving his reasons in the record. Jurisdictions can be defended; unrecorded discretion cannot, and the whole argument about the edition is contained in that difference.

## Judgment Without a System: Weis's Eclectic Page

Where the reassessment shades into controversy is in the individual readings Weis lifts from the first quarto into his Q2 text, because the grounds shift from case to case in ways his reviewer refused to let pass. Some interventions rest on fresh scholarship of the most respectable kind. Research into Shakespeare's persistent debts to Thomas Nashe supports Weis in replacing the second quarto's word phantacies with the first quarto's fantasticoes in Mercutio's catalogue of the new affected breed at 2.4, and in printing atomi where the second quarto's Queen Mab speech offers the stranger ottamie for the little team that draws Mab's wagon over sleeping noses (1.4). These are emendations with an argument behind them, anchored in the playwright's reading and in the probabilities of compositorial error, and they show the eclectic method working as its defenders promise: the editor's learning, applied at the point of need, recovering words that the copy-text's printers had degraded. No reviewer quarrelled with these, and they deserve the quiet they received.

The Queen Mab speech also supplies the case where the method's seams show. Mercutio's wagoner is a small grey-coated gnat, not half so big as a round little worm pricked from the lazy finger of a maid, in the line as Weis prints it (1.4). The trouble, as Smith lays it out, is that no early text prints that line. The first quarto reads pickt where Weis prints pricked, keeping maid; the second quarto reads pricked but gives the finger to a man. Weis takes his verb from the second quarto, where Smith notes he finds it more dramatic and suggestive than the first quarto's pickt, and his noun from the first quarto, and the resulting line, however internally coherent, however well the collocation of pricked and maid suits Mercutio's lewd poetry, is a line Shakespeare's printers never set and his company, on the surviving evidence, never spoke. Smith's sentence on the matter is the review's most quoted: the printed line has no consistent principle of textual selection behind it. The adjudication this article reaches is narrower than Smith's and less comfortable for the editor. Choosing between witnesses by the quality of the resulting poetry is a defensible editorial act when the witnesses are evenly matched and the choice is confessed, as in the collations Weis does print. What the Mab line lacks is not confession but criterion: dramatic suggestiveness in the verb and bawdy potential in the noun are two different currencies, and an editor who pays in both on a single line owes the reader a note explaining the exchange rate. Weis's commentary, so forthcoming elsewhere, lets this one pass in comparative silence.

The same pattern, judgment outrunning its printed reasons, reaches its limit at the end of the garden scene. Romeo's last line before he goes to the Friar stands in the second quarto as *Hence will I to my ghostly Friers close cell*, and in the first quarto as *Now will I to my Ghostly fathers Cell* (2.2). Weis prints *Hence will I to my ghostly sire's close cell*. The word sire stands in neither quarto nor in the Folio. It stands in Arthur Brooke's *Romeus and Juliet*, where the hero resolves to seek his ghostly sire, and Weis, whose knowledge of the source poem is as deep as any living editor's, has imported the source's word into the play's text. Smith's response is the bluntest sentence in her review: there seems to be no possible reason to intervene in the text in this way. She adds a point that cuts deeper than taste, since the choice between father and friar is not neutral in a play whose religious colouring critics debate: the pastoral and Catholic implications that attach differently to the two terms deserved discussion, and the imported sire dissolves a genuine textual alternative into a word no early witness supports. On this line the prosecution wins outright. An editor may emend a copy-text that makes no sense, and may choose between witnesses that divide, but Brooke's poem is not a witness to the play's text, and a reading drawn from it, however elegant the echo, belongs in the commentary, where Weis's learning about sources does such distinguished work, and not in the line Romeo speaks.

## Persons Restored: Petruchio Speaks, and the Nurse Gets a Name

Two smaller decisions show the same editorial temperament from its most attractive side, the determination to recover what print had buried. Petruchio is the play's certified ghost: Juliet's Nurse, dodging a question at the feast, points him out as a young man going out of the door (1.5), and the early texts place a companion of that name beside Tybalt in the street fight, yet in most editions he never speaks, because the words the copy gives him at 3.1, the cry that pulls Tybalt away from the fight, have been printed by editors as part of a stage direction rather than as a line. Weis restores the words to speech and the speaker to the dramatis personae, so that his text of the fight contains a new character with a single line. The decision is small in bulk and large in implication. An editor willing to promote a figure out of a direction's italics is an editor reading the early print as a fallible record of a stage event rather than as a scripture to be tidied, and the promotion has practical consequences in rehearsal, where Tybalt's exit from the fatal fight gains a human agent instead of a typographical shrug. Smith records the novelty with raised eyebrows but without the severity she reserves for the sire, and the distinction is just: here the words restored are words the early text contains, reassigned rather than invented, and the criterion, that print had mistaken speech for direction, is a bibliographical criterion of a recognizable kind.

### Does a new character speak in Weis's text of the fight scene?

Yes. Petruchio, the Capulet guest whom the Nurse names at the feast and whom the early texts set beside Tybalt in the street fight, receives in Weis's text the single line that earlier editors had folded into a stage direction at 3.1, the call that draws Tybalt away, so a ghost figure becomes a speaking one.

The companion piece is the Nurse's name. In the bustle before the wedding, Capulet, driving his household through the preparations, calls out *Look to the baked meats, good Angelica* (4.4), and the question of who Angelica is has teased editors for centuries: a servant otherwise unknown, a slip for the cook, or the Nurse herself, addressed at last by name in the one household where she is family. Weis proposes the boldest of the options, that the famously anonymous Nurse is here revealed as Angelica, and the proposal suits the realist cast of his introduction, which likes its characters furnished with the furniture of lives. Smith's demurrer is delicate and exact: it is not clear who or what is being addressed, Angelica is also a herb of the kitchen, candied angelica belonged to a wedding breakfast's world, and Weis does not gloss the possible culinary pun in the very context where cooks and baked meats crowd the stage. The adjudication splits the honours. As a suggestion carried in an introduction, the naming is legitimate and even illuminating, since it sends readers back to a line most editions leave sleeping. As an editorial act it would need the commentary to do the work Smith asks for, laying the herb beside the woman and letting the ambiguity stand confessed. Weis's instinct, to give the play's people back their names, is the same instinct that freed Petruchio, and it is among the edition's humane strengths; his occasional failure is only to stop and argue the case his instinct has already decided.

Taken together, the two restorations change the social fabric of the printed play in ways a reader feels before any argument registers. The feud's world in Weis's text is slightly more populated and slightly more named than the world of the older editions: Tybalt does not withdraw from the street fight under cover of a stage direction but answers a companion's call, and the Capulet household at dawn contains a woman its master may be addressing by name while the family believes Juliet dead upstairs. Editions train the imagination's census of a play, and a census is never neutral. A Verona with a speaking Petruchio is a Verona in which the feud's violence is a company activity, staffed by young men with names and loyalties, and a Capulet household in which the Nurse can be Angelica is a household whose servants have identities the tragedy can spend. Weis's two smallest decisions thus lean the same way as his largest: toward a play dense with persons, in which the heroine's distinction is not that she alone is real but that she becomes real fastest and furthest among people the edition has taken care to make solid. The editor who centres Juliet begins by populating her world, and the dramatis personae of his text, read as the introduction's first page, already contains the argument in embryo.

## The Dawn Quatrain Weis Gives to Romeo

One decision concentrates the whole argument about the edition's method, and it deserves its own hearing. As Romeo leaves Juliet at the end of the garden scene, the second quarto gives him a quatrain of dawn verse, *The grey-eyed morn smiles on the frowning night*, lines that then appear again at the opening of the next scene, spoken by the Friar as he enters alone with his basket to gather herbs (2.2, 2.3). The first quarto has no repetition: in its text the Friar alone speaks the quatrain, at the head of his scene, and Romeo's parting is shorter. The doubling in the second quarto has fascinated textual critics for generations, because it looks like a fossil of the drafting process, a passage the author wrote, placed, then placed again in a better position without cancelling the first occurrence, the kind of duplication that revision leaves behind. An editor must choose: print the quatrain twice, as the copy-text offers it; give it once, to the Friar, with the first quarto; or give it once, to Romeo, as the second Folio later did.

Weis gives it to Romeo, following the second Folio, and his printed explanation, as Smith quotes it, is that the lines hardly fit with the Friar's meditation on medicinal herbalism. Smith's complaint is not chiefly about the destination but about the missing journey. The edition offers no substantive discussion of the bibliographic theory that might govern the choice, nothing on authorial second thoughts as an explanation of the doubling, and no account of why the first quarto's simple clarification was not taken, although the editor who had adopted two dozen of that quarto's directions wholesale might have been expected to find its single-speech arrangement congenial. A decision of this visibility, on the most famous duplication in the play's text, needed its reasons printed, and the reasons Weis prints are literary where the problem is bibliographical: whether dawn verse suits a herbalist is a question about characterization, while the question the texts pose is about how the duplication arose and which state of the play an edition means to represent. The adjudication here must go against the editor on the process while remaining agnostic on the outcome. Giving the quatrain to Romeo is a choice with the second Folio's precedent and with real theatrical logic behind it, since the lines close the lovers' night in Romeo's voice before the scene turns. What the edition owes its readers, and at this crux withholds, is the argument. The omission matters beyond four lines, because the edition's whole claim on trust is that its judgments can be audited, and at the place where audit is most invited, the ledger is blank.

## The Introduction's Wager: The Play Is Juliet's

If the text is the edition's battleground, the introduction is its manifesto, and the manifesto's thesis is stated by the publisher without hedging: this major new edition argues that the play is ultimately Juliet's. The claim deserves to be taken as more than marketing, because the introduction builds it from the play's own distribution of speech and growth. Romeo enters the tragedy fluent in a borrowed language, the Petrarchan counters of his infatuation for Rosaline, and his verse has to be educated by events; Juliet enters it at thirteen, nearly fourteen, under her parents' roof, and within four days she commands every register the play contains: the formal wit of the shared sonnet at the feast (1.5), the great interrogative soliloquy of the garden, in which she dismantles the philosophy of names while Romeo listens below (2.2), the compressed ecstasy of *Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds* as she waits for the wedding night (3.2.1), the rhetorical storm of her grief and rage at the news of Tybalt's death and Romeo's banishment (3.2), and the solitary terror of the potion speech, where she talks herself through every horror the vault can hold before she drinks (4.3). Weis's poetic analysis, which Smith singles out as the introduction's strength, follows that education of a voice scene by scene. The introduction's Juliet is not a heroine pasted over the play but a reading of where its verbal energy accumulates, and on that evidence the thesis is more than defensible. By the last act Romeo reacts to reports and drinks to a mistake, while Juliet, waking in the vault, reads the whole situation in a breath, finds the dagger, and acts. The play's final competence is hers.

### What does Weis's introduction make of the play's closing couplet?

The introduction makes the closing couplet the text's verdict on its heroine. The Prince ends the play by naming the story as Juliet's and her Romeo's, hers first and his by attachment to her (5.3), and Weis's Juliet-centred reading treats that order as the tragedy's distribution of emphasis, the last of the edition's exhibits for the play being ultimately hers.

The wager has a realist corollary that Smith found daring. Weis furnishes his characters with the coordinates of lives, attending to their ages with a biographer's care, Juliet's fixed by the Nurse's arithmetic, Romeo's left where the texts leave it, a boy whose years the play declines to state while it loads him with a man's consequences. Realist characterization of this kind is unfashionable in an era trained to speak of subject positions, and it exposes the introduction to the charge of naivety. The charge underrates the purpose. Weis is writing for readers meeting the play in classrooms, many of them the age of its heroine, and the realist frame is his bridge between their experience and the text's strangeness: he starts from the persons as the play first presents them, desiring, fearing, and choosing in a household and a city, and lets the strangeness accumulate from the language and the clock. The method risks flattening the play into a case history at its weakest moments, but it buys the introduction its narrative drive, and it sets up the edition's best structural argument, which concerns not persons but time.

## Four Days in Late July: Weis's Time-Scheme and the Case Against Comedy

The introduction's most original argument is built from a calendar. Weis lays out the play's internal schedule hour by hour: the street melee on a Sunday morning in late July, the feast that night, the marriage on Monday, the killings and the banishment within the same crowded Monday, the wedding to Paris first set for Thursday and then dragged forward to Wednesday, the potion drunk on Tuesday night, the tomb at dawn on Thursday, where Capulet, Montague, and the Prince stand over the bodies. Against this schedule he sets the older habit of describing the play's first half as a comedy that turns, the lovers' story framed by blocking parents, a bawdy nurse, a secret marriage, and a friar with a device, all the machinery of romantic comedy driving toward a wedding that the feud then wrecks. Weis's phrase for the counter-evidence is the play's tightly plotted and exiguous time-scheme, and his argument is that a structure under this kind of temporal pressure was never travelling toward comedy's open horizon. Comedy needs elbow room, seasons in which errors can ripen and be forgiven; this play gives its lovers four days, and the Prologue has already announced the ending before the first brawl. The feud's first outbreak and the lovers' first meeting fall on the same Sunday, and every comic device the plot borrows arrives with its fuse already lit.

### How long does Weis allow the lovers, from the first brawl to the tomb?

Four days. Weis's schedule runs from the street melee on a Sunday morning in late July through the feast, the Monday marriage and murders, and the potion taken on Tuesday night, to the discovery at the tomb at dawn on Thursday, a span he calls a tightly plotted and exiguous time-scheme.

The argument is the introduction at its best because it is falsifiable and textual, resting on the play's own scattered clock references gathered into one account, and Weis is candid about its limits in a sentence Smith quotes with approval: quite how literal one dare be when dealing with a Shakespeare play is a moot point. The candour is needed, because the scheme's most famous joint does not hold. The Friar's potion is promised to keep Juliet in a deathlike trance for two and forty hours (4.1), a span that ought to carry her from Tuesday night to Thursday's daylight with the wedding to Paris intervening on Wednesday morning, yet the play's other references compress the interval, and no honest timetable can make every figure agree. Weis acknowledges that the potion does not live up to its chronological promise. A weaker editor would have hidden the crack or harmonized it away; Weis prints it, and the concession strengthens the argument it qualifies, since the claim was never that Shakespeare kept a railway timetable, only that the play's felt time is a narrowing corridor rather than comedy's open field. On that claim the schedule, the announced ending, and the doublings of haste in the dialogue, Capulet dragging the wedding forward, the Friar warning that violent delights have violent ends (2.6), all testify together. The comedy reading survives Weis's attack as an account of the play's materials; as an account of its structure, it leaves the edition's introduction in possession of the field.

## Brooke in the Margins of Weis's Text: Sources and Their Uses

Weis's learning about the play's sources is the deepest any editor of the play has brought to the task, and the introduction and commentary put it to constant work. The principal source is Arthur Brooke's long poem *The Tragical History of Romeus and Juliet* of 1562, which supplied the plot, the feud, the friar, the potion, and the tomb, and behind Brooke stand the Italian novelle of Luigi da Porto and Matteo Bandello and William Painter's prose version in *The Palace of Pleasure*. Where Brooke moralizes at length, warning young readers against disobedience and lust, the play strips the sermon away and lets the events judge themselves, and Weis's notes track the transformation at the level of the line, showing what the playwright kept, what he compressed from Brooke's months into his four days, and what he invented outright, beginning with Mercutio's expansion from a name in the poem to the play's presiding wit. This source work is the commentary's quiet glory, and it also explains the edition's single indefensible line, since the ghostly sire that Romeo acquires at 2.2 is Weis's source scholarship escaping from the margins into the text. The incident deserves its place in any account of the edition precisely because it is uncharacteristic: everywhere else, Brooke is the commentary's instrument for measuring Shakespeare's departures, and the one moment the instrument writes the text it should be measuring stands out as a lapse of discipline by the editor who best understood the distinction.

The compression Weis's notes keep measuring deserves its own emphasis, because it binds the source work to the introduction's clock. Brooke's lovers, after their secret marriage, live as man and wife for a span of months, meeting in the friar's cell and the orchard while the feud smoulders at its own pace, and the poem's catastrophe, when it comes, closes a story that has had time to settle into a marriage. The play annihilates that span. Shakespeare's lovers marry on Monday and are dead by Thursday, their whole married life a single night divided by a killing, and every device that in the poem has leisure to work, the potion, the letter, the sleeping watch, is in the play compressed until its failure is a matter of hours and a closed plague door. Weis's commentary, moving line by line through the scenes with Brooke open beside the text, lets a reader watch the acceleration happen at the level of individual speeches: a passage the poem spreads across a journey the play folds into a soliloquy, a delay the narrator explains the dialogue enacts. No other editor has made the notes carry this measurement so consistently, and the payoff is critical as well as textual, since the introduction's argument against the comedy reading rests on exactly the compression the commentary documents. Source study and structural argument, which in most editions lead separate lives, in Weis's volume are one investigation conducted in two registers, and the unity is the surest sign that the edition was conceived whole rather than assembled from an editor's separate competences.

The dating of the play's composition belongs to the same documentary habit. The edition works within the settlement the evidence supports, a play written in the middle of the 1590s and in print by 1597, with the Nurse's memory of an earthquake eleven years before her charge's third birthday pointing readers back toward 1580 and forward to a composition in the years just before the first quarto. Weis's introduction spends less energy on redating the play than on the calendar inside it, and the distribution of energy is characteristic: the documents he can make speak, the internal schedule, the source poem's wording, the quartos' directions, receive his closest attention, while questions the record cannot settle are left in the state the record leaves them. An editor's silences are part of his text, and Weis's silences fall, consistently, where evidence runs out.

The commentary's source notes also register the layers beneath Brooke. Painter's prose tale, Englished in *The Palace of Pleasure* a few years after Brooke's poem, gave the story a second English life with its own emphases, cooler toward the lovers and franker about the friar's stratagems, and behind both English versions stand Bandello's novella and da Porto's earlier *Giulietta e Romeo*, where the story first took the shape the European tradition would inherit. Weis does not pretend that Shakespeare worked from the Italians directly, and his notes keep the chain of transmission honest: the play's debts run through Brooke, with Painter as a possible tributary, and the Italian texts as the headwaters an editor cites to explain the story's grain rather than its wording. The discipline matters because source study in an edition easily becomes a display of learning for its own sake. In Weis's pages it stays instrumental. A note invokes Brooke when the play's departure from the poem is the meaning of a scene, when a moralizing passage has been struck out, a timescale compressed, a character enlarged, and it falls silent where the poem has nothing to teach. That economy is the commentary's scholarly signature, and it is what makes the single counter-instance, the source's word promoted into the printed line, legible as a lapse rather than a policy.

## Commentary on the Page: How Weis's Notes Work

The Arden page is a two-part instrument, text above and commentary below, with the collations recording textual decisions at the foot, and Weis plays the instrument as well as any editor of his generation. His notes, in the publisher's summary of their range, discuss issues of staging, theme, and meaning, and Shakespeare's use of his sources, and the summary is accurate: a single page of the balcony scene or the tomb may move from a gloss on a dead metaphor to a note on how the lines play from the upper stage, to a parallel in Brooke, to a caution about a word whose sense has shifted since the 1590s. The commentary's distinctive virtue is appetite. It assumes that a student reading the play wants to know everything the editor knows that bears on the line, and it would rather risk a long note than leave a real difficulty unmet. The *Times Literary Supplement* verdict, that introduction and commentary are sensitive, sharp-eyed and clear, registers the result: the notes read as the work of a mind attending to the line in front of it rather than executing a programme.

### How does Weis's commentary handle the play's hardest places?

The commentary refuses to pretend that hard places are easy. At the famous cruces the notes lay out the alternatives and the editor's choice among them with a candour the introduction does not always match, so the notes sometimes supply the very argument a crux was owed, and the reader learns to ask whose words a line contains.

Two choices govern the commentary's character. The first is its hospitality to performance: Weis glosses with the stage in view, noting where a line needs a gesture to complete it, where the first quarto's directions recover business the second quarto leaves implicit, and where modern productions have found meanings the study might miss. The second is its refusal to pretend that hard places are easy. At the play's famous cruces the notes lay out the alternatives and the editor's choice among them with a candour that the textual introduction does not always match, so that the commentary sometimes contains the argument the introduction owed the reader at the dawn quatrain. A reader who uses the whole page, text, collation, and note together, receives a more principled edition than the reader of the text alone, and the discrepancy between the page's full apparatus and the introduction's occasional silences is the fair residue of Smith's case. The edition is most itself not in any single component but in the working relation among them, and it trains its readers, almost without their noticing, to read editorially: to ask of every line whose words these are, what they cost, and what else they might have been.

The commentary's lexical work deserves separate notice, because glossing is where an edition meets its least experienced readers on ground they cannot check. The play's most famous misreading gathers around a single word of the garden scene, where Juliet's *wherefore* asks not where Romeo is but why he is Romeo, why the name Montague stands between the houses, and an edition's note at that line carries the whole speech's philosophy in miniature. Weis's notes throughout treat such words as doors rather than decorations: a gloss opens onto the conceit the word belongs to, the source passage it answers, or the staging fact it implies, so that the student who looks up a hard word is returned to the line with more than a synonym. The same habit governs the notes on the lovers' shared idiom, the religious vocabulary of pilgrim, saint, and prayer that the feast scene bends toward eros, where the commentary tracks how a sacred word changes charge as it passes between speakers. Lexical commentary of this density is slow to write and easy to underrate, and it is the layer of the edition that students will still be using when the introduction's arguments have been absorbed into the common sense of the subject. The prosecution of Weis's text, notably, never reaches his glosses: the notes stand acquitted by every reviewer, and for most of the edition's readers they are the edition.

## An Introduction That Reads Films: Weis on the Play's Afterlife

The Third Series settlement required its editors to treat the play's afterlife as part of its meaning, and Weis, on Smith's own evidence, is most enthusiastic exactly there. His introduction reads the great screen incarnations with the closeness usually reserved for poems: Franco Zeffirelli's 1968 film, with its sunlit Italian locations and its teenage lovers cast at something near the play's own ages; Baz Luhrmann's 1996 *Romeo + Juliet*, which keeps the words and rebuilds Verona as a violent modern city of guns, cars, and television news; and *West Side Story*, the Broadway musical of 1957 and the film of 1961, which translates the feud into the gang territories of New York and the balcony into a fire escape. Smith calls these readings informed, lively, and appealing, and the praise identifies something structural about the edition. An editor who believes the first quarto matters because it records performance will naturally believe that later performances are documents too, evidence about what the play can be made to mean, and Weis's film chapters extend his textual philosophy into the cinema: the play exists in its realizations, and the edition that ignores them has amputated part of its subject.

The stage history in the introduction works the same way, moving from the early texts through the centuries of adaptation to the modern productions Weis singles out, among them Michael Bogdanov's modern-dress staging for the Royal Shakespeare Company and Tim Carroll's work with original practices at the Globe, productions the *Times Literary Supplement* noticed the account for bringing to life. The cumulative effect is to place Weis inside the movement traced in [the twentieth-century rehabilitation of the play](/2027/04/16/twentieth-century-rehabilitation/), the long recovery by which the tragedy rose from a lyrical apprentice piece, patronized by the great nineteenth-century critics, to a central object of scholarly and theatrical attention. Weis's edition is both a product of that recovery and one of its instruments, since every classroom that adopts the Arden Third teaches the play at the rehabilitation's valuation.

The inevitable comparison is with the other great scholarly edition of the modern period, Jill Levenson's Oxford text of 2000, whose virtues are surveyed in [Jill Levenson and the Oxford Edition](/2027/06/18/jill-levenson-oxford-edition/). Levenson's introduction had already made performance the centre of an edition's gravity, and her text, like Weis's, is built on the second quarto with a new respect for what the first quarto knows about the stage. The difference between the two volumes is a difference of emphasis rather than of allegiance. Levenson's play is above all a theatrical event whose meanings are made in performance across four centuries; Weis's play is above all a poem whose performances, from the 1597 quarto's staging memory to Luhrmann's camera, are evidence in a case about language, time, and a heroine. Readers choosing between them are choosing the order in which to ask questions, and the existence of two editions of this quality, differently centred, is itself the strongest sign of the play's recovered standing. Weis does not supersede Levenson; he answers her, and the conversation between the two volumes is where a serious student of the play's editing should begin.

The introduction is also a visual argument, and its illustrations belong to the edition's method rather than to its decoration. The publisher describes a richly illustrated introduction, and the images do the same work as the facsimile appendix at the volume's other end: they put evidence before the reader. Production photographs of Bogdanov's Verona and Carroll's Globe, stills from the Zeffirelli and Luhrmann films, and the printed witnesses reproduced among them compose a single visual archive in which a stage moment, a film frame, and a page of the 1597 quarto stand as documents of the same kind, the play caught in the act of being realized. An older style of edition treated illustration as frontispiece ornament. Weis's illustrations are exhibits, and their presence in the argumentative chapters rather than in a plate section announces the introduction's epistemology: claims about performance are to be shown, not merely asserted, exactly as claims about the first quarto are to be settled by printing the quarto. The teacher who projects these pages in a seminar is using the edition as its maker intended, as a dossier from which students argue, and the dossier form is the final expression of the parallel-text conscience that organizes the whole volume.

## Editing the Ending: Weis's Text in the Tomb

The discrimination by kind of evidence that governs Weis's text is tested hardest in the last act, and the ending his readers receive is the clearest practical consequence of his policy. The first quarto's version of Act 5 is its most compressed, a performed shortening that hurries through the watch, the explanations, and the reconciliations, while the second quarto preserves the full ceremonial close: Romeo's entry into the vault and his address to the dead, Paris's death, Juliet's waking and her refusal of the Friar's escape, the arrival of the watch, the Prince's interrogation, the fathers' promises of golden statues, and the closing couplet in which the Prince sums the story as one of Juliet and her Romeo (5.3). Weis prints the fuller ending, as his copy-text requires, and the choice is worth pausing over because it is the mirror image of his first quarto enthusiasms. Where the performed text remembers staging he takes it; where the performed text merely shortens, he restores the study text's amplitude. The ending in his edition is therefore a ceremony rather than a rush, its repetitions intact, the explanations the watch extracts, the letters produced, the accounts squared before the bodies, and a reader formed by this text learns a tragedy that ends in public accounting rather than in the lovers' private extinction.

The closing couplet rewards the editor's attention precisely because it is the play's last naming of its subject: the Prince calls the story Juliet's and her Romeo's, hers first, his by attachment to her. An introduction that argues the play is ultimately Juliet's could hardly ask the text for a better final exhibit, and Weis's commentary is positioned to make the order of the names legible as the tragedy's own verdict rather than a metrical accident. Whether or not the naming order was designed with that weight, the edition's architecture makes it resonate: four hundred pages after an introduction that centred the heroine, the text's last line arrives arranged as the introduction taught its readers to hear it. This is the deepest way in which editing is criticism, deeper than any single emendation, since no word has been altered and yet the ending's meaning has been prepared by everything the volume placed around it. The editor who prints the full close, frames it with a Juliet-centred argument, and annotates its cruces from the sources has not changed the couplet by a letter, and he has changed what generations of readers will hear in it.

## The Reviewers at Odds: Smith's Prosecution and the Verdicts of Use

The mandatory disagreement of any honest account of this edition is already on the table, and it can now be adjudicated in full. For the prosecution stands Emma Smith in *The Review of English Studies*, the most searching review the volume received. Her summary image is of a Janus-faced book: performance and poetic appreciation come in, bibliography goes out. Weis is a thoroughly modern editor, she writes, when thinking about the play's afterlives and performance possibilities, and a curiously old-fashioned one in presenting an eclectic text in the manner of the older Ardens rather than the third series. The counts in her indictment are specific and, on the evidence assembled above, largely proved: the emendations from the first quarto lack a consistent rationale; the composite Queen Mab line exists in no early text; the dawn quatrain is assigned without the bibliographic argument the crux demands; the word sire is imported from the source poem into the play's text without possible justification. A reviewer who lands four such blows has earned the right to her metaphor.

For the defence stand the verdicts of use. The *Times Literary Supplement* found the introduction and commentary sensitive, sharp-eyed and clear, praised the performance history for bringing Bogdanov and Carroll alive on the page, and concluded that the volume will serve well. Studies in English Literature welcomed the facsimile of the first quarto and the expansive glosses. Jarrod DePrado, reviewing the edition, judged that it implements a number of important updates from the second Arden edition and has easily become the definitive edition of the play. Teachers' testimony, gathered by the publisher, dwells on the clear and spacious text and the wealth of critical and contextual material the classroom receives. None of these witnesses answers Smith's counts, and that is the point: they are testifying about a different question, not what principle governs the text but what the volume does for the people who read, teach, and perform the play.

The adjudication this article reaches is that both parties are right about their own question, and that the questions can be ranked. An edition is an instrument before it is a theorem. Its first duty is to put a reliable, intelligible, richly accompanied text into the hands of its readers, and by that duty Weis's volume stands acquitted with honours: no reader is misled about the state of the evidence who uses the collations and the appendix, no student is left without the context the scenes require, and no rival volume offers the same combination of text, commentary, source learning, performance history, and exhibited evidence. Its second duty is to reason consistently about the words it prints, and by that duty the volume stands convicted on a short charge sheet: one imported word, one composite line, one under-argued assignment, and a habit of letting judgment travel faster than its printed reasons. The conviction is real and should follow the edition through its life, since the next editor's first task will be to re-argue exactly those places. But a short charge sheet against single lines is a different thing from a failed edition, and Smith's Janus, read carefully, convicts the text's theory while acquitting almost everything else in the book, including the two achievements, the first quarto's restoration and the introduction's Juliet, on which the edition's lasting value rests. The prosecution wins the methodological point. The defence wins the edition.

The reception since has followed the defence's question rather than the prosecution's, and the pattern is instructive about what editions are for. Seminar discussion of the volume gathers around the introduction's two wagers, the Juliet thesis and the clock, because those are the claims a class can argue with, while the textual counts in Smith's indictment have migrated into the specialist literature on editing, where they belong and where they will shape the next edition's practice. The volume's double life is thus not a contradiction but a division of labour that the Janus image, turned once more, describes better than its coiner perhaps intended: one face toward the readers who use the book, one toward the scholars who will replace it. Weis, the biographer of a playwright whose works survived by being useful to players before they were precious to scholars, might accept the division as the natural order of things. The edition's standing does not require Smith to be wrong. It requires only that her rightness be filed where it can do its work, in the brief of the next editor, while the volume itself gets on with the work of the present one.

## The Context Weis Declines: Verona, History, and the Introduction's Wager

Smith's review registers one further feature of the introduction that an account of the edition should weigh on its own: historical context is downplayed. Weis's introduction spends its length on language, on the calendar, on performance and film, and gives the reader comparatively little of the matter a historicist introduction would foreground, the Italian city-state politics behind the feud, the English anxieties about duelling and private revenge in the fifteen nineties, the theology of suicide and burial law that stands behind the Friar's haste and the graveyard's rules. The omission is real, and in an edition published into a scholarly climate shaped by new historicism it reads as a deliberate wager rather than an oversight. Weis's bet is that this play, of all plays, reaches its readers through its words and its stage life before it reaches them through its contexts, and that an introduction's scarce pages do most good where the play is most itself, in the sonnet the lovers improvise, the timetable they cannot escape, and the performances that keep remaking them.

The wager can be priced. A student who meets the play only in this edition will know exactly how old Juliet is and exactly how the hours run, and will know less than a rival edition's student about why a friar's cell was a plausible confessional for a Veronese daughter or what an English audience heard in a story of Italian feuds. Teachers who need the contexts can supply them from elsewhere, and the commentary's notes on the sources recover part of the historical ground at line level. But the introduction's hierarchy is unmistakable, and it aligns the edition with the rehabilitation's oldest instinct, that the play's power is formal and theatrical before it is documentary. Whether the wager ages well depends on the discipline's weather, which no editor controls. What can be said on the evidence is that Weis made it knowingly, having written a biography steeped in archival context himself, and that a choice made from knowledge carries a different weight from a gap left by ignorance. The introduction's Verona is a city of speeches and schedules rather than of archives, and the edition asks to be judged, finally, on whether that city is the one the play builds. For most of its readers, on the record of the reviews, it has been.

## The Two Chorus Speeches in Weis's Text

No part of the play tests an editor's handling of the witnesses more quietly than its two Chorus speeches, and the record is stranger than most summaries admit. The Prologue sonnet, *Two households, both alike in dignity* (Prologue.1), stands complete at fourteen lines in the second quarto. The first quarto prints it shortened, at twelve lines. The First Folio omits the Prologue altogether, so that a reader of the 1623 collection meets the play without its announced frame of feud, stars, and two hours' traffic. The second Chorus, the sonnet that opens Act 2 with old desire on its deathbed and young affection gaping to be his heir, has the opposite distribution: the second quarto prints it, the first quarto lacks it entirely, and the Folio, which discarded the Prologue, prints this second speech entire in its place, though without the Chorus heading that identifies its speaker. The two speeches, twins in form, thus had separate fortunes in the play's transmission, one cut by the Folio and kept in shortened form by the performed text, the other dropped by the performed text and preserved, unlabelled, by the Folio.

Weis's Q2-based text prints both speeches entire, as his copy-text presents them, and his collations record the witnesses' divided behaviour for any reader who wants the evidence. The choice looks automatic, and in one sense it is, since an editor who has chosen Q2 inherits its contents. But its consequences deserve a sentence, because they show the edition's literary centre of gravity. A text of the play built from the Folio would lack the Prologue's contract with the audience, the promise that frames every later event as already judged. A text built from the first quarto would lack the second Chorus and with it the play's most explicit statement of the desire that has changed hands between Rosaline and Juliet. Weis's text, following the fuller witness, keeps the play's full formal frame, both sonnets standing like gateposts at the entrances of the first two acts. For an editor whose introduction argues from structure, from the calendar and the announced ending, keeping the frame entire is not a default but a position, and it aligns the text with the introduction's method: the speeches that tell the audience how to judge are given the same authority as the scenes they judge. The collation's quiet record, that the Folio cut one speech and kept the other bare of its speaker's name, preserves the counter-evidence for readers who would rather build the play from performance, and the page, as so often in this edition, contains both the decision and the means of dissenting from it.

## Weis on the Play's Language: The Introduction's Poetic Argument

Smith's judgment that poetic analysis is the introduction's strength points to the part of Weis's case that no textual controversy touches. His reading of the play's language begins from its most famous formal event, the sonnet the lovers share at their first meeting in the feast (1.5), in which Romeo's pilgrim conceit and Juliet's answering wit divide fourteen lines between two speakers and end in a kiss that the form itself has negotiated. For Weis the scene is the play in miniature: a received poetic shape, the sonnet of the Petrarchan tradition Romeo has been practising on the absent Rosaline, becomes in Juliet's hands a dialogue, quick, corrective, and shared, so that the play's central linguistic event is the conversion of monologue into exchange. Romeo's early verse is full of the tradition's counters, love as warfare, as religion, as smoke and fire, and it convinces nobody, least of all Mercutio. Juliet's verse does things. Her garden soliloquy interrogates the relation between names and things with a philosopher's precision, asking what a name is and what it purchases, while the man the speech concerns stands below unheard (2.2). Her welcome to the wedding night turns the sun's horses into an impatient prayer (3.2). Her response to the double blow of Tybalt's death and Romeo's banishment breaks into the play's most violent rhetoric, the serpent heart hid with a flowering face, the beautiful tyrant and fiend angelical of her oxymoron storm (3.2), figures Romeo used as decoration now fired as weapons by a speaker in earnest.

The argument from language is also an argument from distribution. As the acts proceed, the long solo speeches migrate. Romeo's great lyric occasions belong to the play's first half, the window speech in the garden, the dawn parting; after the banishment his verse shortens into reaction, the dream report in Mantua (5.1), the apothecary bargain, the final address to the corpse he believes he is joining. Juliet's solos lengthen as his contract: the Nurse's delay endured in the garden (2.5) and after the wedding (3.2), the potion soliloquy of 4.3 in which she argues herself past the fear of waking mad in the vault, the brief terrible clarity of her waking in 5.3. Weis's introduction tracks this migration of the play's inwardness from its hero to its heroine, and it is the load-bearing evidence for the thesis that the play is ultimately hers, stronger evidence than any claim about sympathy, because it can be checked against the text by any reader with a pencil. The dawn parting in 3.5, where the lovers dispute whether the bird they hear is the lark or the nightingale, is for this reading the hinge of the whole design: the play's last fully shared lyric scene, conducted as a formal disputation about evidence and desire, after which the lovers never meet awake again. An introduction that can make a reader hear that scene as a hinge has done the thing introductions exist to do, and it is no accident that the edition's poetic argument, unlike its textual one, attracted no prosecution from its reviewers.

The frame of the whole argument is the play's sonnet architecture, and Weis's introduction is alert to it in a way that binds his textual and poetic cases together. The play opens with a sonnet spoken by the Chorus, proceeds to a second Chorus sonnet at the head of Act 2, and stages its lovers' meeting inside a third, the shared sonnet of the feast, so that the form associated with private lyric longing is successively public, transitional, and dialogic. Then the form disappears. No sonnet frames the later acts; the verse loosens into soliloquy, dialogue, and the broken exchanges of the tomb, and the disappearance can be read as the play's formal plot, lyric order surrendering to event. An editor's text either preserves that architecture entire or quietly damages it, and the witnesses divide exactly as the Chorus section of this article recorded, the Folio dropping the first panel of the triptych, the first quarto losing the second. Weis's Q2 text keeps all three panels standing, and his introduction's reading of the play's language gains its full force only because his text does. The coincidence is the edition in miniature: the bibliographical choice and the critical argument are the same choice, and where Weis is at his best the two cannot be separated, whatever his practice at the smaller cruces suggests about their separation elsewhere.

## The Edition in Use: Study, Classroom, and Rehearsal Room

An Arden volume justifies itself in use, and the physical and commercial facts of Weis's book belong to its criticism. The volume appeared in paperback at £8.99, priced for the student market the series serves, with xviii + 451 pages carrying the introduction, the text, and the facsimile appendix between one set of covers, together with the bibliography and index a working edition requires. That a student can own the whole scholarly apparatus of the play, including one of its two primary witnesses in facsimile, for the price of a paperback novel is a fact about the democratization of editing that the older series never achieved, and reviewers noticed the classroom consequence. Jenny Stevens, writing as a teacher and lecturer, praised a clear and spacious text to read and annotate and the wealth of critical and contextual material the volume puts into students' hands. The spaciousness is not a trivial virtue in a teaching text: commentary that crowds the page teaches students to skip it, while Weis's page invites the eye downward, and generations of undergraduates will learn the play from these notes whether or not they ever learn the editor's name.

The rehearsal room makes a different demand, and the edition meets it through the decisions already examined: directions that carry a performed memory, a fight scene with its full human complement restored, a balcony scene staged at a window on an upper level. Directors do not work from Arden volumes in the way scholars do, but actors and students of performance increasingly do, and an edition whose text embodies the play's staging history serves them as no purely literary text can. The study makes the third demand, for an edition that can be cited, and here the verdict must be more qualified. Weis's text will be cited for decades, because it is the Arden Third, and its departures from its predecessors are recorded honestly enough to be usable. But the scholars who cite it will re-argue its famous cruces as a matter of course, since the edition itself left those arguments under-printed, and in that sense the volume's afterlife in the study is guaranteed to be argumentative in a way its editor's introduction, with its settled and confident readings, is not. The three rooms thus return three versions of the same verdict that the reviewers reached: an instrument of the first quality, built by a judge whose reasons are sometimes better than his record of them.

That triple service is the practical meaning of the phrase current scholarly text. It does not mean the last word on the play, a status no edition of a Shakespeare text has ever held for long. It means the edition through which the play's other students must pass, the one shelved in every seminar room, cited in every bibliography, and argued with in every footnote that proposes a different reading of a crux. Weis's volume holds that position not by acclamation but by equipment: no rival assembles the same text, commentary, source learning, performance archive, and exhibited evidence between two covers at a student's price, and until a successor assembles more, the position is his.

## Verdict: What Weis's Arden Third Series Settles and What It Leaves Open

The brief for this article asked for a verdict on Weis's contribution and on the state of the edited play, and the evidence supports a clear one. Weis settled the first quarto's standing. After his edition, no editor of this play can return to the simple quarantine under which the 1597 text waited outside the scholarly door as a pirate's garble; the performed text is inside the Arden volume now, in its directions, in its readings, and in facsimile at the back, and the burden of proof has shifted permanently onto anyone who would exclude it. He settled, too, the introduction's centre of gravity. The play that schoolrooms met as a joint tragedy of two lovers, or as Romeo's education in love, is presented in the edition a generation of students will use as Juliet's play, and the presentation is argued from the verse closely enough that future introductions will have to answer it rather than ignore it. And he settled the afterlife's place in an edition, not as an appendix of curiosities but as a continuation of the text's own life, continuous with the performed quarto of 1597 at one end and Luhrmann's Verona Beach at the other.

What he leaves open is the theory of his own text. The next editor of the play for the Arden Shakespeare will inherit Weis's evidence, his Juliet, his clock, and his films, and will have to supply the thing his volume withheld: a stated and consistent account of when one witness may correct another, of what an editor owes a duplication, a source, and a ghost. That the inheritance is so rich is the measure of the edition; that the task remains is the measure of its one great refusal. The cliché this series set against Weis was the frozen text, the play embalmed between the covers of a standard edition. His answer was to make the edition a living instrument again, renewed by performance, by evidence exhibited rather than asserted, and by a heroine restored to the centre of her play. On the record examined here, the answer stands.

One further measure of the edition belongs in the verdict, its effect on the play's scholarly common text. An Arden Third volume becomes, by the series' standing, the text from which the play is quoted in journals, taught in universities, and cited in the notes of other editions, and Weis's volume took that place on publication and holds it. The consequence is quiet and large: the words of the play that the scholarly world now repeats are Weis's words, his modernized Q2 saturated with the performed text's directions, his composite line standing in seminar rooms as the Queen Mab speech's received form until a reader checks the collation, his Petruchio speaking in classrooms where the character had been silent for four centuries. Canonization of this kind is never final, and the disagreements recorded in this article guarantee that the edition's text will be argued with for as long as it is used, which is the healthiest condition a scholarly text can be in. But the standing itself is the edition's final fact. Weis set out to give the play a text as alive as its performance history and an introduction worthy of its heroine, and the measure of his success is that the scholarly world now conducts its arguments about *Romeo and Juliet* inside the house he built, collations, facsimile, Juliet and all.

### Q: How is Weis's edition laid out, from introduction to appendix?

### Q: What did Weis's parallel text King Lear teach him about rival texts?

Weis's parallel text *King Lear* taught him that rival early texts are witnesses to be heard, not criminals to be sentenced. Editing that play in 1993, he printed the quarto and Folio versions on facing pages, granted both equal authority in his notes, and concluded after a full survey that the case for wholesale authorial revision between them remained unproved, declaring cautiously for the quarto. An editor formed by that experience could not approach the two quartos of *Romeo and Juliet* with the old reflex that one is sound and the other stolen goods. The *Lear* work explains the Arden Third's defining gesture, the first quarto's restoration to standing, better than any theory Weis states outright.

### Q: What does the appendix of Weis's edition contain, and how is a reader meant to use it?

The appendix contains a facsimile reproduction of the British Library copy of the first quarto of 1597, the shorter early text of the play that Weis treats as the record of the play in performance. A reader is meant to use it as the edition's evidence, not as a curiosity. Pages of the facsimile can be set against the edited text to see what compression for the stage looks like, how the performed version's stage directions carry the action, and where Weis's patches come from. Emma Smith called the reproduction a welcome addition, and its function is verification: the edition argues that the first quarto deserves authority, and then prints the quarto so that the argument can be tested rather than taken on trust.

### Q: Why does Weis keep the second quarto's 'aloft' inside a stage direction he takes from the first quarto?

Weis keeps 'aloft' because the two early texts are strong in different places, and the direction needs both. The first quarto's entry for the garden scene places Romeo and Juliet at the window, a piece of staging memory that gives the scene its physical grammar, while the second quarto's 'aloft' records the vertical fact of the early stage, that Juliet speaks from the upper level. Weis's composite direction preserves the window from the performed text and the height from the fuller text, so the printed staging carries the whole architecture of the original scene. The collation at the foot of the page discloses the join, and the choice exemplifies the edition's method at its most defensible: witnesses completing one another where each is silent alone.

### Q: In what sense does the introduction present the play as Juliet's?

The introduction presents the play as Juliet's in the sense of ownership of its growth, its inward life, and its ending. Weis's argument, summarized by his publisher as the claim that the play is ultimately Juliet's, follows the heroine's language from the shared sonnet of the feast through the garden soliloquy on names, the ecstasy of the wedding night invocation, and the solitary reasoning of the potion speech, and it finds the play's verbal energy accumulating in her voice while Romeo's remains reactive. In the vault she is the one who reads the catastrophe correctly and acts on the reading. The sense is not that Romeo is minor, but that the tragedy's centre of consciousness, and its final competence, belong to the Capulet daughter.

### Q: What does Weis argue from the play's 'tightly plotted and exiguous time-scheme'?

Weis argues from the time-scheme that the play was never travelling toward a comic ending. Laying out the action's schedule from the Sunday morning street melee in late July to the Thursday dawn at the tomb, he shows a plot with no temporal elbow room, in which the feast, the marriage, the murders, and the potion all fall within four days and the catastrophe is announced in the Prologue before the action starts. Against the common account of the play as a comedy that turns tragic at Mercutio's death, Weis sets the calendar: comedy's machinery of blocking parents, go-betweens, and secret weddings is present, but it operates under a pressure that guarantees its failure from the first day.

### Q: What does Weis concede about the Friar's potion and the play's clock?

Weis concedes that the Friar's potion does not live up to its chronological promise. The sleeping draught is specified in the play to hold Juliet in a deathlike trance for two and forty hours (4.1), which ought to carry her from the night she drinks it past the Wednesday wedding to Paris and into Thursday, yet the surrounding references of the final acts compress the interval in ways no timetable can reconcile. Weis prints the concession himself rather than harmonizing the figures away, and the honesty matters to his method: his time-scheme argues from the play's felt compression, not from a pretence that Shakespeare kept perfect accounts, and the admitted crack is allowed to stand as the limit of the realist reading.

### Q: Why did Emma Smith call the edition Janus-faced?

Emma Smith called the edition Janus-faced because its two faces look in opposite directions, toward performance and toward the archive. Writing in *The Review of English Studies*, she found Weis a thoroughly modern editor when thinking about the play's afterlives and performance possibilities, and a curiously old-fashioned one in presenting an eclectic text in the manner of the older Ardens rather than the third series. The image governs a specific indictment: emendations from the first quarto that lack a consistent rationale, a composite line in the Queen Mab speech that no early text prints, a famous quatrain assigned without bibliographic argument, and one word imported from the source poem. Janus-faced, in her usage, praises the introduction while prosecuting the text.

### Q: What did the Times Literary Supplement praise in Weis's edition?

The *Times Literary Supplement* praised the edition's workmanship as a reading and teaching instrument. Its verdict, quoted by the publisher, found both the introduction and the commentary sensitive, sharp-eyed and clear, and it singled out Weis's account of the play's performance history for bringing out the excitement of modern productions by Michael Bogdanov and Tim Carroll and the innovations of Baz Luhrmann's film. The review concluded that this *Romeo and Juliet* will serve well, a judgment aimed at the volume's users rather than at its textual theory. Set beside Emma Smith's prosecution, the praise defines the edition's divided reception: the reviewers who tested its pages against readers acquitted it, while the reviewer who tested its text against principle convicted parts of it.

### Q: Why does Weis print 'ghostly sire' in Romeo's last line of the garden scene when neither early quarto has the word?

Weis prints 'ghostly sire' because the word stands in Arthur Brooke's poem, the play's principal source, at the corresponding moment. The second quarto gives Romeo the line 'Hence will I to my ghostly Friers close cell', and the first quarto reads 'Ghostly fathers Cell', so an editor choosing between witnesses had two defensible texts. Weis instead built a third, taking his syntax from the second quarto and his noun from Brooke, where the hero resolves to seek his ghostly sire for counsel. The import is the edition's most criticized single act. Emma Smith judged that there seems to be no possible reason to intervene in the text in this way, and the criticism stands, since a source is evidence about a play's making, not a witness to its words.

### Q: What line does Weis give to Petruchio, and where had the early print hidden him?

Weis gives Petruchio the line that draws Tybalt away from the street fight in 3.1, the call that earlier editors had printed as part of a stage direction. The early print had hidden him in that direction's italics: the first texts place a companion named Petruchio beside Tybalt at the fight, and the Nurse points the same young man out among the guests leaving the feast (1.5), yet because his one utterance reached print in a form editors took for direction rather than speech, he stood in the dramatis personae of no standard edition. Weis restores the words to dialogue and the man to the cast. The restoration is the eclectic method at its most attractive, recovering a speaker rather than inventing one.

### Q: How are the collations arranged in Weis's edition, and what do they let a reader test?

The collations are arranged at the foot of each page of the text, keyed to line numbers, in the Arden manner. Each entry records the reading Weis prints and the reading of the copy-text he has departed from, with the early witnesses cited by their sigla, so that a reader can reconstruct the second quarto's wording at any contested point without leaving the volume. What they let a reader test is the editor. Every patch from the first quarto, every adopted stage direction, every emendation stands in the collations open to reversal by a reader who judges differently. In an edition whose method is judgment rather than system, the collations are the accountability mechanism, and Weis's willingness to be audited line by line is the strongest answer to the charge that his eclecticism is unprincipled.

### Q: Which screen versions does Weis's introduction read closely, and what work do they do in his argument?

Weis's introduction reads closely Franco Zeffirelli's film of 1968, Baz Luhrmann's *Romeo + Juliet* of 1996, and *West Side Story* on stage and screen. Emma Smith found these readings informed, lively, and appealing, the place where the introduction is most enthusiastic. Their work in his argument is evidentiary. An editor who treats the 1597 quarto as valuable because it records performance is committed to treating later performances as records too, and the films let Weis show what the play becomes under radically different realizations: Zeffirelli's age-true casting recovering the heroine's youth, Luhrmann's modern Verona proving the feud's portability, the musical translating houses into gangs. The screen chapters extend the edition's textual philosophy, that the play lives in its embodiments, into the medium where most readers meet it.

### Q: Why does Weis dwell on the ages of the characters in his introduction?

Weis dwells on the characters' ages because his introduction reads the play with a biographer's respect for fixed points. Juliet's age is one of the few the text fixes exactly: the Nurse counts eleven years from the earthquake to the weaning and places her fourteenth birthday at Lammas Eve, so the heroine of the tragedy is thirteen (1.3). Romeo's age the text never states, and Weis leaves the blank visible rather than filling it from convention. The dwelling serves his larger method. A reading that stresses the time-scheme's compression and the catastrophe's machinery needs its persons sized correctly, and the spectacle of a child of thirteen out-reasoning every adult in Verona is the factual ground under the introduction's largest claim, that the play is hers.

### Q: What is the strongest objection to Weis's way of editing, and how much of it stands?

The strongest objection is Emma Smith's: that Weis's eclectic text lacks a consistent principle of selection, so that readings are chosen from the first or second quarto, or built from both, by literary preference rather than by bibliographic reasoning. Measured against the evidence, most of it stands at the level of the single line. The composite Queen Mab line exists in no early text; the word sire is imported from Brooke; the dawn quatrain is given to Romeo with a one-sentence justification where the crux required an argument. What does not stand is the extension of the objection to the edition as a whole. The stage directions, the facsimile, the commentary, and the introduction obey intelligible principles, and the collations expose every lapse to correction. The objection convicts decisions, not the book.

### Q: What does Weis's commentary discuss besides the meaning of words?

Weis's commentary discusses staging, theme, and the handling of the sources alongside the meanings of words. A note may explain how a speech plays from the upper stage or in a doorway, recover business that the first quarto's directions preserve, trace a speech's ancestry in Brooke's poem or Painter's prose and measure what Shakespeare changed, or follow a thread of imagery across the play from the Prologue to the tomb. The publisher's summary of the edition lists staging, theme, meaning, and sources as the commentary's fields, and the balance among them is the notes' distinction: the reader is never allowed to forget that the words were written for a stage, by a playwright reading other books, inside a design larger than any single line.

### Q: Which of Weis's decisions would a director feel first in rehearsal?

A director would feel first the stage directions Weis imports from the first quarto, since they govern the physical life of scenes before a word is interpreted. Of the two dozen such directions, Weis adopts all but two, so movement, grouping, and properties throughout the printed play carry the memory of the 1597 staging, from the lovers placed at the window with the second quarto's height retained, to the business of the fights and the tomb. Next would come Petruchio's restored line in 3.1, which gives Tybalt's withdrawal from the fight a human agent and changes the scene's choreography at its hinge. The director's edition and the scholar's are the same book here, which is the deepest sense in which Weis's textual choices are performance choices.

### Q: How does Weis present the play's language in his introduction, and why does poetry stand before history in it?

Weis presents the play's language as its primary evidence, the exceptionally patterned verse of sonnet, conceit, and stichomythia through which the lovers' education can be tracked line by line. His publisher describes an introduction that looks closely at the play's beautiful and complex language, and Emma Smith confirms that poetic analysis is the introduction's strength. Poetry stands before history in it because Weis's central claims are linguistic before they are contextual: the case for Juliet rests on the growth of her verse, the case against comedy rests on a calendar extracted from the dialogue's own references. Historical context is correspondingly downplayed, as Smith notes, a deliberate wager that this most reinvented of plays is best introduced through the thing that survives every reinvention, its words.
