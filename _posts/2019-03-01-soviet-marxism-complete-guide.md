Soviet Marxism is the name of two different things, and nearly every error made about the subject begins with treating them as one. The first is an official doctrine, dialectical and historical materialism in codified form, fixed in the late nineteen thirties and taught thereafter as the philosophy of a state and a bloc. The second is a body of original work in philosophy, in the sciences of mind and language, and in the editing of the classic texts, produced in the same country during the same decades, frequently by men and women whom that state arrested, exiled, silenced, or shot. The doctrine was settled by administrative decision. The scholarship was settled, where it was allowed to be settled at all, by argument, and its best products outlived the institutions that tried to control them.

![A reading table holding a party history, a volume of edited manuscripts and a psychology text, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-81.webp)

Hold the two apart and the familiar picture rearranges itself. The state that published the canonical statement of the doctrine also maintained the institute that collected the manuscripts of Marx from archives across Europe and issued the editions on which the Western humanist reading of Marx was built. The founder of that institute was arrested in 1931 and shot in 1938. The state that condemned a school of psychology as bourgeois error preserved, in its own libraries, the texts from which that school was later reconstructed and carried into lecture halls on other continents. A guide to this subject therefore has to do two things at once: state the doctrine fairly, as a set of claims that were actually believed and taught to enormous numbers of people, and give the scholarship its own hearing, with the fates of its authors recorded in the same passages as their contributions, because in this history the two cannot honestly be separated.

What follows is organised by that division. The codification comes first, because the doctrine is what the name meant to most of the people who encountered it. Then come the disputes of the twenties and the manner of their ending, which established the pattern by which intellectual disputes in that country were closed. Then the scholarship itself, field by field, and the institute whose work produced the strangest fact in the whole history, the fact this article calls the archival paradox. The revival that followed the dictator's death takes up the next portion, and the closing sections weigh the strongest objection that can be brought against the entire enterprise, set out what an English reader can and cannot obtain, and correct the misreadings that recur whenever the subject is summarised too quickly.

## Two things under one name

### What is Soviet Marxism?

two distinct things. First, the official doctrine of dialectical and historical materialism, codified in the late 1930s and taught across the Soviet bloc. Second, a body of original scholarship in philosophy, psychology, linguistics, and textual editing produced in the same country, often by people the state persecuted.

The doctrine had an address and an apparatus. It was taught in every university and party school, examined in every humanities degree, printed in textbooks whose contents were approved before publication, and guarded by institutes whose directors understood that a philosophical error could be treated as a political one. Its propositions can be listed, and in the sections below they are listed, because a doctrine believed by millions deserves the courtesy of accurate statement. Its authority, though, did not rest on the persuasiveness of those propositions. It rested on the fact that no rival statement could be published, taught, or defended in a seminar room without risk. Whatever its merits as philosophy, its position in Soviet intellectual life was that of a text with a police escort.

The scholarship had no such escort, and frequently had the opposite. It was produced in institutes and university departments by people working inside the same system of censorship and appointment, and it ranged from the genuinely original to the dutifully conformist, with most of it, as in any national literature, somewhere between. What distinguishes the Soviet case is the violence of the selection applied to it. Schools were abolished by decree. Journals were closed. Scholars were removed from their posts, arrested in the night, and in a number of documented cases executed, sometimes for work that later generations, including later Soviet generations, recognised as the most valuable the country had produced in its field. The record of Soviet thought is therefore not a shelf of books but a shelf with gaps in it, and the gaps are part of the evidence.

### Why does the distinction between doctrine and scholarship matter?

Because the two failed and succeeded in opposite ways, and judging either by the standards of the other produces nonsense. The doctrine succeeded institutionally for half a century and persuaded almost nobody who was free to disagree. The scholarship persuaded readers across the world, often decades after its authors were dead.

Consider what follows from confusing them. If the doctrine is taken as the whole of Soviet Marxism, then the subject is closed: a catechism, memorised and recited, of interest only to historians of propaganda. If the scholarship is taken as the whole, a sentimental picture results, in which a heroic intelligentsia thought freely under a regime that merely happened to be there. Neither picture survives contact with the record. The doctrine was the environment in which the scholarship breathed, setting the vocabulary it had to use, the questions it was permitted to ask, and the conclusions it was forbidden to reach. The scholarship, in turn, is the reason the doctrine's country cannot be treated as an intellectual desert. Both statements are true together, and the interest of the subject lies in holding them together.

There is also a Western stake in the distinction, and it is larger than is usually admitted. The Marx who was read in Paris, Frankfurt, and Berkeley after the middle of the century, the young humanist Marx of alienation and praxis, was reconstructed from manuscripts that a Moscow institute had gathered, edited, and issued in 1932. The tradition that came to define itself against Soviet orthodoxy, and that treated the codified doctrine as the antithesis of everything it valued, built its founding readings on Soviet editorial labour. That dependence is not a debating point. It is a bibliographical fact, and it changes how the map of twentieth-century Marxism has to be drawn.

## The first codification: a textbook and its fate

The codification did not begin with the text that completed it. Its first systematic statement was a textbook of historical materialism published in 1921 by Nikolai Bukharin, then among the most authoritative theorists of the ruling party. The book attempted something the doctrine later abandoned: a sociology, written in a materialist vocabulary, that treated social formations as systems in equilibrium and disturbance, borrowing analogies from the natural sciences of its day. It was attacked at once, by critics who found its mechanics too crude for a dialectical philosophy, and the attack is instructive, because at that date the attack was conducted in print, by argument, between named opponents who expected to answer and be answered. Within a decade the same kind of disagreement would be closed by resolution. Within two, the textbook's author would be shot after a public trial, and his book condemned as the work of an enemy.

### Who wrote the first systematic textbook of historical materialism?

Nikolai Bukharin, a leading party theorist of the revolutionary generation, published the first systematic Soviet textbook of the doctrine in 1921. It presented society as a system of forces and relations in shifting equilibrium. Later condemned, its author was executed after a show trial in 1938.

The trajectory of that single book contains the codification in miniature. Written when the new state still tolerated, and even required, theoretical argument among its own intellectuals, it became a standard text, then a suspect one, then evidence. Its author's fate is usually told as political history, and so it is; but it is also a fact about the doctrine, because a philosophy whose textbooks become criminal evidence has changed its nature, whatever its propositions say. The propositions of historical materialism, as the series treats them elsewhere, can be examined on their merits. What the Soviet codification added was not a proposition but a procedure: the procedure by which one statement of the doctrine was declared correct and others were withdrawn from circulation.

Between the textbook of 1921 and the canonical text of 1938, the doctrine passed through the controversies described below, and those controversies matter to the codification because they determined what the canonical text would contain and, more importantly, what it would omit. The text that emerged was short, lucid, and closed. It did not argue with opponents; it did not acknowledge that the questions it answered had ever been open. Generations of students met Marxism for the first time in its pages and reasonably concluded that Marxism was a finished system, a set of results to be learned rather than a set of problems to be worked on. That impression, carried into universities and party schools across a continent, is the doctrine's largest historical effect, larger than any single proposition it contained.

## The canonical text and what it claimed

The canonical statement appeared in September 1938 as a chapter of the History of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union (Bolsheviks): Short Course, the party history attributed to a commission of the Central Committee and edited under the closest supervision of Joseph Stalin, who was credited with the philosophical chapter. The chapter bore the title Dialectical and Historical Materialism, and it did in some thirty pages what the controversies of the previous decade had failed to do: it ended them, by providing the authorised answers. Its circulation, as the historian Robert Service records in his biography of Stalin, was among the largest achieved by any book of the century, and it was translated, taught, and examined wherever the Soviet model was installed. The doctrine it set out is examined in its own right in the series guide to [dialectical materialism](/2012/07/01/dialectical-materialism-complete-guide/); what matters here is its form and its claims as a codification.

### What did the codified doctrine actually claim?

The chapter set out four features of the dialectical method, concerning universal connection, motion, the passage of quantity into quality, and internal contradiction, and three features of philosophical materialism, concerning the primacy of matter, its knowability, and the reflection of the world in consciousness.

Around those features the chapter arranged the rest of the system. History was presented as the succession of five formations, primitive communal, slave, feudal, capitalist, and socialist, each arising from the development of productive forces and the relations corresponding to them. The laws of the dialectic, drawn from Engels and from Lenin's notebooks, were stated as laws of nature and society alike, so that the same pattern could be exhibited in a chemical process, a geological stratum, and a revolution. The theory of knowledge was a reflection theory: consciousness copies a material world that exists independently of it, and practice is the criterion by which the copy is checked. Nothing in the system was tentative. Where the philosophical literature of the twenties had spoken of problems, the chapter spoke of features, and the change of noun is the codification in a single word.

Assess the claims fairly and two things can be said. As an exposition for beginners, the chapter had real virtues: it was orderly, it connected philosophy to history and politics, and it gave a reader with no training a map of a large territory. Against that, its method of statement abolished the distinction between a conclusion and a command. A feature of the dialectical method was not something a student might test against a difficult case; it was something a student might be examined on, and behind the examination stood the apparatus that had already decided what befell scholars who stated the matter differently. The codification of the doctrine, and the detailed history of how its author wielded it, belong to the companion article on [Stalin and Marxism-Leninism](/2019/03/15/stalin-and-marxism-leninism/); here it is enough to mark the form. A philosophy that began, in the hands of its founders, as a critique of all received authority had been restated as received authority, complete with numbered features.

### How did a textbook become a catechism?

By acquiring sanctions. A textbook becomes a catechism when departure from its formulations is treated not as error but as disloyalty, when its phrases are the required currency of publication, and when the institutions that teach it also police it. Each of those conditions was deliberately constructed.

The construction was administrative before it was intellectual. Appointments to chairs and institutes passed through party organs. Journals had editors answerable for the line of what they printed. Curricula were approved centrally. A scholar who wished to publish on any philosophical question learned to begin from the authorised formulations and to exhibit agreement with them, whatever the argument that followed, and the habit of exhibition, repeated across a literature, is what turned a chapter of a party history into the effective philosophy of a state. None of this required that the formulations be absurd. Several were respectable summaries of positions with honourable pedigrees in the Marxist tradition. The catechism effect came from the monopoly, not from the content, and it is the monopoly that a reader has to keep in view when weighing anything the system produced, the bad and the good alike.

## The philosophical controversies of the nineteen twenties

The decade before the codification was not an intellectual wasteland awaiting enlightenment from above. It was the most argumentative decade in the history of Marxist philosophy, and its arguments were real. Two broad camps contested the foundations of the doctrine in the journals and institutes of Moscow. On one side stood the mechanists, heirs of the materialism of the nineteenth century, who held that the sciences were converging on a single picture of the world as matter in motion, that philosophy should dissolve into the results of those sciences, and that the dialectic, where it was not a summary of scientific method, was an Hegelian residue the tradition could discard. On the other stood the dialecticians, gathered around Abram Deborin, a philosopher of formidable learning who had edited and introduced the works of Hegel and who insisted that the dialectic was the living core of the doctrine, that the categories of thought had their own structure and history, and that a materialism without dialectics collapsed into the very metaphysics it claimed to oppose.

### What divided the dialecticians from the mechanists?

The mechanists held that natural science was dissolving philosophy into a single materialist picture of matter in motion, making the dialectic a dispensable Hegelian survival. The dialecticians, around Deborin, held that dialectical categories had their own structure and that materialism without them relapsed into metaphysics.

Each camp had serious people and serious arguments. The mechanists could point to the prestige of physics and physiology and ask what a category like the negation of the negation added to a laboratory result. The dialecticians could reply that the sciences themselves generated concepts, contradiction, emergence, the transformation of quantity into quality in phase transitions and chemical combination, which no mere inventory of particles described, and that a philosophy which simply registered scientific results would be a clerk, not a critic. The dispute ran through the journal Under the Banner of Marxism, through the Communist Academy and the Institute of Red Professors, and through the training of a generation. It was, by the standards of any philosophical culture, a good dispute: well informed, sharply posed, and connected to live questions in the sciences it discussed.

Its defect was not intellectual. Its defect was that it took place inside institutions owned by a party that regarded philosophy as a branch of its own authority, and that both camps, at moments of difficulty, reached for that authority as a weapon. The mechanists denounced the dialecticians as idealists, a charge with political resonance. The dialecticians denounced the mechanists as vulgarisers and revisionists, charges with the same resonance in reverse. Each side was, in effect, inviting the arbiter who would eventually abolish the game. When the invitation was accepted, it was accepted against both.

The settlement came in stages, and the stages are worth distinguishing because they became the template. A conference of Marxist-Leninist research institutions in 1929 criticised both camps, the mechanists for dissolving philosophy, the dialecticians for detaching it from practice and from politics. Then, in 1930, a group of younger philosophers associated with Mark Mitin and Pavel Yudin attacked Deborin's school directly, in the press, as a deviation. In January 1931 the Central Committee issued a resolution on the Banner, condemning the Deborin school under the label of Menshevizing idealism, a phrase that fused a philosophical judgement with the name of a defeated political faction. Deborin was removed from his editorial position. The dispute was over, not because an argument had prevailed, but because an institution had spoken.

### Why did neither side of the philosophical dispute win?

Because the arbiter was not a third philosophical position but the party apparatus, which installed its own spokesmen and converted the questions at issue into matters of loyalty. The mechanists were condemned for dissolving philosophy; the dialecticians were condemned for scholastic detachment from political practice.

Deborin himself survived, which distinguishes his fate from those recorded later in this article. He worked on, in diminished circumstances, published on the history of philosophy, and died in 1963, having outlived the dictator who had licensed his disgrace. Several of his associates were less fortunate in the decade that followed. But the survival of the man should not obscure the death of the procedure. After 1931, no philosophical dispute in the Soviet Union could be confident of ending in an argument. Every participant in every later controversy knew that the file could be closed from outside, and that knowledge altered what could be said at the start. A scholar deciding how to phrase a conclusion was also deciding how the conclusion would read to an official who might end the discussion, and the career, with a resolution. This is the condition the strongest objection, examined below, takes as its text.

The pattern generalised quickly, because it was efficient. A leadership that had discovered it could settle philosophy by resolution had no reason to spare the other disciplines, and the nineteen thirties and forties supplied a series of settlements, each announced as a discussion, each concluded by authority. Philosophy was reorganised around the canonical text of 1938. Literary doctrine was fixed by congress and decree. In the sciences the settlements reached their most destructive form, and it is there that the cost of the procedure can be measured most exactly, because in the sciences a false settlement does not merely silence an opinion; it stops a harvest, a laboratory, a line of research that the rest of the world continues.

## Settlement by decree: the pattern at work

### How did the pattern of settlement reach biology?

Through a campaign that presented itself as a scientific controversy and ended as an administrative act. In August 1948 a session of the Lenin Academy of Agricultural Sciences, addressed by Trofim Lysenko, declared genetics a bourgeois pseudoscience and Michurinist biology the sole authorised doctrine.

Lysenko was an agronomist who promised rapid improvements in yields through the inheritance of acquired characteristics and the treatment of seeds, and who understood, as his opponents fatally did not, that the route to victory ran through the favour of the leadership rather than through the judgement of biologists. His report to the 1948 session was approved by the Central Committee before delivery, a fact announced to the assembled scientists, which converted the session from a debate into a ratification. Genetics was removed from curricula. Laboratories were closed or redirected. Geneticists lost their posts, and some lost more than their posts. Nikolai Vavilov, the greatest of them, a collector of seed varieties whose expeditions had built one of the world's great gene banks in Leningrad, had been arrested in 1940 and died in a prison in Saratov in 1943, starved in custody while his science was being prepared for abolition. His fate belongs in the same passage as his contribution, because the two are one fact: the state destroyed the man and the discipline together, and Soviet biology required a generation to recover ground that genetics elsewhere had never lost.

The affair is the purest case of the pattern because its subject matter was checkable. The claims of Michurinist biology could be tested in fields and laboratories, and they failed the tests, year after year, in harvests that did not improve. Yet the settlement held for sixteen years, until the fall of the political patron who protected it, and it held not because the evidence shifted but because the settlement had never rested on evidence. A dispute closed by decree can only be reopened by decree, and the reopening, when it came in the middle nineteen sixties, was likewise an act of authority, a change of personnel at the top rather than a verdict delivered by the scientific community. Soviet genetics revived, but it revived as a ward of the same procedure that had destroyed it.

### Why was cybernetics first denounced and then embraced?

Because the test applied to a new science was not whether it worked but whether its vocabulary could be reconciled with the doctrine, and that test could be answered either way by the officials administering it. Cybernetics failed the test, then passed it, without changing its content.

In the early nineteen fifties, the science of control and communication founded by Norbert Wiener was attacked in the Soviet press as a reactionary pseudoscience, a mechanistic ideology of imperialism; the Short Philosophical Dictionary of 1954 carried the denunciation into reference form. Within a few years the same discipline was being championed by mathematicians and engineers, Sobolev, Lyapunov, and Kitov among them, who published in the journal Problems of Philosophy a defence arguing that the theory of information and feedback was a science like any other and fully compatible with materialism. The military's appetite for computing did the rest. Within ten years cybernetics had institutes, journals, and a council of the Academy of Sciences. Nothing in Wiener's theory had altered. What had altered was the administrative classification, and the episode deserves its place beside the genetics catastrophe precisely because its outcome was benign: it shows that the procedure, not any particular verdict, was the constant. A system that can denounce a science and then adopt it, on grounds unrelated to its truth, has placed the classification of knowledge in the hands of people for whom truth is one consideration among others, and rarely the deciding one.

The linguistics settlement of 1950 displays the pattern from its other side, the side on which authority intervened against an entrenched orthodoxy and, by accident of the argument, in favour of the better position. For two decades the dominant doctrine in Soviet linguistics had been the Japhetic theory of Nikolai Marr, which treated language as a superstructure determined by class and destined to change with the social order. Marr's followers held the chairs and the institutes. In the summer of 1950 the dictator himself published a series of articles in Pravda, under the title On Marxism in Linguistics, declaring that language was not a superstructure, was not class-bound in Marr's sense, and served the whole of society. The Marr school collapsed overnight. Linguists who had built careers on the doctrine recanted in print within weeks.

### What happened when the leader intervened in linguistics?

The dominant school of Nikolai Marr, which had treated language as a class superstructure, was abolished by a newspaper intervention from the head of the state in 1950. Its adherents recanted within weeks, and the discipline was reorganised around the propositions the intervention had laid down.

Nothing in the episode vindicates the procedure. The propositions laid down in 1950 were, in the judgement of most linguists, closer to the truth than the doctrine they displaced, but they were established by exactly the method that had established Marr: occupancy of the commanding heights, followed by an authoritative text. A Georgian linguist, Arnold Chikobava, had helped to brief the intervention against the school that employed him, and the sequel was a discipline freed from one orthodoxy by the imposition of another, milder one. The moral the Soviet scholars themselves drew was not that authority had erred in one case and succeeded in another. It was that no position, however established, however absurd, however sound, was safe from settlement, and no scholar could know in advance whether the settlement would fall on the side of sense. Under such conditions the rational strategy was caution, and caution, generalised across a whole intellectual culture, is a tax on every thought it produces.

Other settlements of the period can be listed more briefly, because the pattern needs no further proof. A joint session of two academies in 1950 imposed a regimented version of Pavlovian physiology on medicine and psychology. A conference of 1951 condemned the resonance theory in chemistry as idealist. An organised discussion of political economy in 1951 prepared the dictator's final economic text, which fixed the doctrine of the economic laws of socialism for the remaining years of his life. In each case the form was identical: a discussion announced, contributions solicited, and a conclusion that had been determined by the distribution of power among the participants and their patrons. The scholars involved understood the form perfectly. Many of the contributions to these discussions were written by men who knew exactly what conclusion was required and supplied the arguments for it, a fact that the strongest objection to Soviet scholarship presses hard, and fairly.

## The doctrine and the scholarship

The argument of this article can be put in a single table. The doctrine and the scholarship were products of the same state, the same decades, and often the same buildings, and they differ in almost every respect that matters for judging them.

| Question | The official doctrine | The scholarship |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How was it produced? | By codification: authorised texts, approved curricula, and formulations settled through party institutions | By research: in institutes, seminars, and editorial projects, by scholars arguing with sources and with each other |
| How were its disputes settled? | By administrative decision: resolutions, editorial removals, and the withdrawal of permission to publish | Where settlement was permitted, by argument and evidence; where it was not, by the same administrative decision applied from outside |
| What did it contribute? | A common vocabulary and a taught system for a bloc, and a map of the tradition for millions of first readers | Original work in psychology, the philosophy of language, value theory, and the editing of the Marxist classics |
| What happened to its authors? | Its codifiers held office and were protected while their line held | A striking number were arrested, exiled, silenced, or shot, several for the work now judged their best |

Read the last row slowly, because it is the row that resists summary. The doctrine's authors were functionaries, and their safety rose and fell with their standing. The scholarship's authors were, in case after case, destroyed by the system whose culture they were enriching, and destroyed not despite their eminence but in several instances through it, since eminence attracted the attention that settled disputes. Any comparison between the scholarship of this tradition and the scholarship produced in the West during the same decades has to state its terms before it begins, or it compares nothing. The comparison made in this article is between the output of a state-directed institutional system, employing scholars on salaries, directing their themes, and punishing their deviations, and the output of individuals and small groups in the universities of other countries, over the same decades, under constraints of fashion, funding, and career that were real but not lethal. Quantity, freedom, and quality can each be discussed across that comparison, but only with the conditions attached. A verdict that omits them, whether it exalts the Soviet achievement or dismisses it, has not performed a comparison at all.

## The scholarship: the psychology of the social formation of mind

Lev Vygotsky died in 1934, of tuberculosis, at the age of thirty-seven, and the school he founded in Moscow in the nineteen twenties had roughly a decade of freedom in which to work. What it produced in that decade became, after a long suppression, one of the most influential bodies of psychological theory of the century. Vygotsky's claim, developed with Alexander Luria and Alexei Leontiev and a circle of younger researchers, was that the higher functions of mind are not the unfolding of a biological programme but the interiorisation of social processes: the child takes in, from interaction with others and above all through language, the tools with which thought is then conducted. Thought and Language, published in the year of his death, argued that word meaning is the unit in which this process can be studied, and introduced the zone of proximal development, the distance between what a learner can do alone and what the same learner can do in collaboration, as the proper measure of intellectual growth.

### What did the psychologist of the cultural historical school establish?

That the higher mental functions are formed socially before they are exercised individually: the child internalises the speech and practices of others, above all through language, and only then conducts thought with those borrowed tools. The claim reorganised the study of learning around collaboration.

The fate belongs beside the contribution. Vygotsky died before the worst years, but his school did not escape them. A decree of 1936 against pedology, the child-study movement with which his work was entangled, abolished the institutional setting of the research and drove his writings from circulation; his books were not republished in his own country for twenty years. His collaborators dispersed into safer topics, neuropsychology in Luria's case, the theory of activity in Leontiev's, and the school survived the way Soviet scholarship so often survived, in fragments, in students' memories, and in manuscripts kept against a better season. The better season came after the dictator's death. Editions appeared from 1956, translations followed, and Western developmental psychology, arriving independently at the limits of purely individual accounts of learning, found in Vygotsky a theory that had been waiting for it for a generation. Jerome Bruner championed the work in America; Michael Cole and his collaborators carried the texts into English and tested the claims against field research. The validation matters for the argument of this article, and it will return when the strongest objection is weighed: here is work produced inside the system, suppressed by the system, and confirmed by researchers who owed the system nothing and worked under no instruction to confirm it.

Vygotsky described himself as a Marxist and meant it as a methodological commitment: the mind was to be explained historically, by its formation in social practice, rather than by introspection or by reflex alone. Whether the theory stands or falls with that commitment is a question psychologists have debated; what is not debatable is that the theory was produced, in a Soviet institute, in the last years when such production was still possible on that scale, and that its author's posthumous treatment, suppression at home and celebration abroad, inverts the usual story told about the doctrine. The doctrine was exported by the state and believed by few who could choose. The psychology was suppressed by the state and adopted by many who could.

## The scholarship: the philosophy of language and the circle

In 1929 a Leningrad scholar named Valentin Voloshinov published Marxism and the Philosophy of Language, a study that treated the word as a social event. Its argument was directed against both of the reigning accounts of language: against the abstract objectivism that treated language as a finished system of forms, and against the individualistic subjectivism that treated it as the expression of an inner life. Against both, the book proposed that the sign is material, social, and ideological through and through, that consciousness itself is bathed in signs borrowed from intercourse with others, and that the proper object of a philosophy of language is the utterance in its social situation. The book appeared from within the circle of Mikhail Bakhtin, the literary theorist whose studies of Dostoevsky and of Rabelais later acquired a worldwide readership, and the question of how much of the circle's published work Bakhtin himself drafted has occupied scholars ever since. The dispute over authorship is genuine and unresolved, and it does not need resolution here: what matters is that a Soviet circle, working in the last years of relative openness, produced a philosophy of language that the Western human sciences absorbed, decades later, as a founding text of the study of discourse.

### Who wrote the philosophy of language that came from the Bakhtin circle?

The book of 1929 appeared under the name of Valentin Voloshinov, a member of the circle around the literary theorist Mikhail Bakhtin, and scholars continue to dispute how the drafting was divided between them. The study treats the word as a social and ideological event.

The fates, again, belong in the passage. Voloshinov died of tuberculosis in 1936, in obscurity. Bakhtin was arrested in 1929 and exiled to Kazakhstan, spent years in provincial posts and in internal banishment, lost a leg to illness, and saw his major work published only in the nineteen sixties, when a group of younger Moscow scholars pressed for his recovery. He died in 1975, famous at last in his own country and beginning to be famous abroad. The circle's work survived the way the manuscripts of an earlier century survived, in single copies and private keeping: the Rabelais study, completed before the war, was defended as a dissertation amid official hostility and printed a quarter of a century after it was written. A reader who asks what Soviet conditions did to scholarship should be shown this case alongside the executions, because it represents the commoner injury: not death, but delay, the deferral of a life's work past the lifetime of its author and into a world that received it as a discovery.

## The scholarship: value theory and its author's end

Isaak Rubin worked at the Marx-Engels Institute in Moscow, and his Essays on Marx's Theory of Value, first published in 1923 and revised through the decade, is the founding text of what later scholarship calls value-form theory. Rubin's argument recovered a dimension of the opening chapters of Capital that both orthodox economists and orthodox Marxists had neglected: that value is not a property embodied in things but a social form, that abstract labour is constituted in exchange rather than merely measured by it, and that the fetishism of commodities is not a decorative chapter but the key to the whole analysis. The reading was contested in its day by economists who found it too philosophical, too Hegelian, too little concerned with the quantitative questions they regarded as the theory's core. It was, in the strict sense, a scholarly dispute about a text, conducted in journals and seminars.

### What did the value theorist contribute before his arrest?

A reading of the opening of Capital that restored the theory of the value form to the centre of the analysis: value as a social form constituted in exchange, abstract labour as its substance, and commodity fetishism as the key to the whole rather than an ornament.

Rubin was arrested in December 1930, in the widening circles of accusation that preceded the public trials, and was shot in November 1937. His book disappeared from Soviet discussion; to cite it favourably was dangerous, and the interpretation of value theory that the codified doctrine required had no room for it. The recovery came from outside and from below. In the nineteen sixties and seventies, as a new generation in several countries returned to the opening chapters of Capital with philosophical questions, Rubin's essays were found to have asked those questions first and answered them with a precision the new literature had to match. The English translation of 1972 carried the work into the debates of Western Marxist economics, where the value-form reading became one of the live options in the interpretation of Marx, a position argued over by scholars who in many cases learned only later what had happened to the man who founded it. Here the pattern of the cultural historical school repeats with a grimmer ending: production under the system, destruction by the system, validation by strangers.

## The institute and the manuscripts: the archival paradox

In 1921 the Soviet government founded, in Moscow, an institute devoted to the collection and publication of the works of Marx and Engels. Its director, David Ryazanov, was a scholar of the old revolutionary generation, a man who had spent years in the archives and libraries of Europe before the revolution and who understood, as few of his contemporaries did, that the Marxist tradition possessed no reliable edition of its founders' writings: texts were scattered, excerpts circulated in corrupted forms, and whole manuscripts lay unpublished in the custody of the German social democratic party. Ryazanov set out to remedy this with an energy that became legendary among archivists. He negotiated, purchased, photographed, and copied. Agents of the institute worked in Berlin, Paris, London, and Amsterdam. Materials that could not be bought were photographed page by page, so that Moscow accumulated a working archive of the tradition's papers more complete than any single collection in the West.

### What did the institute collect, and from where?

The unpublished manuscripts, notebooks, correspondence, and annotated books of Marx and Engels, gathered from party archives, libraries, and private hands across Europe, much of it photographed page by page when the originals could not be purchased, until Moscow held the tradition's most complete working archive.

From this archive Ryazanov launched the first historical-critical edition of the complete works, the Marx-Engels Gesamtausgabe, whose volumes began to appear in 1927 to the respect of scholars who shared none of the institute's politics. The standards were those of the best European editorial practice: complete texts, variants recorded, chronology established, commentary restrained. And in 1932 the institute's labours delivered the two publications that changed the reading of Marx everywhere. The Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844, written by the twenty-six-year-old Marx in Paris and never published in his lifetime, appeared in the edition, and in the same year The German Ideology, the great unpublished polemic of 1845 and 1846 in which historical materialism was first worked out, was printed in full. The young Marx, the philosopher of alienation, estranged labour, and species life, entered the tradition's bloodstream through a Moscow edition.

The Western response began at once. Herbert Marcuse, then a young philosopher in Germany, reviewed the manuscripts in 1932 and recognised immediately that they altered the foundations of the doctrine: here was a Marx whose categories were philosophical and humanist before they were economic. The line runs from that review through the Marx of Erich Fromm's study of 1961, through the Parisian readings of the postwar decades, through the Yugoslav Praxis school and the Budapest school and the whole humanist reconstruction that defined Western Marxism against what it took to be Soviet dogma. Every stage of that line rests on texts that Western readers possessed because a Soviet institute had collected and published them. The editions were not propaganda. They were scholarship of the first rank, performed to standards the tradition's Western custodians had not attempted, and they were performed by an institution of the Soviet state.

Ryazanov's fate completes the fact. He was a scholar with a tongue: he quarrelled with the leadership, defended the independence of his institute, and made remarks about the dictator that were repeated. In February 1931 he was arrested, in the penumbra of the case being constructed against a supposed Menshevik centre, and exiled to Saratov. The institute he had built was merged into a larger Marx-Engels-Lenin Institute under a safer director, and the edition slowed to the pace of political convenience. Ryazanov was arrested again in the Terror and shot in January 1938. The manuscripts he had rescued remained in the archive he had assembled. The edition he had founded was eventually resumed, and in the nineteen seventies a second critical edition, prepared with the participation of institutes in Moscow and Berlin and later stewarded by an international foundation, took up the work on the basis of the collections he had made.

### What ended the founder's work at the institute he built?

Arrest. The director who had assembled the archive and launched the critical edition was seized in 1931, exiled to Saratov, and shot in 1938, while the institute was merged under safer management and its editorial programme slowed to the pace political convenience allowed.

This is the fact this article names the archival paradox, and it is worth stating without decoration. The manuscripts on which Western Marxist humanism was built were collected, edited, and published by a Soviet institute whose founder the Soviet state executed. The tradition that defined itself against Soviet Marxism depended on Soviet scholarship for its founding texts. The paradox is not a rhetorical flourish; it is a problem for every simple map of the century's thought. It means that the line between the doctrine and the scholarship runs through the middle of Western Marxism itself, since the West's most cherished alternative to the doctrine was printed in Moscow. It means that the Soviet state's relation to the tradition it claimed to embody was double from the start: custodian and executioner in one institution, sometimes in one building. And it means that the assessment of Soviet scholarship cannot proceed by assessing the regime and transferring the verdict. The transfer fails on the evidence of the archive itself. The textual basis of the modern reading of Marx is treated in depth in the series guide to the [MEGA2 and the Marx manuscripts](/2013/07/01/mega2-marx-manuscripts-guide/).

## The fates, gathered

The cases recorded above were chosen because their work mattered internationally, but the pattern they exhibit was general, and a guide that confined itself to the famous would misrepresent it. Across the disciplines, the settlement of disputes by authority and the terror that followed it consumed scholars in numbers this article, by its own protocol, does not total. The protocol deserves a word of explanation. Figures for the victims of the purges in intellectual life are contested, assembled from archives opened unevenly and from records kept by the apparatus that did the work, and an article that declined to print a number it could not warrant would be practising the same discipline it recommends to students of the economic record. What can be stated without a number is the shape of the thing: that in philosophy, economics, history, linguistics, biology, and literary study, leading figures were arrested; that some were shot, some died in camps and prisons, some survived to be rehabilitated, in a number of cases posthumously and in a few cases in time to resume their work; and that the survivors carried the knowledge of what had happened into every seminar they later taught.

### What became of the scholars whose disputes were settled by authority?

The range ran from disgrace to death. Some lost posts and editorial chairs and lived on in obscurity; some were exiled inside their own country; some were arrested and shot; some died in custody. Rehabilitation, where it came, came by the same authority that had condemned them.

Certain features of the pattern reward attention because they explain the texture of what followed. The first is randomness at the margin: eminence was no protection, obscurity was no protection, loyalty was no protection, and the files show scholars denouncing colleagues in the morning and arrested by the winter, which taught everyone that the rules of survival could not be learned. The second is the survival of schools through persons: a teacher's students, scattered to provincial institutes, kept manuscripts, methods, and reading lists alive in private, so that when conditions eased, the reconstruction of a field could begin from living memory rather than from books alone. The third is the double character of rehabilitation, which restored names without restoring the procedure that had destroyed them: a scholar could be declared innocent by the same kind of act that had declared him guilty, and the declaration, in both directions, was a decision rather than a finding. Soviet intellectual life after the terror was thus built on ground that everyone knew could move again, and the caution that knowledge produced is the single most important fact for assessing the work of the later decades.

## The doctrine in the classroom

To understand what the codified doctrine was, follow it into a lecture hall. From the late nineteen thirties until the end of the state, every student in every Soviet university, whatever the faculty, passed through courses in dialectical and historical materialism, and the same requirement, modelled on the same texts, was installed across the states of the bloc after the war. The lecturer worked from an approved programme. The textbook literature, of which the successive editions associated with the names of Konstantinov and others are representative, expanded the canonical chapter into volumes: the four features became chapters, the three features became chapters, the five formations became a course in themselves. Examinations tested the formulations. A candidate's grasp of the quantity-quality law was demonstrated by producing the authorised examples, water heated to steam, the accumulation of capital, the leap of the revolution, in the authorised order.

### How was the doctrine actually taught?

As a finished system to be reproduced. Lecturers worked from approved programmes and textbooks that swelled the canonical chapter into volumes, and examinations rewarded the accurate restatement of authorised formulations and examples rather than the testing of those formulations against difficult cases.

Two judgements on this vast pedagogical enterprise are possible, and both are partly true. The first is the judgement of its critics: that it was an indoctrination machine, and that its product, in the philosophical sense, was not belief but fluency, the ability to write and speak the dialect without inward conviction, a skill whose hollowness became visible when the system ended and the vocabulary evaporated within a few years. The second judgement is less often heard and should not be dismissed: the enterprise put a connected account of philosophy, history, and society, however rigid, into the hands of millions of first-generation students, peasants' and workers' children, who encountered through it the names of Heraclitus and Hegel, the concept of a social formation, the very idea that history has a structure that can be argued about. Some fraction of those students were stung by the ideas the catechism was embalming, and went looking for the originals. The post-Stalin revival described below was staffed by exactly such readers, men and women who had met the doctrine as a school subject and refused to leave it there.

The bloc dimension matters for the same reason. The doctrine in its codified form was the philosophy of a system of states, examined in Warsaw and Budapest and Prague in translations of the same textbooks, and the internal critics of those states, whose story belongs to the series article on [actually existing socialism in Europe](/2019/06/01/actually-existing-socialism-europe/), were formed in argument with it: they had been taught the five formations and the reflection theory by the state they criticised, and their criticism frequently proceeded by taking the doctrine's promises more literally than its custodians did. A doctrine that is taught to everyone becomes, among other things, a common language in which the regime can be indicted. That unintended consequence belongs in any honest account of what the codification did.

## The fields at the edge of politics

The settlement procedure fell with very unequal weight on the different disciplines, and the inequality is the key to the map of what Soviet scholarship achieved. Where a field's conclusions bore on the legitimacy of the order, the settlement was swift and total: history, political economy, and philosophy in its political reaches lived under direct supervision. Where a field's objects were remote from legitimation, where its methods were technical and its results checkable by practitioners, the constraint bound more loosely, and in the interstices some remarkable work was done. The gradient was never announced and never stable, and scholars learned to read it the way sailors read weather. But its existence explains why the same decades that produced the genetics catastrophe also produced work in mathematics, logic, and parts of psychology that the world still uses.

The emblematic case is an economist-mathematician. In 1939 Leonid Kantorovich, a young Leningrad mathematician consulting for a plywood trust, published a pamphlet on the mathematical method of production planning, in which he set out the technique later known as linear programming: the allocation of scarce resources among competing uses by the solution of a system of linear relations, with shadow prices, his resolving multipliers, emerging from the mathematics itself. The work was, in substance, a contribution to the theory of planning of the first importance, produced inside the planned economy at the moment of its consolidation. It was also, in the eyes of the doctrine's custodians, deeply suspect, since its multipliers behaved like prices and its logic resembled the marginal analysis the doctrine had condemned as bourgeois. Kantorovich was not arrested; he was something quieter and almost as effective. He was marginalised, steered toward safer mathematics, and his economic work slept for twenty years, until the thaw permitted its republication and development. In 1975 he received the Nobel memorial prize in economics, jointly with an American who had reached related results independently, for the theory of optimal allocation of resources. The planned economy had generated the mathematics of its own rationalisation and then declined, for a generation, to use it.

### Where did the constraint on scholarship bind least?

In the fields whose objects stood furthest from the legitimation of the order and whose results could be checked by practitioners: mathematics and mathematical economics at their most technical, textual editing, parts of psychology and logic, where conclusions could be verified without asking what they implied for the doctrine.

Logic tells the same story in a smaller compass. Formal logic had been denounced in the twenties as a bourgeois survival and removed from the schools, dialectics being held to supersede it. In the nineteen forties the subject was quietly restored to the curriculum, by administrative decision once again, and a generation of logicians, among whom Sofya Yanovskaya in Moscow edited the logical writings of Marx and trained students in the new mathematical logic, built a field that by the sixties was in full conversation with its Western counterpart. Alexander Zinoviev's work on many-valued logic and the logic of scientific knowledge belonged to this revival before its author's satirical writings on Soviet life made him an exile. The pattern in each case is identical: a field condemned as ideology, restored as technique, and fruitful exactly to the extent that its practitioners could present their results as instruments rather than as claims about the world the doctrine governed.

Psychology occupied a middle position on the gradient, close enough to the doctrine's concerns to be supervised, distant enough in its laboratory practice to produce the cultural historical school's work before the supervision tightened. Textual editing stood at the far end: the collation of manuscripts and the establishment of chronologies could be represented, and in Ryazanov's institute were represented, as a service to the tradition that no vigilance could fault, which is one reason the finest Soviet scholarship of the period is bibliographical. The gradient explains the otherwise puzzling distribution of excellence across the Soviet intellectual landscape, and it supplies, as will be seen, the core of the answer to the strongest objection: the objection is decisive where the gradient ran steep, and it weakens exactly where the work could be, and was, independently checked.

## History writing under the doctrine

No field stood closer to legitimation than history, and none shows the settlement procedure in a harsher light. In the nineteen twenties the dominant school was that of Mikhail Pokrovsky, a historian of the revolutionary generation who had set out to write Russian history as the history of class forces and economic interests, stripping the tsars and generals of their customary halo. His school held the institutes and the journals. After his death in 1932 the school was condemned, in a campaign conducted through the middle thirties, for abstract sociologising, for neglecting the narrative of the state, and for errors that were discovered in his work in proportion as the leadership's own view of the national past changed. His books were withdrawn. His students were arrested in numbers. History was rewritten around the Short Course of 1938, whose chapters on the party's past became the authorised narrative, and the writing of history in the Soviet Union settled into its mature form: a discipline in which the conclusions of any work on sensitive periods were known before the research began, because they were the conclusions of the canonical text.

### What did the condemnation of a dead historian's school accomplish?

It announced that no scholarly reputation was settled beyond revision by authority, not even posthumously, and that the narrative of the past would be aligned with the requirements of the present leadership. The rewriting of textbooks and the arrest of the school's students enforced the announcement.

Yet even here the gradient operated, in time if not in topic. Work on periods remote from the revolution, on medieval charters and ancient economies and the archaeology of the steppe, could achieve standards that foreign specialists respected, and did. The archives, administered by the same state, preserved materials with a completeness that later researchers, including the state's severest critics, would rely on. And within the permitted narrative, craftsmanship survived in footnotes and editions, in the publication of documents whose contents sometimes sat awkwardly with the text they were published to support. A full account of Soviet historiography would be a history of ingenuity under supervision, and it would end with the observation that when the supervision finally relaxed, the profession's first act was to ransack its own archives for the record of what had been done to it.

## The strongest objection: scholarship under constraint

Everything said so far in praise of the scholarship is exposed to an objection that must now be stated at its full strength, in the form given to it by its most formidable proponents. Leszek Kołakowski, who had been a philosopher inside the Polish party before becoming its historian and anatomist, argued across the volumes of Main Currents of Marxism that the Soviet codification was not a degeneration external to the doctrine but its institutional truth, and that the intellectual production of the system had to be judged as a whole, as the output of an apparatus. Andrzej Walicki, in his studies of Marxism and the idea of freedom, pressed the connected point about assessment: where the conditions of production are what they were, the ordinary courtesies of scholarly judgement lapse. Put the objection in its sharpest form, as these critics invite us to do. Scholarship produced under conditions in which the wrong conclusion could cost a career or a life is not scholarship in the ordinary sense, since its conclusions cannot be attributed to the evidence rather than to the constraint. A historian of chemistry does not ask which results of an alchemist were sound while the patron who funded the laboratory stood ready to execute him for the wrong transmutation; the entire product is classified by its conditions. On this view, the operation performed by guides like this one, sifting a valuable remainder from the Soviet record, is a retrospective rescue operation, an act of piety toward individual victims dressed as a judgement about their work, and the honest verdict is the wholesale one: the system produced ideology, and where it produced more than ideology, the surplus is accident, not achievement.

### What is the strongest objection to treating Soviet work as scholarship?

That conclusions reached where a wrong answer could end a career or a life cannot be credited to evidence over fear, so the whole output must be classified by its conditions, and sifting a valuable remainder from it is rescue, not judgement.

The objection deserves respect before it deserves an answer, because its premise is a fact. The constraint existed. It was not a metaphor, not a matter of chilly climates and self-censorship of the kind every academy knows, but a machinery of appointment, publication, arrest, and death, applied within living memory of everyone who worked under it. Any assessment of this body of work that begins by discounting the constraint has forfeited its claim to be believed, and the answer offered here begins by conceding two of the objection's three claims outright. It holds for the politically exposed areas. In the writing of the history of the revolution and the party, in the political economy of the Soviet system itself, in the philosophy of the state and of law, the conclusions of published work were fixed in advance to a degree that makes the ordinary language of scholarship inapplicable, and the sifting operation, applied there, does become rescue: what survives from those fields is evidence about the system, not contributions to the subjects they profess. And the constraint is unquantifiable. No instrument exists that could measure, for a given book or a given career, how much of the conclusion was evidence and how much was prudence, because the prudence operated inside the author's own deliberation, deleting arguments before they were formed. Whoever claims to know the size of the distortion claims to know something unknowable.

The answer begins where the concession ends, with the observation that the constraint did not bind uniformly, and that its unevenness is not a conjecture but a matter of record. The gradient described above, from the supervised centre to the technical periphery, meant that in identifiable fields the ordinary mechanisms of scholarship continued to operate: results could be tested by practitioners, errors could be found by rivals, and a conclusion's survival depended on its performance against the object rather than on its consonance with a text. The test of this claim is not the sincerity of the scholars, which is as unknowable as the size of the distortion. The test is independent validation, the subsequent history of the work in hands that owed the system nothing. By that test, a definite body of Soviet work passes. The cultural historical psychology was taken up, tested, extended, and in places corrected by researchers in America and Europe who worked in freedom and under no inducement to flatter it. The value-form reading founded by Rubin was recovered because economists and philosophers in several countries found that it solved problems in a text they could all inspect. The editions of the manuscripts met the standards of the most demanding editorial tradition in Europe and were used, without embarrassment, by scholars engaged in refuting the regime that produced them. Kantorovich's mathematics was confirmed by parallel discovery abroad. In each case the validation is external, dated, and public, and it discriminates: it confirms some Soviet work and, by its absence, leaves the rest unconfirmed. A wholesale verdict cannot explain why the validation falls where it falls. The gradient can.

### Which Soviet work was later validated by independent research abroad?

Work whose results could be checked against an object open to all inspectors: the cultural historical psychology, tested and extended in Western developmental research; the value-form reading, recovered by scholars solving problems in a shared text; the manuscript editions; the mathematics of optimal allocation.

There remains the objection's deepest stratum, and it should be met rather than evaded. Even where the work was sound, the critic may say, its production under those conditions corrupts the credit: the institute that edited the manuscripts also served the cult, the psychologist's school survived by silences, and to honour the work is to launder the system. The reply is a distinction, and it is the distinction on which this whole article rests. Credit for work attaches to the work and to its makers, not to the conditions that failed to prevent it. The makers, in the cases that matter here, paid for their work at rates no Western scholar was asked to pay, and the payment is recorded in this article in the same passages as the contributions, precisely so that the honour cannot be mistaken for a verdict on the system that exacted it. The system is judged, in these pages, by the doctrine it imposed and the scholars it destroyed. The scholarship is judged by the only test that has ever separated scholarship from its counterfeits, which is whether disinterested successors, free to discard it, found that they could not. On that test the verdict is mixed, field by field, name by name, and a mixed verdict is what the evidence supports. The wholesale verdict, whether of dismissal or of celebration, is the one conclusion the record will not bear.

## Soviet Marxism after the dictator: thaw, revival, and limits

The dictator died in March 1953, and three years later his successor denounced his crimes to a closed session of the party congress, in the speech that told the delegates, in detail they had not been permitted to print, what the terror had done to the party itself. The speech did not free Soviet intellectual life, and it was not intended to. But it changed the price of speech. Conclusions could still end careers; they had ceased, in the ordinary course, to end lives, and across the disciplines scholars began the long work of discovering how much of their inheritance could be salvaged and how much had to be discarded in silence. Genetics crept back toward respectability over a decade. The suppressed psychologists were republished. Historians began, cautiously and then less cautiously, to write about the revolution as an event with alternatives. In philosophy, the thaw produced the most interesting development of the late Soviet period: a generation that took the doctrine's founding texts seriously enough to think with them, against the catechism that claimed to summarise them.

### What survived and revived in philosophy after the dictator's death?

A generation of philosophers returned to the founding texts against the catechism: work on the method of the classic, on the nature of the ideal, on logic and the methodology of science, conducted in institutes and journals under supervision that persisted but no longer killed.

The emblematic figure is Evald Ilyenkov. Born in 1924, a veteran invalided out of the war, he came to philosophy through the Moscow University of the late forties and made his name with a study of the method of Marx's Capital, published in 1960 as The Dialectics of the Abstract and the Concrete in Marx's Capital. Its argument was a direct challenge to the catechism's picture of knowledge. Against the textbook doctrine that thought moves from the concrete of perception to abstractions that merely generalise it, Ilyenkov reconstructed Marx's actual procedure: the ascent from the abstract to the concrete, in which a science begins from a simple, abstract determination, the commodity, the value form, and rebuilds the whole as a system of developing contradictions, so that the concrete of theory is richer than the concrete of perception. The book treated the dialectic not as a set of features to be recited but as the logic of a specific inquiry, and it treated Capital as a work of philosophy in its own right. Young philosophers across the country read it as a liberation, which, within the limits of its world, it was.

Ilyenkov's second great theme was the ideal. The catechism's reflection theory located consciousness inside the individual skull, as a copy of the material world formed in the brain. Ilyenkov argued, in essays that circulated for years before full publication, that the ideal exists outside the individual head as well: in the forms of human activity embodied in things, in language, in the norms of a culture, in the shape of a tool and the plan of a city, so that a child becomes conscious by mastering a world already idealised by the labour of previous generations. The argument drew on the cultural historical psychology, on Spinoza, and on the manuscripts of 1844, and it was aimed at the centre of the official philosophy, since it implied that the catechism's materialism was itself a crude, pre-Marxist affair. For this he was rewarded with the characteristic sanctions of the later period: not arrest, but obstruction. His works were delayed, cut, and published abroad more easily than at home; his position at the university was destroyed in an early episode, and he worked at the Institute of Philosophy under recurrent campaigns against his influence on the young. He died in 1979. His books, translated and retranslated, continued to gather readers, and his standing in the international literature on Marx's method has risen steadily since.

### Who was the philosopher of abstraction and the ideal?

Evald Ilyenkov, whose study of the method of Capital rebuilt the doctrine of the ascent against the textbook account of knowledge, and whose essays argued that the ideal exists in the shaped world of human practice and culture, not only inside individual consciousness.

The strangest and most moving episode of the revival connects Ilyenkov's philosophy of the ideal to a school for deaf-blind children at Zagorsk, outside Moscow, where the psychologist Alexander Meshcheryakov and his colleagues undertook the education of children who could neither see nor hear. On the doctrine the catechism taught, such children stood at the limit of the human: no perception, no reflection, no mind. In practice, the educators built a world of shared activity, household labour performed hand in hand, objects named by touch in a developing system of signs, until language arrived through doing. Four of the school's pupils completed degrees at Moscow University. Ilyenkov wrote about the experiment with undisguised philosophical intent: here, he argued, was the formation of mind from social activity exhibited in its purest form, the ideal arising not from the brain's inspection of the world but from a child's induction into the practices of others. The episode deserves its place in any account of the period because it shows the revival's characteristic move, the return to the founding insight of the cultural historical school, that mind is made between people before it is exercised within them, and its testing in the most demanding laboratory imaginable.

### What did the education of deaf-blind children demonstrate?

That a mind could be formed, in children deprived of both sight and hearing, through induction into shared practical activity and a system of signs built on touch: the social formation of consciousness exhibited in its purest and most demanding case, against the textbook theory of reflection.

Around Ilyenkov the revival had other centres. In logic, the work begun by Yanovskaya's generation matured into a substantial literature. In Kiev, Pavlo Kopnin developed a logic of scientific research that treated the categories as instruments of inquiry rather than as features of the universe. In Moscow, the methodological circle gathered by Georgy Shchedrovitsky, which had begun in the fifties as a seminar of young philosophers and psychologists, elaborated a theory of activity and of collective thought work that survived, characteristically, by migrating to the edges of the academy, into design institutes and management consultancies where its questions could be asked as practical ones. Merab Mamardashvili, a philosopher of consciousness of genuine originality, lectured in Moscow to audiences who treated his courses as events, was progressively excluded from teaching, and spent his last years working in Georgia. The revival, in short, was real, and it was conducted under a supervision that had changed its instruments rather than its intentions. The sanction of the later period was not the camp but the closed door: the unpublished manuscript, the withdrawn course, the foreign travel refused, the institute reorganised. Scholars of the revival learned to write for the drawer and for the seminar, and a good deal of the period's best work circulated in typescript among trusted readers, a literature within the literature, whose recovery after the state's end revised the period's reputation upward.

Two limits must be marked, because a guide that celebrated the revival without them would repeat the sentimental error corrected at the outset. The first is that the revival never recovered the freedom of the twenties, let alone exceeded it: the questions that touched the legitimacy of the order remained as closed as before, and the philosophers of the revival, whatever their private views, published within a perimeter they did not choose. The second is that the revival's best work was unknown to the doctrine's official custodians in any sense that mattered: the catechism continued to be taught, examined, and reprinted in the same decades, unaltered by the philosophy being written in the institutes of the same city. The pair under the single name persisted to the end. When the state ended, the catechism vanished with the apparatus that had required it, leaving almost no trace in the intellectual life that followed, while the scholarship, the editions, the psychology, the philosophy of the ideal, became part of the shared patrimony of universities everywhere, where it is read without any need to ask the reader's indulgence. That differential survival is the final piece of evidence for the distinction with which this article began, and the question of whether the system was socialist at all, which the differential survival raises but does not answer, is taken up in the series article [Was the USSR Socialist?](/2019/05/15/was-the-ussr-socialist/).

## The economics debate of the twenties: scholarship before the settlement

Philosophy was not the only field in which the twenties argued in earnest. The economic debate of the decade, conducted between the theorists of the ruling party over the strategy of industrialisation, was a genuine intellectual event, and its suppression is the clearest demonstration that the settlement procedure destroyed analysis the regime itself needed. The question was stark: a backward country, its industry ruined by war and civil war, had to accumulate the means of industrialisation from somewhere, and the candidates were the peasantry, foreign loans that were not on offer, or the slow growth of a market economy under political supervision. Evgeny Preobrazhensky, an economist of the old revolutionary generation, gave the hardest answer its classic statement in The New Economics of 1926: the law of primitive socialist accumulation, by which the state sector would draw resources from the private, peasant economy through unequal exchange, with the deliberateness of the enclosures and the colonial drain that had financed early capitalism. Bukharin, the textbook author of 1921, answered for the alternative: a gradual growing into socialism, the peasantry enriched and drawn into cooperation, accumulation at a pace the alliance with the countryside could bear.

### What did the theory of primitive socialist accumulation claim?

That a socialist state in a backward country had to accumulate for industrialisation by drawing resources from the peasant economy through unequal exchange, as deliberately as early capitalism had accumulated from its colonies, because no other source of the necessary surplus existed.

The debate was conducted in books, journals, and congresses, and it was about the fate of the country in the most literal sense: the grain collections, the price scissors, the tempo of the factories. It was settled as the philosophical debate was settled, by the victory of a faction and the reclassification of the defeated positions as deviations, and then it was settled again, more terribly, when the victorious leadership adopted a tempo of accumulation beyond anything Preobrazhensky had proposed and exacted it from the countryside by force. Preobrazhensky was expelled, exiled, arrested, and shot in 1937. The analysis for which he was destroyed had identified, correctly, the central mechanism of the strategy that destroyed him: the transfer of the surplus from agriculture to industry. Soviet economics after the settlement never again produced a debate of that quality on a question of that importance, because the lesson had been learned by everyone: the important questions were the dangerous ones. The political economy of the mature system became a literature of exegesis, commentary on the canonical texts and on the plan's categories, from which the Kantorovich episode stands out precisely because its author had found the one safe idiom, mathematics, in which a real question could still be asked.

## The literary front: formalism, narrative, and the long delay

Literary study supplied the twenties with another school whose fate rehearses the pattern. The formalists of Petrograd and Moscow, Viktor Shklovsky, Boris Eikhenbaum, Yuri Tynyanov, and their associates in the Society for the Study of Poetic Language and the Moscow Linguistic Circle, proposed to study literature as literature: the device, the defamiliarisation by which art makes the familiar strange, the evolution of genres as a struggle of forms rather than a reflection of the author's soul or class. The school was attacked through the twenties as an evasion of ideology, and by the turn of the thirties it had been dissolved as an organised force; Shklovsky published a recantation in 1930, announcing the end of formalism in terms his readers understood as coerced, and the school's members dispersed into scholarship of safer kinds, historical editing, translation, the study of the classics. Their concepts survived underground and abroad, carried into Western criticism by way of Prague structuralism, and returned to Russia decades later as imports.

From the same milieu and the same years came a work whose trajectory outdoes even Vygotsky's for strangeness. In 1928 Vladimir Propp, a Leningrad scholar of folklore, published his Morphology of the Folktale, an analysis of a hundred Russian wonder tales that reduced their plots to a sequence of thirty-one functions performed by a small cast of roles. The book was little noticed abroad and criticised at home for its formalism. Thirty years later its English translation fell into the hands of the structuralist generation, and Propp became, posthumously in effect though he lived until 1970, one of the founders of narratology, his functions debated by Lévi-Strauss and built into the study of narrative everywhere. The pattern is by now familiar to the reader of this article, and its repetition across psychology, folklore, value theory, and textual editing is itself the evidence: the system kept producing work of the first rank in the fields at the margin of its attention, and kept failing to know what it had produced until the rest of the world told it.

### How did a study of folktales become a founding text of narrative theory?

By treating the plot as a structure of functions rather than a gallery of characters. Propp showed that a hundred wonder tales could be analysed as variations on a single sequence of thirty-one functions, an argument ignored at home and adopted, a generation later, by structuralism abroad.

The literary settlement proper came in 1934, when the first congress of Soviet writers, addressed by party spokesmen and by Maxim Gorky, installed socialist realism as the sole authorised method in literature, defined in terms of the depiction of reality in its revolutionary development. The definition's vagueness was functional: it could license or condemn almost anything, and the judgement in each case belonged to the institutions. The contrast with the scholarship is instructive. In literature the doctrine did not merely supervise a field; it prescribed the field's product, and the result, across half a century, was a literature whose peaks were achieved by evasion, by the historical novel and the village prose and the science fiction in which the present could be discussed in costume. The writers' fates replicate the scholars' in a darker register, and they belong to a history of Russian literature rather than to this guide; what belongs here is the structural point, that the settlement procedure, applied to a field whose product is public and symbolic, produced conformity more complete than in any science, because there was no object against which a forbidden result could be checked and vindicated. A novel cannot be replicated. A folktale morphology can.

## The doctrine examined as philosophy

Neutrality in a guide of this kind requires that the doctrine be assessed as a philosophy and not only as an instrument, and the assessment, conducted without the apparatus, is not the rout its detractors sometimes expect. Take its components in turn. The claim that the world is material, knowable, and independent of consciousness is a respectable philosophical position with a long pedigree, and the Soviet textbooks stated it with a clarity that compared well with the evasions of some Western contemporaries. The claim that practice is the criterion of truth, whatever its abuses in application, descends from the second of Marx's theses on Feuerbach and raises a genuine problem about the relation of theory to action. The five formations offered students a comparative frame for world history at a time when much Western teaching offered none. A reader who meets these propositions for the first time in the canonical text has met real ideas, and the millions who were educated on them were not educated on nothing.

### Where does the codified doctrine fail as philosophy?

At the points where its method replaces inquiry: in laws of the dialectic stated so generally that no event could count against them, in a reflection theory that cannot explain error or novelty, and in a presentation that converts every open problem of the tradition into a settled feature.

The failures are equally definite. The laws of the dialectic, as codified, suffer from the defect the dialecticians of the twenties might have diagnosed had they been free to turn their tools on the catechism: stated at the level of generality the textbook required, quantity passing into quality and the unity of opposites describe everything and explain nothing in particular, since no conceivable event could embarrass them. The reflection theory of knowledge, pressed hard, cannot account for the creativity the same textbooks celebrated in science and art, nor for systematic error, which a copy theory must treat as a smudge rather than as the structured product of a standpoint. And the doctrine's treatment of its own history was its weakest point philosophically: a philosophy that teaches that thought reflects being and develops through contradiction presented its own development as a series of victories without remainder, its controversies as the unmasking of enemies, its texts as a succession of clarifications. The tradition's founders had been unsparing about the ideologies of others. The codified doctrine exempted itself from the treatment, and that exemption, more than any single proposition, is what separates it from the scholarship produced under its roof, whose best practitioners applied the tradition's methods to everything within reach, including, in Ilyenkov's case, the doctrine's own theory of knowledge.

A comparison with the tradition it claimed to continue sharpens the verdict. The series guide to [Western Marxism](/2015/11/01/western-marxism-complete-guide/) traces a body of work produced in freedom and frequently in defeat, argumentative, revisable, and plural. The Soviet doctrine was none of those things, and yet the two traditions are not related as darkness to light, for the reason the archival paradox establishes: the Western tradition's founding texts were Soviet editions, its humanist Marx a Moscow publication. The honest map shows two heirs of one estate, one of which kept the library and lost the argument, the other of which won the argument with books the first had printed.

## The seminar and the file: how scholars adapted

Between the doctrine and the scholarship stood the working scholar, and a guide that omitted the adaptations by which such people preserved both their lives and their subjects would leave the period's texture unrecorded. The adaptations were various. There was the protective citation, the paragraph of authorised formulae with which a work opened and which its readers learned to step over, as one steps over a threshold, into the argument proper. There was the strategic topic, the migration toward periods, languages, and technicalities at the gradient's safe end, which stocked Soviet scholarship with superb medievalists, orientalists, and editors whose choice of century was a biography in itself. There was the footnote as contraband, the dissenting source quoted at a length its refutation did not require, so that a reader could find, in the apparatus of a loyal work, the materials of a disloyal conclusion. And there was Aesopian language proper, the art of writing so that the intended reader understood and the supervising reader could not object, an art with a long Russian pedigree that the Soviet period refined into a national accomplishment and whose masterpieces include works of scholarship whose surface argument and real argument diverge like two texts bound in one cover.

### How did scholars preserve real questions under supervision?

By a repertoire of adaptations: protective citation of authorised formulae, migration toward technical and remote topics, the footnote that quoted a forbidden source at unnecessary length, and Aesopian phrasing that let the intended reader grasp an argument the supervising reader could not formally fault.

The adaptations had a cost that should be entered on the ledger beside the terror's. A literature written in code loses readers who lack the code, including posterity and foreigners, and Soviet scholarship is full of works whose significance was legible to their first audience and is recoverable now only by reconstruction. Worse, the habits of indirection outlived their necessity in some quarters, and the first post-Soviet generation of scholars complained that their teachers could not write a straight sentence on a sensitive topic even when the sensitivity had lapsed. Set against the cost, the achievement: the adaptations kept questions alive. The seminar that could not discuss the terror could discuss the Paris Commune with an intensity its participants understood. The lecture course on the history of philosophy became, in the hands of a Mamardashvili, a course on consciousness itself. A reader approaching the period's texts should therefore read them as their first readers did, alert to the threshold paragraphs, the disproportionate footnotes, and the sudden concreteness with which a remote example is handled, for those are the places where the scholarship is speaking.

## The physicists' exception: when the state needed the heretics

One episode of the settlement years runs against the pattern, and its exception proves the rule with unusual clarity. Through the nineteen thirties and forties, relativity and quantum mechanics were attacked in the philosophical press as idealist deviations, much as genetics had been attacked as bourgeois, and after the genetics settlement of 1948 preparations began for a corresponding conference of physicists that would align their science with the doctrine. The conference never took place. It was postponed and then quietly abandoned, and the reason, established by later historians from the archives, was brutally practical: the state's atomic project depended on the very physicists marked for correction, and the project's overseers informed the leadership that a Lysenkoist settlement of physics would damage the weapon programme. The physicists were left alone, not because their arguments prevailed, but because their usefulness outbid their heresy.

### Why did physics escape the settlement imposed on biology?

Because the state needed its physicists for the atomic project more than it needed their conformity. The planned conference on the model of the genetics session was abandoned when the weapon programme's overseers warned that ideological correction of physics would damage work the leadership valued above doctrine.

Individual physicists had, in fact, argued, and argued well. Vladimir Fock, a theorist of the first rank, defended the new physics in print against its philosophical critics, contending that the doctrine, properly understood, had nothing to fear from relativity, and his defence is a small masterpiece of the period's characteristic genre, the rescue of a science by exegesis. But the Fock case should not be sentimentalised into a victory of argument. Genetics had its defenders of equal quality, and they were destroyed. The difference between the two sciences was not the cogency of their apologists but the nature of their deliverables: a bomb is a deliverable the state can test, and a harvest failure can be blamed on the weather, on saboteurs, on the peasants, on anyone but the doctrine that prescribed the sowing. Where results were unambiguous and urgently required, the settlement procedure suspended itself. The exception thus confirms the analysis offered throughout this article. Argument in that system was effective exactly in proportion to the speaker's independent leverage, and the purest scholarship flourished, as the gradient predicts, where the object itself could speak: in the laboratory whose results a minister needed, in the manuscript whose readings any scholar could verify, in the folktale corpus whose functions could be counted by anyone who doubted them.

## The editions after the founder: the second life of the archive

The archive Ryazanov assembled outlived its founder by more than half a century, and its later history extends the paradox rather than resolving it. The merged institute in Moscow continued the publication of the classics at the pace politics allowed, issuing through the decades the standard Russian editions of Marx and Engels on which scholarship inside the bloc depended. The first critical edition, interrupted by the founder's arrest and the war, was never completed on its original plan. In the nineteen seventies a second attempt began, the edition known to scholars as MEGA2, prepared jointly by institutes in Moscow and East Berlin, working from the collections the founder had gathered, and after the end of the states that sponsored it the edition passed to an international foundation seated in Amsterdam, under whose stewardship volumes continue to appear. The editorial standards of the enterprise, complete texts, full apparatus, the printing of drafts and variants that earlier editions had suppressed, descend in direct line from the institute of the nineteen twenties.

### How did the critical edition survive its founder and its sponsors?

On the strength of the archive itself. The collections assembled in the nineteen twenties were so complete that every later edition, including the international edition that succeeded the Soviet and East German sponsors, had to be built from them, and the editorial standards set by the founder became the enterprise's permanent constitution.

Consider what this means for the map of the tradition. The most durable achievement of Soviet Marxism, the achievement on which all future study of its subject depends, is a work of custody: the gathering and faithful reproduction of texts. The doctrine that the same state imposed is read now only by historians. The catechism's millions of copies are landfill and collectors' items. The archive is consulted daily, in reading rooms the founder never saw, by scholars whose conclusions he could not have predicted and some of which he would have deplored. There is a lesson in the distribution of survival that extends beyond this subject. Institutions perish by their claims; they endure by their services. The institute served the texts, and the texts have kept its name attached to the tradition's foundations long after the state that shot its director has itself become a subject for historians.

## The doctrine abroad: a world language and its speakers

For roughly four decades the codified doctrine was the most widely taught philosophy on earth. The Short Course and its successor textbooks were translated into the languages of the bloc and of the fraternal parties beyond it, and the institutes of Moscow trained teachers who carried the formulations home. In the universities of Eastern Europe the doctrine arrived as an import with an army behind it, and its local history repeats the Soviet pattern in miniature: native philosophical traditions of real distinction, Polish logic, Czech structuralism, Hungarian aesthetics, were subordinated, suppressed, or driven into the interstices, and in the interstices, predictably, much of the bloc's best work was done. The Hungarian philosopher György Lukács, the most considerable Marxist thinker to live under the system, spent his Soviet exile in the archives of Ryazanov's institute, where he read the manuscripts of 1844 in the year of their publication and wrote the studies that made him the humanist tradition's elder; his later career in Budapest, minister in a revolutionary government in 1956, deported, restored, is the bloc's intellectual history in one life. The Polish school of the history of ideas, from which Kołakowski emerged, formed itself in argument with the catechism it had been taught. The Yugoslav Praxis philosophers built a journal and a summer school on the Adriatic into an enclave where the doctrine could be criticised in Marx's name, until the authorities closed the enclave by administrative means familiar to any reader of the Soviet chapters above.

### What happened to native traditions where the doctrine was installed?

They were subordinated to the imported catechism and driven into the interstices of academic life, where, by the same logic that operated in Moscow, much of the bloc's most durable work was produced: in archives, in logic, in the history of ideas, at the gradient's safe end.

The export had one consequence its exporters did not intend. A doctrine taught identically in a dozen capitals created a single conversation across the bloc, in which a criticism framed in Warsaw could be read in Budapest and a heresy in Prague recognised in Moscow, because all parties had been examined on the same features and formations. When the end came, it came to the whole conversation at once. But that ending belongs to the history of the states rather than of the scholarship, and the point for this guide is the narrower one: the doctrine travelled internationally as a text and a curriculum travel, while the scholarship travelled as ideas travel, and the two careers, though they shared a name and a flag, proceeded independently and ended differently.

## The readers: belief, fluency, and the space between

No account of the doctrine is complete that treats its millions of students as a passive surface. The evidence of memoirs, of the period's jokes, and of the revival itself points to a distribution of responses as wide as in any compulsory creed. There were sincere believers, peasants' sons and daughters for whom the doctrine was the first intelligible account of the world they had been given, and whose belief survived disappointments that would have cured a cynic. There were fluent performers, for whom the formulations were a professional dialect, spoken at examinations and meetings and laid aside at the kitchen table, where the period's real philosophy was conducted in anecdotes. And there was the consequential minority between them, the readers in whom the catechism's own materials caught fire: who noticed that the chapter on the dialectic sat oddly with the practice around them, who found in the institute editions the young Marx the textbook never mentioned, who learned from the doctrine's universal claims to ask universal questions about the doctrine's home. The revival was recruited from this minority, and so, in the end, was the generation that brought the system down, many of whose members described their education as an argument with the textbook they had been set. A doctrine that aspires to explain everything will be used to explain its own failure, by its own best students, in its own vocabulary. That too is a form of influence, and the ledger of Soviet Marxism is not closed until it is entered.

### What did compulsory doctrine do to its most serious students?

It equipped them with the vocabulary of their dissent. The minority who took the universal claims literally, and measured the system against them, supplied both the philosophical revival of the later decades and, in the end, a share of the generation that dismantled the system itself.

## The scholarship and the West: channels of influence

The influence of Soviet scholarship on the West ran through four channels, and distinguishing them prevents the exaggeration to which the subject is prone. The first channel was textual, and it was decisive: the editions. Without the Moscow publications of 1932 there is no humanist Marx, no Marx of the Paris manuscripts around whom the postwar Western tradition organised its self-understanding, and no scholarly text of The German Ideology on which the modern account of historical materialism's founding rests. The second channel was disciplinary and delayed: the psychology of Vygotsky and the narratology of Propp entered Western fields a generation after their production, through translation, and were absorbed as discoveries, their Soviet origin acknowledged but their Soviet context, the supervision and suppression that had delayed them, often left unexamined by the absorbing disciplines. The third channel was polemical: Rubin's value-form reading, recovered in the nineteen seventies, and Ilyenkov's dialectics, translated in the same years, entered Western Marxist debates as live positions, argued with by scholars who treated their authors as contemporaries, which in the republic of letters is the only immortality on offer. The fourth channel was negative and vast: the doctrine itself, whose career in the West was that of an anti-model, the thing against which Western Marxists of every school defined their Marxism, so that the codified system shaped even the thought of those who never read a page of it, by supplying the orthodoxy their originality was measured against.

### How did Soviet scholarship reach Western readers?

Chiefly through four channels: the editions that supplied the founding texts of the humanist reading; delayed disciplinary absorption, as with the psychology and the folktale morphology; polemical recovery of the value-form and dialectical writings; and the doctrine's career as the anti-model against which Western schools defined themselves.

Weighed with the comparison basis stated earlier in view, the balance is this. The Western traditions, produced by individuals and small groups in universities, outproduced the Soviet system in open argument, on the state and its philosophy, in the critique of political economy as a going research programme, in everything that required the freedom to conclude. The Soviet system, at its interstices, produced work that the West needed and had not produced: the archive, the editions, the psychology of the social mind, the morphology, the value-form reading. Neither tradition can be written out of the century's Marxism without falsifying it, and the attempt to do so, which the polemics of the period encouraged on both sides, is the final misreading this article exists to correct.

## The untranslated shelf: a note on sources

A large part of the Soviet philosophical and scholarly output discussed in this article is untranslated, and a reader who wishes to follow the argument into the sources should know what the English shelf holds and where it ends. The obtainable core is substantial. The canonical chapter of 1938 was translated at once and is available in the English Short Course. Bukharin's textbook of 1921 appeared in English within four years of its Russian publication. Vygotsky is well served: Thought and Language and the posthumous collection Mind in Society are standard English texts, and the secondary literature in English is large. Voloshinov's philosophy of language has been available in English since 1973. Rubin's essays on value theory appeared in English in 1972 and have been reprinted. Ilyenkov is partly served: his essays on dialectical logic were published in English in 1977, and his book on the method of Capital circulated in an English edition from the Moscow foreign languages press, scarce but findable, with further essays translated in journals since.

### Which works can an English reader actually obtain?

The canonical texts, the textbook of 1921, the psychology, the philosophy of language, the value essays, and a portion of the dialectical writings of the revival, all in established translations. The controversies of the twenties, the bulk of the journals, and most of the revival's literature remain untranslated.

Beyond that shelf the reader meets the untranslated mass, and its shape should be stated plainly, with the Russian titles given and translated, as the discipline of this series requires. The philosophical controversies of the twenties ran through the journal Pod znamenem marksizma (in English, Under the Banner of Marxism), whose files are the indispensable source for the mechanist and dialectician dispute and which exists in English only in extracts. Deborin's own writings, including his Dialektika i estestvoznanie, Dialectics and Natural Science, are effectively closed to the English reader. The institute's early archival bulletins, Byulleten Instituta, the Institute Bulletin, in which the manuscripts were first described, are untranslated. Kopnin's work on the logic of research, Shchedrovitsky's methodological writings, and the greater part of the revival's journal literature in Voprosy filosofii, Problems of Philosophy, await translators. The consequence for Western scholarship has been a systematic distortion: the Soviet tradition is known in English disproportionately through its victims and its émigrés, and through the handful of texts that translation, that most political of lotteries, happened to select. Judgements of the whole formed on that sample should be advanced with the sample in mind, and the reader of this guide now knows enough to discount them, including, where the evidence runs out, the judgements of this guide.

## The Asiatic mode: a concept abolished by discussion

Among the settlements of the turn of the thirties, one deserves separate notice because its object was a category of the founding texts themselves. Marx, in his writings on India and in the drafts of his later work, had entertained the notion of an Asiatic mode of production: societies organised around state-managed irrigation and self-sufficient village communities, without private property in land, and resistant, in his account, to the internal dynamics that drove European history through its formations. The notion sat awkwardly in the codified scheme. Five formations, in a single sequence through which all peoples passed, left no room for a divergent Eastern path, and the political implications of the concept, which could be and were applied to Russia itself, made it more than awkward. In 1931 a discussion organised in Leningrad among orientalists and historians concluded, in the approved manner, that the Asiatic mode was an erroneous construction, a survival of bourgeois scholarship, and the concept was struck from the Soviet literature. Textbooks conformed. The five formations marched unopposed.

### Why was the Asiatic mode of production struck from the doctrine?

Because it broke the single sequence. A divergent path for the societies of the East contradicted the five formations through which all peoples were required to pass, and the concept's possible application to Russia gave the theoretical error a political charge the settlement could not tolerate.

The sequel measures the cost. Soviet oriental studies, which had scholars fully capable of testing the concept against the records of China, India, and the ancient Near East, spent decades working around the vacancy, describing hydraulic bureaucracies and communal tenures in a vocabulary forbidden to name the pattern they formed. Western scholarship, free to use the concept, used it mostly to abuse it, in polemics about oriental despotism that the archives might have disciplined. When Soviet scholars returned to the question in the thaw, in the discussions of the sixties, they found the evidence their predecessors had been forbidden to organise waiting in their own institutes' publications. The episode is a paradigm of the settlement's economics: the decision of 1931 purchased doctrinal tidiness at the price of a generation's work in a field where the country's scholars held real advantages of language, archive, and proximity. Multiplied across the disciplines, that is the true cost accounting of the procedure, and it should be set against the doctrine's defenders' favourite figure, the numbers of students taught and textbooks printed. A system can teach millions and still impoverish knowledge, if what it teaches them is which questions may not be asked.

## Philosophy of science: the semi-sheltered field

Between the supervised centre and the technical periphery lay a field whose history illuminates the gradient from within: the philosophy of natural science. Its practitioners interpreted the new physics and biology to the doctrine and the doctrine to the scientists, a brokerage that made them suspect to both parties and indispensable to each. Bonifatii Kedrov, a chemist turned philosopher who directed the Institute of Philosophy in the postwar years, built the field's institutions, editing the journal that carried its debates and writing the classification of the sciences that the textbooks adopted. Kedrov's career shows the brokerage's possibilities and its price: he defended the integrity of the sciences against crude philosophical interference where he could, framed the defence in the doctrine's own vocabulary where he had to, and late in life used his standing to support the rehabilitation of genetics' memory. Around him, philosophers of physics such as Mikhail Omelyanovsky spent careers on the interpretation of relativity and quantum theory, work that required them to know the physics at a professional level and the doctrine at a professional level and to keep the two in a relation that satisfied the physicists without provoking the custodians. Much of this literature is unreadable now, embalmed in the period's courtesies. Its function, though, was real: it maintained, inside the official philosophy, a territory where the results of the sciences were the senior partner, and from which, when conditions eased, a more honest philosophy of science could be and was rebuilt.

### What did the philosophers of science broker between?

Between the laboratory and the catechism. They translated the results of physics and biology into terms the doctrine could accept and the doctrine's demands into terms the scientists could survive, and the brokerage, however embalmed its prose, kept a territory where scientific results were the senior partner.

The field also supplied the revival with some of its personnel and its habits. A scholar trained to ask what a physical theory actually says, and to distinguish that question from what the theory may be used to prove, had acquired the central skill of the later philosophy. The traffic ran both ways. The scientists, for their part, learned from the brokers which formulations were safe, and the memoirs of the period record physicists composing the philosophical paragraphs of their papers with the same care, and the same irony, as the scholars composing their protective citations. The whole arrangement was a tax on everyone's time and talent. It is recorded here because it explains a fact that puzzles outsiders: how a system that settled biology by decree could simultaneously sustain, in physics and its philosophy, work that foreign specialists treated as belonging to their own conversation. The gradient, once more, is the explanation, and the gradient was drawn not by the nature of the questions but by the way power was distributed among those who asked them.

## Cybernetics and the dream of the planned mind

The adoption of cybernetics, recorded above as a reversal of classification, had a sequel that belongs to the history of the doctrine's ambitions. In Kiev, Viktor Glushkov, a mathematician of the cybernetic institute, proposed from the early nineteen sixties a nationwide system of computer centres that would gather the economy's information and assist, and progressively automate, its planning: the All-State Automated System, known by its Russian initials as OGAS. The proposal was, in effect, Kantorovich's insight rebuilt with electronics, the rationalisation of the plan by the machine, and it attracted the characteristic response of the mature system. It was neither adopted nor refuted. It was studied by commissions, praised in principle, reduced in scope, starved of the authority it would have needed over the ministries whose information it proposed to make transparent, and finally shelved in the form in which it might have mattered. The ministries understood, without needing philosophy, that a transparent economy is an economy in which the bargaining on which their power rested would be visible, and they defended their opacity with the system's own weapons, procedure and delay.

### What became of the proposal to automate the plan?

It was praised, commissioned, reduced, and shelved. The ministries whose bargaining power rested on controlling information declined to surrender that control to a transparent network, and the system that had denounced cybernetics as heresy and then adopted it as technique could not adopt it as a mirror.

The episode closes the arc that began with the settlement of the philosophical disputes. A doctrine that had fixed the theory of the economy in 1938 could contemplate computers as instruments of calculation but not as instruments of knowledge, because knowledge, in a system built on administered information, is a redistribution of power. Glushkov's network threatened no one's philosophy. It threatened everyone's files. The contrast with the physicists' exception is exact: the atomic project concentrated information in a priesthood the leadership trusted, while the automated plan would have dispersed information across the apparatus that the leadership governed by dividing. Soviet cybernetics therefore flourished as a science and failed as a reform, and its failure is the doctrine's history in miniature, the story of a system that could absorb any technique except the one that required it to know itself.

## Aesthetics and the Moscow Lukács

One further field of the scholarship deserves its paragraph, because it produced the period's most ambitious attempt to rebuild a Marxist aesthetics from the texts. In Moscow in the nineteen thirties, around the journal Literaturnyi kritik, a circle associated with Mikhail Lifshitz, and in its orbit the exiled Lukács, conducted what its members called the current: a campaign for a classical, realist aesthetics against both the vulgar sociology that reduced literature to class labelling and the modernism they regarded as the art of a decaying order. The circle's method was archival and philosophical at once. Lifshitz assembled from the writings of Marx and Engels a systematic aesthetics they had never written, published as a documentary volume in 1933, and the work of recovery, the demonstration that the founders had a coherent view of art rooted in their account of labour and alienation, fed directly into the humanist reconstruction that the manuscripts of 1844, published in the same city in the same years, were making possible. The circle was dissolved by the end of the decade, its journal closed, its members scattered to safer work; Lifshitz survived, quarrelsome and unrepentant, to become in the thaw years a patriarch of independent aesthetic judgement, feared by the official critics he despised. The episode belongs in this guide as a reminder that the scholarship's map includes territories the standard summaries omit, and that the institute's city in the thirties contained, within a few streets, the editorial office of the catechism, the archive of the manuscripts, and a seminar rebuilding the founders' aesthetics against the catechism's custodians.

## The jurist of the commodity form

The law supplied the twenties with a theorist whose construction rivalled Rubin's in elegance and whose fate replicated it almost exactly. Evgeny Pashukanis, a jurist who held high office in the new state's legal institutions, published in 1924 his General Theory of Law and Marxism, which proposed that the legal form itself, the relation between abstract rights-bearing subjects, was the juridical counterpart of the commodity form: as the product becomes a commodity in exchange, so the person becomes a subject in law, and a society that abolished commodity production would see law, not merely this or that law, wither away. The argument had the austere beauty of the best of its decade. It explained why the bourgeois legal order, for all its class content, took the specific form of universal rules between formally equal persons, and it predicted that a socialist order would transcend the legal form rather than perfect it. For a decade it structured Soviet jurisprudence.

### What did the commodity form theory of law claim?

That the legal relation between formally equal, rights-bearing subjects mirrors the exchange relation between commodity owners, so that law in its developed form is the juridical shadow of the market, destined to wither with the market rather than to be perfected by socialism.

The theory's embarrassment was the state that employed its author. A regime engaged in building an apparatus of codes, courts, and procurators had limited use for a doctrine that treated law as a bourgeois form in dissolution, and as the thirties consolidated the state's legal machinery, Pashukanis was required to recant positions, then denounced, then arrested in January 1937 and shot in September of that year. The jurisprudence installed in his place, associated with Andrei Vyshinsky, the prosecutor of the public trials, defined law as the will of the ruling class expressed in legislation and guaranteed by state coercion, a definition whose serviceability to the apparatus that pronounced it requires no comment. The contrast between the two theories is the contrast of this article in legal dress. One was an analysis, produced by argument, which explained a form and accepted the explanation's consequences for its author's own side. The other was an instrument, produced by requirement, which justified the apparatus that required it. Pashukanis's book survived its author in the usual way: kept alive abroad, where legal theorists of several schools continued to work with it, and recovered at home when the thaw permitted the republication of what the settlement had buried. The commodity form theory remains the most influential Marxist account of law ever written, and it was written by a man its own state shot as an enemy of the people.

## The thaw in economics: an argument resumes, in a lower voice

Economics after the dictator's death illustrates both the revival's reality and its perimeter. In September 1962 a Kharkov professor of engineering economics, Evsei Liberman, published in Pravda an article proposing that enterprises be judged and rewarded by profitability rather than by the gross output indicators whose perversities every manager knew: the nail factory rewarded by weight that produced a few enormous nails, the glass works rewarded by area that produced panes too thin to use. The article opened a discussion that ran for years in the press and the institutes, the first public argument about the system's economic mechanism since the twenties, and it led, in 1965, to reforms that enlarged enterprise autonomy and elevated profit and sales among the success indicators. The reforms were real, and they were contained: as their logic pressed toward prices, autonomy, and the discipline of the customer, it pressed toward questions the perimeter excluded, and before the decade closed the reform had been administratively digested into the system it was meant to change.

### What did the profitability discussion of the sixties reopen?

The question the twenties had argued and the settlement had closed: by what indicators a planned economy should judge its enterprises. The discussion restored public argument about the mechanism for the first time in a generation, and its containment marked the perimeter within which argument was again permitted.

For the purposes of this guide, the episode's value is diagnostic. The economists of the sixties were not the innocents of the twenties; they argued in full knowledge of what had happened to their predecessors, and their caution, the framing of systemic questions as technical adjustments, the retreat from conclusions at the perimeter's edge, is the constraint operating in its mature, non-lethal form. Nothing in the discussion was shot. A good deal in it was simply never concluded. The mature system's characteristic product was not the corpse but the unfinished argument, the commission that did not report, the proposal praised and shelved, of which Glushkov's network is the grandest instance. A reader who grasps the difference between the two periods, between settlement by terror and settlement by digestion, has grasped the periodisation of Soviet intellectual life more surely than any list of congresses can teach it.

## The world that argued: institutions of the twenties

The arguments of the twenties required a habitat, and the habitat deserves a description, because its destruction, more than any single resolution, is what ended the arguing. At its centre stood the Communist Academy in Moscow, a research institution founded in the revolution's first year to build the new scholarship, with sections for philosophy, economics, law, and history, its own journals, and a library assembled for the purpose. Beside it the Institute of Red Professors trained the cadres who were to staff the universities, admitting workers and party members of the revolutionary generation to an education compressed, intense, and, at its best, serious. The journals did the arguing in public: the Banner for philosophy, the Academy's Herald for the whole front, specialist periodicals for economics, law, and literary study, in whose pages the mechanists and dialecticians, the formalists and their opponents, the economists of accumulation and their critics, met in the confidence that print was where such meetings belonged. Around the institutions stood the publishing houses, state and cooperative, which issued translations of the world's philosophy and science at prices students could pay, so that a reader in Moscow in 1928 could buy Hegel and Ricardo, the manuscripts' first descriptions and the newest physics, in editions whose print runs the figures protocol of this article forbids it to number and whose cheapness every memoir attests.

### What made the arguments of the twenties possible?

A habitat: research academies with journals of their own, an institute training the new professoriate, publishing houses issuing the world's classics cheaply, and a settled expectation, inherited from the underground and the emigration, that print was where the tradition's questions were decided.

The habitat was not a liberal one. It was party-owned from the first, its appointments political, its perimeter patrolled, and its freedom always a licence rather than a right. What it possessed, and what the settlement withdrew, was internal plurality: more than one centre, more than one journal, more than one patron, so that a scholar refused in one place could argue in another and a position condemned by one institute could be defended from a second. The settlement's deepest act was not any single condemnation but the unification of the habitat, the mergers and closures that left each field with one institute, one journal, one line of appointment, one door. Ryazanov's institute was merged; the Academy was absorbed into the Academy of Sciences; the journals were thinned to a single voice per discipline. A scholar of the forties who wished to argue had nowhere else to go, and that, more than fear, though fear was plentiful, is why the arguing stopped. Monopoly, in scholarship as in trade, is measured not by the intentions of the monopolist but by the absence of the second door.

## Translation and commentary: the quiet scholarship

A final territory of the scholarship, easily overlooked because its products are other men's books, is the vast Soviet enterprise of translation and commentary. The tradition's Russian readers were served by editions of the classical philosophers prepared to standards that scholars abroad used and praised: Hegel above all, whose works the institute generation edited and whose philosophy Deborin's school, whatever its fate, taught a country to read; Kant and Spinoza in scholarly Russian dress; the Greek and Latin classics in the great series of the Academy's press. In the postwar decades the enterprise extended to the contemporary West. The Institute of Philosophy maintained specialists on every Western school, and its critical literature, surveys of existentialism, of analytic philosophy, of phenomenology and structuralism, written to refute their subjects, had the paradoxical effect of informing its readers about them with an accuracy the refutations could not spoil. A generation of Soviet philosophers learned what was thought in Paris and Oxford from hostile textbooks, as a generation of Western students learned Marx from hostile ones, and the symmetry was not lost on either. Arseny Gulyga's biographies of Kant and Hegel, works of genuine scholarship in the guise of a permitted genre, were read in the West in translation. Teodor Oizerman, the doyen of the history of philosophy, spent a long career writing the discipline's official history and, within its constraints, preserving the standards of a field whose first Soviet generation had included men the terror took. The quiet scholarship rarely appears in summaries of the period, because it produced no doctrines and few martyrs. It produced instead the condition of all the rest: a readership, maintained across two generations of supervision, that knew the tradition whole.

## The doctrine's other classroom: the colonial world

The codified doctrine had a second readership beyond the bloc, and its history complicates any account that treats the catechism as a purely European imposition. From 1921 the Communist University of the Toilers of the East in Moscow trained cadres from the colonised world, and its successors and sister institutions taught, in translation and summary, the same formations, the same features, the same authorised history, to students who carried the formulations home to movements fighting empires. Among the students who passed through Moscow's classrooms in the twenties were men who would lead the revolutions of China and Vietnam. For such readers the doctrine's rigidity was not its salient property. Its salient properties were its portability, a complete account of history and strategy between two covers; its promise, that the colonised peoples stood inside the main line of history rather than at its margin; and its provenance, a state that had broken with the empires and offered its texts, its teachers, and its example as proof that the break could be made. The scholarship this article has praised had no comparable export: nobody founded a movement on the morphology of the folktale. The doctrine travelled because doctrines, unlike scholarship, are built to travel, and the historian who weighs its career must enter its world readership, and the uses that readership made of it, on the credit side of a ledger whose debit side this article has not concealed.

### Why did the codified doctrine travel where the scholarship could not?

Because it was built to travel: a complete, portable account of history and strategy, teachable in a classroom and carryable in a suitcase, promising its readers in the colonised world a place in history's main line and a state as proof the promise could be kept.

The traffic was not one way. The questions the colonial students brought with them, about the peasantry, the national bourgeoisie, the stages a colonised society might skip or combine, pressed on the doctrine's custodians and exposed, earlier than any Western critique, the strain between the five formations and the histories the formations were meant to organise. The doctrine answered by codifying harder. But the strain is recorded in its texts, in the special theses and exceptions for the East that accumulate around the scheme like scaffolding around a building whose design the scaffolding contradicts. A reader tracing the doctrine's world career learns to watch the exceptions, for they mark the places where reality filed its objections.

## The woman question: theory, codification, silence

The early Soviet state legislated on the family with a radicalism no contemporary state matched, and its theory of the woman question was correspondingly ambitious. Alexandra Kollontai, the most original of the revolution's writers on the subject, argued in essays and fiction of the early twenties that the emancipation of women required not merely legal equality and labour, which the new state proclaimed, but the socialisation of housework and childcare and a transformation of intimate life itself, a new morality for a new society. The party's women's department carried such arguments into factories and villages until its abolition in 1930, on the ground that the woman question had been solved in principle and its separate organisation was superfluous. The codified doctrine thereafter treated the question as closed: the formations had no category for the household, the textbooks no chapter for the double burden, and the settlement of the middle thirties, which recriminalised abortion and re-sacralised the family in law, was accompanied by no theoretical discussion at all, since the theory had declared the matter settled in advance.

### How did the codified doctrine handle the woman question?

By declaring it solved. Once emancipation was defined as legal equality and participation in production, the theory had no category for the household's unpaid labour or the double burden, and the conservative family legislation of the middle thirties arrived without theoretical discussion, the question having been closed in advance.

The case earns its place in this guide because it isolates, in a single topic, the difference between the doctrine and a scholarship. A scholarship would have had to follow the evidence: the persistence of the double day, the segregation of the labour market, the retreat of the socialised services the twenties had promised. The doctrine, having settled the question by definition, could register none of this, and Soviet writing on women and the family dwindled for decades into celebration and demography. When empirical sociology revived in the sixties, the time-budget studies its researchers conducted, measuring in hours the working day of women and men, became some of the most quietly subversive documents of the late period, scholarship doing, with stopwatches, the work the doctrine had forbidden to theory. Kollontai, long exiled into diplomacy, was read again. The pattern needs no underlining by now: a question closed by definition, a reality that persisted unmeasured, and a recovery that began, at the gradient's technical end, with counting.

## Sociology: the discipline that had to be invented twice

Sociology had a peculiar status in the Soviet scheme: officially it did not exist. Historical materialism, the textbooks explained, was itself the science of society, and empirical sociology was classified as a bourgeois discipline, a classification that spared the doctrine the embarrassment of data. The cost was that the society which proclaimed itself the most scientifically governed on earth knew remarkably little, in any systematic and publishable form, about itself: about its own stratification, its mobility, its opinions, its villages emptying and its cities filling. From the late fifties a group of younger researchers began to rebuild an empirical discipline under the protective label of concrete social research, founding a professional association in 1958 and institutes in the following decade, and conducting the surveys, of workers' time, of readers' tastes, of young people's plans, whose results circulated as the period's most reliable mirror. The sociologists' position reproduced the old gradient in a new field: their methods were technical and their samples checkable, which protected them, while their conclusions touched the legitimation of the order, which exposed them, and their history oscillated accordingly between expansion and discipline.

### Why did empirical sociology have to be rebuilt under a protective label?

Because the doctrine claimed its place. Historical materialism presented itself as the science of society, leaving empirical inquiry classified as a bourgeois substitute, so the researchers of the revival worked as concrete social studies, protected by their methods and exposed by their conclusions.

The field's most famous document was never published at all. In 1983 a Siberian sociologist and economist, Tatiana Zaslavskaya, prepared for a closed seminar in Novosibirsk a report on the mechanism of the economy's stagnation, arguing that the system's relations of production had fallen behind its productive forces, in the catechism's own terms, and that the interests of its administrative stratum blocked the adjustment the doctrine prescribed. The report leaked, circulated in the West under the name of its city, and was read in Moscow as what it was: the doctrine's categories turned, by a scholar working inside its institutions, into an indictment of the doctrine's custodians. Within a few years the vocabulary of that report had become the vocabulary of the state's own reform programme. The episode is a fitting close to the sociology story and a parable of the whole subject: the last service the codified doctrine performed for its country was to supply the terms in which its failure was diagnosed, by a sociologist the doctrine had spent fifty years insisting could not exist.

## The degree and the dissertation: how a scholar was certified

A word on the machinery of certification will help readers weigh the scholarship's conditions, because the machinery differed from the Western pattern in one decisive respect. The Soviet scholar's degrees, Candidate of Sciences and the higher Doctor of Sciences, were not conferred by universities alone but attested by a central commission in Moscow, which reviewed dissertations and could annul them. The dissertation defence was a public ritual, with official opponents appointed to criticise the work, and in the supervised fields the ritual included an invisible participant: the requirement that the work's conclusions conform, and be seen to conform, to the doctrine's positions on its topic. Scholars learned to build the conformity into the frame, the introduction and conclusion written in the required idiom, the middle chapters left to the evidence, a structure so standard that readers navigated by it instinctively. The central attestation also gave the settlement procedure a quiet instrument, usable without arrests: a degree annulled, a defence postponed indefinitely, a specialty struck from the approved list, could end a scholarly life in silence. Against this must be set the machinery's other face. The same centralisation maintained, across an enormous country, standards of linguistic and textual training that the best dissertations exhibit on every page, and the ritual of public defence before appointed opponents, whatever its corruptions in the supervised fields, preserved in the technical fields a genuine culture of examination. The work praised in these pages was produced by people certified in this machinery, and its virtues and its deformations both bear the machinery's stamp.

## The first settlement: the expulsion of the philosophers

The procedure by which the new state managed thought had a rehearsal before the codification, gentler than what followed and instructive for that reason. In 1922 the government drew up lists of intellectuals judged incorrigibly hostile: historians, economists, sociologists, and a notable company of philosophers, among them Nikolai Berdyaev, Semyon Frank, and Nikolai Lossky, religious thinkers of international standing. The listed were arrested, offered the choice of exile or worse, and shipped from Petrograd to Germany in the autumn of that year, in the vessels remembered as the philosophers' steamers. The expulsion was, by the standards the state itself would soon set, an act of moderation, and it was understood at the time as a warning shot: the universities were to be cleared of independent authority, and the clearing would henceforth be administrative. The exiles built in Berlin, Prague, and Paris the institutions of Russian thought abroad, whose journals conducted, for two decades, the argument about Russia's future that could no longer be conducted in Russia. The episode belongs at the head of this article's story because it established the grammar of everything after: a list, an administrative act, a field cleared. The later settlements differ from it in scale and in mercy, not in kind, and the scholars of the twenties who watched the steamers leave understood, as their memoirs attest, exactly what had been announced.

### What did the expulsion of the philosophers announce?

That intellectual authority independent of the state would be treated as a problem of administration. The lists, the arrests, and the ships of 1922 cleared the universities by decree and founded an emigration that continued abroad the arguments henceforth forbidden at home.

## The settlement of the history of philosophy

The last great settlement of the dictator's years fell on philosophy itself, and its text survives, which allows the procedure to be examined in the leadership's own words. In 1947 Andrei Zhdanov, the party secretary charged with ideology, addressed a discussion convened to judge a new history of Western European philosophy by Georgy Aleksandrov, the head of the party's propaganda apparatus and a corresponding member of the Academy. The book was faulted, in the discussion and in Zhdanov's concluding speech, for objectivism: it had treated the bourgeois philosophers with scholarly respect, expounding their systems before criticising them, and had thereby, in the speech's terms, failed to exhibit the partisan spirit that a Marxist history required. Philosophy was defined, in the speech's most quoted sentence, as a weapon in the struggle, and its history as the record of materialism's war on idealism, to be written as one writes the history of a war, from one side.

### What did the intervention on the history of philosophy demand?

Partisanship as a method. A history of philosophy was to be written as the record of materialism's struggle against idealism, expounding opponents in order to defeat them, and scholarly respect for the systems of the past was condemned as objectivism, a failure of vigilance.

The consequences ran through the discipline for a generation. Histories of philosophy were rewritten as battle chronicles. The teaching of the subject acquired its mature form, in which every thinker arrived pre-classified, and the classification, materialist or idealist, progressive or reactionary, preceded and largely replaced the reading. Yet the episode contains the usual Soviet complication. Aleksandrov, the condemned author, was not arrested; he was demoted, survived, and returned to direct the Institute of Philosophy, where he presided over the discipline his disgrace had regimented. And the very demand for a history of philosophy as a discipline, however deformed, sustained the chairs, the translations, and the training from which the revival's historians of philosophy emerged. The settlement of 1947 is thus the doctrine's whole career in one episode: a text condemned for the scholarly virtue of fairness, a method prescribed by a politician's speech, a discipline narrowed and preserved by the same act, and a condemned man administering the consequences.

## The commune drafts: the archive argues back

One publication of the institute years deserves separate mention because it shows the archive unsettling the doctrine from inside the tradition's own texts. In 1881 Vera Zasulich, a Russian revolutionary then in Geneva, wrote to Marx asking whether the Russian peasant commune, the obshchina, was doomed to dissolve before capitalism, as the scheme of Capital seemed to require, or whether it might serve as the point of departure for a different development. Marx, in the last years of his life, drafted reply after reply, four drafts survive, wrestling with the question, and sent a short, guarded answer. The drafts, in which Marx entertained the possibility that Russia might avoid the capitalist path if the commune were preserved and a revolution in the West supported it, lay unpublished until Ryazanov printed them in 1924 in the archive journal of his institute. Their publication armed every subsequent argument about unilinear history with the founder's own hesitation. The codified doctrine, with its five formations in single file, had no comfortable place for the drafts, and Soviet discussion of them was correspondingly muted for decades. But they were in print, in the institute's edition, citable, and in the thaw discussions of the formations they returned to the argument, a text the custodian state had published and could not unpublished. The episode generalises the archival paradox into a principle: an edition, once made to scholarly standards, escapes its makers' control, and the most subversive act available to a scholar in a supervised system was sometimes simply to publish the founder accurately.

### What did the commune drafts do to the codified scheme?

They documented the founder's own refusal of the single sequence. Marx's drafts for his reply on the Russian commune entertained a path that skipped the capitalist formation, and their publication by the institute in 1924 left the hesitation permanently in print, citable against the five formations in single file.

## The recovery: how the record was reassembled

When the state ceased to exist, the reassessment of its intellectual history began from the archives it had kept, and the first finding of the reassessment was the archive's own doubleness: the files of the settlement procedure, the denunciations, the protocols of the discussions, the records of arrest and rehabilitation, had been preserved by the apparatus that conducted them, in the same bureaucratic completeness with which the institute had preserved the manuscripts. The history of Soviet thought since then has been written substantially from those files, supplemented by the memoirs of survivors and the drawers of the unpublished. Its main lines can be summarised. The suppressed works were published: the manuscripts kept in typescript, the lectures preserved in students' notes, the books withdrawn in the thirties and recoverable from the closed collections where librarians, at their own risk, had preserved rather than destroyed them. The fates were documented name by name, in the reference works of the memory societies and the commissions on rehabilitation, which established the dates and circumstances that this article has relied on for the scholars it names. And the reputations sorted themselves by the test proposed earlier in these pages: the work that disinterested successors found they needed survived, and the rest, the vast literature of exegesis and denunciation, passed into the custody of historians as evidence about its producers.

### How was the record of Soviet thought reassembled after the state ended?

From its own files. The apparatus had preserved the protocols of its settlements and the records of its victims in bureaucratic completeness, and the reassessment proceeded by publishing the suppressed works, documenting the fates name by name, and letting the surviving scholarship be sorted by use.

The reassessment also corrected the West's picture in one respect worth recording. Western scholarship, dependent on the translated sample and the émigré testimony, had underestimated the extent of the revival and the quality of the late period's best work, much of which existed only in Russian and some only in typescript. The recovery of the drawers revised the period upward, as this article has noted, without revising its verdict on the system: the better the late work proved to be, the more clearly its conditions stood condemned, since the conditions had permitted that work only in the interstices and had exacted, as the price of the interstices, the silence of its authors on the questions that mattered most. A tradition is not acquitted by the excellence it failed to prevent.

## The dialectics of nature: another manuscript, another fault line

The institute's publications armed one more argument that ran through the whole Soviet period and beyond it. In 1925 Ryazanov's archive journal published, for the first time, the manuscripts of Engels's unfinished Dialectics of Nature, the notes in which Engels had attempted to exhibit the dialectical laws at work in the sciences of his day. The publication fixed the text at the centre of the Soviet doctrine: the dialectics of nature became the catechism's charter, the proof that the laws of thought and society were laws of the universe. In the same years, the Western tradition's most influential early work, Lukács's History and Class Consciousness of 1923, had relegated the dialectics of nature in a famous reservation, treating the dialectic as a relation between subject and object in history rather than a law of mindless matter. The fault line between the two Marxisms, the Soviet and the Western, thus ran through a manuscript question: whether the founder's collaborator had founded a philosophy of nature or had merely sketched one the tradition could decline. Soviet philosophy could not decline it, since the canonical chapter of 1938 taught the laws as universal, and the whole apparatus of the philosophy of natural science described above existed to service that teaching. Western Marxism, free to choose, mostly declined, and treated the Soviet insistence as the catechism's tell. The episode deserves its place here because it shows the two traditions divided not by politics alone but by bibliography: each built its philosophy on the texts its circumstances had made central, and the Soviet centrality of the Engels manuscripts was itself an artefact of a Moscow edition.

### What divided the two traditions over the dialectics of nature?

A manuscript and its weight. The Soviet doctrine, chartered by the Moscow publication of Engels's notes in 1925 and codified in the canonical chapter, taught the dialectical laws as laws of the universe; the Western tradition, following an early reservation, treated the dialectic as historical and let the philosophy of nature go.

## The thaw's historians: the narrative revised from inside

The revival in history writing produced the period's most consequential single book and its author's characteristic fate. Alexander Nekrich, a historian at the Academy's Institute of History, published in 1965 a study of the catastrophe of June 1941, the German invasion that found the Soviet command unprepared. The book documented, from the records, the warnings received and disregarded, the officers destroyed in the purges of the military in the years just before, the orders that forbade response to provocation. Its argument was conducted entirely within the permitted frame: the errors were the dictator's, the system and the party were their victim and their corrector. Even so framed, the book crossed the perimeter, because it treated the founding catastrophe of the state's mythology as a subject for evidence. The discussion organised at the institute in 1966 turned into a demonstration in the book's favour by the historians present, which settled its author's fate by the mature procedure: not arrest, but expulsion from the party, the loss of his post, and a decade of obstruction ending in emigration in 1976. The book itself was withdrawn.

### What did the study of the invasion's first days establish about the perimeter?

That the perimeter ran through the founding mythology. A documented account of the catastrophe of the invasion, argued entirely within the permitted frame of blaming the dictator, was nonetheless withdrawn and its author expelled, because the catastrophe's causes touched the system's account of itself.

Beside Nekrich's case stands the larger enterprise of Roy Medvedev, a historian and teacher who spent the sixties compiling, from interviews, memoirs, and the published record, a history of the terror from inside the tradition, Let History Judge, which circulated in typescript at home and was published abroad in 1971. Medvedev wrote as a Leninist against the cult, believing the record, honestly told, would reform the system that had produced it, and his book's existence, a work of scholarship assembled by a private citizen without archive access, from the testimony the system had tried to scatter, is among the period's defining facts. The two cases mark the perimeter's exact location in the historical field. The terror could be documented, at ruinous personal cost, by a scholar working outside the institutions. It could not be documented, at any cost compatible with a career, from inside them. Between those two sentences lies the whole economy of late Soviet scholarship.

## The encyclopedia problem: doctrine in reference form

The doctrine's relation to fact is nowhere more visible than in its reference books. The Great Soviet Encyclopedia, the state's flagship work of reference, was revised across its editions in step with the settlements, its articles on persons rising and falling with their subjects. The famous instance came after the fall of Lavrentiy Beria, the executed chief of the security apparatus, when subscribers to the encyclopedia received, by post, a replacement gathering with instructions: the pages carrying the article on Beria were to be cut out and the enclosed pages pasted in, which treated instead, at convenient length, the Bering Strait. The episode is often told as a joke, and it is one, but its structure deserves the scholar's attention. A reference work is a promise that the record is stable enough to consult. A reference work revised by mailed instructions announces that the record is a function of the present distribution of power, and that the reader's duty is not to know but to keep the book in conformity. Every Soviet scholar who used the encyclopedia, which is to say every Soviet scholar, worked in the knowledge that the shelf itself was supervised. The institute's editions of Marx stand at the opposite pole of the same culture: texts established so that no instruction could revise them, variants printed so that no editor's preference could hide them. The distance between the encyclopedia and the edition is the distance this article has been measuring from its first page.

### What did the replacement pages sent to encyclopedia subscribers announce?

That the record itself was administered. A reference work whose articles on persons could be exchanged by post, the fallen minister's biography replaced by a strait's geography, declared that the past would be kept in conformity with the present, and every scholar who consulted the shelf knew it.

## The unofficial seminar: scholarship outside the institutions

The late period developed, alongside the official scholarship, an unofficial one, and a complete guide should register its existence and its limits. In the kitchens and borrowed rooms of Moscow, Leningrad, and the other university cities, circles of scholars and students met through the sixties and seventies to read and argue outside the curriculum: the young philosophers who gathered around the memory of the suppressed schools, the readers of typescripts, the audiences who filled any hall where a Mamardashvili was announced to lecture on a permitted topic and spoke, as his hearers understood, about everything. The typescript literature, samizdat, carried essays, translations, and whole books from hand to hand in editions of a dozen copies, each retyping a small act of conspiracy. This unofficial culture preserved the period's intellectual honour in the eyes of posterity, and its testimony dominates the memoirs. But a guide concerned with scholarship must add the qualification its participants themselves would have insisted on: a seminar in a kitchen cannot run an archive, collate an edition, equip a laboratory, or train a generation, and the work that required institutions, which is to say most of the work this article has praised, had to be done inside the institutions, under their conditions, by the arts of adaptation described above. The unofficial culture was the conscience of Soviet scholarship. Its body remained, necessarily, official.

## The catechism's reach: school, army, and the study circle

The doctrine's presence in the university has been described; its presence in the rest of life was wider. In the schools, the senior classes met the formations and the authorised history in courses whose textbooks descended from the Short Course. In the armed forces, political education was a standing duty, conducted by officers of a separate political administration, on programmes that carried the doctrine's elements to every conscript. In the party itself, a system of study circles and political schools enrolled millions in courses of ascending formality, from the elementary circle reading the canonical history to the Higher Party School in Moscow, whose diplomas were passports to the apparatus. The total effect, across two generations, was the creation of the largest population ever subjected to a single philosophical curriculum, and the creation, within it, of the distribution of responses recorded above: belief, fluency, and the consequential minority for whom the curriculum's own texts became instruments against it. For the historian of philosophy the episode has no parallel. Doctrines have been established by states before, but none had at its disposal the printing capacity, the school system, and the seventy years that this one commanded, and the experiment's result is therefore a datum of the first importance for any theory of how ideas spread: saturation teaching, sustained over generations, produced not conviction but a repertoire, and the repertoire outlasted the conviction, where any existed, by exactly the length of time the apparatus continued to require its performance.

### What did saturation teaching of a single doctrine produce?

Not conviction at scale, but a repertoire: a shared vocabulary and a set of formulations that a whole population could perform, within which belief, irony, and dissent all had to conduct themselves, and which evaporated as a living creed within years of the requirement's removal.

## Marxology: the study of Marx as a Soviet profession

It remains to notice the strangest professional specialisation the system produced. In the country where Marxism was the state doctrine, the scholarly study of Marx, his texts, his biography, the chronology of his notebooks, the identification of his sources, became a recognised profession with its own institutes, journals, and international standing, practised under the name its Western colleagues also used, Marxology. The profession's existence was double-edged in the usual way. Its practitioners commanded resources no Western scholar could match: the archive, the collected library, the staff. Its conclusions, where they touched the authorised image of the founder, were supervised with a vigilance proportional to the founder's importance, and the authorised image, serene, systematic, and complete, excluded the hesitations, the abandoned drafts, and the unfinished character of the actual Nachlass. Yet the profession's daily work, the dating of a notebook, the deciphering of a hand, the tracing of an excerpt to its source, was scholarship of the ordinary, checkable kind, and its results were used by the international community of Marx scholars without distinction of camp. When the international edition passed to its Amsterdam foundation, the community it served included the heirs of the Moscow institute as colleagues, a continuity the founder of that institute, who had imagined exactly such a community and been shot by the state that employed him, would have recognised as the vindication of his side of the argument.

## The Western argument about the Soviet mind

The assessment of Soviet thought was itself a battlefield in the West, and a reader of this guide should know the positions, since the guide's own procedure was formed in argument with both. One school, associated with the totalitarian model and with historians such as Richard Pipes and Martin Malia, held that the system was of a piece: that the doctrine, the party, and the terror formed a single logic, and that the intellectual production of such a system could be read as the ideology of that logic, with the exceptions classified as camouflage or accident. The other school, the revisionist historians of the Soviet Union such as Sheila Fitzpatrick and her generation, reconstructed the society from its archives and its everyday life, and found in the record a complexity the model flattened: real belief alongside performance, real scholarship alongside exegesis, a population negotiating its system rather than merely suffering or serving it. The argument between the schools was conducted, on the revisionist side, substantially from the Soviet archives after their opening, which gave the dispute the unusual property of being progressively settled by evidence.

### How did Western scholars divide over the assessment of Soviet thought?

Between a school that read the intellectual production as the ideology of a single totalitarian logic, exceptions classified as accident or camouflage, and a school that rebuilt the society's picture from archival evidence and found belief, scholarship, and negotiation where the model predicted only performance.

This guide's procedure owes something to each and is captive to neither. With the first school it holds that the doctrine and the terror were connected, that the settlement procedure was the system's characteristic act, and that no assessment is honest that treats the constraint as weather. With the second it holds that the record, examined closely, refuses the wholesale verdict: that the validation of specific works by disinterested successors is a fact no model may wave away, and that the separation of doctrine from scholarship is not a rescue operation but a finding, forced on any reader who follows the evidence field by field. A reader who wishes to test the finding has the means: the texts are listed below, the editions are in the libraries, and the argument, unlike the disputes this article has described, will be settled by whoever shows up to have it.

## The Tartu school: semiotics at the edge of the map

The gradient that governed Soviet scholarship had a geography as well as a logic, and its most instructive product grew at the union's western edge, in the Estonian university city of Tartu. There, from the early nineteen sixties, Yuri Lotman, a scholar of Russian literature formed in Leningrad, built a school devoted to semiotics, the study of sign systems, which treated a culture as a set of languages and texts whose interactions could be modelled with a rigour borrowed from linguistics and information theory. The school's journal, Sign Systems Studies, founded in 1964, was the oldest periodical of its field in the world. Its summer schools, held at a university sports base in the Estonian countryside, drew the Moscow structuralists and the boldest of the younger scholars into days of argument of a freedom participants remembered for decades. Lotman's own work ranged from the structure of the artistic text to the semiotics of Russian culture, and his concept of the semiosphere, the continuum of signs within which alone any single text can mean, gave the school's programme its widest statement.

### Why did a school of semiotics flourish in a provincial university?

Because the periphery was a shelter. A technical method, a small language's university, and a field whose objects were texts rather than policies placed the Tartu school at the gradient's safe end, where its members could build an international discipline under the flag of a permitted specialism.

The school's relation to the doctrine was the mature system's characteristic bargain, struck at a high level of intelligence on both sides. Semiotics was not Marxism, and its practitioners did not pretend otherwise in private; in public, the work was framed in the vocabulary of materialist science, and the framing sufficed, because the field's objects, poems, films, rituals, myths, stood at a distance from legitimation that the custodians could tolerate. In exchange the school delivered what the system chronically lacked and dimly valued: an intellectual enterprise of international standing, cited abroad, visited by foreign scholars, a proof that the country's universities could still originate a discipline. The foreign visitors carried the work out; the translations made Lotman, in his last decades, the most internationally read Soviet humanist of his generation. The Tartu case thus refines the article's model in one respect. The gradient's safe end was not merely a refuge for remnants of older schools. It was a place where new schools could be founded, provided their founders mastered the arts of framing and chose objects the centre could afford to ignore. The tragedy of the arrangement is the selection it imposed: a culture's ablest minds, choosing their questions by their distance from power, will answer many questions brilliantly except the ones their own society most needs answered.

## The recovery of Bakhtin: a reputation rebuilt by the young

The posthumous careers of the suppressed were sometimes, in the thaw, careers resumed in life, and the most affecting instance is the recovery of Mikhail Bakhtin. In the early nineteen sixties a group of young Moscow scholars, among them Vadim Kozhinov and Sergei Bocharov, learned that the author of the Dostoevsky book of 1929, long presumed dead or vanished, was living in obscurity in Saransk, teaching at a provincial institute. They visited him, pressed for the republication of his work, and carried his cause into the Moscow literary world with the persistence of disciples. The Dostoevsky book appeared in a revised edition in 1963; the Rabelais study, written before the war, was published in 1965; the old man, who had lost a leg and most of his manuscripts, one book famously surviving because its paper had been used, in the war years, for purposes no archivist would have chosen, found himself in his seventies the centre of a school. He died in 1975, and the international fame that followed, as the translations spread his concepts of dialogism and carnival through the humanities of the world, exceeded anything the Moscow disciples had promised him. The episode is recorded here for what it shows about the revival's mechanics. The recovery was not decreed. It was accomplished by the young, acting through the interstices of the institutions, a journal's editor persuaded here, a publishing plan amended there, and it succeeded because the thaw had made such advocacy survivable. Between the settlement and the revival stands this difference: in the one, the young made their careers by denouncing the old; in the other, a decisive minority made theirs by rescuing them.

## The question the doctrine could not ask

Running beneath every episode in this article is a single absence, and naming it gathers the argument. The doctrine was a theory of society that could not be applied to the society that taught it. Historical materialism offered categories for feudalism, for capitalism, for the transition between them; for the social formation in which its own textbooks were printed it offered only a destination, socialism, defined by the absence of the categories' usual objects, private property and exploiting classes, and therefore proof against analysis. A Soviet scholar who asked, with the doctrine's own instruments, who held power in the Soviet Union, by what mechanism the surplus was distributed, what interests the administrative stratum pursued as a stratum, had stepped outside the perimeter in a single question, whatever the loyalty of the asking. Such questions were asked, in private, in the unofficial seminars, in the end by the Novosibirsk report in that same vocabulary, and their suppression is the deepest of the settlements, deeper than genetics, because it protected not a doctrine about seeds but the doctrine about everything. The scholarship this article has honoured worked, in field after field, around that central silence, and the silence shaped it as surely as the censorship did: the brilliance expended on folktales, on manuscripts, on the psychology of children and the logic of science, was brilliance diverted from the one subject on which the tradition claimed expertise. A complete assessment must hold both truths in one sentence, as this article has tried to do throughout: the work was real, and the work was displaced, and the displacement is the system's signature on every page of it.

## The Luzin affair: the settlement that stopped short

Mathematics, the freest of the Soviet sciences, has its own settlement story, and its ending, uniquely, was a reprieve. Nikolai Luzin, the founder of the Moscow school of function theory and the teacher of a brilliant generation, was attacked in the summer of 1936 in a campaign in the central press, accused of servility to foreign science, of publishing his best results abroad, of heading a group hostile to the Soviet reconstruction of mathematics. A commission of the Academy heard the case over days of denunciation that followed the period's script in every particular but the last: Luzin was condemned in terms that stopped short of the fatal classifications, retained his membership of the Academy, and lived to work, diminished and cautious, until 1950. His school, the famous Lusitania of the Moscow mathematicians, scattered into the branches of mathematics its members founded across the country. Historians of the episode have established how close the campaign came to the standard ending and how the intervention of colleagues, and the evident absurdity of annihilating the country's most distinguished mathematical school on the eve of its industrial mobilisation, arrested it. The reprieve is as instructive as any catastrophe in this article. It shows the settlement procedure operating as a procedure, with a throttle: the pressure could be applied, measured, and, where a field mattered indispensably to the state and its practitioners stood together, the campaign of denunciation could be stopped. Genetics had no such defenders in place and no such deliverable. The difference between the fate of Luzin and the fate of Vavilov is not a difference in their cases' merits. It is a difference in the distribution of protection, which is to say, in politics, which is to say, in everything the scholarship was supposed to be decided by and was not.

### What does the reprieve of a mathematician show about the settlement procedure?

That it had a throttle. Where a field's value to the state was undeniable and its defenders organised and placed, the denunciation drive could be halted short of destruction; where the defenders were isolated and the deliverable blamable on others, as in genetics, it ran to the end.

The Moscow mathematics that Luzin's pupils built became, in the postwar decades, one of the glories of the country's science, the school of Kolmogorov and Gelfand drawing students into seminars whose standards the world's mathematicians acknowledged. Its relative freedom was purchased by the field's uselessness to legitimation and its usefulness to everything else, the exact position the gradient predicts, and its members, who knew the history of 1936 as other schools knew their founding charters, maintained the field's defences with a corporate vigilance that amounted to a constitution. The lesson the mathematicians drew and transmitted was not about mathematics. It was about the state: that survival was a collective enterprise, that the perimeter was negotiated, and that a discipline which wished to think had first to organise.

## The pedagogy of the collective

One more field completes the map, because it shows the scholarship addressing, in the twenties, the question the doctrine later forbade: how the new society's people were to be made. Anton Makarenko, a teacher who took charge of colonies for homeless and delinquent children in the Ukraine of the civil war's aftermath, built in his communes a pedagogy of the collective: self-governing detachments, productive labour, discipline exercised by the group upon its members in the name of ends the group had adopted. His account of the work, The Road to Life, published in parts through the thirties with Gorky's patronage, became the most widely read Soviet book on education, and his lectures on the collective were studied wherever the century attempted education for citizenship. Makarenko's standing in his own country oscillated with the pedagogical settlements; pedology's fall in 1936 damaged the scientific child study adjacent to his work, and his own canonisation, when it came, embalmed him into a monument whose texts were honoured more than read. Abroad, his influence ran through the century's experiments in residential education and restorative discipline, acknowledged by practitioners who had never heard the doctrine's name. His case fits the article's pattern with one variation: the work was practical before it was theoretical, its validation the survival and flourishing of children whom the period had discarded, and its author's difficulties arose not from heresy but from the system's preference, in education as elsewhere, for the administered over the achieved.

## Misreadings corrected

Five misreadings recur in the summaries of this subject, and the argument so far supplies the correction of each. The first is that Soviet Marxism was uniformly worthless. The record refutes it in the only way such claims can be refuted, by instances that survive inspection: an edition, a psychology, a morphology, a theory of law, a mathematics of planning, each validated by successors who were free to discard them. The second misreading is that the official doctrine was Marx's own philosophy, an error the doctrine encouraged and its enemies had no reason to discourage. The doctrine was a codification, made at a date, by identifiable men, from a selection of the founder's and his collaborator's writings, with the hesitations edited out and the open problems closed; the manuscripts the same state published are the standing refutation of its claim to completeness. The third misreading is that the philosophical disputes of the period were resolved intellectually, that the mechanists lost an argument and the geneticists lost a debate. They lost their journals, their chairs, and in a documented number of cases their lives, to decisions taken in committees, and the distinction between losing an argument and being abolished is the distinction on which the whole subject turns. The fourth misreading is that Soviet scholarship had no international influence, which the shelves of any university library refute: the humanist Marx, the developmental psychology of the social mind, the structural study of narrative, and the value-form reading of Capital all entered the world's scholarship from Soviet sources. The fifth misreading is the sentimental inversion of the first, that the humanist Marx was recovered against Soviet scholarship, in spite of Moscow. He was recovered from it, from the institute's editions, by readers the institute's founder would have recognised as his intended audience. The school that defined itself against the doctrine was, bibliographically, the doctrine's own archive's child.

### How should a reader weigh a text against the institution that preserved it?

Separately, and in both directions. A text's survival under a murderous institution neither acquits the institution nor condemns the text; the text is weighed by the ordinary tests of evidence and argument, and the institution by its conduct toward the makers and their rivals.

## What is worth reading, and in what order

The reader who wishes to test this article's claims against the sources has a manageable shelf, and the order matters. Begin with the doctrine itself, in the canonical chapter, read entire and without intermediaries: it is short, it is lucid, and everything else in the subject is a commentary on or a flight from it. Read next the young Marx's Paris manuscripts, in the edition tradition the institute founded, and register the distance between the young philosopher of alienation and the catechism that claimed him; the distance is the subject in miniature. Then Vygotsky, Thought and Language above all, for the scholarship at its most fertile, and Voloshinov on language for its most elegant. Rubin's essays on value will repay the reader who knows the opening of Capital, and will change the reading of it. Propp's morphology is the most pleasurable book on the list and the easiest to test, since the tales are in every library and the functions can be checked against them by any sceptic. Ilyenkov's monograph on the ascent is the revival's monument and the hardest book here; it should be read last among the primaries, with Capital open beside it. Of the secondary literature, Kołakowski's great history remains the indispensable adversary, to be read with the gradient in mind and with the knowledge that its author, who had lived inside the system he anatomised, would have rejected this article's distinctions as too merciful, which is a reason for reading him first among the critics and arguing as one goes. The examination of the subject for assessment purposes is treated in the companion exam guide in this series, and the wider question of the system's character, which this article has deliberately routed rather than answered, belongs to the articles already linked in these pages.

## The five formations examined

The doctrine's philosophy of history deserves the same fair statement as its philosophy of nature, since it was the portion of the system most widely taught and most consequential for the humanities. The scheme, fixed in the canonical chapter, arranged the whole of human history as five formations in succession: the primitive communal order, slavery, feudalism, capitalism, and socialism, each generated by the development of the productive forces, each giving way when its relations of production fettered those forces, the transitions accomplished, in the classical cases, by revolution. As a teaching instrument the scheme had real power. It gave students of every subject a single frame in which the ancient world, the middle ages, and the modern economy could be compared, and it directed attention, permanently, to the material organisation of societies, to who worked, who appropriated, and by what right. Generations of historians trained in it carried its questions into archives where its answers did not survive, which is the history of every fruitful scheme.

### Where does the five formations scheme break against the evidence?

At its edges and its joints: in societies that fit no formation, in transitions that took centuries or ran backward, in the coexistence of modes the scheme required to succeed one another, and above all in the formation it could not examine, the one in which the scheme itself was taught.

Its weaknesses were the weaknesses of its strength. A scheme that explains everything in advance must classify the recalcitrant cases by force, and Soviet historiography's files are full of the forcing: the ancient East assigned to slavery or to feudalism by turns, the Russian commune debated under the shadow of the abolished Asiatic mode, the nomadic empires fitted with formations they had never heard of. The thaw's discussions of the scheme, in the journals of the sixties, reopened several of these files with an honesty that surprised foreign observers, and established, within the perimeter, that the scheme was a research programme with anomalies rather than a table of the elements. But the perimeter held at the decisive point. The fifth formation, the one in which the examiners themselves lived, was not available as an object of the same analysis, and a philosophy of history that exempts its own present from history has announced, in its structure, the limit this article has traced in every field: the doctrine could be about anything except the conditions of its own utterance.

## The classic in its homeland: how Capital was read

A tradition is known by the reading of its classic, and the Soviet reading of Capital passed through phases that summarise the whole history. In the twenties the book was a research programme: Rubin and his opponents argued over its first chapter as over a living text, the institute prepared its scholarly apparatus, and the reading was conducted by men who assumed the argument was open. In the codification the book became a quarry: the canonical statements were extracted, arranged under the features and formations, and the whole reassembled as the doctrine, so that a student could be examined on Marx without the inconvenience of Marx's hesitations, his abandoned plans, his six-book design of which the published volumes were the fragment. In the thaw the book became a programme again, for the revival: Ilyenkov's reconstruction of its method was, in effect, a demonstration that the classic, read whole, refuted the catechism that claimed to contain it, and the Capital seminars of the sixties and seventies, in Moscow and in the provincial universities, were the revival's true classrooms, where the young learned to read a text against its authorised summary. The sequence is worth a student's attention because it is not peculiar to this tradition. Every classic that a state adopts suffers the three ages: argument, quarry, and recovery, and the recovery, in this case, was performed by the adopted state's own scholars, in the adopted state's own institutes, with the adopted state's own editions. The archive, once more, had outargued the apparatus.

## Ethics: the field that arrived late

Moral philosophy was the doctrine's poor relation, and its history is a small parable of the system's priorities. The revolutionary generation had expected morality to wither with the state: the early jurists and educators treated bourgeois morality as a class instrument and the new morality as a matter of discipline and devotion to the collective, to be produced by practice rather than theorised. The codified textbooks accordingly gave ethics a chapter of exhortation, and when the party programme of 1961 appended a moral code of the builder of communism, twelve precepts of honesty, collectivism, and intolerance of parasitism, the code's form, a list to be learned, was the doctrine's ethics in miniature. A professional ethics arrived only in the thaw, when scholars such as Oleg Drobnitsky began to reconstruct the subject as an inquiry into the nature of moral consciousness rather than a branch of instruction. Drobnitsky's work on the concept of morality, published in the year of his early death in 1977, treated moral demands as a distinct form of social regulation, irreducible to law, custom, or utility, and argued the case with a patience that owed nothing to exhortation. The field he founded survived him in his students. Its lateness is the datum: a doctrine that proposed to remake humanity had, for its first four decades, no theory of the moral life worth the name, because a theory would have had to ask what its subjects actually valued, and that question, like the sociologists' questions, belonged to the class the perimeter excluded.

## The Institute of Philosophy: a biography of the centre

The institutions of the scholarship have appeared in these pages one by one; the central one deserves a paragraph of biography, since its history is the discipline's in concentrate. The Institute of Philosophy of the Academy of Sciences was founded in 1929, in the settlement's opening season, and inherited the personnel and the problems of the bodies it replaced. Its directors were, by turns, the victors of the disputes it housed: Deborin himself in its first years, then the men who had destroyed him, Mitin among them, then, after the war, Aleksandrov, the condemned historian of philosophy of 1947, restored to administer the discipline his fall had regimented. Its journal, Problems of Philosophy, founded in the year of the Zhdanov intervention, carried the discipline's official life and, in the thaw, much of its real one, its pages the arena in which the revival's positions were first printed and first attacked. The institute's building in Moscow contained, across the decades, the catechism's administrators and the catechism's most dangerous readers, sometimes in the same corridor and occasionally in the same person at different hours. A foreign visitor of the seventies, shown the library and the seminar rooms, might have been attending any great institute of philosophy in Europe, until the conversation turned, as it always turned at the same invisible fence, and the visitor learned where the perimeter ran by the shape of the silence beyond it.

## The last decade: a doctrine unrevised

The doctrine outlived the revival that had refuted it in practice, and its last decade has a pathos the guide should record. The textbooks of the nineteen eighties were, in their philosophical chapters, the textbooks of the fifties with the names updated: the same features, the same formations, the same examples, printed for students whose teachers, in the institutes, were reading the recovered Bakhtin and the unexpurgated manuscripts. No congress revised the catechism, because revision would have required a decision about what the doctrine was for, and that decision belonged to the class of questions the system had organised itself not to take. When the end came, between 1989 and 1991, the doctrine was not refuted, defeated in argument, or formally abolished. It was discontinued, like a journal whose subscription has lapsed: the courses were renamed, the chairs retitled, the examinations quietly dropped, and the largest philosophical teaching enterprise in history closed without a closing ceremony. Philosophers who had spent careers inside it dispersed into the new landscape, some to reinvent themselves as historians of the tradition they had served, some to carry its genuine questions, the formation of mind, the logic of the classic, the theory of the ideal, into a world that received them as contributions rather than as doctrine. The scholarship survived the transition as this article's model predicts, because it had never depended on the requirement that sustained the catechism. It depended on readers, and the readers, at home and abroad, were still there.

### How did the codified doctrine end?

Without refutation and without ceremony. When the apparatus that required it ceased to exist, the courses were renamed and the examinations dropped, and a teaching enterprise of seventy years closed by discontinuation, while the scholarship it had sheltered and suppressed entered the general inheritance.

## The balance sheet

Gather the argument. Soviet Marxism names a doctrine and a scholarship. The doctrine was a codification, fixed between a textbook of 1921 and a canonical chapter of 1938, taught to the largest student body any philosophy has ever had, and sustained, in the last analysis, not by its arguments but by the monopoly that forbade their rivals. Judged as philosophy it is a mixed and mostly minor achievement, clear in exposition, rigid in method, and structurally incapable of examining the society that proclaimed it. Judged as an historical force it is enormous, for it formed the vocabulary in which a third of the world discussed society, and supplied, to its own best students, the terms of its indictment. The scholarship was the work of the doctrine's subjects, produced inside the monopoly and frequently against it, in fields sorted by a gradient of danger that the scholars learned to read like weather. Judged by the single test that finally separates scholarship from its counterfeits, the verdict of disinterested successors, its best work stands: the editions that founded the modern reading of Marx, the psychology of mind's social making, the philosophy of language from the Bakhtin circle, the value-form reading, the morphology of the tale, the legal theory of the commodity form, the mathematics of the plan its authors were forbidden to build. The men and women who made that work paid for it at rates recorded in this article beside their contributions, by design, because the two facts are one fact, and any account that separates them, the honour roll from the execution list, has misunderstood both. The archival paradox stands as the tradition's emblem: the state that shot the founder of the institute lived on the institute's labours, and the school that set itself against the state was built from the institute's books. Two things under one name; the guide's whole labour has been to keep them two, and to judge each by the standard proper to it.

## Bogdanov: the heretic before the doctrine

Behind the controversies of the twenties stood an older figure whose shadow the codification was designed, in part, to exorcise. Alexander Bogdanov, physician, philosopher, economist, and the revolutionary leader who had rivalled Lenin before 1917, had built in exile and revolution a complete alternative Marxism: a philosophy of empiriomonism that recast materialism as the organisation of collective experience, a universal science of organisation, tektology, set out in the years before and during the war, and a cultural programme, the Proletkult, which enrolled hundreds of thousands of workers in studios to build a proletarian culture of their own. Lenin had attacked the philosophy in 1909 in a book-length polemic; the political rivalry was older and deeper than the philosophy. In the Soviet state Bogdanov withdrew from politics to science, founded the institute for blood transfusion in Moscow, and died in 1928 in an experiment on himself, exchanging blood with a young patient in the procedure he was testing. His tektology, untranslated for decades and unmentionable in the codified literature except as error, was recognised in the second half of the century, by the systems theorists and cyberneticians of several countries, as a founding anticipation of their disciplines: the first general theory of organisation, feedback, and regulation, written by a Marxist heretic in the years when the doctrine that condemned him was still fluid. The pattern of this article could ask for no purer instance, and it predates the pattern's machinery: the anticipation suppressed, the author anathematised, the validation delivered by strangers two generations later, in a field, cybernetics, that the doctrine first denounced and then adopted without ever learning whose ground it was adopting.

### What did the science of organisation anticipate?

A general theory of systems before the name existed: the principles by which organisations of every kind, mechanical, biological, and social, maintain, regulate, and transform themselves, set out by a condemned heretic and recognised by the systems sciences two generations after his death.

## The alienation debate: the revival argues with itself

The thaw's most sensitive philosophical argument concerned a word from the manuscripts of 1844. If alienation, the estrangement of human beings from their products, their activity, and one another, was a category of the young Marx that the editions had restored to the tradition, did the phenomenon persist under socialism, in the society that had abolished private property in the means of production? The question was unavoidable once the manuscripts were read, and it was dangerous in proportion to its unavoidability, since an affirmative answer, pressed, described the doctrine's own society in the doctrine's founder's most damning vocabulary. The discussion ran through the journals of the sixties, in Moscow and in conversation with the humanist Marxists of Poland, Yugoslavia, and Czechoslovakia, who pressed the affirmative with fewer inhibitions. The Soviet settlement, reached by the period's mature method of attrition rather than decree, distinguished the phenomenon's essence, abolished with private property, from its survivals, residues of the old order in consciousness and practice, which the building of the new society would progressively remove. The distinction allowed the category to be taught and the conclusion to be controlled. Yet the debate's existence matters more than its verdict. A generation of philosophers had learned to ask, in print, whether the founding category of the humanist Marx applied at home, and the care with which the answer was framed taught every reader the exact location of the fence. The revival's other debates, on the ideal, on contradiction, on the method of the classic, were conducted in the same spirit and under the same condition: real questions, seriously argued, inside a perimeter whose existence no participant mentioned and every participant observed.

## Florensky: the scholar the categories could not hold

One figure of the period escapes the article's classifications, and his escape is itself evidence. Pavel Florensky was a priest and a mathematician, a philosopher of religion and an electrical engineer, who wrote before the revolution a theology of the icon and the pillar of truth, and who chose, after it, to remain and serve the new state's science, working on the electrification programme, on dielectrics and insulating materials, in the laboratories of the industry ministry, while continuing, in the drawer and in lectures remembered by students, the philosophy for which there was no longer a chair. He was arrested in 1933 on a fabricated charge and shot in December 1937, in the same season as Rubin, whose field could hardly have been further from his. His scientific papers were used for decades without his name; his theological and philosophical manuscripts survived in his family's keeping and were published in the thaw and after, revealing to a new generation a mind in which the exact sciences and the philosophy of symbol had been one enterprise. Florensky was not a Marxist, and his presence in this guide requires its reason: he demonstrates that the settlement procedure was never, at bottom, about Marxism. It was about independent authority in any field of thought, and it fell on the theologian-engineer and the value theorist with the same indifference to the content of their work, because the offence it punished was not error but standing: the possession, by a mind, of a ground on which the state had not built.

## Cosmism and the noosphere: the unofficial inheritance

A final current deserves registration, because it flowed beneath the doctrine for the whole period and surfaced, at the end, into the common culture. Russian cosmism, the current associated with the librarian-philosopher Nikolai Fedorov, who taught that humanity's common task was the regulation of nature and the resurrection of the dead, and with Konstantin Tsiolkovsky, the provincial schoolteacher whose mathematics founded astronautics, survived the codification as an unofficial inheritance: unmentionable in the textbooks, present in the culture of the space programme, whose engineers had read their Tsiolkovsky and some of whom knew their Fedorov. Vladimir Vernadsky, the great geochemist, gave the current its scientific form in the concept of the noosphere, the sphere of thought succeeding the biosphere as a geological force, developed in works written partly in emigration and published in his homeland across decades of negotiation with the custodians. Vernadsky survived, laden with honours, by the protection his science's usefulness afforded, the physicists' exception in geological dress. The cosmist inheritance matters to this guide for a diagnostic reason. It shows that the intellectual life of the country was never exhausted by the doctrine and the scholarship this article has surveyed: beneath both ran older Russian currents, religious, cosmic, utopian, which the doctrine could neither absorb nor extinguish and which resurfaced, when the doctrine lapsed, as if from a captivity of seventy years. A map of Soviet thought that shows only the pair this article has surveyed is a map of the name, not of the country, and the guide's distinction should be carried with that caution attached.

## The Academic Affair: the historians' turn

The settlement reached the Academy of Sciences itself at the turn of the thirties, in the case remembered as the Academic Affair. The Academy, the heir of the imperial institution, had retained into the Soviet period a measure of self-government, electing its members and guarding its standards, and its historians included scholars of the old school whose authority rested on the archive rather than on the doctrine. In 1929 the government imposed new elections that seated the party's candidates, and in the following two years the security organs constructed a case against a supposed monarchist organisation of academics. Sergei Platonov, the dean of Russian historians, was arrested in 1930, exiled to Samara, and died there in 1933. Evgeny Tarle, the historian of Napoleon, was exiled to Alma-Ata and then, in the period's most instructive reversal, recalled, restored, and installed as the regime's favoured historian, his gifts placed at the service of the patriotic narrative the leadership now required. Dozens of scholars, archivists, and librarians were arrested in the case's widening circles. The affair's lesson was the habitat lesson of the twenties applied to the republic of learning itself: the Academy's autonomy, the last institutional second door in Russian scholarship, was closed, and the Academy was reorganised as an arm of the state it had advised. Tarle's survival, at the price of service, and Platonov's death, at the price of standing, frame the choices the settlement offered the old scholarship, and the country's historians spent the following decades working in the space between those two examples.

### What did the Academic Affair close?

The last institutional independence in Russian learning. The Academy's self-government was abolished, its historians arrested or conscripted, and the republic of scholarship was reorganised as a department of the state, completing the unified habitat that the settlements had required.

## Philosophy in the war and after: the tightening

The war of 1941 to 1945 suspended the settlement in the name of survival, and the suspension is worth recording because it reveals what the procedure was for. As the state mobilised every resource, the doctrine's custodians relaxed their grip: patriotic history displaced class history in the schools, the church was reconciled, the historians of the old school were honoured, and philosophy, like everything else, was conscripted into the single task. The relaxation ended with the victory. The years from 1946, remembered under the name of the party's ideological secretary as the Zhdanov period, brought the tightening recorded above: the decrees on literature and the journals, the philosophy settlement of 1947, the genetics session of 1948, the campaigns against cosmopolitanism that darkened the period's last years with an ethnic edge the earlier settlements had mostly lacked. The sequence, relaxation under existential threat and tightening in triumph, confirms the analysis this article has maintained throughout. The settlement procedure was not an aberration of the doctrine, a fever of its youth or its senility. It was an instrument of rule, applied and relaxed as rule required, and the scholarship's history is unintelligible unless the instrument is kept in view, in the war years' mercy as in the peace years' rigour.

## Publishing the doctrine: the book as an instrument

Behind the doctrine stood a publishing system, and its economics explain the doctrine's material presence in the world. The state's political publishing house and its counterparts in the republics issued the classics, the textbooks, and the canonical histories in editions planned like steel output, priced for the student and the study circle, and distributed through a network that reached every institute and garrison library in the country. The protocol this article follows forbids the numbers, and the memoirs make them unnecessary: no reader of the period's testimony doubts the saturation. What deserves analysis is the system's double effect on the scholarship. The same apparatus that flooded the country with the catechism also printed, in the same years and from the same warehouses, the scholarly editions, the translations of the world's philosophy, the journals of the revival, and the recovered works of the thaw, because a publishing monopoly does not distinguish between its products as its critics do; it prints what the plan assigns, and the plan, in its bureaucratic wisdom, assigned both the embalming and the antidote. The scholars of the revival understood the arrangement and used it, fighting for their titles' places in the plans of the houses with the same arts they used in the journals. The history of Soviet thought is in part a history of print runs and publishing plans, of paper allocated and withheld, and the student who learns to ask of any Soviet text the material questions, who printed it, in what series, under whose editorship, with what print history, holds a key that opens many of the period's locked doors.

## Questions the article leaves open

A guide that has insisted on the difference between settled and open questions should end its argument by exhibiting its own. Was the codification a betrayal of the tradition or one of its possible outcomes, latent in the founders' own claims to science? The article has presented the evidence for the structural reading and the biographical one, and the question survives both. Could the scholarship of the interstices have been produced without the doctrine that sheltered and maimed it, in a Russia that had taken another road? The counterfactual is unanswerable and irresistible, and the honest guide leaves it on the table. How should the validation test be weighted where the successors who validated a work shared its tradition's premises, as in the value-form recovery, against the cases, the psychology and the morphology, where the validators owed the tradition nothing? The article has treated all validation as one currency; a stricter accounting would grade it, and the grading would change some verdicts at the margin. And the largest question, which the series reserves for its own articles: what does the career of the doctrine imply for the tradition it claimed to complete, whose other heirs, in freedom, produced their own orthodoxies and their own excommunications in miniature? The Soviet case is the tradition's extreme instance, not its only one, and the reader who has followed the argument this far has the instruments to pursue the comparison without a guide's supervision.

## The optimal planning school: economics argues in mathematics

The thaw's economics had a philosophical wing, and its story completes the Kantorovich arc. Around the rehabilitated mathematics of optimal allocation there grew, in the sixties, a school of economists, Vasily Nemchinov its elder, Viktor Novozhilov its theorist of value and cost, Kantorovich its founder in absentia from pure mathematics, who proposed nothing less than the reconstruction of the planned economy on the basis of the optimal plan: prices formed as shadow prices from the plan's own constraints, enterprises judged by their contribution to the optimum, the whole apparatus of the catechism's political economy, its categories of value and price under socialism, rebuilt as a branch of applied mathematics. The school acquired an acronym, SOFE, the system of optimal functioning of the economy, an institute in Moscow, and the enmity of the orthodox political economists, whose discipline it threatened to dissolve. The controversy ran for two decades in the journals and the Academy, conducted in the mature period's manner: no arrests, no resolutions, a war of commissions, appointments, and textbook commissions, in which the orthodox held the teaching and the optimal school held the argument. Its outcome was the period's characteristic stalemate. The mathematics was never refuted and never installed, and the economists of the school grew old in institutes whose recommendations the ministries filed. When the end came, the school's veterans observed that the system had possessed, for thirty years, a worked-out theory of its own rationalisation, produced by its own scholars, and had declined it for the same reason it declined Glushkov's network: a rational plan is a transparent one, and transparency was the one reform the apparatus could not survive. The episode is the strongest evidence for the answer this article gave to the strongest objection. Here was work in the most politically exposed of fields, the theory of the economy itself, which met every test of scholarship its conditions allowed, and whose failure was not intellectual but institutional, a verdict delivered not on its arguments but on its implications.

### What did the optimal planning school propose, and why did it fail?

It proposed to rebuild the planned economy on the mathematics of optimal allocation, with prices formed from the plan's own constraints. It failed institutionally rather than intellectually: rational planning means transparent planning, and the ministries' power rested on the opacity the school proposed to abolish.

## The reprint culture: the thaw recovers its past

The thaw's characteristic literary act was the reprint. Across the sixties the publishing houses, pressed by editors and scholars who had waited decades, issued the recovered works of the suppressed: Vygotsky's psychology, the formalists' essays in new collections, Bakhtin's books, the memoirs and documents of the revolutionary generation in editions whose footnotes sometimes argued, quietly, with their texts. The reprint was a scholarly genre with its own politics. To republish a work was to rehabilitate it without rehabilitating its author, to restore a text while leaving the settlement that destroyed its maker formally intact, and the negotiations over each title, its introduction, its apparatus, the names its editor might mention, were conducted with the seriousness of diplomacy. The young read the reprints as revelations, which they were: a student of 1965 could hold, in a single season, the psychology of 1934, the morphology of 1928 in its new fame, and the Dostoevsky book of 1929, and could hardly avoid the conclusion that the country's intellectual golden age had occurred before its doctrine hardened, a conclusion the reprints' editors intended and their supervisors, in the main, did not examine. The reprint culture also built the bridge by which the suppressed tradition reached the West in those years, since the translations followed the Moscow editions, and the Western discovery of Bakhtin and Vygotsky was mediated by the thaw's choices of what to recover. Every canon is an artefact of such choices, and the Soviet canon of the recovered, which this article has largely followed, was chosen by the editors of the sixties as surely as the catechism was fixed by the editors of the thirties.

## The journals as territory: Novy Mir and the thaw's public sphere

The thaw acquired a public sphere, of a kind, in its thick journals, and the most famous of them shows the period's possibilities and its perimeter in a single editorial history. Novy Mir, under its editor Alexander Tvardovsky, a poet of peasant origin and unimpeachable war record, became in the fifties and sixties the journal in which the country discussed itself: its village prose, its war prose, its cautious economics, and, in November 1962, with the personal approval of the party leader, a short novel by an unknown former prisoner, Alexander Solzhenitsyn, describing one day in a labour camp. The publication was a settlement in reverse, the apparatus using its own procedure, a decision at the top, to license what the apparatus had forbidden, and its effect on readers was the effect of a dam's first crack. The sequel restored the pattern. The approval was withdrawn with the leader who gave it; the journal was besieged by the custodians; Tvardovsky was removed in 1970 and died the following year; the novelist was expelled from the writers' union, awarded the world's prizes, and in 1974 arrested and deported. The episode belongs to literary history, and its presence in this guide is justified by its structure, which is the structure of every episode the guide has recorded: a question opened by authority, argued in the open while the licence lasted, and closed by authority when the licence lapsed. The scholars of the institutes watched the journal's fate as a barometer of their own weather, and drew the conclusion the barometer indicated, that the thaw was a policy and not a settlement, revocable at the pleasure of its makers.

## Sources and their standing

The reader who wishes to go behind this guide should know the character of its evidence. The doctrinal texts are public and stable: the canonical chapter, the textbooks, the resolutions, printed in editions whose texts do not vary. The record of the settlements rests on the published protocols, the press campaigns, and, since the archives opened, on the files of the apparatus itself, whose completeness in matters of repression is a grim scholarly resource. The fates of individuals rest on the rehabilitation records and the reference works compiled from them, which this article has followed for every scholar it names, and on the memoir literature, used with the caution memoirs require, since the survivors wrote with scores to settle and losses to justify. The scholarship's quality rests on the test this article has applied throughout, the later career of the work among readers who owed it nothing, which is a matter of public bibliographical record. Where the evidence is contested, the contested figures above all, the article has declined to print numbers it could not warrant and has said why. A guide that recommends a discipline to its readers is obliged to practise it, and the discipline, in this subject, is the whole of the law: state the comparison, name the source, separate the text from the institution, and let no verdict travel further than its evidence.

## The settlers: a professional type

Every system produces the human type its procedures require, and the settlement procedure produced the philosopher-administrator, whose career deserves a paragraph of anatomy. Mark Mitin, who had led the attack on Deborin's school in 1930 as a young militant of philosophy, became the type's exemplar: editor, academician, director, deputy, a man whose philosophical production consisted largely of the period's required judgements, delivered in the period's required venues, and whose authority rested on his reliability in delivering them. Around such figures the discipline's official life organised itself for two generations. Their textbooks defined the teachable, their editorials defined the permissible, their presence on commissions defined careers. It would be an error, and this article's method forbids it, to treat such men as mere villains of the story. They were, several of them, able organisers who kept institutes funded and journals printed through decades in which both might have lapsed, and the revival's scholars worked in buildings the settlers maintained. But the type's existence is the settlement's deepest institutional legacy, deeper than any resolution: a discipline in which the road to eminence ran through the administration of other men's conclusions will reproduce administrators, and the reproduction continued, by the ordinary logic of appointments, long after the terror that had created the type had been disowned. When the doctrine lapsed, the type survived it, retitling its expertise, which is why the intellectual history of the successor institutions, a subject beyond this guide's perimeter, cannot be written without the Soviet chapters.

## The doctrine abroad among the faithful: the Western parties

The codified doctrine's career in the Western communist parties completes its international history, and complicates it in a familiar direction. The parties of France and Italy, mass organisations with their own intellectuals, received the catechism as the doctrine of the movement's centre, taught it in their party schools, and reproduced its formulations in their journals, while their best philosophers quietly built other Marxisms in its shadow. In Paris, the generation of Louis Althusser constructed, inside the party itself, a reading of Marx whose theoretical anti-humanism was aimed at the humanist fashion and whose every premise contradicted the catechism's: against the doctrine's seamless system, a Marx of ruptures and problems; against its reflection theory, a theory of knowledge as production; against its five formations recited as laws, a history of conjunctures and overdeterminations. The party tolerated its heretic as the Soviet institutes had tolerated theirs, at the price of his confinement to the seminar room, and the parallel is not accidental: a mass party, like a state, discovers that its intellectuals are most useful when their conclusions are inaudible. In Italy, the party of Gramsci's inheritance maintained a relative pluralism in which the doctrine coexisted with the prison notebooks' very different Marxism, and the coexistence produced the West's most intellectually serious communist culture. The general law the cases suggest is the gradient once more, translated into organisational terms: the doctrine's grip on a party's thought varied inversely with that party's need to persuade people free to leave. Where the party governed, the catechism reigned; where it competed, the catechism became one voice among others, and usually not the interesting one.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research frontier on this topic is archival and bibliographical. The settlement files, the discussion protocols, the rehabilitation records, and the publishing plans together permit a history of Soviet thought written from the procedure outward, and much of it remains undone, above all for the provincial institutes and the republics' academies, where the gradient's local variations are unmapped. The untranslated mass described in this article is the second frontier: the journals of the twenties, the revival's full literature, and the drawers' typescripts await the editions and translations by which alone the English sample will cease to distort the whole. Journalists covering the afterlife of the doctrine should attend to the editions rather than the anniversaries: the critical edition's progress, volume by volume, is the tradition's real continuing story, and the archive's doubleness, keeper and killer in a single building, is the frame that keeps the story honest. For the manuscripts' editorial history in depth, the series guide linked in this article's institute section is the starting point, and the comparison with the Western traditions should be stated, in every piece, with its conditions attached.

## The sixties generation: a self-portrait

The revival had a generation, and the generation, in its memoirs, has left a self-portrait that historians now use as a source and a symptom. They called themselves the children of the twentieth congress, the men and women who were students when the secret speech was read to closed meetings in 1956 and who drew from it a lifelong conclusion: that the truth about their country could be known, and that knowing it was their profession's first duty. They staffed the institutes of the revival, edited its journals, wrote its books, and conducted its seminars, and they understood themselves, with justice, as the recovery's agents. The self-portrait requires the historian's discount in one respect. The generation's memoirs, written after the end, cast its members as dissenters in waiting, and the record shows a more interesting truth: that they were, most of them, believers in the tradition they served, reformers of the doctrine rather than its enemies, whose heresies were committed in the founder's name and whose disappointment, when it came, was the disappointment of men and women who had expected the system to become what its texts promised. That expectation, naive as it may look in retrospect, was the revival's engine, and its extinction in the stagnation years, as the generation aged into the administration it had hoped to reform, is the late period's saddest plot. The scholarship survived them, as it had survived their predecessors, by passing into other hands and other countries, indifferent to the hopes of its makers.

## Three documents: the procedure in its own words

This article's argument can be closed by returning to the documents, three of which exhibit the procedure entire. The first is the resolution of January 1931 on the Banner, a text of a few hundred words in the party's central organ, which converted a philosophical school into a political deviation by the application of a label, and which no participant in the dispute had been invited to answer. Read it for its form: it does not argue, it finds; it does not persuade, it instructs the editors. The second is the report of the agricultural academy's session of August 1948, printed with the announcement that its text had been approved by the Central Committee, in which the country's geneticists learned that their science had been abolished by acclamation. Read it for its theatre: the speeches of recantation, the ovations recorded in the transcript, the unanimity performed for the record. The third is the letter that accompanied the replacement pages for the encyclopedia, instructing subscribers in the mechanics of their own forgetting, the cutting and the pasting by which a minister became a strait. Read it for its tone: courteous, procedural, addressed to citizens as to clerks of their own libraries. Three documents, three registers, one procedure. Against them set a fourth document, of a different kind: the title page of the 1932 volume of the manuscripts, with its editor's name, the name of a man seven years from his execution, printed in the year of his disgrace's beginning, on the book that would found a tradition. The archive and the apparatus issued from the same state in the same decade. The reader who holds all four documents has the subject, and has it in the only form in which it finally exists, as paper.

## The craft of the edition: inside the institute

The institute's achievement can be made concrete by following a single manuscript through its rooms. A notebook of Marx, held in Amsterdam or Berlin, would be photographed entire, the photographs bound and shelved in Moscow beside the institute's own holdings. A collator would establish the text page by page, distinguishing the author's hand from copyists, dating the entries by ink, paper, and the internal evidence of addresses and events, recording every deletion and interlineation in an apparatus that let a reader in another century reconstruct the writing's growth. Translators and commentators worked to a rule the founder enforced personally: the edition was to serve readers of every persuasion, its notes confined to fact, its introductions restrained, so that a hostile scholar in Frankfurt and a loyal one in Moscow would cite the same page with the same confidence. That rule, more than any single discovery, is the institute's legacy, because it made the edition a common good of the whole tradition rather than a weapon of its Soviet branch. The scholars who kept the rule were not naive about their employers. They understood that an edition built to be used by enemies could not be withdrawn by friends, that accuracy was the one form of property the apparatus could not confiscate without destroying the asset it prized. In the founder's rooms the craft and the politics were a single calculation, and the calculation proved correct for a hundred years: regimes fell, the sponsoring states vanished, the edition continued, because no successor, of any colour, could afford to redo work done that well or to be seen destroying it. When the international foundation took up the enterprise, it took up the founder's rule with the founder's archive, and the scholars who staff the edition now, in Berlin and Amsterdam and Moscow's reading rooms, work to standards set by a man whose name their predecessors were forbidden, for decades, to honour.

## The mind and the brain: the argument that would not settle

Among the doctrine's philosophical problems one had a life of its own, because it touched the catechism's foundations and could be argued, within limits, as a genuine puzzle. If consciousness is the reflection of matter in the brain, what exactly is the relation between the thought and the tissue, and can the thought be identified with the physiological process without remainder? The textbooks answered with the reflection formula and moved on. The specialists worried at the question for decades, in the philosophy of psychology and in the disputes that followed the Pavlov session of 1950, which had attempted to conscript the study of higher nervous activity into the doctrine's service. Philosophers of the revival returned to the problem with better tools: if the ideal is a form of activity rather than a secretion of the organ, as Ilyenkov's school argued, then the mind's location is a badly posed question, and the badly posed question had consumed a literature. The debate's interest for this guide is its demonstration that even a catechism contains live wires. Generations of students met the psychophysical problem first in its textbook form, recognised the formula's inadequacy by the light of their own reading, and were led by that recognition into the real philosophy being written in the institutes. The doctrine's custodians understood the danger in principle and could do nothing about it in practice, because a philosophy taught to everyone will be thought about by someone, and thought, once started on a real problem, does not reliably stop at the fence.

## The anecdote: the period's folk philosophy

No account of the doctrine's life among its subjects is complete without its unofficial commentary, the political anecdote, the joke form in which the population conducted its own philosophy of the system. The anecdote was the period's most efficient genre: unprintable, unattributable, and immortal, it carried analyses of the doctrine's claims in a form the settlement could not reach. The jokes about the examinations, about the peasant asking whether communism is a science and being told that if it were, it would have been tested on dogs, about the lecturer proving the radiant future while the audience asked about the sausage, performed the function the journals could not: they registered, in public and in common, the distance between the taught world and the lived one. Scholars of the period's folklore have collected the cycles and studied them as documents, and their verdict belongs in this guide. The anecdote was not dissent in any organised sense, and it coexisted with belief, with loyalty, even with office. It was the sound of a population thinking in the only register left entirely its own, and its existence qualifies every claim made about the doctrine's reception, whether the claim is that the population believed, or that it never did. It did something more complicated, and it joked about the complication, which is how a reader knows the thinking was real.

## The examination hall: how the doctrine examined

The doctrine's deepest impression on its subjects was made not by its books but by its examinations, and the examination ritual deserves description because it explains the fluency the population acquired. The candidate drew a ticket, a printed card bearing two or three questions and sometimes a quotation for commentary, withdrew to a desk to prepare notes for a set interval, and then defended the answers orally before a commission. The questions were drawn from the approved programme, and their phrasing telegraphed the expected shape of the reply: a question on the features of the dialectical method wanted the features, in order, with the canonical illustrations; a question on a formation wanted its productive forces, its relations, its superstructure, and its historical fate. Originality was not penalised in principle. It was simply unrewarded, and in the supervised subjects it could be dangerous, since a novel answer to a settled question invited the commission to wonder where the novelty came from. The rational candidate therefore learned the period's central intellectual skill, which was reproduction with conviction, the delivery of the authorised answer in the tone of a person who had reached it independently. Memoirs of the period return to the examination hall with a consistency that suggests it was the era's true classroom: the ablest students describe the private game of saying the required thing while thinking the permitted second thing, and the less able describe the simpler relief of discovering that the required thing, once memorised, asked nothing further of them. A system gets the intellectuals its examinations select for, and the Soviet examinations selected, across two generations, for memory, composure, and the management of the gap between the spoken and the thought. The revival's scholars were men and women who had passed those examinations at the highest level and then spent their careers narrowing the gap in print, a few pages at a time, which is one reason their prose so often reads as an answer delivered to an invisible commission that might, at any moment, ask a dangerous follow-up.

The state examination in philosophy, taken at graduation, was the ritual's summit, and its folklore is extensive: the ticket with the question the candidate had prayed against, the professor known for asking what the textbook left out, the commission member asleep through the features and awake for the formations. Beneath the folklore lay a serious function the system's defenders could cite without irony. The examinations guaranteed a floor. Every graduate of every institute in the country, from the capital's faculties to the provincial pedagogical colleges, had at least handled the same conceptual apparatus, could deploy the same vocabulary of formations and contradictions, and had read, or been examined on, the same short list of classic texts. No other educational system of the century achieved so uniform a philosophical literacy, and the uniformity, whatever its price in freedom, is a fact the historian must enter before entering the price. The argument of this article has been that the price was the procedure by which the uniformity was maintained, and that the scholarship's distinction was to have been produced by people who paid it and thought anyway.

## The obituary page: how the discipline buried its own

A discipline reveals itself in its obituaries, and the Soviet philosophical obituary was a genre with rules as strict as the dissertation's. Printed in the journals over the signatures of colleagues and institutes, it recorded the deceased's posts, works, and services in a prose from which whole categories of fact were absent by convention: no arrest could be mentioned where one had occurred, no disgrace acknowledged, no gap in a bibliography explained, so that the reader learned to reconstruct a life from the silences, the decade in which the works stopped, the institute that changed without comment, the honours that arrived posthumously and without apology. The rehabilitation obituary, a subgenre of the thaw, restored a name to print in the form of a commemoration that could not state what the name was being restored from. Against this official genre the period set its private counterpart, the memorial evening, the apartment gathering at which the suppressed were named plainly and their works passed from hand to hand, and the samizdat necrology, in which the community kept its own register of the dead. The two registers, the journal's and the kitchen's, together constitute the discipline's memory of itself, and the historian who reads only the first will conclude that Soviet philosophy had no martyrs, while the historian who reads only the second will conclude it had nothing else. The truth, as this article has maintained from its first page in every field it surveyed, is that it had both, in the same institutions and frequently in the same careers, and that the obituary page, read with the kitchen's register open beside it, is the shortest introduction to the whole subject that exists.

## The science city: an enclave by design

The geography of the gradient acquired, in the thaw, a deliberate monument. In 1957 the state founded outside Novosibirsk a science city, Akademgorodok, a campus of institutes set in Siberian forest, intended to concentrate talent far from the capital's ministries and to purchase, with autonomy and good housing, the loyalty of the scientists the economy and the arsenal required. The purchase worked in the unintended direction as well. Distance from Moscow lengthened the leash: the science city's seminars argued with a freedom the capital's institutes envied, its economists built the school of mathematical economics described in these pages, its sociologists conducted the surveys that mapped the society's real structure, and its club life, its poets and its discussion circles, made it a byword for the thaw's possibilities. The Novosibirsk report of 1983, the doctrine's vocabulary turned against its custodians, was a product of the enclave's sociology institute, and its authors understood their setting precisely: far enough from the centre to write the truth in a closed document, close enough to the centre for the document to matter. The enclave's history after the thaw repeated the national pattern in miniature, its autonomy trimmed by degrees, its boldest figures disciplined or drawn to Moscow, its institutes settling into the mature system's routines. But its founding fact remains the period's most eloquent admission. The state itself, at the height of its confidence, concluded that first-rate thought required a protected space, built one at the edge of the taiga, and then spent thirty years discovering that the protection, once granted, could not be confined to the thoughts it had ordered.

## The lecture circuit: the doctrine carried by voice

Beneath the universities stood the lecture circuit, the largest teaching apparatus of all, and the least studied. The society founded in 1947 for the dissemination of political and scientific knowledge maintained a corps of lecturers, staff and volunteer, who carried the authorised word to factories, farms, barracks, and housing blocks in talks whose titles were approved and whose attendance was organised by the institutions visited. At its height the circuit delivered the doctrine, and a great deal of genuine popular science beside it, to audiences who would never open a textbook, and its lecturers were a recognised profession with their own training, their own journals of method, and their own folklore of difficult questions from the floor. The question from the floor was the circuit's glory and its hazard. A worker asking why the shops were empty if the formation was advancing, a soldier asking whether the Americans had features of the dialectic too, could not be answered from the approved text alone, and the lecturers who survived the circuit developed the arts of the safe improvisation, the answer that satisfied the hall, the organiser, and the lecturer's conscience in three different senses. Historians of the doctrine who confine themselves to its printed texts miss this oral empire entirely, and with it the mechanism by which the catechism became a vernacular: not read, but heard, in the voice of a neighbour with a briefcase, in a clubroom smelling of wet coats, while the snow came down outside on a country that was being told, in the same words everywhere, what the world was and why it could not be otherwise. That the words were doubted changed nothing about their reach, and their reach, sustained across two generations, is the background against which the scholarship's quieter survival must be measured: the doctrine had the loudspeakers, and the scholarship had the libraries, and in the end the libraries outlasted the loudspeakers.

## The Short Course as narrative: the history inside the doctrine

The canonical text of 1938 was a philosophy chapter inside a history, and the history deserves the same scrutiny as the philosophy, because for its tens of millions of readers the narrative was the doctrine's proof. The Short Course told the party's story as a sequence of struggles against deviations, each chapter closing with the defeat of an error and the vindication of the correct line, until the line and the leader became indistinguishable and the party's past arranged itself as the biography of its own correctness. Figures who had made the revolution vanished from its pages or survived as villains; episodes that the participants remembered otherwise were retold in the authorised version; and the retelling acquired, through the study circles and the examinations, the status of the past itself for readers who had no other. The narrative's method is worth a student's attention because it is the settlement procedure applied to memory: a dispute about the past, the dispute among the revolution's makers about what they had done and why, closed by an authoritative text whose conclusions no archive was permitted to contradict. The historians who later reconstructed the actual record, from the memoirs the narrative had tried to bury and the files it had tried to seal, worked against the Short Course page by page, and their footnotes, which cite it in order to correct it, are the measure of its dominion: for half a century, even its refutation had to be written in its margins. When the narrative was finally withdrawn from the schools in the thaw's first years, replaced by a soberer party history that restored some names and softened some verdicts, the withdrawal was itself conducted by the old procedure, a new authorised text succeeding the old without any public reckoning with the method that had produced both. The philosophy chapter outlived the narrative that carried it, passing into the textbooks in its own right, which is why this article has treated the chapter as the codification and the book as its vehicle. But the vehicle shaped the reception: a philosophy learned as the moral of a story about traitors and victories was learned as a loyalty before it was learned as an argument, and the generations examined on it carried that order of learning into everything they later thought.

The narrative also had its foreign career, and it closes the doctrine's international history with a symmetry the guide should not omit. Translated in the movement's publishing houses on every continent, the Short Course became the history of the revolution for communists who had no other source, and its version of the twenties, the deviations defeated, the line vindicated, structured the self-understanding of parties that had never seen Moscow. When the secret speech of 1956 disowned the book's hero, the parties abroad discovered that their history had been a translation of a settlement, and the discovery, repeated in capital after capital, began the long unravelling of the doctrine's world authority a generation before the state itself expired. The book that had unified a world movement's memory thus became the instrument of its fragmentation, as each party rewrote the past the centre had written for it. No other text of the doctrine had so long a reach or so complete a reversal, and the historian of Soviet Marxism who reads only its philosophy will miss the form in which it conquered: not the chapter of features, but the story of a party that had always been right.

## The restricted collection: the library's inner room

Every great Soviet library kept an inner room, and the institution of the restricted collection, the spetskhran, is the doctrine's material form in the republic of books. Into it went the works the open shelves could not carry: the writings of the defeated and the executed, the émigré press, the foreign scholarship, the early editions whose editors had become enemies, the very manuscripts' first printings in some cases, preserved and forbidden in the same act. Access required a permission, graded by the reader's standing and the stated purpose of the research, and the librarians who administered the room became, by the nature of their office, the custodians of the country's forbidden memory. The institution's doubleness completes the pattern this article has traced in the archive and the encyclopedia. The state did not, in the main, burn its heretics' books. It shelved them under lock, catalogued them with professional care, and admitted to them, on application, the scholars whose work required the poison, trusting the permission system to keep the antidote and the disease in the same hands. Generations of researchers learned their subjects in that room, reading the condemned with a pencil, under rules that forbade the removal of notes beyond a certain frankness, and emerged to write the permitted works in which the forbidden reading shows, to those who know, like a watermark. When the locks came off at the end, the librarians of the restricted collections performed the transition's most symbolic labour, wheeling the carts of the inner room out to the open shelves, where the executed and the exiled took their places in the alphabet at last, between the same neighbours from whom the settlement had separated them for fifty years.

## The counter-archive: the papers that left

Against the Moscow archive there grew, across the century, a counter-archive in the West, and the two collections together hold the tradition's papers as neither could alone. The exiles of 1922 and the emigrations that followed carried out letters, drafts, and whole fonds: the papers of the Menshevik leadership, assembled in Paris and New York by the historian Boris Nicolaevsky, himself a collector of Ryazanov's stamp and temperament, who understood that the revolution's losers would need an archive if their version were ever to be checked; the papers of Trotsky, sold by his widow to Harvard and opened to scholars by stages; the collections of the Hoover Institution, which gathered the records of the revolution's enemies and victims with the same professional neutrality the Moscow institute had preached. The counter-archive's existence disciplines any simple story about custody. The tradition's memory was kept, across the century's division, by two archival civilisations that despised each other and shared a method, and the modern scholarship of the period, this article's included, is written from both, citing Moscow for the manuscripts and New York for the men who were shot for editing them. Ryazanov and Nicolaevsky, the custodian the state executed and the custodian the state exiled, never met in their archival maturity. Their collections meet on every page of the serious literature, which is the closest thing to justice the archive affords.

## The edition in the digital age: the founder's revenge

The story this article has told ends, fittingly, in a reading room that is no longer a room. The critical edition whose first volumes Ryazanov launched has continued under its international stewardship into the age of the digital facsimile, and the manuscripts his agents photographed on glass plates and film are consulted now as images on screens, by readers in countries the institute's couriers never reached, without permission, without a ticket, without a supervisor reading over the shoulder. The founder's rule, that the edition serve every persuasion and hide nothing, has been extended by the technology into a universality he could not have imagined and would have recognised as his intention. Against this survival set the doctrine's digital fate: the catechism's texts are scanned too, in the great digitisation projects, and are read by historians exactly as the encyclopedia's replaced pages are read, as evidence. The two things under the one name have thus arrived at their final separation. One of them is a literature, consulted daily by people who need it. The other is a record, consulted occasionally by people who study it. The state that produced both, and that shot the maker of the first while compelling the second, has itself become a subject for the reading rooms, where its files and its manuscripts now sit in the same catalogues, under the same rules of access, judged by the same readers. The archive, in the end, was the stronger institution, because it had promised its users nothing but the truth of its texts, and kept the promise.

## Coda: the two shelves

A library that holds this subject holds it on two shelves, and the shelves face each other. On the first stand the doctrine's monuments, the Short Course in its dozens of languages, the textbooks in their successive editions, the resolutions and the encyclopedias with their replaced pages, a literature whose print history no figure in this article has been permitted to total and whose collective weight, in every sense, exceeds the second shelf many times over. On the second stand the scholarship's survivors, a few dozen volumes at most in any one library: the psychology, the morphology, the value essays, the philosophy of language, the editions of the manuscripts in their lengthening row. The first shelf is consulted by historians of power. The second is consulted by everyone, including, on the evidence of their footnotes, the historians of power. A reader who has followed this article from its opening distinction to this closing image has the whole argument in the form of furniture, and needs only one further instruction, which the subject itself supplies: when a tradition is summarised for you, ask which shelf the summary came from, and whether the summariser has visited the other.

## How this guide should be used

Read the article whole once, for the argument, and thereafter use it as a map. The codification sections supply the doctrine's texts and dates; the settlement sections supply the procedure and its cases; the field sections supply the scholarship, each with its fates attached; the objection section supplies the adversary at full strength and the answer with its concessions; the closing sections supply the sources, the reading order, and the open questions. A reader preparing for assessment should convert the central table into notes and rehearse the separation it performs until the separation is automatic. A reader preparing to teach should begin from the seminar design in the teachers' layer. A reader preparing research should begin from the untranslated shelf and the counter-archive, where the undone work is. Every reader should keep the article's discipline when leaving it: state the comparison, name the source, weigh the text and the institution separately, and let no verdict travel further than its evidence.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is Soviet Marxism?

The name covers two distinct things that this article separates throughout. The first is the codified doctrine, diamat in its familiar abbreviation, fixed between Bukharin's textbook and the canonical chapter of 1938, and taught as the philosophy of the Soviet state and its bloc for half a century. The second is the body of original scholarship produced in the same country in philosophy, psychology, linguistics, law, and textual editing, much of it by scholars the state persecuted. Judging either by the other produces the standard errors about the subject: the doctrine was an instrument of rule that also carried real ideas to millions, and the scholarship was a genuine intellectual achievement produced under conditions designed to prevent it.

### Q: Who codified the official Soviet doctrine?

The codification was a process with several authors rather than a single act. Bukharin's textbook of 1921 gave historical materialism its first systematic Soviet statement. The philosophical disputes of the twenties were closed by party resolution as the thirties opened, which determined what the doctrine would exclude. The canonical text was the chapter on dialectical and historical materialism in the Short Course party history of 1938, issued under Stalin's supervision and credited to him, which set out the dialectical method in four features and philosophical materialism in three. Thereafter the doctrine was maintained by the textbook writers and philosopher-administrators of the institutes, whose work was administration as much as authorship.

### Q: How were philosophical disputes settled in the Soviet Union?

By administrative decision rather than by argument, in a procedure that became the system's signature. A dispute conducted in journals and institutes would be closed by a party resolution, an editorial removal, a conference whose conclusion was approved in advance, or, in the dictator's own interventions, by an authoritative text in the central press. The mechanist and dialectician controversy was ended by the resolution of 1931; genetics was abolished by the academy session of 1948; the linguistics orthodoxy of Marr was ended by the leader's newspaper articles of 1950. In the later period the procedure softened into attrition: commissions that did not report, defences postponed, journals reorganised. In neither period did the losing side lose because its arguments had been answered.

### Q: What happened to Soviet Marxist scholars?

The range ran from protection to execution, and the distribution followed the gradient of political exposure described in this article. Scholars in the supervised fields, history, political economy, philosophy in its political reaches, jurisprudence, faced denunciation, dismissal, arrest, and in documented cases death: Rubin the value theorist and Pashukanis the jurist were shot in 1937, Ryazanov of the institute in 1938. Others were exiled inside the country, driven from their posts, or silenced into safer topics, and survived to be rehabilitated, some in time to resume work. Those at the technical edge of their fields, editors, mathematicians, psychologists after the thaw, more often kept their lives and their posts, at the price of the adaptations, protective citation, strategic topics, Aesopian phrasing, that the period's scholarship everywhere exhibits.

### Q: Did Soviet scholarship influence Western Marxism?

Decisively, and through identifiable channels. The Moscow institute's editions of 1932, the manuscripts of 1844 and The German Ideology, supplied the texts from which the West's humanist Marx was assembled, from Marcuse's review of the year of publication through Fromm and the postwar schools. Vygotsky's psychology entered Western developmental research a generation after its production and was extended there. Propp's morphology founded a branch of narrative theory after its translation. Rubin's value-form reading and Ilyenkov's dialectics arrived in Western debates as positions to be argued with. Beyond the positive channels, the codified doctrine influenced the West negatively, as the anti-model against which the Western schools defined their own Marxism, so that even its opponents worked in a landscape it had shaped.

### Q: What was the Marx-Engels Institute?

The research institute founded in Moscow in 1921, under David Ryazanov, to collect and publish the complete works of Marx and Engels to scholarly standards. Its agents gathered manuscripts, correspondence, and annotated books from across Europe, by purchase and by photography, until its shelves held a working archive of the founders' papers without rival anywhere. From it Ryazanov launched the critical edition in 1927 and published, in 1932, the early manuscripts and The German Ideology, the publications on which the modern reading of Marx depends. Ryazanov was seized in 1931, exiled, and executed in the terror of 1938; the institute was merged into a larger body under safer direction, and its collections passed in time to the successor edition and its international stewardship.

### Q: What was the Lysenko affair?

The destruction of Soviet genetics by administrative settlement. Trofim Lysenko, an agronomist promising rapid yield improvements through doctrines of inherited acquired characteristics, defeated the geneticists not in scientific debate but through political patronage. At the agricultural academy's session in August 1948, his report, pre-approved by the Central Committee, declared genetics a bourgeois pseudoscience; the discipline was removed from teaching, its laboratories closed or redirected, its practitioners dismissed. Nikolai Vavilov, the country's greatest geneticist, had already died in prison in 1943. The settlement endured until its patron fell, sixteen years on, and the recovery of Soviet biology took a generation. The affair is this article's purest case of a dispute closed by decree, since the claims at issue were checkable and failed every check the fields could apply.

### Q: What survived after Stalin?

More than the obituaries of the doctrine suggest. In scholarship: the cultural historical psychology, republished from 1956 and carried into world psychology; the recovered Bakhtin, restored to print by his young disciples; the editions and the archive, which passed to the successor edition; and a philosophical revival, Ilyenkov above all, that rebuilt the study of the classic's method against the catechism. In the doctrine: everything, unchanged. The textbooks, the features, the formations, and the examinations continued unrevised to the end of the state, coexisting with the revival in the same institutes. The differential survival after the state ended is the final evidence: the catechism disappeared with the machinery that had required it, while the scholarship survived into the universities of the world as a living literature.

### Q: Is any Soviet philosophy worth reading?

Yes, with the field distinctions this article maintains. Ilyenkov's book on the method of Capital is a major reconstruction of its procedure and repays any reader of Marx. Voloshinov's philosophy of language founded a way of studying discourse that the human sciences still use. The historiography of philosophy and the translations maintained standards the West used. The codified textbooks, by contrast, are worth reading as documents, for what the doctrine claimed and how it taught, rather than as philosophy to be argued with. The test applied throughout this guide is the verdict of disinterested successors: the works that free readers found they needed are the works to read, and they are identifiable, several of them available in the English translations listed in the article's note on sources.

### Q: What is the archival paradox?

The fact, named in this article, that the founding texts of the Western humanist Marx reached print through a Moscow institute whose director the state later shot. The Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts and The German Ideology reached the world in the Moscow editions of 1932; the postwar West's humanist Marxism, which understood itself as the doctrine's opposite, therefore rested on Soviet editorial work for its founding texts. The paradox is bibliographical, not rhetorical, and it disciplines every simple cartography of the century's thought: the boundary between doctrine and scholarship runs through Western Marxism itself, and judging the scholarship by transferring the regime's verdict will not work, because the regime's own archive refutes the transfer.

### Q: How does the archival paradox grade the tradition's debt to Soviet scholarship?

It converts the debt from a matter of influence into a matter of foundations. Western humanist Marxism did not merely borrow from Soviet scholarship; it was built on texts that existed for it because a Moscow institute had gathered and issued them, to standards the West had not attempted. The grading follows: the debt is owed to the scholarship, specifically to the editorial labour of Ryazanov's institute, and it is owed in the teeth of the doctrine, whose custodians shot the editor. Any account of the tradition that credits the West with recovering the humanist Marx against Moscow reverses the direction of the debt, and the paradox is the instrument that detects the reversal.

### Q: What should a student separate between a text's survival and the institution that preserved it?

The verdicts. A text is weighed by evidence, by argument, and by the judgement of disinterested successors; an institution is weighed by what it did, to the text's makers, to its rivals, and to the conditions of inquiry itself. The Soviet institute that preserved the manuscripts also served the state that shot its founder, and neither fact cancels the other: the preservation does not launder the institution, and the institution's crimes do not contaminate the text. Students who merge the verdicts fall into one of the two standard errors, dismissing the scholarship because of the regime or honouring the regime because of the scholarship. The separation is this topic's core skill, and it generalises to every tradition whose books were kept by powers that burned their authors.

### Q: Why did the founder of the institute that published the early manuscripts himself perish?

Because he possessed independent standing in a system that was abolishing it. David Ryazanov had built the institute into an authority of its own, defended its independence, quarrelled with the leadership, and spoke of the dictator with a freedom the new order no longer licensed. In the construction of the case against a supposed Menshevik centre in 1931, his eminence and his tongue made him a target; he was arrested, exiled to Saratov, and, in the terror's widening circles, arrested again and shot in January 1938. No charge against him concerned the quality of his editions, which the state continued to use. His fate demonstrates the settlement's true object: not error, which the editions did not contain, but authority that the apparatus had not created and could not command.

### Q: How does the doctrine and the scholarship table order the comparison?

By forcing the two products of the same state onto a single set of questions and letting the answers diverge. The table in this article asks of each how it was produced, how its disputes were settled, what it contributed, and what happened to its authors. The doctrine answers: by codification, by administrative decision, a taught system for a bloc, office and protection for its makers. The scholarship answers: by research, by argument where argument was permitted, contributions the world still uses, arrest and execution for a striking number of its makers. The ordering matters because comparison without stated terms is rhetoric; the table also carries the article's comparison basis, a state-directed institutional system set against the individuals and small groups of the Western universities, over the same decades, under constraints that were real on both sides and lethal on one.

### Q: What separates scholarship produced under constraint from scholarship produced freely?

Not the quality of individual works, which must be judged case by case, but the conditions of correction. Freely produced scholarship can be wrong in public and be corrected in public; its errors are found by rivals with careers to make by finding them. Scholarship under constraint loses that machinery in the exposed fields: conclusions are fixed in advance, rivals are dangerous, and the correction of error waits on the pleasure of the authority that imposed it. The practical test this article applies is therefore external and retrospective: whether disinterested successors, working freely, validated the work against its object. By that test some Soviet scholarship stands with the scholarship of any country, and the rest is evidence about the system that produced it. The separation is a finding, not a courtesy.
