An amendment trail is a record of distance travelled, and few Indian statutes have travelled further from their enacted text than the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 travelled on a single day in September 2005, when the Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act 2005 substituted its central provision. As enacted, the Act performed a deliberate split. It gave Hindu women full ownership of whatever property they held, converting the limited estate of classical law into absolute ownership, and it placed daughters among the first class of heirs to a father's separate property. Yet it preserved, untouched, the institution through which the largest pool of family wealth in much of India was actually held: the Mitakshara coparcenary, a body of male descendants holding ancestral property by birth, into which no daughter was born. That amending Act, Act 39 of 2005, closed that split in the coparcenary itself. It substituted Section 6 of the principal Act, made the daughter of a coparcener a coparcener by birth in her own right in the same manner as a son, deleted the two provisions that had singled out women for disability, and removed the clause that had kept agricultural tenancies outside the Act. What it did not do was finish the argument. Fifteen years of litigation followed over a question the substituted text appeared to answer on its face: whether a daughter whose father had died before the amendment commenced could claim the status the amendment conferred by birth. Two benches of the Supreme Court answered that question in opposite ways, and a third was required to settle it. The trail therefore runs in three stages: a compromise enacted in 1956, a correction enacted in 2005, and a meaning settled only in 2020.

## The Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act 2005 and the Settlement It Disturbed

The principal Act, the Hindu Succession Act, 1956, Act 30 of 1956, came into force on 17 June 1956 as one of the four statutes into which the Hindu Code project was broken after the consolidated Bill failed. Section 14 was its celebrated provision: any property possessed by a female Hindu, whenever acquired, was to be held by her as full owner and not as limited owner, which abolished at a stroke the woman's limited estate that classical law had imposed. For the separate property of a Hindu male dying intestate, Sections 8 to 13 and the Schedule erected a modern scheme under which the widow, the sons, the daughters and the mother took together as Class I heirs in equal shares. On that side of the Act, the daughter of 1956 was already an heir of the first rank, and a daughter inherited her father's self-acquired house or his bank balance on precisely the terms her brother did.

The other side of the Act was Section 6 as originally enacted, and it governed the property that mattered most. In a joint Hindu family governed by the Mitakshara school, which covers most of India outside Bengal and Assam, ancestral property is held by a coparcenary: a narrower body within the joint family consisting, under the classical doctrine the Act preserved, of a male holder and his lineal male descendants within the degrees the doctrine recognised, each acquiring an interest by the fact of birth and each holding that interest undivided, fluctuating with births and deaths in the family, until a partition severed it. Original Section 6 provided that when a male Hindu died holding an interest in such property, his interest devolved by survivorship upon the surviving coparceners, and not under the succession scheme of the Act at all. A daughter, not being a coparcener, acquired no interest by birth, could demand no partition, and watched the ancestral estate pass among her brothers, her father and her nephews by a rule in which she had no part.

The proviso to the original Section 6 softened the rule without disturbing its structure. If the deceased coparcener left surviving him a female relative specified in Class I of the Schedule, or a male relative claiming through such a female, his interest devolved not by survivorship but by testamentary or intestate succession under the Act. To compute that interest, Explanation 1 supplied the device of the notional partition: the deceased's share was deemed to be the share he would have received had a partition taken place immediately before his death. The device gave a daughter a route into a fraction of her father's notional share on his death. It gave her nothing during his lifetime, nothing she could demand, nothing she could alienate, and nothing that made her a member of the body that owned the estate. A son held by birth and took again by succession; a daughter waited for a death and took only from the slice the fiction carved out of it.

Around Section 6 stood the supporting architecture of the compromise. Section 23 deferred a female Class I heir's right to claim partition of a dwelling house wholly occupied by the family until the male heirs chose to divide their shares, granting her residence instead, and granting even residence to a daughter only if she was unmarried, deserted, separated or widowed. Section 24 disqualified three classes of widow, the widow of a predeceased son, the widow of a predeceased son of a predeceased son, and the widow of a brother, from succeeding as widows if they had remarried by the date the succession opened. Section 4, sub-section (2), declared that the Act did not affect laws providing for the prevention of fragmentation of agricultural holdings, the fixation of ceilings, or the devolution of tenancy rights in such holdings, which had the practical effect of leaving the devolution of agricultural land to state tenancy codes, several of which prescribed orders of succession that preferred male lineal descendants. Section 30 permitted a male Hindu to dispose by will of his interest in coparcenary property, a testamentary power expressed only in the masculine. Each of these provisions was known to be a disability when enacted. Each survived 1956 because the codification settlement of that decade, reached after the fate of the consolidated Hindu Code Bill had shown the price of comprehensiveness, treated the male coparcenary as the ground on which the reformers could not yet stand.

### How did the 1956 Act leave daughters outside the coparcenary?

It did so by preserving the classical rule that membership of the coparcenary is acquired by birth and then confining that birthright to male descendants. Section 6 sent a deceased coparcener's interest to the surviving coparceners by survivorship, so a daughter took no interest by birth and could seek no partition in her father's lifetime.

She reached ancestral property only through the proviso, which let her inherit a computed fraction of his notional share when he died leaving a Class I female heir.

The preservation was a choice, not an oversight, and its history is told in the account of [the Hindu Code Bills and the codification struggle](/2016/09/15/hindu-code-bills-history-explained/), where the coparcenary appears as the institution the reformers of the nineteen forties and fifties attempted to abolish outright and then, defeated, chose to preserve. The Hindu Law Committee under B. N. Rau had recommended converting the Mitakshara coparcenary into a scheme nearer to the Dayabhaga pattern, and the draft Hindu Code carried provisions that would have given daughters shares at the source. The enacted Act of 1956 took the succession chapters and left the coparcenary standing, an outcome that legislators of the period defended as the most that could be carried and that critics of the period, including women's organisations that had campaigned for the Code, recorded as the central surrender of the settlement. The consequence was structural. Because ancestral and joint family property in agricultural and trading families alike was held in coparcenary form, the equality the Act conferred on separate property governed the smaller and shrinking portion of family wealth, while the birth based regime it preserved governed the larger portion. A legal historian reading the Act whole sees two statutes stitched together: a succession code of recognisably modern design, and a preserved enclave of the classical law the code had been drafted to replace.

## A Code in Pieces: The Personal Law Settlement the Amendment Inherited

The 1956 Act was never a free standing code. It was one piece of a codification undertaken statute by statute after 1951, alongside the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956 and the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956, and the shape of that piecemeal settlement is set out in [the complete guide to personal laws in India](/2016/09/01/personal-laws-india-complete-guide/), which owns the map of how the codified Hindu law sits among the uncodified and differently codified systems it neighbours. Two features of the settlement matter for the amendment trail. The first is that Parliament legislated for Hindus as a defined statutory class: by Section 2, the Act applies to Hindus by religion, to Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs, and to converts, and it does not apply to Muslims, Christians, Parsis or Jews, nor, by Section 2, sub-section (2), to members of Scheduled Tribes unless the Central Government directs otherwise. Reform of Hindu succession was therefore always reform within one personal law among several, and the equality arguments that carried it were arguments about what Parliament owed the class it had chosen to codify. The second feature is the federal one. Succession sits in the Concurrent List, in Entry 5 of List III of the Seventh Schedule, which covers marriage, divorce, adoption, wills, intestacy and succession, joint family and partition. Parliament and the state legislatures hold the field together, and a state amendment to the central Act, made with the President's assent under Article 254, can prevail within that state over the central text. That constitutional fact, unremarkable when the Act passed, is the reason the next stage of the trail happened where it did: not in Parliament, but in the state legislatures of the south and west, a generation before the Centre moved.

The companion statute of the cluster illustrates what piecemeal codification meant in practice. The Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 had already shown, a year before the Succession Act, that Parliament could recast a sacramental institution into a statutory one with conditions, registration and divorce, and its treatment in [the account of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955](/2016/10/01/hindu-marriage-act-1955-explained/) supplies the contrast a succession reader needs: marriage law was rebuilt as a code of status and exit, while succession law, dealing with property rather than status, was rebuilt only in the half of its field where property was separately held. Money, in the codification decade, was where reform slowed down. The pattern is one the wider series meets repeatedly: the state legislates readily about the ceremonies of family life and hesitates over the assets that underwrite it, and the hesitation of 1956 set the agenda that the state amendments of the nineteen eighties and then the central amendment of 2005 successively took up.

## The Old Section 6 in the Courts: The Notional Partition Jurisprudence

Between 1956 and 2005 the courts built, on the proviso to the old Section 6, a body of doctrine that determined what a daughter or a widow actually received when a coparcener died, and the 2005 amendment cannot be understood without it, because the substituted section inherited the device at its centre, the notional partition, and the litigation of 2015 to 2020 turned on what that device had and had not done. The leading authority is Gurupad Khandappa Magdum v. Hirabai Khandappa Magdum, (1978) 3 SCC 383, where the Supreme Court worked out the computation the proviso required. A widow claimed in her deceased husband's interest. The Court held that the notional partition demanded by Explanation 1 must be carried through honestly: the deceased's interest is the share he would have taken on a partition immediately before his death, and on such a partition the widow herself, under the Mitakshara law the Act presupposes, would have been entitled to a share equal to a son's when a partition takes place between her husband and his sons. She therefore took twice over: her own share on the notional partition, and then a Class I share in the husband's computed interest as it devolved. The judgment is the clearest demonstration of the old law's strange arithmetic, in which a woman's entitlement depended on stacking a fiction upon a succession, and it produced outcomes that were generous to widows in particular families while leaving the structural position, no right by birth, untouched.

The companion doctrine limited the fiction from the other side. In State of Maharashtra v. Narayan Rao Sham Rao Deshmukh, (1985) 2 SCC 321, the Court held that the notional partition of Explanation 1 does not bring about an actual disruption of the joint family: it is a device for quantifying the deceased's interest and nothing more, the family continues joint, and the shares it computes do not sever the coparcenary for other purposes. A quarter century later, that proposition became load bearing in Vineeta Sharma, where the bench reasoned from it that a notional partition on a father's death before 2005 had never actually divided the estate, and so could not be said to have closed the coparcenary against a daughter whose membership the amendment recognised. The line of authority is a reminder that amendment trails are built out of case law as well as statutes: the substituted Section 6, sub-section (3), writes the notional partition into the new devolution rule in terms, and a reader who knows what the device meant in the hands of the courts of 1978 and 1985 can see why the bench of 2020 treated it as a measuring instrument rather than an event.

The old jurisprudence also fixed the position of the daughter's claim during the father's lifetime, which was no claim at all. Because she was not a coparcener, she could not sue for partition; because the proviso operated only on a death, her expectation was a spes successionis in the ancestral estate, an expectancy the law would not protect against alienation by the karta within his powers or against the father's own testamentary disposition of his undivided interest under Section 30. High Courts applied these propositions with consistency across the decades, and the reported cases of the period show daughters litigating at the margins of the scheme: on whether property was coparcenary or separate in the father's hands, on whether a blending of separate property into the joint stock had occurred, on the widow's computation under Gurupad. The scheme's defenders in the courts treated it as a coherent allocation the legislature had chosen; its critics in the literature, among whom Bina Agarwal's A Field of One's Own of 1994 is the most consequential statement, treated the coherence itself as the problem, arguing that a legal order which routed women's access to land through dependence on male kin reproduced in property the subordination it claimed elsewhere to dismantle. Agarwal's argument, addressed to land rights for rural women in South Asia, supplied much of the intellectual case the Law Commission's 174th Report later converted into draft clauses, and the trail from the monograph of 1994 to the statute of 2005 runs through the Commission's report in a straight line.

## The States Move First: Coparcenary Reform Before the Centre

The first breach in the preserved coparcenary was made not by amendment but by abolition. The Kerala Joint Hindu Family System (Abolition) Act, 1975, in force from 1 December 1976, ended the joint family system in Kerala altogether: members of a joint family came to hold their shares as tenants in common, as if a partition had taken place among all of them, and the right to claim property by birth disappeared for sons and daughters alike. Kerala, where the Marumakkathayam system of matrilineal descent had already given the taravad a structure unlike the Mitakshara heartland, chose equality by levelling the institution away. The four states that followed chose the opposite route: they kept the coparcenary and admitted daughters to it.

Andhra Pradesh legislated first among them. The Hindu Succession (Andhra Pradesh Amendment) Act, 1986, brought into force with effect from 5 September 1985, inserted a new Chapter IV-A, Sections 29A, 29B and 29C, into the principal Act as it applied in that state. Section 29A provided that the daughter of a coparcener in a Mitakshara joint family shall become a coparcener by birth in her own right in the same manner as a son, with the same rights and liabilities, a formulation the central amendment of 2005 would later take over almost word for word. Tamil Nadu followed with the Hindu Succession (Tamil Nadu Amendment) Act, 1989, Act 1 of 1990, in force from 25 March 1989, inserting Sections 29A to 29C in a new Chapter II-A to the same effect. Karnataka enacted its amendment in 1990 and brought it into force on 30 July 1994, taking the different drafting route of inserting Sections 6A, 6B and 6C beside the central Section 6. Maharashtra completed the set with its amendment of 1994, in force from 22 June 1994, inserting Sections 29A to 29C in Chapter II-A. Each of these was a state law amending a central Act on a concurrent subject, and each operated within its state because it had received the President's assent, which is what Article 254 requires before a state text can hold a field against a parliamentary one.

The state amendments shared one large limitation, and it defines their place in the trail. Each conferred coparcenary status on the daughter who was unmarried when the state provision commenced, and each withheld it from the daughter who had already married. The reasoning of the period treated marriage as the point at which a daughter passed into her husband's family and was presumed provided for, not least through dowry, and the state draftsmen wrote that social assumption into the operative text by confining the new status to daughters unmarried on the appointed day, together with daughters born afterwards. The consequences were litigated for years: a daughter married a day before commencement in Hyderabad or Madras or Bombay or Bangalore took nothing by birth, while her unmarried sister took a full coparcenary share, and the constitutionality of the classification was attacked on equality grounds in the very states that had enacted it. The state amendments also produced a map problem. Because the applicable law depended on the state in which the family and its property sat, on the date of the daughter's marriage, and on whether a partition had already been effected under the state text, two branches of one family holding land across a state border could find the same woman a coparcener in one state and a stranger to the coparcenary in another. The reform was real, and in the states that enacted it a generation of daughters acquired rights their mothers had lacked. It was also, by design, a reform of the south and west only, leaving the populous Mitakshara heartland of the north and centre under the unamended central Section 6, and leaving everywhere the distinction between the married and the unmarried daughter that the reform's own logic could not defend.

The lesson the state phase taught, and the one the Law Commission later generalised, is about the mechanics of Indian social legislation on concurrent subjects. A state amendment can prove a reform's workability within a contained population: the Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu texts demonstrated for two decades that admitting daughters to the coparcenary produced partition claims, litigation and adjustment, but no collapse of the joint family as an economic unit, and that the sky predicted to fall by opponents of the change stayed up. What a state amendment cannot do is produce uniformity, because its field stops at the state line and because the assent mechanism of Article 254 leaves the central text intact everywhere else. The trail from 1985 to 2005 is therefore a case study in the federation legislating in sequence: experiment in the states, evidence from the experiment, generalisation by the Centre. The series has met the same sequence in reverse in other clusters, where a central reform was left unconformed in state law; here the sequence ran the productive way, and the central amendment, when it came, adopted the state drafting nearly verbatim while discarding the state limitation, which is the strongest evidence that the Centre had read the state record closely.

The politics of the state amendments repay a sentence each, because they explain the drafting the Centre later copied. The Andhra Pradesh amendment was the property measure of N. T. Rama Rao's Telugu Desam government, which had come to office on a programme addressed in visible part to women voters and which treated the daughter's share in ancestral property as the statutory form of a promise the campaign had made in general terms; the Act's commencement was carried back to 5 September 1985, so that the reform reached families as they stood when the promise was made rather than when the drafting was finished. The Tamil Nadu amendment of 1989 belonged to the government of M. Karunanidhi, whose administration presented it within a wider set of measures on women's property and marriage assistance, and the state chose the Chapter II-A drafting form that Maharashtra would take up in 1994. In each case the reform was carried by a regional party government for a state electorate, at a time when the question had no national parliamentary vehicle, and the pattern is the federal one this series records in several clusters: the states nearest the social practice legislated first, and the Centre, when it moved, legislated on evidence the states had generated and in language the states had tested. It is also worth recording what the state phase did to the families it covered before the Centre acted. Partition suits by unmarried daughters in the amending states through the nineteen nineties tested the new texts against the old defences, hostile witnesses to the fact of partition, deeds of relinquishment taken at betrothals, and the contention that the daughter had been given her share in the form of marriage expenditure, and the reported decisions of those years, applying state texts of near identical wording, are the rehearsal for the national litigation that followed 2005, in which the same defences reappear at a higher level of authority.

## The Law Commission, the Bill, and the Passage of 2005

The bridge between the state experiments and the central amendment was the Law Commission of India's 174th Report, submitted in 2000 under the title Property Rights of Women: Proposed Reforms under Hindu Law, during the chairmanship of Justice B. P. Jeevan Reddy. The Commission examined the 1956 Act as a whole and treated the exclusion of daughters from the coparcenary as its principal surviving discrimination. It recommended that the daughter of a coparcener be made a coparcener by birth in the same manner as the son, following the state models but without their confinement to unmarried daughters; that the dwelling house restriction in Section 23 be deleted; that the remarriage disqualification in Section 24 be deleted; and that the agricultural holdings saving in Section 4, sub-section (2), be deleted so that the Act's scheme would run on agricultural land as on other property. The report also addressed the rules governing a Hindu female's own intestate succession, which are taken up below, and recommended their reform as well, a recommendation Parliament has not enacted. The Commission's reasoning was anchored in the constitutional text: it treated the sex based allocation of a birthright as an anomaly within a code that Parliament had enacted under a Constitution promising equality before the law and prohibiting discrimination on grounds of sex, and it pointed to the state amendments as proof that the reform was administrable. A reader tracing the 2005 Act back to its source finds the Commission's draftsmanship underneath it at nearly every joint, which makes the 174th Report the single most consequential document in this trail after the Act itself.

The Commission's report is also worth reading for the road it considered and declined. Abolition of the coparcenary on the Kerala pattern, the deletion of the right by birth for sons as well as its non conferral on daughters, was before it as a coherent alternative: it would have produced equality by levelling down, converted every joint holding into a tenancy in common at a stroke, and ended the divergence between the Mitakshara and Dayabhaga parts of the country in the law of ancestral property. The Commission declined it in favour of equalisation, on reasoning that runs through its report: the joint family remained a living economic institution for a large part of the population the Act governed, the state amendments had shown coparcenary equality to be workable within it, and a reform that took a birthright from sons in order to avoid giving one to daughters would visit the costs of transition on families that had organised themselves around the institution for reasons unconnected with the discrimination. The choice mattered beyond its moment. Equalisation preserved the coparcenary as the vehicle and therefore preserved every doctrine built on it, the karta's powers, survivorship's remnants in the other subsections, the fiscal unit of the undivided family, and with them the entire body of case law through which the amendment would have to be worked out. Abolition would have produced a shorter trail and a quieter one. That Parliament adopted the Commission's choice without serious contest is the reason this article is an amendment trail of fifteen years of litigation rather than an obituary of the joint family, and the choice represents, in legislative form, the preference for repairing an institution over retiring it that runs through the whole of the Hindu code project from Rau's committee onwards.

The Hindu Succession (Amendment) Bill, 2004 was introduced in the Rajya Sabha on 20 December 2004, a date that would acquire operative significance because the enacted text later chose it as the line before which completed transactions were protected. The Bill was examined by the Department Related Parliamentary Standing Committee on Personnel, Public Grievances, Law and Justice in the ordinary course, and it carried the Law Commission's architecture with one design decision of the government's own that proved fateful: the Bill, following the Andhra Pradesh model, retained the Mitakshara coparcenary as an institution and admitted daughters to it, rather than following Kerala into abolition of the joint family system. The Statement of Objects and Reasons recorded the government's stated grounds: the exclusion of daughters from coparcenary ownership was itself the discrimination to be removed, the state amendments had shown the way, and equal rights in ancestral property were presented as a measure of social justice for women rather than as a restructuring of the family. The Rajya Sabha passed the Bill on 16 August 2005 and the Lok Sabha on 29 August 2005. The Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005, Act 39 of 2005, received the assent of the President on 5 September 2005 and came into force on 9 September 2005.

Two features of the passage deserve notice because they structured everything that followed. The first is what the debate did not settle. The Bill's central clause said that the daughter shall by birth become a coparcener, and the question whether that birth based status reached daughters whose fathers had already died, in families where succession to the father's interest had arguably already opened and closed, was not answered by any express transitional provision beyond the saving for earlier dispositions and partitions. Parliament legislated the status and left its temporal reach to be inferred from the word birth, which is why the courts, and not the legislature, wrote the next chapter of the trail. The second feature is the combination of models. The enacted amendment took the Andhra Pradesh route on the coparcenary, admitting daughters to the institution, and the Kerala route on the doctrine of pious obligation, abolishing the liability of descendants for an ancestor's debts as a matter of religious duty for debts contracted after commencement. It was, in the draftsman's sense, a composite: the coparcenary preserved and equalised, its most burdensome incident removed, and the disabilities of Sections 23 and 24 struck out in the same short statute of seven sections. The amendment's brevity is worth registering. Seven sections of an amending Act redrew the property position of half the population governed by the principal Act, and the economy of the drafting, which changed status rather than machinery, is a large part of why its interpretation generated a decade and a half of appellate litigation.

## The Argument Over the Bill: Fragmentation Against Equality

The passage of the 2005 Act was not contested in Parliament on party lines to any disabling degree, but the arguments deployed around it deserve record, because they recur in every later debate on this statute and because the stance of this series requires each side at its strongest. The Bill was piloted by the Union Minister for Law and Justice, H. R. Bhardwaj, whose case for it rested on the constitutional text and the state record: the exclusion of daughters from a birth based institution was discrimination the Constitution's equality clauses could not be reconciled with in a code Parliament itself maintained; four states had operated daughter coparcenary for up to two decades without the disintegration of the joint family that had been predicted; and the reform had been recommended in terms by the Law Commission after study. Women's organisations, prominently including the All India Democratic Women's Association, which had campaigned on the demand through the nineteen nineties, pressed the further point that rights in ancestral property were the material foundation on which other formal rights depended, since a woman with a share she could realise was a woman who could refuse a violent household, fund litigation, or leave. Brinda Karat, who had argued the women's movement's case on the issue in print and in Parliament, treated the amendment as the single most important economic measure for women of its parliamentary period, a characterisation that reflects the movement's own ordering of its demands.

The opposing arguments, made in the debates and in the commentary of the period, were of three kinds. The first was institutional: that admitting daughters, who on marriage join another household, would multiply claims against the joint estate, accelerate the fragmentation of agricultural holdings already subdivided to the edge of viability, and burden the family firm with partition suits from members resident elsewhere. The second was transactional: that titles settled over generations on the faith of the old law, including sales by kartas to outsiders, would be thrown into doubt, an argument the savings drawn at 20 December 2004 were designed to answer and which in the event was deployed less against the Bill than, later, in the retrospectivity litigation. The third was cultural and was rarely stated in Parliament in its blunt form, though it saturated the surrounding discussion: that a daughter provided with dowry at marriage had received her portion in the form the society recognised, and that to give her a coparcenary share in addition was to count the same provision twice. The reformers' answer to the third argument was the empirically grounded one the Law Commission had assembled: dowry is a transfer to the marital family, not an asset the woman controls, and treating it as her portion is the mechanism by which exclusion disguises itself as provision. On the fragmentation argument, the state record was again the answer in evidence: holdings in Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu had continued to be cultivated and partitioned under the same pressures that operate on all Indian land, and no distinctively daughter driven fragmentation had been identified in two decades of the regime. The debate is worth preserving in the trail because the same three arguments, institutional, transactional and cultural, reappeared in the affidavits and submissions of the Prakash litigation ten years later, transposed from reasons against enactment into reasons against retrospective construction, and were there answered by a bench that treated the first as a policy matter concluded by Parliament, the second as met by the savings clause, and the third as the very premise the amendment had rejected.

## What the Amendment Changed, Provision by Provision

Section 2 of the amending Act performed the central operation: it substituted a new Section 6 for the original. The substituted Section 6, sub-section (1), provides that on and from the commencement of the amending Act, in a joint Hindu family governed by the Mitakshara law, the daughter of a coparcener shall by birth become a coparcener in her own right in the same manner as the son; shall have the same rights in the coparcenary property as she would have had if she had been a son; and shall be subject to the same liabilities in respect of that property as a son. Three consequences are written into that single sub-section. Status is conferred by birth, which is the language of the classical coparcenary itself, so the daughter's interest arises on her birth and not on any death, marriage or demand. The rights are measured by a statutory fiction of sonship, which imports into her hands the whole bundle a son held, including the right to demand partition, to alienate her undivided interest within the limits the law sets, and to take by survivorship among coparceners. And the liabilities travel with the rights, so the daughter is answerable for family debts and obligations on the same footing, a point the drafting made deliberately: the amendment conferred membership, not a protected share of benefits with the burdens stripped away. A proviso to sub-section (1) protects the past: nothing in it affects or invalidates any disposition or alienation, including any partition or testamentary disposition of property, which had taken place before 20 December 2004.

Sub-section (2) of the substituted section provides that any property to which a female Hindu becomes entitled under sub-section (1) shall be held by her with the incidents of coparcenary ownership and shall be capable of being disposed of by her by will or other testamentary disposition. Sub-section (3) then rebuilds the devolution rule that the original Section 6 had stated through survivorship. Where a Hindu dies after the commencement of the amending Act, his interest in the property of a joint Hindu family governed by Mitakshara law devolves by testamentary or intestate succession under the Act, and not by survivorship, and the coparcenary property is deemed to have been divided as if a partition had taken place. Within that notional division, the daughter is allotted the same share as a son; the share of a predeceased son or daughter is allotted to their surviving children; and the share of a predeceased child of a predeceased son or daughter is allotted to that child's children, so the stirpital pattern runs through daughters' lines as through sons'. Sub-section (4) abolishes the doctrine of pious obligation for the future: after commencement, no court shall recognise any right to proceed against a son, grandson or great grandson for the recovery of a debt due from his father, grandfather or great grandfather solely on the ground of the pious obligation under Hindu law, with a saving for debts contracted before commencement and for alienations made in satisfaction of them. Sub-section (5) repeats the temporal protection in partition terms: nothing in the section applies to a partition effected before 20 December 2004, and the Explanation defines partition, for this purpose, as a partition made by a duly registered deed of partition or a partition effected by a decree of a court. That Explanation, inserted for certainty, later became the textual ground on which claims founded on unregistered family arrangements and oral partitions were fought.

The remainder of the amending Act is deletion and consequential repair, and the deletions carry as much meaning as the substitution. Section 3 of the amending Act omitted sub-section (2) of Section 4 of the principal Act, the saving that had kept tenancy and agricultural holdings legislation outside the Act, so that the Act's scheme extended to agricultural land in place of the state orders of devolution the saving had preserved, a change whose practical reach is examined below. Section 4 of the amending Act omitted Section 23 of the principal Act, ending the rule that a female Class I heir could not claim partition of the family dwelling house until the male heirs chose to divide, and with it the graded rights of residence that had marked the daughter as a tolerated occupant of her own inheritance. Section 5 omitted Section 24, ending the disqualification of the three classes of remarried widows. Section 6 of the amending Act amended Section 30 of the principal Act by substituting the words disposed of by him or by her for disposed of by him, so that the testamentary power over an undivided coparcenary interest, which the original Act had conferred in terms on a male Hindu, stood conferred on a female Hindu in the same words, matching the power sub-section (2) of the new Section 6 had already given her. Section 7 amended the Schedule, adding to Class I four heirs of the fourth generation claiming through daughters: the son of a predeceased daughter of a predeceased daughter, the daughter of a predeceased daughter of a predeceased daughter, the daughter of a predeceased son of a predeceased daughter, and the daughter of a predeceased daughter of a predeceased son. The Schedule amendment is the quiet completion of the design: once daughters are coparceners and heirs in their own right, their descendants must be placed in the order of succession on the same pattern as sons' descendants, and the 2005 Act did that placing expressly rather than leaving it to construction.

One concept does quiet work throughout the section and should be isolated: coparcenary property itself. The substituted Section 6 operates on the property of the joint family, and the threshold contest in most litigation is not the daughter's status, which the amendment settles, but the character of the asset, which it does not. Property is coparcenary property when it is ancestral in the claimant's hands, inherited from a paternal ancestor within the degrees the doctrine specifies, or when it has been acquired with the aid of joint family funds or thrown by its separate owner into the common stock with the intention of abandoning separate claims, the doctrine of blending that the courts administered under the old law and administer still. Property the father acquired by his own exertion without the aid of the joint estate is his separate property, in which the daughter's route is Class I heirship on his death and not coparcenary during his life, and the evidentiary presumptions the courts apply, that a family possessed of an ancestral nucleus is presumed to have acquired its later assets from that nucleus until the contrary is shown, decide more shares at trial than the text of Section 6 ever has. The amendment's equality is therefore an equality within a defined fund: it equalises the membership of the body that owns the fund, and it leaves the prior questions, what the fund contains and whose earnings enlarged it, to the doctrines the 1956 Act inherited and did not codify.

Read as a whole, the amending Act is a status statute. It created no institution, no authority, no registry and no procedure; it altered who holds by birth, deleted three disabilities, and trusted the ordinary courts and the ordinary law of partition to work the change out. That trust had a price, because the one operative question the text left open, the position of the daughter whose father had died before 9 September 2005, went to the heart of the largest class of potential claims: every family in which a coparcener had died between the enactment of the principal Act and the amendment, which is to say the overwhelming majority of families holding coparcenary property at all. Before turning to the litigation, the practical operation of the amended text is best seen as a decision, and the table below routes the question a claimant actually asks through the variables that decide it.

## Working the Shares: The Arithmetic of the Two Regimes

The difference between the old and new law is easiest to hold in mind as arithmetic. Take a Mitakshara family in which a father dies holding ancestral property, leaving a widow, two sons and a daughter, and no will. Under the law before 2005, the daughter was not a coparcener. Because the deceased left Class I female heirs, the proviso to the old Section 6 diverted his interest from survivorship into succession, and Explanation 1 measured it by a notional partition. On that partition, taken immediately before his death, the coparceners were the father and the two sons, and the father's share was one third. That one third then devolved under Sections 8 to 10 on the Class I heirs, the widow, the two sons and the daughter, each taking one quarter of it, which is one twelfth of the whole estate. The daughter's entire entitlement in the ancestral property was that one twelfth, acquired only because her father had died, while each son held his own third as coparcener and a further twelfth by succession, eleven twelfths of the estate in the family's male line against the daughter's single twelfth counting the widow's shares in both columns. If the same father had died leaving only sons, the proviso would not have engaged at all and the entire estate would have passed by survivorship without the Act's succession scheme touching it.

Under the substituted Section 6 the same family computes differently at every step. The daughter is a coparcener by birth, so the coparcenary at the father's death consists of the father, the two sons and the daughter, and sub-section (3) deems a partition to have taken place for the purpose of devolving the father's interest. The father's interest is one fourth. That fourth devolves by intestate succession on the Class I heirs, the widow, the two sons and the daughter, each taking one quarter of it, a sixteenth of the whole. The daughter therefore holds her own fourth as coparcener, together with a sixteenth by succession, five sixteenths against the one twelfth of the old computation, and each son holds the same five sixteenths as she does, the widow taking a sixteenth by succession together with such share as the notional partition doctrine of Gurupad would assign her in the working out. The equality the amendment enacted is visible in the fractions: son and daughter emerge identical, and the daughter's entitlement no longer waits on the father's death, because her fourth was hers from birth and could have been realised by a partition suit in his lifetime. Vary the facts and the structure of the answer stays constant. If the father leaves a predeceased daughter survived by her children, sub-section (3) allots the share that daughter would have taken to her surviving children within the notional division, so the stirpital pattern follows her line down. If a coparcener dies after 2005 leaving a will, his undivided interest passes under the will by force of Section 30 as amended and sub-section (3)'s direction that devolution is by testamentary or intestate succession, but what passes is only his computed interest, and no will of his can touch the shares his daughters and sons hold in their own right, which is the point at which the birth based scheme most sharply limits the father's disposing power by comparison with a regime of separate property.

A boundary doctrine completes the computation, and it explains why each generation's partition shrinks the coparcenary fund from which the next generation's birthrights are drawn. In Commissioner of Wealth Tax v. Chander Sen, (1986) 3 SCC 567, the Supreme Court held that property which a son inherits from his father under Section 8 of the Act is the son's separate property in his own hands, and does not become joint family property of the son and his own sons, because the father's interest that devolved was itself computed and severed by the succession the Act ordains. The holding, delivered on the pre 2005 text, carried into the amended regime without disturbance: the share a coparcener takes by succession on a father's death under sub-section (3), whether that coparcener is a son or a daughter, is taken as an heir and held separately, while the share held by birth remains coparcenary until severance. A family's estate after a death accordingly sits in two legal forms side by side, the surviving coparcenary continuing in the undivided residue, and a set of computed shares held outright by the heirs who took them, available to their own testamentary disposition and devolving under the heir's own succession. Advisers who miss the distinction misdescribe the title; courts that miss it misdirect the decree, and the daughter's position depends on holding it clearly, since her birth share and her inherited share in the same estate are different kinds of property with different incidents.

The computation also shows what the amendment withheld. The widow in the example is not a coparcener, before 2005 or after it: coparcenary membership runs by birth in the line, and a wife enters the family by marriage. Her protection remains the Class I share on succession, the Gurupad entitlement on a partition between her husband and sons, and the absolute ownership of whatever she holds under Section 14. Critics of the amendment's design, including commentators who supported it, have noted that a scheme of equality by birth necessarily leaves the spouse of a coparcener in a derivative position until a death, and that the daughter in law in the marital home stands further from the birthright than the daughter in the natal one. The amendment answered the question it was asked, the exclusion of daughters, and the example shows both the completeness of that answer and the boundary at which the next set of questions, about wives and widows as such, begins.

## The Incidents of the New Status: Alienation, Management and the Will

Coparcenary membership is a bundle, and the litigation after 2005 has worked through its strands one at a time. The strand of management has been met already in the karta cases. The strand of alienation runs in two directions. A coparcener's undivided interest can be dealt with by the coparcener herself: sub-section (2) of the substituted section confirms her testamentary power over it, and the general law of the Mitakshara schools governs her power to alienate it inter vivos, which the schools have historically stated differently, the Bombay school permitting a coparcener to alienate his undivided interest for value without the consent of the others, the other schools restricting gifts and sales in varying degrees. What the amendment changed is not the content of these rules but their personnel: every rule of alienation that spoke of a coparcener speaks of the daughter too. In the other direction, the karta's power to alienate joint family property for legal necessity or for the benefit of the estate, the classical power by which a manager binds the interests of coparceners who never consented, binds and benefits daughters on the same terms as sons, and a daughter challenging an alienation by the karta stands in the same position as a son challenging it, required to plead and prove the absence of necessity or benefit, entitled to the same accounting. The equalisation of burden here is real and is sometimes missed in celebratory accounts: the daughter of 2005 acquired not only a share but exposure, to family debts contracted within the karta's powers, to the costs of the family's litigation, and to the obligation, written into sub-section (1) itself, of the same liabilities as a son.

The right to demand partition, which the substituted section places in the daughter's hands in terms, carries with it the classical mechanics of severance, and those mechanics are worth a paragraph because they determine what a daughter must actually do. A coparcener severs her status in the joint family by an unequivocal declaration of intention to separate, communicated to the other coparceners; division by metes and bounds follows, by agreement or by suit, but the severance of status dates from the declaration, and with it the conversion of the fluctuating undivided interest into a defined share that can be alienated, settled or devised. Before 2005 that declaration was a male coparcener's instrument. After 2005 it is equally the daughter's, and its strategic significance in practice is considerable: a daughter who does not wish to sue her family for a physical division can nonetheless fix her share by severance, protect it against the karta's alienations beyond his powers, and hold it as her separate property within the meaning of Section 14, while the family continues to occupy and work the estate. Much of the amendment's real operation has taken this quiet form, in family arrangements that record the daughter's separated share without a decree, and the volume of such arrangements, precisely because they conclude without litigation, is the part of the trail no reporter records.

The testamentary strand cut the other way, in the daughter's favour, by shrinking what a father can do. Under a regime of separate property a parent may disinherit a child entirely, and the father's self acquired property under the Act remains his to will away from his daughter as from his son. His undivided coparcenary interest he may also will, under Section 30. But the daughter's own interest, held by birth, is not his to give: it never formed part of his disposable estate. The practical consequence, frequently decisive in family arrangements, is that a will which purports to settle the whole ancestral estate on the sons alone is effective only as to the testator's computed share, and the daughter's claim proceeds against the remainder regardless of the document's terms. Families that understood this early adjusted their arrangements to it, by partition, by settlement with the daughter's participation, or by gifts of separate property in acknowledged substitution; families that did not produced a recognisable class of litigation in which an elaborate testamentary scheme fails at its foundation because it disposed of property the testator did not own. The point generalises into the trail's working moral: a status conferred by birth is the hardest kind of right to draft around, and the conveyancers of India absorbed that fact about Section 6 faster than the appellate courts did.

## The Fiscal Shadow: The Hindu Undivided Family as a Tax Unit

One register in which the amendment's consequences arrived immediately, and without litigation, is taxation, because the joint family of the succession law is also the Hindu undivided family of the Income Tax Act, 1961, which Section 2(31) of that Act counts as a person for assessment. A coparcenary enlarged by the admission of daughters is a different taxable constituency from the one the revenue had administered: the karta who files the family's return acts for a body whose membership changed in 2005, the daughter who is a member of the family of her birth remains a member notwithstanding her marriage, with the result, unfamiliar before 2005, that a married woman is a member of the Hindu undivided family of her birth and, by marriage, of the family into which she has married, holding her coparcenary rights in the first and her rights as a member's wife in the second. Partition of the family, total or partial, carries the succession event into the assessment through Section 171 of the Income Tax Act, which governs the assessment of a family after partition and which, since the amendment of that section in 1978, refuses recognition to partial partitions for tax purposes while continuing to recognise a total partition recorded by the assessing officer's finding. A daughter's partition suit is therefore also a fiscal event for every member of the family, dividing not only the estate but the unit of assessment, and the advice given in such suits since 2005 has had to run the succession computation and the tax computation together. The fiscal shadow matters to the trail for a structural reason: it is the channel through which the amendment reached families that never litigated. A karta revising the family's assessment position, a bank taking a mandate from a reconstituted family, a purchaser's counsel searching title against a family that included daughters the old record omitted, each met the substituted Section 6 in the ordinary course of business, and the amendment's social penetration owes as much to these administrative encounters as to the celebrated litigation that fixed its meaning.

## Fifteen Years of Litigation: Prakash, Danamma and Vineeta Sharma

The question the courts had to answer was compressed into one phrase of the substituted section: by birth. The daughter becomes a coparcener by birth. If the status is in fact conferred by birth, it is difficult to see how it can depend on the father's survival to a date in 2005, since a son's coparcenary status never depended on his father surviving to any date at all. If, on the other hand, the amendment is a statute altering vested rights of succession, the ordinary presumption against retrospectivity presses the other way: on a coparcener's death before 2005, his interest had already devolved, by survivorship or under the proviso to the old Section 6, and a daughter claiming in 2006 would be asking the court to unsettle a devolution completed years earlier. High Courts divided on the point in the years after commencement, and the suits in which it mattered were not marginal: they were the ordinary partition suits of propertied families, in which the presence or absence of daughters in the array of sharers changed every fraction in the decree.

The High Court phase of the divergence deserves its own record, because the Supreme Court's conflict of 2015 to 2018 was inherited from it. Several High Courts, reading the substituted section in the first years after commencement, gave the daughter's status its natural temporal reach. The Karnataka High Court's decision in Pushpalatha N. V. v. Padma, delivered in March 2010, is the fullest of them: the court treated the amendment as a piece of social legislation to be construed in favour of the class it benefited, held that the daughter's right by birth operated on the antecedent fact of birth, and subjected its own conclusion to one condition of its devising, that the daughter should have been born after the principal Act commenced in 1956. Other High Courts required, in varying formulations, that the father be alive at commencement, reasoning from the presumption against disturbing successions that had opened on earlier deaths. By the time the question reached the Supreme Court, therefore, the country had several working rules in simultaneous operation, and partition decrees in different states on indistinguishable facts were being drawn on different fractions. The Pushpalatha condition, birth after 1956, did not survive the eventual settlement: the 2020 bench's reasoning admits no such condition, and Danamma itself, in which daughters born before 1956 were given shares, had already displaced it in practice. The episode is a precise small model of the larger trail. A status statute with an unwritten transition generates divergent readings in the courts of first resort; the divergences harden into regional law; and the eventual national settlement, when it comes, must not only choose between the readings but also decide what becomes of the decrees drawn while the choice was open, which is the part of the problem no single holding can fully solve.

One early decision cut across the debate on a different point and survived everything that followed. In Ganduri Koteshwaramma v. Chakiri Yanadi, (2011) 9 SCC 788, decided on 12 October 2011, the Supreme Court held that the rights of a daughter under the new Section 6 are not lost by the passing of a preliminary decree in a partition suit. A partition suit is not disposed of until the final decree divides the property by metes and bounds, and a preliminary decree determining shares can be amended to give effect to a change in the law that supervenes while the suit is pending. The holding mattered because partition litigation in India is slow, and a rule that froze shares at the preliminary decree would have denied the amendment to precisely the families already in court. The 2020 bench expressly confirmed the holding, and it remains the procedural foundation on which the substantive settlement rests.

The restrictive answer came first at the level of final authority. In Prakash v. Phulavati, (2016) 2 SCC 36, a bench of Anil R. Dave and A. K. Goel JJ., by a judgment delivered on 16 October 2015, held that the amendment is prospective in operation. The rights it conferred, the bench held, are applicable to living daughters of living coparceners as on 9 September 2005, irrespective of when those daughters were born; there was neither express provision nor necessary intendment for retrospective effect; and dispositions and partitions before 20 December 2004 remained in any event protected. The facts showed the rule's edge: the claimant's father had died in 1988, and on the bench's construction she took nothing as a coparcener, because the coparcenary into which she claimed to be born had, for succession purposes, been reconstituted on her father's death seventeen years before the amendment. The reasoning treated the father's death as the operative event and the daughter's claim as an attempt to reopen it. A later bench of R. K. Agrawal and A. M. Sapre JJ. in Mangammal v. T. B. Raju, decided in 2018, reiterated the same construction, so that by the middle of the decade the restrictive view had the weight of two decisions and the profession largely advised on it.

The opposite answer came from a bench of coordinate strength. In Danamma v. Amar, (2018) 3 SCC 343, decided on 1 February 2018 by A. K. Sikri and Ashok Bhushan JJ., the father had died in 2001, leaving sons, daughters and a widow, and the Court nevertheless allotted the daughters shares in the coparcenary property, reasoning from the text of the substituted section: the status of a coparcener is conferred by birth, the words in the same manner as the son admit of no condition about the father's survival, and devolution on a death is a later stage that follows the constitution of the coparcenary rather than creating it. The result in Danamma could not be reconciled with the rule in Prakash, and the conflict was structural rather than incidental: one bench made the father's survival on 9 September 2005 a condition of the daughter's status, the other treated that status as arising from an event, her birth, that necessarily preceded the amendment in every case in which the question arises. Litigants in the same legal position received opposite answers depending on which decision the court seised of their suit followed, and a reference to a larger bench became unavoidable.

### Why did the Supreme Court need three decisions to settle one section?

Because two benches of two judges each read the same words to opposite conclusions, and a bench of that strength cannot overrule its coordinate. The 2015 decision made the father's survival at commencement the condition of the daughter's right; the 2018 decision granted shares where the father had died in 2001.

Only a three judge bench could choose between them, and the choice it made in 2020 turned on treating birth, not the father's death, as the event to which the statute fastens the right.

The settlement came in Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma, (2020) 9 SCC 1, decided on 11 August 2020 by a bench of Arun Mishra, S. Abdul Nazeer and M. R. Shah JJ. The Court held that the provisions of the substituted Section 6 confer the status of coparcener on the daughter of a coparcener by birth, whether she was born before or after the amendment, in the same manner as a son and with the same rights and liabilities. It is not necessary, the Court held, that the father coparcener be living on 9 September 2005, because the right in the coparcenary is by birth and the father's survival is not the event that creates it. The Court characterised the operation of the provision in terms that define its place in the law of retrospectivity: the provision is retroactive in its application, because it confers a right by reference to an antecedent event, the birth of the daughter, while the right itself is claimable on and from 9 September 2005, and it does not disturb the dispositions, alienations, partitions and testamentary dispositions protected by the proviso and by sub-section (5), which had taken place before 20 December 2004. Prakash v. Phulavati and Mangammal v. T. B. Raju were overruled. Danamma was held partly incorrect to the extent its reasoning ran contrary to the decision, its result on the conferral of shares being absorbed into the larger proposition the bench laid down. The Court added the clarifications that give the judgment its practical reach. The statutory fiction of a notional partition under the old proviso did not bring about an actual partition or a disruption of the coparcenary; it existed only to ascertain the share of a deceased coparcener, and it cannot be used to defeat the daughter's claim. A daughter is entitled to her share in pending proceedings for final decree and in appeals, notwithstanding that a preliminary decree has been passed on the narrower view. And on the evidence of partition, the Court held that in view of the Explanation to sub-section (5), a plea of oral partition is not the statutorily recognised mode of effecting a partition and cannot ordinarily be accepted to defeat a daughter's claim; such a plea can be sustained only in exceptional cases where the oral partition is supported by public documents and is finally evinced in the same manner as a partition by decree. The bench closed with a direction that pending matters of this kind be decided expeditiously, as far as possible within six months, an acknowledgment that the fifteen year interval between enactment and settlement had itself become a species of denial for the women whose claims waited on it.

The reasoning repays attention because it locates the amendment in a doctrine rather than in a sentiment. The Court treated the unobstructed heritage of classical Mitakshara law, the interest a coparcener acquires at birth by the fact of being born into the family, as the organising idea: a coparcenary is formed by birth and extended by birth, survivorship being only the mode by which an interest passes within it on a death, not the mechanism by which membership is created. Once membership is understood as a birth event, the condition imposed by Prakash, a living father at commencement, appears as an addition to the text rather than a reading of it, since no provision of the substituted section makes the daughter's status wait on anyone's survival. The opposing argument, which had prevailed in 2015, was at its strongest a finality argument: that succession opening on deaths before 2005 had vested shares in identifiable persons, that purchasers and sharers had ordered their affairs on those shares, and that a social reform statute should not be read to divest them without express words. The 2020 bench met that argument at the level of the savings the statute itself contains: Parliament had drawn the finality line at 20 December 2004 and protected the transactions on the far side of it, and a court that moved the line to the father's death would be substituting its own transition scheme for the legislature's. Stated so, the settlement is a lesson in how amendment trails end. The text supplied a status, a date of claim and a saving; the litigation supplied a gloss the text never contained; and the larger bench removed the gloss by holding the parties to the transition scheme Parliament had actually enacted.

The aftermath in the High Courts followed the pattern the bench had directed. Suits and appeals that had been decided on the Prakash rule while they remained within the appellate system were reopened to the extent the proceedings permitted, shares were reworked in final decree proceedings on the Ganduri Koteshwaramma principle, and the oral partition plea, which had been a standard paragraph of written statements in partition suits for generations, contracted to its new statutory dimensions, succeeding only where the family could produce the public documents, a registered deed in the record, mutation entries of long standing, prior decrees or revenue proceedings, that evince a partition in the manner the Explanation contemplates. Two questions the judgment reserved by silence continued to be worked out below. The first is the treatment of the daughter's claim where the coparcenary property had been sold by the karta to a stranger before 20 December 2004 in circumstances short of a partition: the savings protect dispositions as such, and the contest moves to the alienation's validity under the ordinary law. The second is the interaction of the settlement with the state amendment states, where a claimant may hold under either text and the choice between them is a pleading decision with consequences for shares computed at different dates. Neither question has required a return to a larger bench, which is itself evidence about the settlement's completeness on the point it decided: a holding that leaves only boundary questions to the ordinary appellate process has done the work a reference asks of it. The direction for disposal within six months was not, in the nature of Indian civil litigation, a timetable the system met, but its presence in the operative part of the judgment has been cited in case management of partition suits since, and it stands as the bench's acknowledgment that in this field delay is not neutral between the parties, because the claimant outside possession waits on a right the defendant inside possession enjoys in the meantime.

## The Amending States After 2005: Two Texts on One Field

A question the central amendment left oddly untidy was the fate of the state texts that had preceded it. The Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka and Maharashtra amendments were not repealed by the Act of 2005, and they remain on the statute book as state chapters of the principal Act. For most purposes the central text, being wider, does the work: a married daughter in Maharashtra who took nothing under the state amendment because she had married before 22 June 1994 takes her coparcenary status under the central Section 6, which knows no such distinction, and the state text's limitation survives only as a historical layer that a pleader must still check, because claims crystallised under the state regime, partitions effected under it, and decrees passed on it continue to govern the families they concluded. The layering matters in both directions. A daughter unmarried at the state commencement in a state amendment family held her status from the state date, which for Andhra Pradesh reaches back to 5 September 1985, twenty years before the central Act, and transactions between the state date and 2005 must be tested against the state text that was law when they occurred. The decision table above compresses this into two rows, but the underlying reality is that in four states the trail has two parallel tracks between 1985 and 2005, and a competent search of a family's position in those states runs the tracks in sequence: the state amendment for its period and class, the central amendment thereafter, and the savings clauses of each for the transactions of its time. Kerala's track is single and different in kind, since abolition left no coparcenary for the central amendment to equalise, and the central Act applies in Kerala to succession generally with its coparcenary provisions addressing an institution the state had already dissolved. The map is a standing illustration of Entry 5 of the Concurrent List at work, and of why succession, alone among the great codified subjects, cannot be stated for India in one paragraph without the words in that state, on that date, doing quiet but decisive work inside it.

## The Boundaries of the Settlement

The 2020 decision settled the temporal reach of the substituted section, and its boundaries are as much a part of the trail as its holding. The settlement does not reopen what the statute saved: a partition effected before 20 December 2004 by registered deed or court decree stands, and a disposition or testamentary disposition of that vintage stands with it, so a daughter whose family divided its estate by registered deed in 2003 has no coparcenary claim to revive, however unequal the division. The settlement does not convert a succession already completed under the old proviso into a partition suit, where the property has since passed through the hands the law then recognised and no proceeding is pending; its operative field is the subsisting coparcenary and the pending or future proceeding. And the settlement operates only within the institution the amendment addressed, which is why the question of scope, which families and which schools of law the Act touches at all, decides more claims at the threshold than the retrospectivity question ever did.

### Which families does the Hindu Succession Act govern at all?

It governs Hindus by religion, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs, and persons who are not Muslims, Christians, Parsis or Jews where the statutory conditions of Section 2 are met, including converts to Hinduism. It does not extend to members of Scheduled Tribes unless the Central Government directs its application to them.

Within its field, the coparcenary provisions belong to the Mitakshara school, so the amendment's birth based membership has no purchase in Dayabhaga families, where no coparcenary exists.

The Dayabhaga boundary deserves a sentence of its own, because it is the commonest threshold error in advice given outside the Mitakshara states. Under the Dayabhaga school prevailing in Bengal and Assam, sons acquire no interest by birth in their father's property during his lifetime, the father holds with fuller powers of alienation, and succession opens on his death under the general scheme of the Act, in which daughters have always taken as heirs of the first class to property that devolves. There is no coparcenary for a daughter to enter, and a claim framed under the substituted Section 6 fails at the first step, not because the daughter is excluded but because the institution is absent. Kerala presents the mirror image: there the institution existed and was abolished, so claims run in the tenancy in common the abolition statute created. The amendment trail, for all its national prominence, is a trail within one school of one personal law, and the decision table above records the consequence row by row.

## Edge Cases the Courts Worked Through

The settlement of the main question left the boundary cases, and the boundary cases are where most real claims live. The first concerns the daughter born before the principal Act itself. In Danamma the appellants included daughters born before 1956, and the objection that a woman born before the Act commenced could not take under a scheme enacted that year was answered by the structure of the law: the amendment conferred status by birth on the daughter of a coparcener, without a condition about her own date of birth, and the 2020 bench confirmed that the status attaches whether the daughter was born before or after the amendment, the operative requirements being her birth into the coparcenary and a claim advanced on or after 9 September 2005 against a state of affairs the savings clauses do not protect. The second concerns the decree already passed. Vineeta Sharma distinguishes sharply between the preliminary decree, which declares shares and can be amended as Ganduri Koteshwaramma had held, and the concluded partition, whether by final decree worked out and executed or by registered deed, which the savings protect where it predates 20 December 2004. Between those poles sit the decrees passed on the Prakash view between 2015 and 2020 in suits that remained pending in appeal or in final decree proceedings, and the 2020 judgment's direction that daughters be given their shares in pending proceedings notwithstanding a preliminary decree was framed to reach them. The third concerns alienations in the window: a disposition by a karta or a coparcener between 20 December 2004 and 9 September 2005 is not within the savings, and falls to be tested under the amended law, with the alienee's protection resting on the ordinary doctrines about alienations for necessity rather than on the transitional clause. The fourth concerns adoption. A daughter adopted into a family is, by Section 12 of the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956, deemed to be the child of her adoptive parents for all purposes from the date of the adoption, her ties with the family of her birth being replaced by those the adoption creates, subject to the provisos that preserve property already vested in her and prevent the adoption from divesting any estate vested before it. Within the adoptive family she therefore stands as a daughter for the purposes of the succession law, and her coparcenary position in that family follows the status the adoption confers, while in her birth family she retains what had already vested in her and acquires nothing further by the birth tie the law deems replaced. The fifth concerns the daughter who died before the amendment leaving children: she never held the status herself, and her children claim not through her coparcenary but through the stirpital allotments of Section 6, sub-section (3), and through their own position as heirs where the succession scheme reaches them, a route the Schedule amendment of 2005 widened by placing the children of predeceased daughters' daughters in Class I in terms.

One further boundary, which the edge cases generate repeatedly, concerns the daughter's own descendants in her natal family. A coparcenary is a body constituted by birth into a particular family line, and membership is not transmitted through a daughter to children born to her in her marital family: her son is a coparcener by birth in his father's coparcenary, not in his maternal grandfather's. The daughter's children therefore reach her natal family's property, when they reach it at all, as heirs rather than as coparceners, through the stirpital allotment that Section 6, sub-section (3), makes to the children of a predeceased daughter within the notional division, and through the Class I entries the 2005 Schedule amendment added for the fourth generation claiming through daughters. The distinction is not a drafting oversight but the geometry of the institution: the amendment admitted the daughter to the body into which she was born, and it did not redraw the boundaries between families, which marriage and birth together fix. Claims framed for grandchildren through a daughter must accordingly be pleaded as succession claims to a computed share, and they fail when they are framed as claims to coparcenary membership in the grandfather's family, a confusion the trial courts met often enough after 2005 for the distinction to be worth stating in an article of this kind.

Each of these edges was predictable from the text, and the trail's later cases are best read as the profession and the courts locating the boundaries of a status statute that Parliament had written without a transitional code beyond its two savings dates. The contrast with a statute that writes its transition in full, fixing the treatment of pending proceedings, prior decrees and intermediate transactions clause by clause, is instructive for the drafter of the next amendment in this field, who has the fifteen years of this litigation as a priced catalogue of what an unwritten transition costs.

## What the Amendment Did Not Settle: The Woman's Own Estate

The deepest limit on the 2005 reform lies outside Section 6 altogether, in the provisions the amending Act left untouched: Sections 15 and 16, which govern the intestate succession to the property of a female Hindu. Section 15, sub-section (1), lays down a general order: her property devolves first upon her sons and daughters, including the children of any predeceased son or daughter, and her husband; then upon the heirs of her husband; then upon her mother and father; then upon the heirs of her father; and lastly upon the heirs of her mother. Section 15, sub-section (2), then displaces that order for property she herself inherited. Property inherited from her father or mother devolves, in the absence of children and grandchildren, not on her husband's heirs under the general order but upon the heirs of her father. Property inherited from her husband or her father-in-law devolves, in the same absence of issue, upon the heirs of her husband. The scheme is source based: the property is sent back towards the family it came from. For a male Hindu, Sections 8 to 13 contain no corresponding rule; his inherited property merges into his estate and devolves under one order whatever its source. The asymmetry is therefore exact and structural. A son who inherits from his mother keeps the property within his own line of succession; a daughter who inherits from her father may see it returned on her death, if she dies childless, to her father's heirs in priority to her husband's, while property she inherited from her husband returns to his heirs in priority to her parents. The rules were defended at enactment on the ground that they prevented property from passing permanently out of the family of origin, a rationale that assumes a woman's marriage transfers her proprietary allegiance, and they have been criticised on precisely that ground by commentators on the Act and by the Law Commission, which addressed these provisions in its 174th Report and recommended their reform. Parliament, which in 2005 enacted the Commission's recommendations on the coparcenary, on the dwelling house, on remarriage and on agricultural land, did not enact its recommendation here. The result is a statute that makes a woman a full coparcener in her father's family and a full owner of everything she holds, and then distributes her own estate by rules that treat her as a conduit between two families rather than as the root of one.

The practical weight of the asymmetry falls on childless widows and on women who die intestate holding inherited property, classes of case in which litigation between the woman's natal and marital families is a staple of the succession courts. It also has a drafting consequence that advisers repeat to clients: because Section 15 applies only on intestacy, a will displaces the source based rules entirely, and the testamentary freedom Section 30 secures is the instrument by which women routinely exit a scheme Parliament has declined to amend. A reform assessment of the 2005 amendment that stops at Section 6 therefore overstates the settlement. The amendment equalised the acquisition of property by birth. It did not equalise the devolution of a woman's property at death, and the brief for the next amendment in this trail is already written in the Law Commission's unrepealed recommendation.

The companion rules in Section 16 deepen the pattern in a way worth stating precisely, because the two sections are usually read apart and their combined effect is the real grievance. Rule 1 of Section 16 prefers heirs in an earlier entry of Section 15 over those in a later, and Rule 2 provides that where two heirs succeed together they take per capita and not per stirpes, departing from the stirpital pattern the male scheme applies among the descendants of predeceased children. Rule 3 directs that the devolution of a female's property is worked out as if she had been the relevant male relative: property that Section 15, sub-section (2), sends to the heirs of the father is distributed among those who would have taken had the father died intestate holding it, and likewise for the husband's heirs. The machinery is elegant and its direction is constant, which is that the woman's own line, her parents first under one clause and her husband's family first under the other, is arranged around her rather than through her. A worked instance makes the stakes concrete. A woman inherits an ancestral house share from her father under the post 2005 law, holds it as full owner under Section 14, and dies intestate and childless, survived by her husband and by her brother. Under the general order of Section 15, sub-section (1), her husband, standing in the first entry, would take before her parents' line is reached; but because the property came to her by inheritance from her father, sub-section (2), clause (a), displaces the order and sends the house share to the heirs of her father, which is to say to her brother, and the husband takes nothing of it. Reverse the source and the outcome reverses with it: property she inherited from her husband, if she dies childless and intestate, returns under clause (b) to her husband's heirs, and her mother and brother take nothing of it, however long she held and improved it. No rule of the male scheme performs either manoeuvre. The Law Commission's recommendation on these provisions, made in the same 174th Report that Parliament mined for the rest of the 2005 Act, proposed to recast the order so that a woman's property would devolve on principles nearer to those governing a man's, and the recommendation's presence in the very report the government acted on is what converts Parliament's silence here from an oversight into a decision, one that the next amendment in this trail will have to revisit on a record that is already complete.

## Equal by Birth, Unequal by Land Record: Agricultural Land

The omission of Section 4, sub-section (2) in 2005 was drafted as a completion of the same design: with the saving gone, the Act's succession scheme, coparcenary included, runs on agricultural land as it runs on a house or a trading stock. In the revenue records of several states, it does not. The devolution of tenancy rights and of agricultural holdings in India is governed in practice by state land reform and tenancy statutes, several of which prescribe their own orders of succession to a tenure holder's interest, and those orders were framed in an era when succession to land was treated as an incident of the state's land policy rather than of the family's personal law. The pattern is visible in the older codes: Section 171 of the Uttar Pradesh Zamindari Abolition and Land Reforms Act, 1950 prescribed an order of devolution for a bhumidhar's interest that ran through male lineal descendants before reaching female heirs, and Section 50 of the Delhi Land Reforms Act, 1954 likewise preferred male lineal descendants in the devolution of a bhumidhari holding. Provisions of this kind, in the states where they survive in original or recast form, continue to be invoked before revenue authorities when a tenure holder dies, and the revenue officer recording mutation of the holding applies the code he administers. Litigation then has to establish, holding by holding and state by state, whether the field is occupied by the central Act after 2005 or by the state tenancy text, whether the state text was amended in conformity, and whether an order of succession that prefers males can stand against the constitutional promise the 2005 amendment was enacted to honour. The position therefore varies by state in a way the statute book presents badly: the central reform is uniform, its landward edge is not, and the completeness of a daughter's inheritance depends in several states on whether the family's principal asset is recorded as a house, in which case the amended Act plainly governs, or as a field, in which case a second statute must be fought through first. The wider treatment of acquisition and land records in [the guide to land acquisition law in India](/2013/10/01/land-acquisition-law-india-guide/) shows the same junction from the other side: land in India is administered through state records and state codes, and a central statute that does not reach into that machinery changes rights faster than it changes records.

Mutation practice compounds the statutory gap. A coparcenary right exists by birth and requires no registration to arise, but its enjoyment against the world runs through the revenue record, and entries in that record across much of rural India continue to be made on the death of a recorded holder in favour of sons, with daughters omitted unless they apply and press. The omitted daughter's remedy is the partition suit or proceedings before the revenue and civil courts, each with its own pace, and the economics of that remedy, the cost of suing one's brothers over land the family regards as its indivisible base, explain a part of the distance between the right as settled in 2020 and the right as recorded. The series thesis of this cluster is written in that distance. A reform enacted centrally and left unconformed in the cognate state statutes that administer its subject matter produces a patchwork in which the citizen's right depends on the accident of which code the local officer opens, and the agricultural land chapter of the Hindu Succession trail is an instance of the mechanism in its purest form: not resistance to the reform, which no state legislature has voted, but non-conformity, which no state legislature has had to vote at all.

## The Right in Practice: Pressure, Release Deeds and Proof

Between the settled right and its exercise stand the practices of the family itself. The commonest is the relinquishment obtained at marriage: a deed or a writing, sometimes registered and sometimes not, by which a daughter releases her interest in the joint family property in favour of her brothers, frequently executed at or around her wedding and frequently explained within the family as the counterpart of the expenditure the wedding involved. The legal position of such instruments is a live question that this article describes rather than resolves. A coparcener may release her undivided interest, and a release executed with capacity, understanding and the formalities the law requires can bind; but releases are attacked, with varying success, on grounds that range from want of registration where the instrument operates on immovable property, to absence of consideration, to the timing problem that a release of a mere expectation differs in law from the release of a subsisting coparcenary interest, to the contention that an instrument extracted as the price of a marriage the family arranged is not the free act the law of relinquishment presupposes. Vineeta Sharma's treatment of informal arrangements bears on the practice from the other side: a family that produces, against a daughter's claim, an unregistered writing said to record an old partition or settlement faces the Explanation to Section 6, sub-section (5), and the requirement that a partition be evidenced in the statutory mode. The honest summary is that the release deed is the point at which the social practice of daughters standing aside meets a statutory text that stopped cooperating with the practice in 2005, and the volume of litigation over such deeds is the measure of the meeting.

A second practical frontier is management. A coparcener's bundle includes the possibility of acting as karta, the manager of the joint family and its property, a position classical law and much practice reserved to the senior most male coparcener. In Sujata Sharma v. Manu Gupta, decided on 22 December 2015, the Delhi High Court held that the eldest coparcener, where she is a woman, is not disqualified from being the karta of a Hindu undivided family, reasoning that Section 6 confers on the daughter the same rights as a son and that the right to manage is among them, the statutory bar the court was invited to find having no foothold in the amended text. The decision is a High Court decision and its reasoning has been widely followed in practice, where the karta's identity matters for the family's tax assessments, bank mandates and dealings with its property. It illustrates the way the substituted section propagated: once status by birth was conceded, the incidents of status followed by construction, one by one, in forums the amendment never mentioned.

### What evidence must a daughter produce to make good a claim?

The claim is proved as a partition claim is proved: by establishing the family and its governing school, the coparcenary character of the property, and her birth into the coparcenary, and by meeting any plea of prior partition on the documents the statute recognises.

In practice the record is assembled from title documents and revenue entries for the property, the deed or decree relied on for any alleged partition, the release deed if one is said to exist, and the family tree that connects her to the coparcener from whom the coparcenary descends.

The assembly of that record is where claims are quietly won and lost, because the documents live in the family's possession, in the sub-registrar's index, in the tehsil's mutation register and in the decree sheets of suits decided before many claimants were born, and a daughter who has been outside the management of the property for decades begins at an evidential disadvantage her brothers do not share. The discipline of keeping the title documents, the partition record and the family papers together, in a form that can be produced complete when a claim is framed or resisted, is the practical counterpart of the legal right, and it is the point of this article at which the examination reader and the claimant reader part company. For the reader preparing on this cluster for the civil services or judicial services examinations, the trail repays being kept as a structured file of propositions, dates and benches, and [practising the succession propositions through the ReportMedic viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) is the natural way to test whether the sequence from Prakash to Vineeta Sharma can be reproduced under examination conditions. This article states the general position of the law on the material it covers; it is information and analysis, and it does not substitute for advice on an individual case, where the state of the title, the dates of the transactions and the forum's own limitation rules will decide what a court can still be asked to do.

## The Reform Measured: What Two Decades of State Operation Showed

Because four states operated daughter coparcenary for up to twenty years before the Centre acted, the 2005 amendment is one of the few Indian social reforms whose effects could be studied in advance of its national life, and the studies belong in the trail because they were part of the material before the Law Commission and because they discipline the claims made for the national reform since. The best known evaluation is the study by Klaus Deininger, Aparajita Goyal and Hari Nagarajan of the state amendments, published under the title Inheritance Law Reform and Women's Access to Capital, which compared the likelihood of a daughter inheriting land before and after the state reforms in the amending states. Its findings, stated at the level of confidence the authors themselves claimed, were that the amendments did raise the probability that a daughter would inherit land, that the effect, while real, fell well short of the parity the texts promised, and that compensating behaviour by parents, including larger dowry transfers and the use of testamentary freedom over separate property, absorbed part of the reform's force. Later work on the same amendments associated them with gains in measures adjacent to property, including the age at which daughters were married and their years of schooling, on the reasoning that a daughter with a legal claim is a daughter whose natal family invests in her differently. The measured picture, taken whole, is neither the transformation of the celebratory account nor the nullity of the sceptical one: it is a reform that moved outcomes in the direction of its text, at a pace set by the practices, the records and the litigation capacity of the families it governed. That measured picture is the correct frame for the national amendment's record after 2005, in which the Supreme Court's fifteen year delay in settling the text's reach, the state of the revenue records, and the economics of partition litigation each predict, and together explain, a real but incomplete movement between the right and its enjoyment. An amendment trail that reported only the statute and the judgments would flatter the reform; the evidence from the states is what allows the trail to say, with support, that the flattery would be a distortion in the opposite direction from the scepticism it replaced.

## The Cumulative Arc: What the Trail Shows

Read end to end, the amendment trail of the Hindu Succession Act has a shape the series meets across Indian social legislation, stated here in its sharpest instance. The first movement is compromise codification: Parliament in 1956 enacted the equality it could carry and preserved the institution it could not, recording in Sections 6, 23 and 24 the exact points at which the settlement stopped. The second movement is federal experiment: four states and one abolition statute demonstrated over two decades that the preserved institution could be equalised or ended without the consequences its defenders predicted, and the Law Commission converted their demonstration into a national draft. The third movement is central enactment in economy form: a seven section amending Act that changed a status, deleted the disabilities and trusted construction to do the rest. The fourth movement is judicial completion under conditions of judicial conflict: a restrictive bench, an expansive bench, and a larger bench that settled the question by returning to the organising idea of the institution itself, birth, and to the transition line Parliament had drawn in 2004. Each movement took roughly a generation. The daughter whose exclusion the Hindu Code debates of the nineteen forties protested was a grandmother by the time the Supreme Court confirmed that her right had never depended on her father's survival, and the six month direction in Vineeta Sharma is the judgment's own comment on the pace of the preceding movements.

The verdict on the amendment itself is not difficult, and the stance this article takes is that the evidence supports it plainly. The amendment strengthened the statute it amended, in the precise sense that it removed the contradiction at the statute's centre: a succession code founded on equal Class I heirship cannot coherently preserve, for the largest class of property it governs, a birth based body from which daughters are excluded by sex. The state amendments had already proved administrability; the central amendment's refusal to copy their married and unmarried distinction was its best drafting decision, since that distinction had reproduced, inside the reform, the premise the reform existed to reject, that a daughter's proprietary membership migrates at marriage. Against this, the countervailing observations recorded in this article stand as unfinished business rather than as arguments the other way: the social practice of relinquishment, which operates on the claimant rather than on the text; the Section 15 asymmetry, which awaits the amendment the Law Commission recommended and Parliament has not passed; and the agricultural land non-conformity, which awaits the unglamorous work of aligning state tenancy codes and revenue practice with a central Act that has, since 2005, already answered the question of principle. Within the wider map of the field, this amendment is the load bearing member of the cluster that [the guide to women's rights laws in India](/2015/11/01/womens-rights-laws-india-guide/) sets out, because it is the one reform in that field that altered the distribution of assets rather than the regulation of conduct, and its trail is the standing evidence that asset reforms, once enacted, still require a generation of litigation and administration before the record matches the right.

What the trail predicts about the next amendment can be stated with some confidence, because the pattern of the last one is so legible. The next change in this statute will most plausibly come where the Law Commission's file is still open, in Sections 15 and 16, and it will come, if the sequence holds, only after state level or judicial pressure has made the asymmetry untenable in litigation, and it will leave its own transitional question for the courts unless Parliament has learned the lesson of 20 December 2004 and writes the transition into the text. The second prediction concerns land: the conformity of state tenancy succession orders with the central Act will be settled piecemeal, in revenue appeals and constitutional challenges state by state, unless a state legislature takes the initiative its predecessors took in 1986 and amends first. The trail of the Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act 2005 is therefore closed as to the coparcenary and open as to everything the coparcenary touches, which is the condition in which Indian amendment trails generally end: the section settled, the system still in motion, and the record of the settlement itself the best guide to where the pressure will build next.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the 2005 amendment to the Hindu Succession Act change?

The Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005 substituted Section 6 of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 so that the daughter of a coparcener becomes a coparcener by birth in her own right in the same manner as a son, with the same rights and the same liabilities in coparcenary property. It provided that a deceased coparcener's interest devolves by testamentary or intestate succession rather than by survivorship, abolished the doctrine of pious obligation for debts contracted after its commencement, omitted Section 4, sub-section (2), Section 23 and Section 24, extended the testamentary power in Section 30 to female Hindus in terms, and added four heirs claiming through daughters to Class I of the Schedule.

### Q: Is a daughter a coparcener by birth?

Yes. Section 6, sub-section (1), of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956, as substituted in 2005, provides that in a joint Hindu family governed by the Mitakshara law the daughter of a coparcener shall by birth become a coparcener in her own right in the same manner as the son. Her membership arises from her birth into the family and not from any death, demand or grant. She holds the same rights in the coparcenary property as she would have had if she had been a son, including the right to seek partition, and she is subject to the same liabilities. The position is confined to Mitakshara families, since the Dayabhaga school has no coparcenary.

### Q: Must the father have been alive for a daughter to be a coparcener?

No. After Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma, (2020) 9 SCC 1, the settled position is that the daughter's coparcenary right is conferred by birth and does not depend on her father having been alive on 9 September 2005, when the 2005 amendment commenced. A three judge bench overruled Prakash v. Phulavati, which had confined the amendment to living daughters of living coparceners. The right is claimable from the date of commencement, and it does not disturb dispositions, alienations, partitions or testamentary dispositions completed before 20 December 2004, which the substituted Section 6 expressly protects. The daughter must herself be living and claiming; the father's survival is not a condition.

### Q: What did Vineeta Sharma decide?

In Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma, (2020) 9 SCC 1, decided on 11 August 2020 by a bench of Arun Mishra, S. Abdul Nazeer and M. R. Shah JJ., the Supreme Court held that the substituted Section 6 confers coparcener status on a daughter by birth, whether she was born before or after the 2005 amendment, and that the father need not have been alive at commencement. The Court held the provision retroactive in application because it fastens on the antecedent event of birth, overruled Prakash v. Phulavati and Mangammal v. T. B. Raju, held Danamma v. Amar partly incorrect in reasoning, restricted pleas of oral partition, and confirmed that daughters take shares in pending proceedings notwithstanding preliminary decrees.

### Q: Do daughters inherit agricultural land equally?

Under the central Act, yes in principle, with a state level qualification that decides many real cases. The 2005 amendment omitted Section 4, sub-section (2), of the Hindu Succession Act, which had kept laws on fragmentation, ceilings and the devolution of tenancy rights outside the Act, so the Act's scheme, including the daughter's coparcenary right, extends to agricultural land. In several states, however, tenancy and land reform statutes prescribe their own orders of devolution for agricultural holdings, and revenue authorities continue to apply them when recording succession to a holding. Whether a daughter's equal right is given effect without litigation therefore depends on the state and on the character of the holding, and the position is not uniform across India.

### Q: What happens to a Hindu woman's own property when she dies?

If she dies intestate, Sections 15 and 16 of the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 govern. Under the general order in Section 15, sub-section (1), her property devolves first on her sons, daughters and husband together, then on her husband's heirs, then on her mother and father, then on her father's heirs, and lastly on her mother's heirs. Property she inherited is treated differently: what she inherited from her parents returns, if she leaves no children, to her father's heirs, and what she inherited from her husband or father-in-law returns to her husband's heirs. There is no corresponding source based rule for a man's property, and the Law Commission has recommended reform of these provisions, which the 2005 amendment left untouched.

### Q: Which provisions restricting women were deleted in 2005?

Three. Section 23 of the Hindu Succession Act, which had barred a female Class I heir from claiming partition of a dwelling house wholly occupied by the family until the male heirs chose to divide, and which gave a daughter a right of residence only if she was unmarried, deserted, separated or widowed, was omitted. Section 24, which disqualified the widow of a predeceased son, the widow of a predeceased son of a predeceased son, and the widow of a brother from inheriting as widows if they had remarried when the succession opened, was omitted. Section 4, sub-section (2), which had excluded tenancy and agricultural holdings legislation from the Act's operation, was also omitted, extending the Act's scheme to agricultural land.

### Q: Did any states give daughters coparcenary rights before 2005?

Yes. Kerala took the different route of abolishing the joint family system altogether by the Kerala Joint Hindu Family System (Abolition) Act, 1975, in force from 1 December 1976. Four states amended the Hindu Succession Act to admit daughters to the coparcenary: Andhra Pradesh with effect from 5 September 1985, Tamil Nadu from 25 March 1989, Karnataka by an Act of 1990 brought into force on 30 July 1994, and Maharashtra from 22 June 1994. Each state amendment confined the new status to daughters who were unmarried when it commenced, a limitation the central amendment of 2005 did not copy. The central Act adopted the state drafting model while discarding that distinction.

### Q: Does marriage take away a daughter's coparcenary right?

No, not under the central law. The substituted Section 6 draws no distinction between married and unmarried daughters, and a daughter remains a coparcener in the family of her birth after her marriage, holding her birth based share alongside her rights in her marital family. This is one of the points on which the central amendment deliberately departed from its models: the earlier state amendments in Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka and Maharashtra had conferred the status only on daughters who were unmarried when those provisions commenced. A daughter who married before her state's amendment began takes nothing under that state text, but she takes her coparcenary status under the central amendment, which governs the field without the marital condition.

### Q: Can a daughter be the karta of a Hindu undivided family?

Yes, where she is the senior most coparcener. The karta is the manager of the joint family and its property, and the office belongs by convention to the senior most coparcener. In Sujata Sharma v. Manu Gupta, decided on 22 December 2015, the Delhi High Court held that a female coparcener is not disqualified from being karta: since the 2005 amendment confers on the daughter the same rights as a son in the coparcenary, the right to manage the family's affairs is among them, and no provision of the amended Act bars her from the office. The eldest daughter of the family in that case was recognised as karta for purposes including the family's dealings and assessments.

### Q: Can a daughter make a will of her share in coparcenary property?

Yes. Sub-section (2) of the substituted Section 6 provides that property to which a female Hindu becomes entitled as a coparcener shall be held by her with the incidents of coparcenary ownership and shall be capable of being disposed of by her by will or other testamentary disposition. Section 30 of the Act, amended in 2005 to read disposed of by him or by her, confirms the testamentary power in general terms for any Hindu over an interest in coparcenary property. The power extends to her undivided interest; on her death without a will, that interest devolves under sub-section (3) by intestate succession. Her will cannot dispose of any other coparcener's share.

### Q: Is a daughter liable for the debts of the joint family like a son?

In substance, yes. Sub-section (1) of the substituted Section 6 makes the daughter a coparcener subject to the same liabilities in respect of the coparcenary property as a son, so an alienation by the karta for legal necessity or benefit of the estate binds her interest as it binds a son's, and she takes the estate with its lawful burdens. One historic burden was removed by the same amendment for the future: sub-section (4) abolished the doctrine of pious obligation, so no court can proceed against a son, grandson or great grandson for an ancestor's debt contracted after the commencement of the amendment solely on that doctrine. Debts contracted before commencement, and alienations made to satisfy them, remain governed by the older law.

### Q: How is the share of a deceased coparcener calculated after the 2005 amendment?

By a statutory fiction. Under Section 6, sub-section (3), when a Hindu dies after the commencement of the 2005 amendment, his interest in Mitakshara coparcenary property devolves by testamentary or intestate succession and not by survivorship, and the property is deemed to have been divided as if a partition had taken place immediately before his death. On that notional partition the deceased takes the share he would have received, the daughter is allotted the same share as a son, and the children of a predeceased son or daughter take the share their parent would have taken. The deceased's computed share then passes to his Class I heirs in equal portions. The fiction measures the share; it does not itself dissolve the family.

### Q: Are partitions and sales completed before 20 December 2004 protected from a daughter's claim?

Yes, by the statute's own savings. The proviso to Section 6, sub-section (1), provides that the daughter's coparcenary right does not affect or invalidate any disposition or alienation, including any partition or testamentary disposition of property, which took place before 20 December 2004, the date the amending Bill was introduced in the Rajya Sabha. Sub-section (5) adds that nothing in the section applies to a partition effected before that date, and its Explanation defines partition as one made by a duly registered deed of partition or effected by a decree of a court. A daughter's claim therefore proceeds against the coparcenary as it stands after those protected transactions are excluded, and a completed registered partition of 2003 is not reopened by the amendment.

### Q: Can an oral partition be set up to defeat a daughter's claim?

Ordinarily, no. In Vineeta Sharma the Supreme Court held that, in view of the Explanation to Section 6, sub-section (5), which recognises a partition made by a duly registered deed or by a decree of a court, a plea of oral partition is not the statutorily recognised mode of partition and cannot be accepted merely on oral assertion to defeat a daughter's claim. The Court left a narrow opening: in exceptional cases an oral partition may be sustained where it is supported by public documents and is finally evinced in the same manner as a partition by decree would be. Families relying on old unregistered arrangements therefore face a heavy burden of documentary proof, and an unregistered family settlement produced only when a daughter sues will rarely carry it.

### Q: If a partition suit was already pending when the law changed, can the decree be redrawn for a daughter?

Yes, if the suit has not reached a final decree. A partition suit is not concluded by the preliminary decree, which only declares the shares; the suit continues until the final decree divides the property by metes and bounds. In Ganduri Koteshwaramma v. Chakiri Yanadi, (2011) 9 SCC 788, the Supreme Court held that the benefit of the 2005 amendment must be given to daughters in a pending suit and that a preliminary decree can be amended to rework the shares. Vineeta Sharma confirmed the position and directed that a daughter be given her share in pending proceedings for final decree and in appeals, notwithstanding that a preliminary decree had been passed on the narrower view of the law.

### Q: Does the 2005 amendment change anything for Dayabhaga families?

Very little, because the institution the amendment addresses does not exist under that school. The Dayabhaga school, which governs Hindus in Bengal and Assam, knows no coparcenary: sons take no interest by birth in their father's property during his lifetime, the father holds with full powers of disposition, and succession opens on his death under the general scheme of Sections 8 to 13 for a male and Sections 15 and 16 for a female, in which daughters have always been heirs of the first class. The substituted Section 6 speaks only of a joint Hindu family governed by the Mitakshara law. A Dayabhaga daughter's position therefore continued after 2005 much as before, resting on heirship rather than on birth based membership.

### Q: If a daughter married before her state's amendment began, which law decides her claim?

The central amendment. The state amendments in Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka and Maharashtra conferred coparcenary status only on a daughter who was unmarried when the state provision commenced, so a daughter who had already married took no status under the state text. The Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005 contains no married and unmarried distinction, and it operates across India from 9 September 2005. Her claim is therefore made under the substituted Section 6 of the central Act, with the same savings for transactions completed before 20 December 2004. The state text remains relevant to the family's history, because partitions and decrees concluded under it in its own period continue to stand, but it no longer confines her.

### Q: Does a release deed signed at marriage bar a daughter's claim?

It can, but it does not do so automatically, and the question is contested in practice. A coparcener may relinquish her undivided interest, and a release executed voluntarily, with capacity and with the formalities the law requires for dealings in immovable property, is capable of binding her. Releases taken at or around a daughter's marriage are nevertheless attacked on grounds that include want of registration, absence of consideration, the distinction between releasing a subsisting interest and signing away a mere expectation, and the circumstances in which the signature was obtained. A family that answers a claim with an unregistered writing must also meet the partition standard the Explanation to Section 6, sub-section (5), sets. Whether a particular deed bars a particular claim is a question of fact and document, decided case by case.

### Q: Why does the coparcenary amendment make no difference in Kerala?

Because Kerala had abolished the institution the amendment equalises. The Kerala Joint Hindu Family System (Abolition) Act, 1975, in force from 1 December 1976, ended the joint Hindu family system in that state: members of a joint family came to hold their shares as tenants in common, as if a partition had been effected among all of them, and the right to claim an interest by birth ceased for sons and daughters alike. With no coparcenary in existence, the substituted Section 6 has nothing to operate on in Kerala, and succession there runs under the general scheme of the Hindu Succession Act on the footing the abolition statute created. Kerala reached equality three decades earlier than the Centre, by removing the birth based institution rather than by admitting daughters to it.
