## The Play in German Criticism: The Verdict in Advance

German criticism did not merely receive Romeo and Juliet. It rebuilt the play a second time, in another language, and then spent two centuries arguing about what it had made. The verdict of this article is that the German tradition is the most consequential foreign reception the play has anywhere had, and that its consequence rests on labour rather than on the blood-kinship its loudest champions claimed. Three German acts decided the matter. August Wilhelm Schlegel read the tragedy as a single organism whose every part served one impression of love against hate, and he translated it into verse that Germans could mistake for an original. The nineteenth century then institutionalized that reading: a complete corpus, a learned society founded at Weimar in 1864, a yearbook, a university philology, an editor in Nikolaus Delius who edited the English text from its originals on German soil. The twentieth century, finally, corrected the inheritance from inside, when Levin Ludwig Schücking demanded history instead of rapture, when the theatres rebelled against the canonical translation, and when Wolfgang Clemen rebuilt the study of the play's imagery on developmental evidence.

![Romeo and Juliet in German criticism, from the wandering stage to the Weimar society - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

Each act carried a distortion inside its achievement, and this article weighs both. Schlegel's canonization fixed the lovers as lyric absolutes and pushed Verona's civic machinery, the feud, the marriage market, the friar's casuistry, toward the margin of German attention. The national claim that crested in 1864 and again in Friedrich Gundolf's Shakespeare und der deutsche Geist of 1911 confused adoption with ancestry, and the confusion had to be dismantled by the tradition's own scholars before the tradition could be trusted. What survives the dismantling is the stronger thing: Roger Paulin's conclusion, in The Critical Reception of Shakespeare in German 1682-1914, that Shakespeare can without further ado be called a German classic, akin to Goethe or Schiller. Paulin's sentence is a finding about work done, translations made, editions printed, societies kept, schools stocked, and it stands precisely because it needs no metaphysics of the German spirit to support it.

The stations follow in the order the tradition built them. A German audience met the lovers first not in print but on the boards of travelling players, in a cut and clown-stuffed adaptation. Print arrived in the 1760s, in Christoph Martin Wieland's prose, and the stage promptly answered with Christian Felix Weiße's radical reworking of 1767, which held German theatres for two generations. Criticism prepared the ground in parallel: Lessing cleared the French rules away, Herder proclaimed a northern genius of nature, and the young Goethe kept a Shakespeare festival at Frankfurt in 1771. Only then came 1797, the year Schlegel published both his essay on the play in Schiller's journal Die Horen and his verse translation, and the double event from which everything later in this article descends.

A word on the record itself, since the article's verdict depends on trusting documents over slogans. The German stations rest on dated primary evidence at every step: Morhof's page of 1682, the Horen essay and the translation volumes of 1797, the Vienna lectures in their published form, the title pages and prefaces of the completed edition, the Gesellschaft's founding acts and its Jahrbuch run from 1865, Delius's edition and his 1846 polemic, Gundolf's Habilitation of 1911. Around the primaries stands a modern scholarship of the reception that is itself largely German in origin or in subject: Roger Paulin's history of the critical reception from 1682 to 1914, Hermann Conrad's manuscript study of 1911, the Birmingham survey of the Jahrbuch's first fifty years, Balz Engler's account of the translation quarrel, and the Arden edition of the wandering players' text. Where a station in this article rests on a single witness, the text says so; where the tradition's own mythology conflicts with the documents, the documents govern, and the conflicts are flagged at their stations rather than smoothed away. That discipline is itself the German tradition's best legacy, learned from its philologists.

## Verona on the Wandering Stage: Romio und Julieta Before Print

The play's German life begins a full century before German criticism existed, and it begins as theatre without an author anyone could name. From the 1590s onward, companies of English players, the so-called English comedians, carried London repertory across northern Europe, performing first in English and then, as they settled into German courts and cities, in German versions hammered out for the road. Among the texts that survive from that traffic is a late seventeenth-century manuscript copy of an adaptation of the Veronese tragedy under the title Romio und Julieta. It is not a translation. It is a working stage text, shortened against the original, its information carried by gesture and spectacle where the audience's language ran thin, and its comic dimension enlarged far beyond anything in the source.

The clearest measure of the enlargement is a character who exists nowhere in Shakespeare's play. Pickelherring, the stock clown of the German wandering stage, walks through Romio und Julieta with a prominence the adaptation's makers clearly thought indispensable, and nineteenth-century scholars, meeting him in the manuscript, pronounced his scenes devoid of taste. Kareen Seidler's research on the wandering-stage texts reads the clown differently: the adaptation brings out, in farcical register, the comic element that the original itself carries in its first two acts, the servants' brawling, the Nurse's garrulity, Mercutio's wit, before the catastrophe forecloses it. On that reading the German players were the first critics the play had outside England, and their criticism was performed rather than written: they decided, with their cuts and their clown, that the tragedy of the lovers was also, for half its length, a comedy of the city that killed them.

Two features of this first station governed everything that followed. The first is that Germany met the play as material to be worked, not as a monument to be guarded. The wandering text took liberties that English editorial culture would spend centuries debating, and it took them without anxiety, because no doctrine of fidelity yet existed to forbid it. The second is that the adaptation tradition never entirely died. When Weiße rewrote the play for the bourgeois stage in 1767, when Gotter and Benda turned it into a Singspiel with a happy ending in 1776, they were extending the players' licence, not breaking a rule. The German argument about Romeo and Juliet has always been, at bottom, an argument between the two inheritances of this opening century: the players' freedom to remake, and the scholars' duty to recover. Schlegel's greatness was to fuse them, to remake the play in German so exactly that the remaking could pass for recovery. The century after him was spent discovering the seam.

The documentary record of the wandering stage also fixes a point the later national claim preferred to blur. The play reached German audiences through English actors performing for courts and fairs, in a Europe where theatrical goods crossed borders as a matter of trade. Its Germanness, when Germans began to assert one, was an achievement of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, built by named translators, critics and editors whose work can be dated and examined. That is why this article insists on the dated record at every station. The tradition is impressive enough in its documents. It has never needed the fog of ancestral affinity in which Gundolf and the tercentenary orators wrapped it.

### How did the wandering players change the play before print ever carried it?

They shortened it, visualised it and clowned it. The surviving Romio und Julieta cuts the text for a company playing to audiences with limited English, transfers exposition from speech to action and spectacle, and installs the stock clown Pickelherring in scenes the original does not give him, enlarging the farce until it rivals the tragedy for space.

What the players could not transmit was the verse. Their Verona spoke prose hammered into shape for comprehension, and the poetry of the balcony scene, the shared sonnet at the feast, the aubade at dawn, waited a hundred years for a German form. When Romeo cries at his first sight of Juliet, "O, she doth teach the torches to burn bright!" (1.5.44), the line's work is done by image and cadence together, and cadence is the first casualty of a stage adaptation built for plot. The German tradition's central problem was therefore set before its first critic wrote a word: how to give a German audience not the story of the lovers, which any troupe could carry, but the poem of the lovers, which only a translator-poet could rebuild. Wieland's prose would carry the story to readers in the 1760s. The poem waited for Schlegel.

## Clearing the Ground: Lessing, Herder and the Prose Decades

German print met Shakespeare late and coolly. The first mention of the playwright in German letters stands in Daniel Georg Morhof's Unterricht von der Teutschen Sprache und Poesie of 1682, where the name appears in a list of English poets, cited in the train of Dryden's dramatic theory rather than from any direct acquaintance with the plays. For two generations after Morhof the name circulated in that fashion, one item in a catalogue, while the Leipzig critic Johann Christoph Gottsched built German letters on French foundations and treated the English stage as a byword for irregularity. The first sustained piece of German Shakespeare criticism broke the pattern in the 1740s: Johann Elias Schlegel, uncle of the two brothers who would later define German Romanticism, set Shakespeare beside the Silesian dramatist Andreas Gryphius in his Vergleichung Shakespears und Andreas Gryphs. The comparison was an act of rehabilitation. Against the reigning charge that Shakespeare's beauties came wrapped in barbarity and obscurity, the elder Schlegel argued that the Englishman's command of character placed him with the ancients as a model for admiration, crudities and all. German criticism of the play begins in that gesture, the weighing of irregular genius against imported rules, and the gesture would be repeated at every station down to Gundolf.

Translation began in the same decades, in fragments and under the enemy's flag. Caspar Wilhelm von Borcke, Prussian envoy in London, published a German Julius Caesar in 1741, the first printed translation of a Shakespeare play into any language, rendered in the rhymed alexandrines the French party prescribed, so that the first German Shakespeare arrived wearing the uniform of the school that despised him. Luise Gottsched, the Leipzig leader's wife and herself a translator of the English periodical essay, tried her hand at Shakespeare passages in the same years, the first such attempt by a German writer of standing. The fragments matter less for their quality than for their lesson, which the century would learn slowly: Shakespeare in German dress kept bursting the dress. The alexandrine couplet could carry Caesar's rhetoric; it could not have carried the balcony scene, where the verse thinks in exchanges, interruptions and a sonnet built by two speakers. Every German advance on this play, from Wieland's prose surrender to Schlegel's verse submission, is a footnote to that discovery, that the form was not clothing but body.

Gotthold Ephraim Lessing turned the gesture into a campaign. In the seventeenth of his Briefe, die neueste Literatur betreffend in 1759 he attacked the Gottsched school's French orientation head-on and held up the English drama as the living alternative; in the Hamburgische Dramaturgie of 1767 to 1769 he dismantled the neoclassical reading of Aristotle piece by piece and made Shakespeare the standing counterexample to the unities of time and place. Lessing wrote no reading of Romeo and Juliet, and Julius Charles Hare, introducing Schlegel's essay to English readers in 1820, judged the limitation exactly: Lessing cleared the ground by demolition, substituting doctrines of his own, and his criticism remained chiefly negative. The judgement holds. But demolition was the necessary first labour, because the tragedy of Verona is precisely the kind of play French rules condemned on sight: a action spanning street brawl and sonnet, prose servants and rhymed lovers, a friar's botany lecture and a tomb. "From ancient grudge break to new mutiny" (Prologue.3), the Chorus announces, and the play that follows keeps the Prologue's promise of mixed matter for five acts. A criticism armed with the unities could only call that mixture a defect. Lessing's campaign made it possible, a generation later, for a German critic to call it a design.

The reading text arrived between the campaign's two fronts. Christoph Martin Wieland, later of Weimar, published German versions of twenty-two plays between 1762 and 1766, the first substantial Shakespeare in the language, and Romeo und Julia stood among them. Wieland worked in prose, as Eschenburg after him would do, and he worked as an improver: where passages offended his taste he cut them, and where they puzzled him he smoothed them, so that the Germany of the 1760s read a Shakespeare pruned by the very Enlightenment judgement the plays were busy refuting. The cost for this play was specific. A tragedy whose meaning lives in its verse contracts, the sonnet the lovers build together at the feast, Juliet's metrically argued farewell at dawn, "Wilt thou be gone? It is not yet near day" (3.5.1), becomes in prose a striking story with striking speeches, its formal skeleton dissolved. Johann Joachim Eschenburg revised and completed Wieland's enterprise between 1775 and 1782, supplying the remaining plays and a standard of prose accuracy on which the century's stages, Friedrich Ludwig Schröder's Hamburg company among them, could draw. Prose was the German play's condition for thirty years: available, actable, and diminished.

The stage answered print with the wandering players' old licence, raised to literature. Christian Felix Weiße, writing for the new German national theatre movement, produced his Romeo und Julia in 1767 and declined to call it an adaptation at all, claiming the text as his own. He built instead a bourgeois tragedy, the bürgerliches Trauerspiel he helped to name, leaning heavily on David Garrick's English adaptation, and his reworking dominated the stages of the German lands deep into the nineteenth century; scholars of its circulation describe it as the most successful German play of the eighteenth century, touring from the capitals to Hungary and Transylvania and persisting in the provinces into the 1820s. Nine years later Friedrich Wilhelm Gotter and the composer Georg Benda converted the material into the Singspiel Romeo und Julie, first performed at the Ekhof Theatre in Gotha on 25 September 1776. Gotter stripped out most of the comedy, made Julie the strongest figure in the piece, and, in the operatic convention of the lieto fine, let the lovers live: Juliet wakes in the vault before the dagger falls, and the work closes on a duet of joy. Caroline Schlegel's letters catch the opera inside the Jena circle in 1797, the role of Juliet sung nearly entire in a private house while her husband's verse translation was in the press. The two Germanys of the play, the improvers' and the restorers', could be heard, in the same season, in the same rooms.

The improvers' texts, meanwhile, were becoming European goods in their own right, a fact the national story prefers to forget. Weiße's reworking, carried across the continent by touring companies, was translated into French by Louis-Sébastien Mercier and reached Moscow's stages in the 1790s, so that audiences in two capitals met the Veronese lovers in a German bourgeois rewrite of an English adaptation of Shakespeare. The wandering players' pattern had repeated at a higher level of print: Germany was not only receiving the play but re-exporting it, processed. Friedrich Ludwig Schröder's Hamburg company showed the countervailing discipline at work in the same decades. Schröder, the era's defining German actor-manager, built his Shakespeare productions by restoration, taking Eschenburg's more accurate prose as his base and returning passage after passage that the adaptation tradition had cut; his Hamlet versions are the documented type of the method, and the method, applied across his repertory, taught German audiences to expect more of the text each season. Between Weiße's ownership and Schröder's restoration the German stage rehearsed, in practice, the argument the critics would soon conduct in print: how much of the foreign play a national theatre may remake before the remaking becomes the work. Schlegel's answer of 1797, almost nothing, and his demonstration, the verse text itself, settled the argument for the study. The stage, being a practical institution, went on compromising for another century, and the Jahrbuch's theatre reports preserve the compromises in detail.

What turned admiration into a programme was Johann Gottfried Herder. His essay Shakespear, published in the manifesto volume Von deutscher Art und Kunst in 1773, rejected the French devotion to the unities outright and presented the English dramatist as a genius of nature and history, the model from which a German national drama might grow rather than a foreign master to be copied. Herder's Shakespeare is a force of cultural weather, different in each age and people, and the essay's logic would echo through the whole tradition: if the plays belong to no single nation's rules, then the nation that understands them most inwardly may claim a share in them. The claim's respectable form begins here, as an argument about interpretation. Its degenerate form, the claim of blood, begins when later writers forgot that interpretation, not descent, was the ground.

The young Goethe gave the programme its festival. At Frankfurt on 14 October 1771 he organized a private Shakespeare jubilee and delivered the speech Zum Schäkespears Tag, declaring the date William's day and hailing a dramatist of unbounded nature against the prison of the rules. Goethe's mature judgement cooled, and his late essay Shakespeare und kein Ende, written in 1813, weighed the plays as reading matter with a classicist's reservations about their stage life; but the festival of 1771 fixed the social form of the German devotion. Shakespeare in the German lands would be celebrated the way saints and national poets are celebrated, with feast days, monuments and, in time, a society. The criticism this article traces grew inside that cult, and its best practitioners were those who could keep the cult's warmth and refuse its conclusions.

## Weimar and Jena: Two Ways of Owning the Play

The generation that inherited the prose decades divided into two camps over the manner of the possession, and the division runs through everything German criticism later did with the play. At Weimar, Goethe and Schiller built a classicism that admired Shakespeare enormously and trusted him conditionally. Schiller's great essay Über naive und sentimentalische Dichtung, published in Die Horen in 1795 and 1796, enrolled Shakespeare among the naive poets, those who are nature rather than seeking it, with Homer for company: the highest praise the system had to give, and a classification that quietly set the terms of the admiration. The naive poet is not to be imitated by a sentimental age, only studied as a lost wholeness. When Schiller took Shakespeare into the Weimar theatre, the trust showed its conditions: his adaptation of Macbeth, first performed at Weimar on 14 May 1800, cut and regularized the original for the classical stage, the improver's licence of Wieland's generation exercised by a greater writer. Weimar's way of owning a play was to correct it into the house style.

At Jena, a few hours away, the Schlegel circle proposed the opposite discipline: surrender to the work's own form, however irregular, and let criticism follow where the form leads. The irony of the geography is that the camps shared a journal. Schlegel's Romeo essay appeared in Schiller's own Die Horen, the Weimar organ, in the same years that carried Schiller's naive and sentimental argument, and the two texts can be read as a single debate about the Englishman conducted inside one periodical. Schiller's Shakespeare is a standard of nature set over modern writing, admirable and inimitable. Schlegel's Shakespeare is a colleague, whose procedures can be learned, whose mixture of comic and tragic is a technique rather than a mystery, and whose plays can therefore be rebuilt in German by a translator willing to submit to them line by line. The Veronese tragedy became the Jena Exhibit A precisely because it embarrasses the Weimar programme most: a work of sonnets and street brawls, botany and bawdy, that no house style could regularize without killing it, and that rewards the translator's submission with its whole design intact.

Goethe himself, the presiding figure of the Weimar camp, kept a lifelong double attitude that his two documents frame. The youth of 1771 had kept the festival and preached unbounded nature; the elder statesman of Weimar classicism directed a court theatre, staged the plays, and in Shakespeare und kein Ende in 1813 weighed them with a practitioner's scepticism about their fitness for the boards of his day. German criticism inherited both Goethes along with both camps. Its classicism gave the play institutional shelter, court stages, school syllabuses, the Gesellschaft in Goethe's own town. Its Romanticism gave the play its German voice and its reigning interpretation. The stations that follow are the long negotiation between the shelter and the voice, and the negotiation was never concluded, because each new German generation found it had to re-earn the play from one side or the other.

## 1797: Schlegel's Essay, Schlegel's Romeo and the Verse Restored

The decisive year of the play in Germany is 1797, and it is decisive twice over, because August Wilhelm Schlegel published two works in it that have never since been separated. In Schiller's journal Die Horen appeared his essay Ueber Shakespeare's Romeo und Julia, the first extended analysis of the play in any language that treated it as an achieved whole rather than a bundle of beauties and faults. In the same year began the nine volumes of his Shakespeare translations, and Romeo und Julia stood in the opening instalment. Criticism and translation were one act. The essay told German readers what the play was; the translation gave them the play itself, in German blank verse that kept the original's measure, its alternation of verse and prose, its rhymes where the original rhymes. Sixteen of Schlegel's versions appeared between 1797 and 1801, with Richard III following in 1810, and the enterprise re-founded the German text of the play on a principle Wieland and Eschenburg had not attempted: that the form is part of the meaning, and a translator who flattens the form has translated the story and lost the work.

The essay's method was the novelty. Where earlier criticism, German and English alike, had proceeded by extracting characters and sentiments, Schlegel tested the play as an organism, asking of each scene and each subordinate figure what office it performs in the total design. John Bligh, surveying Shakespearean character study, credits the essay with inaugurating a new era in the criticism of the plays and regrets that it passed into oblivion outside Germany; inside Germany it set the terms. The minor characters' qualities, on Schlegel's showing, are dictated by the whole: the Nurse's earthiness, Mercutio's brilliance, the Friar's worldly botany are not decoration but pressures in a system whose poles he named as love and hatred. The play's own arithmetic supports the polarity in a single line of Juliet's, spoken at the moment knowledge arrives: "My only love sprung from my only hate" (1.5.138). Schlegel's reading made that line the play's thesis, the two houses' enmity and the two lovers' passion as one force in two directions, and German criticism worked inside his polarity for a century and a half.

For the translation, the polarity had practical consequences. A prose Romeo could carry the story of the feud and the tomb; only verse could carry the lovers' progressive mastery of shared forms, the sonnet they compose between them at the feast, the exchanges in which Juliet out-reasons every elder in the play. Schlegel's German keeps the feast sonnet a sonnet, keeps the dawn parting an argument conducted in verse periods, and lets Juliet ask "What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other word would smell as sweet" (2.2.43-44) in lines whose balance survives the crossing. Wieland had given Germany the fable. Schlegel gave it the instrument.

### What did Schlegel's verse Romeo keep that Wieland's prose had flattened?

It kept the measures. Schlegel renders blank verse with blank verse, prose with prose and rhyme with rhyme, so the play's formal signals survive translation: the lovers' shared sonnet is still a sonnet, the servants still speak prose, and the verse contracts by which the tragedy thinks remain available to a German audience as evidence.

One more hand belongs in this station, and the documentary recovery of it is a model of what German philology could do. The canonical text of the Schlegel translations, Romeo und Julia among them, passed through the hands of the translator's wife, Caroline Schlegel. Hermann Conrad's study, published in the Deutsche Revue in 1911, examined the surviving manuscripts and demonstrated that Schlegel neither prepared the printer's copies of most plays nor read the proofs: Caroline made the fair copies from his drafts, chose among the variant readings he habitually left standing unmarked on the page, and entered improvements of her own, visible in her handwriting across the manuscripts. The exceptions Conrad found were the Henry VI plays and probably Richard III, prepared after the couple's separation in 1801. The finding does not diminish the translation; it corrects its mythology. The German Romeo, the text two centuries of readers would treat as a solitary genius's mirror of Shakespeare, was in part an editorial collaboration, and the woman who performed it went as uncredited in the enterprise as Dorothea Tieck would go in its completion. Modern scholarship's restoration of both names is among the tradition's honourable late acts, and it matters for this play above most: the balcony scene that Germany memorized reached print through a copyist's judgement calls.

Schlegel himself drew the large conclusion in the Vienna lectures a decade later, and German opinion drew it with him: that the plays were understood, in the full sense, only in Germany. Hare's 1820 introduction reports the assertion as a commonplace of German literary society and answers it with an Englishman's wry concession that England's reviewing culture had earned the slight. The assertion is the seed of the national claim this article must weigh at its proper station. Here it is enough to mark its birth date and its birth defect. It was born in 1797 to 1808, out of real achievements, an essay without parallel and a translation without rival. Its defect was to convert those achievements, which were acts of labour performed by identifiable people, into a national endowment, which required no labour at all.

## The Vienna Lectures and the Canonization of the Lovers

Schlegel delivered his Vorlesungen über dramatische Kunst und Literatur at Vienna in 1808, before an audience that included the diplomatic and literary society of the capital, and the published text of 1809 to 1811 became the constitution under which German criticism of the drama operated for generations. The course surveys the theatre of Europe from the Greeks forward, and its treatment of Shakespeare is its centre of gravity. When the lectures reach the Veronese tragedy, they gather the Horen essay's analysis into a single sustained figure. The play is presented as a picture of love and of love's mournful destiny in a world too rough for it: two beings formed for each other unite in secret amid hostile circumstances, are tried by blow upon blow, and in a voluntary death rejoin in and beyond the grave. Schlegel's paired formula for the achievement, the deification and the funeral procession of love, became the German tradition's most quoted sentence about the play, and the lecture presses the opposites together until they fuse: the sweetest and the most bitter, love and hatred, festivals and graves, fullness of life and self-destruction stand side by side, melted into one total impression whose residue in the mind he compares to a single endless sigh.

Beneath the rhapsody the lecture keeps the essay's engineering. Schlegel singles out the Friar's first entrance as the example of the poet laying his train long beforehand: the potion on which the catastrophe turns will be believed because Laurence is introduced gathering herbs and moralizing their double nature, the speech in which the same rind holds poison and medicine, "Within the infant rind of this small flower / Poison hath residence and medicine power" (2.3.23-24). The friar who can discourse so has earned the audience's credit for the sleeping draught two acts before he brews it. Design, not accident, is the lecture's refrain, and it answers in advance the objection, standard in the rules-bound criticism Lessing had fought, that the ending hangs on a missed letter and a mistimed waking. In the German account the accidents are real and subordinate: they operate inside a structure whose every pathos has been prepared, and the preparation is the meaning. The full anatomy of Schlegel's Romantic reading, its roots in the Jena circle and its quarrel with classical form, is traced in the companion study of [the German Romantics and Schlegel's reading](/2026/10/30/schlegel-german-romantics-reading/); what concerns the German tradition is what the lectures did at home. They enthroned an interpretation. Generations of German readers, schoolmasters and directors approached the play through Schlegel's polarity of love and hate and his verdict of organic unity, and approached it, overwhelmingly, in his words.

Enthronement has a price, and the German tradition paid it in attention. A reading that makes the play a song of praise and an elegy on love itself directs the ear to the lovers' lyric flights and away from the city's prose: the marriage negotiations in which Juliet is priced, the civic machinery of the Prince's edicts, the economics of the feud. The lovers in Schlegel's account barely inhabit a society; they inhabit a climate. Later German scholarship would have to recover the society from inside the poem Schlegel had canonized, and the recovery, when it came, came from the philologists and the sociologists rather than from the poets. It is the pattern of the whole German reception: each generation's gift becomes the next generation's obstruction, and the tradition advances by correcting its own classics. The lectures themselves became a classic of that kind, so completely identified with the German understanding of the play that to argue with the German Romeo, for a hundred years, was to argue with Schlegel twice over, once as critic and once as translator.

## Completing the Corpus: Tieck's Circle and the Unnamed Translators

Schlegel stopped. The sixteen plays of 1797 to 1801, with Richard III added in 1810, left the corpus open, and the task of closing it fell to his friend Ludwig Tieck, the Romantic writer whose own Shakespeare labours had run since his youth and whose long-promised critical work on the dramatist Hare was already awaiting in 1820. Tieck's editorship of the completed edition is the second foundation of the German text, and its history is a parable of Romantic production. The first volumes appeared from 1825; the publisher Georg Reimer, subscriptions sold and subscribers restive, watched the project stall; Schlegel refused to resume; Tieck, who in the event translated only Pericles himself, could not finish alone. The work restarted in 1830 and stood complete in 1833, because two further translators had joined it in near-anonymity.

They were Wolf Heinrich Graf von Baudissin, diplomat and man of letters, who translated twelve of the plays, and Tieck's daughter Dorothea. Her share, established by later scholarship, was large and choice: Timon of Athens, Cymbeline, Coriolanus, Macbeth, The Winter's Tale, The Two Gentlemen of Verona and the Sonnets. Romeo and Juliet is not among her plays for the plainest of reasons: Schlegel had translated it in 1797, before she was born. Tieck's prefaces referred to Dorothea only as his young friend, with the masculine form doing quiet work, and the title pages of the enterprise carried two names, Schlegel and Tieck. Baudissin's name was added to the title page after the first edition. Dorothea's never was, nor was Caroline Schlegel's, whose editorial hand in the founding volumes Conrad would document eighty years later. The corpus that Germany would call Schlegel-Tieck is thus a four-handed work wearing a two-man label, and the label itself became a critical fact: the unity of the brand taught readers to hear the whole canon in one voice, Schlegel's, and to judge the later, weaker versions by the standard of the founding plays.

### Why does the finished German Shakespeare carry only two names?

Because the brand outranked the record. Tieck announced two unnamed friends as his helpers and later named Baudissin alone; Dorothea Tieck, who translated seven plays and the Sonnets, was veiled as her father's young friend, and Caroline Schlegel's documented editorial work on the founding volumes was never announced. Later scholarship restored the names the title page withheld.

The editor himself deserves a paragraph in his critical capacity, because the completion's authority rested on a reputation the edition only partly shows. Tieck had lived with the English drama since his Göttingen youth; his Altenglisches Theater of 1811 gave German readers translations of Shakespeare's contemporaries; in 1817 he travelled to England to gather materials for a comprehensive work on Shakespeare that he never finished, and from 1825, settled at Dresden as adviser to the court theatre, his semi-public readings from the dramatic poets drew audiences from far beyond Saxony. The unfinished book is the German tradition's great might-have-been: the one Romantic besides Schlegel equipped to write its critical summa, whose Shakespeare wisdom survives dispersed in editions, prefaces and conversation. Delius's attack of 1846 drew its sting from exactly this eminence. If Tieck's criticism rested on translations and theatrical memory rather than on the edited text, then the whole Romantic settlement, the completion included, stood on ground the new philology intended to repossess. The completion and the critique are the same event seen from two sides: the corpus closed by the poets in 1833, and the bill for its authority presented by the scholars thirteen years later.

Rivalry tested the brand and confirmed it. Johann Heinrich Voss, the celebrated translator of Homer, with his sons Heinrich and Abraham, published a complete Shakespeare in nine volumes between 1818 and 1829, a version with its own claims to fidelity and its own poetic pedigree. It could not displace the Romantic text. The Cambridge History's judgement on the successors is blunt: the romantic translators had done their work so well that later productions, Voss's and even the handsome Bodenstedt edition of 1867 with its team of poets, could hold only a subordinate and supplementary value. The reasons were not sentimental. Schlegel's founding plays had arrived first, had been absorbed into the schools and the stage repertory, and had set the German ear's expectation of how the lovers should sound. A German Juliet of 1830 learned her part from Schlegel's lines as a matter of course, and the audience that wept at "My only love sprung from my only hate" wept in his cadence. Translation had become tradition, and tradition is the strongest of all critical arguments because it no longer presents itself as one.

The completed corpus changed the play's German situation in a subtler way as well. While Romeo and Juliet stood among sixteen famous versions it was a masterpiece among masterpieces; inside a complete national Shakespeare it became a component of an estate, administered by institutions. The Gesellschaft, the Jahrbuch and the universities took over from the poets, and the next station of the tradition is the story of that administration: what the philologists kept, what they corrected, and what their keeping cost.

## Philology Takes Possession: Ulrici, Gervinus, Delius and the Weimar Society

The generation after the completion set about converting enthusiasm into knowledge. Hermann Ulrici's Shakspeare's dramatische Kunst of 1839, Englished in 1846 as Shakespeare's Dramatic Art: And His Relation to Calderon and Goethe, read the canon through a Hegelian instrument: each play embodies a governing idea, and criticism's task is to trace the idea's working through character and structure. Applied across the century by Ulrici's many followers, the method gave German readings of the Veronese tragedy a characteristic cast. The lovers ceased to be merely Schlegel's lyric absolutes and became the bearers of a proposition, love's claim against the world's arrangements, with the feud demoted to the proposition's resistant medium. Georg Gottfried Gervinus, whose four-volume Shakespeare appeared in 1849 and 1850, worked the same ground with a historian's conscience, deducing the moral law of each action and reading the plays as documents of ethical life. It was Gervinus who set down the sentence the national claim would quote for a century: Shakespeare, he wrote, has become a German poet almost more than any of our native writers. The sentence deserves its fame and its scrutiny. In 1849 it reported a fact of saturation, the plays naturalized by translation, stage and school. Read backward from 1914 it looks like a title deed. The distance between those two readings is the distance this tradition travelled, and Gervinus, a liberal historian of unimpeachable seriousness, belongs to the first.

The editor arrived with Nikolaus Delius of Bonn. Delius had announced his programme early and combatively in Die Tieck'sche Shakespearekritik of 1846, an examination of Tieck's criticism that found it wanting in the one virtue the new age demanded: knowledge of the text. Tieck had written about Shakespeare for decades with a poet's authority, Delius argued in substance, on the basis of translations and adaptations, while the original words went unexamined. Between 1854 and 1860 Delius published at Elberfeld his edition of the works in eight volumes, the English text established from the early editions with German introductions and commentary, an enterprise that earned him the first chair of English studies at a German university and that English editors themselves drew upon when the Leopold Shakespeare took his text for its copy. For the German life of Romeo and Juliet the edition marks the moment the tradition acquired a second foundation beside Schlegel's. The stage and the schoolroom would go on speaking the translation; the study now possessed the original, edited at Bonn with a rigour the Romantics had not attempted, and every later German argument about the play's language could be conducted on evidence.

### What made Delius's Elberfeld edition a different kind of book from the Schlegel-Tieck volumes?

The Schlegel-Tieck volumes give a German poem, a translation that reads as literature in its own right. Delius gives the English text itself, established from the quartos and the Folio and surrounded by German introductions and notes. The one book is the play naturalized; the other is the play documented, and German criticism after 1860 argues from the second.

The institutions followed the edition as the edition had followed the translation. On 23 April 1864, the tercentenary of the playwright's birth, the Deutsche Shakespeare-Gesellschaft was founded at Weimar, the first society of its kind anywhere and among the oldest literary societies in continuous existence. Its Shakespeare-Jahrbuch began in 1865 and made German Shakespeare study a periodical discipline, bibliographies, theatre reports and essays between the same covers year after year. A study of the Jahrbuch's first fifty years finds the expected preoccupations of the age in its pages: psychological character study pursued at times to unreal extremes, a standing concern with the plays' moral and educational value, and contrasts between England and Germany coloured by the political weather. The Society also took charge of the text it celebrated, issuing in 1867 a carefully revised Schlegel-Tieck under Ulrici's supervision, so that the canonical translation passed into institutional custody. Shakespeare had become, as the celebrants liked to put it, the third classic of the German shelf beside Goethe and Schiller; in 1904 the Gesellschaft set the claim in stone with Otto Lessing's monument in Weimar's Ilm park, a few minutes' walk from the houses of the other two.

For this play the institutional century had a double effect. It secured the text, the canon and the audience beyond any other country's provision, and it embalmed the reigning interpretation. A school tradition that teaches the lovers through Schlegel's polarity, in Schlegel's lines, under a society founded to honour the achievement, does not easily produce a heretic. The heresies, when they came, came from the universities the system itself had built. But before the correction, the claim reached its maximum, and the maximum must be faced on its own ground.

### What did the Weimar monument of 1904 add to the argument?

It moved the claim from print to topography. A journal can assert that Shakespeare belongs beside Goethe and Schiller; a monument in the Ilm park, raised by the Gesellschaft in the town where German classics lived and worked, installs him in their company in bronze and public space, where the assertion needs no argument, only presence.

## The Stage Under Reverence: The Play in the German Repertory

Between the philologists' desks and the readers' shelves stood the stage, and the German stage's relation to the play across the long nineteenth century was one of possession by inheritance. The Schlegel-Tieck text became the acting text, as it had become the reading text, and the frequency of German Shakespeare performance in the period outran England's own, a fact the Cambridge libraries' survey of the translations states without surprise. Ferdinand Freiligrath's poem of 1844, opening with the line "Deutschland ist Hamlet", belongs to a neighbouring play, but it registers the climate in which every German Romeo grew up: the dramatist had been absorbed into the nation's account of itself, so that a German audience met the Veronese lovers as figures of its own inner life, doubled and doomed like the nation of the poem. The balcony speech needed no cultural translation. "O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?" (2.2.33) asked, in German mouths, the German question of the century: why does the name a person is born to govern the life the person might choose?

Reverence, however, is a preservative with side effects, and the theatre historians of the Weimar Republic record them plainly. In the decades before the First World War, German Shakespeare production manifested an awed reverence toward the text, with actors, directors and designers regarding the playwright in the same light as Goethe and Schiller; the plays were national liturgy before they were theatre. The experimental energy of the Weimar years in German Shakespeare staging was a direct reaction against that liturgy, an attempt to recover the plays as events rather than monuments. The reaction matters for the criticism this article traces because it is the stage's version of the scholars' correction, arriving in the same decades: Schücking's demand for history over rapture in 1919, Rothe's demand for speakable lines in the theatre, and the directors' demand for a Shakespeare who could survive contact with a modern stage all belong to one movement. The German tradition, having made the play a classic, had to rescue it from the classic's fate, and the rescue was conducted, characteristically, by Germans arguing with the German inheritance.

## The Play in the Jahrbuch: Fifty Years of German Homework

What the institutional century actually wrote, year by year, can be sampled in the house journal, and a Birmingham thesis that surveyed the Shakespeare-Jahrbuch from 1865 to 1914 gives the sample a shape. The journal mixed pure scholarship with general criticism, bibliographies with theatre reports, and its habits set the terms in which the play was discussed in German for two generations. Three habits stand out in the survey. Political interpretation tracked the political weather with embarrassing fidelity: contributors drew parallels between Elizabethan England and Bismarck's Germany, and the contrasts the journal loved best, between England and Germany, were coloured by ideas of a Nordic culture that both nations were invited to share. Humanistic readings of the dramatist's religion bent toward German religious attitudes of the day. And the psychological character study, the Romantic inheritance in academic dress, was pursued with a thoroughness the survey judges sometimes unreal, lovers and friars furnished with inner biographies no text had supplied.

The survey's findings convict the Jahrbuch decades of the besetting German sin this article has tracked, the conversion of the plays into mirrors for the nation's self-image, and they should be allowed their full force. A journal that reads Verona as a parable of Bismarckian order has stopped reading Verona. But the same volumes performed the unglamorous labour on which all later correction depended: the annual bibliography of German Shakespeare writing, the catalogues of the Gesellschaft's library printed in its pages, the theatre reports that preserved the record of the German stage's encounters with the text. When Schücking demanded history in 1919, the archive he demanded it from existed because the Jahrbuch had been keeping it for fifty years. The institutional temper is like that throughout this story: it embalms the interpretation and preserves the evidence, and the evidence outlives the embalming. Romeo and Juliet in the Jahrbuch years is a play at once over-explained and perfectly documented, waiting for readers with better questions. The twentieth century supplied them, from inside the same institutions, which is the strongest argument the German tradition can make for its own machinery: it built the means of its correction into the means of its custody.

## A Classic in the Schoolroom: The Canon as Daily Bread

Possession on this scale is finally a matter of distribution, and the German machinery of distribution deserves its own station. The Gesellschaft's revised Schlegel-Tieck of 1867 put the canonical translation under institutional imprint; the cheap reprint series that defined German reading habits did the rest. Reclam's Universal-Bibliothek, the yellow booklets sold at railway stalls for a few pfennigs, issued Schlegel's Romeo und Julia in 1876, and the play entered the satchels of generations of school pupils in the same format as Goethe's Faust and Schiller's Wallenstein. The Jahrbuch's early volumes, as the Birmingham study of its criticism notes, returned persistently to the plays' moral and educational value, and the concern was not decorative. A society that believes a foreign dramatist forms its nation's youth will build schools around him, and the German schoolroom became the treaty's most durable enforcement: the lovers' German was memorized, parsed and examined until Schlegel's cadences were indistinguishable, for most readers, from the play itself.

The translation's own canonization can be dated by its scholarship. When Rudolf Genée published A. W. Schlegel und Shakespeare in 1903, a full-length appraisal of the translations as works of German literature, the versions had crossed the threshold that separates a rendering from a classic: they had become objects of study in themselves, with their own textual history, their own variants and their own defenders. No other nation's Shakespeare translation had acquired that status by that date, and the fact reframes the Rothe quarrel before it begins. What the twentieth-century theatres rebelled against was not a crib but a monument of German prose and verse, and the rebellion's violence, which Engler records, measures the monument's weight. The schoolroom explains the other half of the German pattern, its conservatism of feeling. Generations who met Juliet first at a desk, in Schlegel's lines, under a teacher's exposition of Schlegel's polarity, carried the reading as part of their formation, and a criticism formed at school is the hardest kind to argue with, because it presents itself not as an interpretation but as the memory of the work.

There is a cost ledger here too, and this article enters it plainly. The same machinery that put the play in every satchel fixed a single authorized encounter with it: one translation, one polarity, one set of examination answers. The German schoolroom's Romeo is Schlegel's Romeo at two removes, and the distance shows in the tradition's recurring surprise whenever the play's prose world, its bawdy, its economics and its civic violence, is pointed out as if new. The recoveries mounted by Schücking's generation and by the imagery scholars were necessary precisely because the canon had done its work so well. A tradition that teaches a play perfectly teaches one play; the German archive is unusual in preserving, beside the taught version, the documents of every earlier and rival version, from the clown's manuscript to Weiße's reworking, so that the audit this article performs can be performed at all.

## The German Claim Weighed: Gundolf and the Rhetoric of Kinship

Every tradition tells a lie about itself at its most confident moment, and the German tradition's confident moment came in two waves. The first was institutional: at the 1864 founding celebrations it was proclaimed that Shakespeare was not a foreign poet, but one whom England must share with Germany on account of his inborn Germanic nature. The second was intellectual: in 1911 Friedrich Gundolf, Stefan George's disciple and the most brilliant German literary scholar of his generation, published his Habilitation, Shakespeare und der deutsche Geist, arguing that the affinity binding Shakespeare to German culture is at least as great as, and perhaps greater than, the affinity binding him to his own country. Gundolf's book is a work of real learning about the reception, the very book a historian of the German stations must quarry, and its thesis is the national claim raised to philosophy: not that Germany had worked hardest on the plays, but that the plays' spirit was German by nature and had come home.

The distinction is the whole case, and this article adjudicates it against Gundolf. As a claim about labour, the German assertion is unanswerable, and the stations of this article are its evidence: the earliest sustained criticism, the model translation, the complete corpus, the first society, the foundational edition, the yearbook's unbroken run. As a claim about nature it dissolves under its own archive. The play reached Germany through English travelling players and a manuscript with a clown in it; its heroine's German voice was written by a Saxon professor and copy-edited by his wife; its canonization was proclaimed at Weimar by men who knew they were performing an act of adoption. Nothing in that record is ancestral. It is biographical, the biography of a culture choosing a writer and then earning the choice. Gundolf, whose own works the Nazi regime would ban in 1933 on account of his Jewish descent, stands as the tradition's warning against its favourite metaphor: a criticism that grounds possession in spirit and blood will find the ground repossessed by worse hands. The corrective was already available inside his own evidence, and the scholars who came after him took the reception history he had assembled and discarded the metaphysics he had wrapped it in. Paulin's modern history of the same ground reaches Gervinus's conclusion by the labour route alone.

Gundolf's formation explains the thesis's shape. He had joined Stefan George's circle in 1899 and edited its Jahrbuch für die geistige Bewegung in 1910 and 1911, preaching the circle's cultural politics: history as the succession of great formative spirits, nations as vessels of a Geist that elects its bearers. A criticism formed in that school does not ask what a culture did with a writer; it asks what spirit the writer and the culture share, and it finds, predictably, kinship. Shakespeare und der deutsche Geist is the George programme applied to the reception this article has documented, and its persuasiveness in 1911, three years before the war with England, is part of its evidence: the argument told an educated nation, anxious about its standing between French civilization and English commerce, that its most beloved classic was its own by nature. The stations tell a plainer and better story, in which the beloved classic is its own by work. Gundolf assembled much of the material for the plainer story himself, which is why his book remains on the shelf of everyone who studies the German reception, read for its archive and resisted for its moral.

The comparative evidence sharpens the adjudication. France, whose classical stage the German campaign defined itself against, hissed English actors off a Paris stage in 1822 and naturalized the plays only partially and intellectually; the tangled, brilliant French negotiation is traced in the companion study of [the play in French criticism](/2027/05/14/french-criticism-romeo-and-juliet/). Russia received the dramatist substantially through German mediation, Karamzin rendering Julius Caesar in 1786 from Eschenburg's German prose, and built its own possession later and by other means, as the companion study of [the play in Russian criticism](/2027/05/28/russian-criticism-romeo-and-juliet/) sets out. Germany's distinction in the comparison is neither blood nor even priority of love. It is continuity of work: no other culture maintained, across two centuries and every political catastrophe, an unbroken institutional attention to the plays, translating, editing, staging, teaching and arguing without intermission. Continuity is a fact. Kinship is a metaphor. The tradition is great in proportion as it rests on the fact.

## Correction From Within: Schücking, Rothe, Clemen and the Postwar Rebuilding

The correction began in the universities. Levin Ludwig Schücking, professor at Leipzig and the dominant German Anglicist of his time, published Die Charakterprobleme bei Shakespeare in 1919, Englished in 1922 as Character Problems in Shakespeare's Plays, with a programme aimed directly at the Romantic inheritance: against the subjective character analysis that treated the dramatis personae as souls to be psychoanalysed at leisure, Schücking demanded the conventions of the Elizabethan stage and the probable attitudes of the original audience as the controls on interpretation. His Sociology of Literary Taste followed in 1923. For the Veronese tragedy the programme cuts at Schlegel's root. A criticism that begins from the first audience's conventions asks what a London crowd made of a secret marriage, a friar's plot and a double suicide, and the lovers it recovers are less like lyric absolutes and more like figures in a legible social machine. Schücking overshot, as correctors do: convention-knowledge explains the frame and cannot by itself account for the balcony scene's surplus of life, the sense, which Schlegel named better than anyone, of a poem that exceeds its machinery. But the direction of German study after him is his. The rhapsodic mode survived in the schools; the universities went historical, and the play's German criticism divided into the two tempers this article has tracked from the start, the players' heirs and the scholars', now wearing academic dress.

The theatre's correction was louder. Balz Engler's survey of German Shakespeare translation describes the first half of the twentieth century as an argument about the merits and defects of the Schlegel-Tieck version, and its loudest voice was Hans Rothe, whose new translations and critical writings attacked the canonical text for its distance from the world of the theatre. The theatres, Engler records, had begun to tire of the Schlegel-Tieck language, which belonged to another age and represented the bourgeois educational tradition from which the stage wished to break. Rothe's objection, applied to this play, has force: a Romeo whose German breathes the air of 1800 asks a modern company to perform an heirloom. His remedy, wholesale replacement by versions cut for theatrical effect, has less. The adjudication the tradition itself reached was a partition. Schlegel-Tieck kept the reading public, the school and the prestige stage, while new versions multiplied for companies that wanted them, and the argument, which Engler shows still running at full heat in the 1970s, did the tradition the service of proving that its central text was alive enough to fight about. Gundolf himself had belonged to the improvers' camp, labouring with Friedrich Schröder on revisions of the canonical text rather than replacements, a detail that usefully complicates his portrait: the philosopher of Germanic affinity spent his practical hours at the workbench of textual labour, where affinity counts for nothing and only the line matters.

Wolfgang Clemen supplied the correction's masterpiece. His Munich doctorate of 1936 took Shakespeare's images for its subject, and its revised English form, The Development of Shakespeare's Imagery of 1951, arranged the plays as a growth: the imagery of the early work iterative and decorative, laid on the surface of the verse, the imagery of the maturity fused with action and character. The book gives the Veronese tragedy its own chapter in the early sequence, and the placement is the argument. Where Schlegel had heard the play's image-world as a single fused impression, Clemen traced its figures developing inside the verse, light against dark, poison in the flower, the machinery of comparison learning its trade. Spurgeon's English census had counted the images; Clemen asked what the images were doing there and how their doing changed, and the question, German in its formation and exported in English dress, set the terms for imagery study everywhere. Clemen went on to help rebuild English studies in Germany after the war and in 1964 founded the Munich Shakespeare Library, among the greatest collections of Shakespeare scholarship outside Britain. The institution builder and the imagery scholar are the same figure the tradition kept producing, from Delius forward: the German contribution at its best is infrastructure, libraries, editions, yearbooks, within which readings can be checked.

The quarrel's long tail confirms the partition. Engler's survey names the camps of the mid-century argument: the improvers, Gundolf and Friedrich Schröder among them, who would revise the canonical text along lines Schlegel himself had suggested, and the wholesale retranslators, Richard Flatter and Rudolf Schaller the named examples, who would start again. Several onslaughts on the canonical version's strong position, Engler notes, were unsuccessful, and the unsuccessful onslaughts are data: a translation that survives fifty years of organized attack by the profession's best verse-makers has passed a test no committee can administer. The bilingual Studienausgabe was the quarrel's institutional child, born of the admission that the canonical poem needed a prose servant rather than a prose rival. German Romeo criticism in the twentieth century thus ends where its philology began, with the two-text settlement: Schlegel for the ear, the document for the mind, and an argument between them that has never needed to stop.

The postwar settlement formalized the partition between the poem and the document. The society itself divided with the divided country, and the western Gesellschaft sponsored the Englisch-deutsche Studienausgabe der Dramen Shakespeares, a bilingual study edition under the general editorship of Werner Habicht of Bonn, whose design Balz Engler explained to an international readership in 1974: the English text faced by a German prose rendering meant not to rival Schlegel but to gloss him, explaining every passage for the student. Ulrich Suerbaum's survey of the state of German Shakespeare translation in the Shakespeare-Jahrbuch West of 1972 took the Zwischenbilanz, the interim balance, of the whole quarrel. Taken together, Habicht's edition and Suerbaum's balance sheet mark the moment the German tradition stopped asking whether its Shakespeare was a classic and started asking, with an editor's humility, what its readers needed next. That is the temper in which Paulin wrote the reception's history in 2003 and reached the verdict this article adopted at its head. The broader recovery of the play's standing in the same century, its rise from apprentice work to major tragedy in the Anglophone account, is traced in the companion study of [the twentieth-century rehabilitation of the play](/2027/04/16/twentieth-century-rehabilitation/); the German case differs from it instructively, since Germany had never demoted the play in the first place and had instead to correct an excess of possession.

### What did the Englisch-deutsche Studienausgabe set out to give German readers?

A working text rather than a rival poem. Under Werner Habicht's general editorship for the western Shakespeare Society, the study edition prints the English play with a plain German prose rendering whose office is explanation: every passage glossed, the student carried through the original, and Schlegel's classic left standing as literature while the crib does the teaching.

## What German Criticism Distinctively Heard in the Play

Stand back from the stations and a profile emerges, five emphases that recur from the 1740s to the postwar rebuilding and that mark the German hearing of the play off from the French and English hearings of the same centuries. The first is form as fate. German criticism, from the elder Schlegel's weighing of beauties against crudities through the younger Schlegel's organic test to Brooke-like questions it asked a century before England did, treated the play's construction as its meaning: the mixed mode, the sonnet inside the feast, the friar's botany preparing the potion. Where French criticism heard a scandal of irregularity and English criticism of the same period heard a gallery of characters, the German ear heard an architecture, and the Veronese tragedy, whose plot is a machine of timings, rewarded the attention. The machine's smallest gear is a sentence of the Friar's about his plants, and it was a German who first pointed out, in 1797, that the gear had been cut in advance.

The second emphasis is the idea. Ulrici's Hegelian instrument and Gervinus's moral history taught German readers to ask what proposition a play enacts, and the habit, applied to this tragedy, produced the durable German framing of love against the world: the lovers as a value asserting itself against family, city and chance. The framing survives, secularized, wherever the play is still read as the collision of a feeling with an order, and its German origin is visible in its abstraction. The English tradition of the same decades preferred the lovers as persons; the German preferred them as a case. Both preferences distort, and the play is large enough to convict both, but the case-reading had one advantage the person-reading lacked: it kept the feud, the Prince and the marriage market inside the interpretation, as the proposition's necessary resistance, long after Schlegel's lyric climate had thinned them.

The third emphasis is character, and with it the tradition's most instructive internal quarrel. The Jahrbuch decades pursued psychological character study with a thoroughness the Birmingham survey calls at times unreal, inventing childhoods and complexes for the lovers in the Romantic manner. Schücking's correction of 1919, the demand for convention and the first audience's attitudes, was the German tradition disciplining its own excess with its own favourite instrument, history. The quarrel matters beyond Germany because it rehearsed, early and on German soil, the twentieth century's general argument about whether a dramatic figure is a person or a function. On this play the evidence supports a partition, and the German record contains both halves: the figures are functions in a machine of feud and timing, as the conventional reading shows, and they are imagined with a surplus that no function requires, as the balcony scene testifies whenever it is read aloud. "O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?" (2.2.33) is a heroine interrogating a name, which is a convention of the feud plot, and it is also, irreducibly, a voice thinking. German criticism's distinction is to have built the two strongest cases for the two halves and preserved both in its archive.

The fourth emphasis is the text itself. No other national tradition made the editing of the English original a domestic industry while its stage performed a translation: Delius at Elberfeld, the Gesellschaft's revisions, the Studienausgabe's bilingual patience. The doubleness is the German reception's signature and its health. A culture that only translates will mistake its version for the work, as Germany repeatedly did in its confident decades; a culture that only edits will lose the work's life in its own language. Germany did both, quarrelled about both, and left the quarrel documented, which is why its archive, rather than its slogans, is the model the rest of the world consults. The fifth emphasis follows from the fourth: development. Herder's historical sense, the Schlegels' organicism, Clemen's growth of imagery all belong to one instinct, to read the play as a living thing with a history inside itself and a history after itself. The instinct produced the tradition's best single book on the play's language and its most characteristic error, the conversion of history into destiny, of which Gundolf's affinity thesis is the monumental case. The error and the achievement share a root, and the German archive, characteristically, preserves the evidence for both.

## The Archive Opens: Modern Scholarship Reads the Reception Itself

The tradition's latest station is reflexive: German scholarship, and scholarship about Germany, has taken the reception itself for its object, and the play's German documents have begun to receive the editorial honours once reserved for the English text. The emblematic event is the return of the clown. Romio und Julieta, the wandering players' adaptation with which this article opened, appeared in 2020 in an edition and translation by Kareen Seidler and Lukas Erne in the Arden Shakespeare's Early Modern German Shakespeare project, supported by the Swiss National Science Foundation, with the German text published open access beside it. A seventeenth-century stage adaptation, dismissed by nineteenth-century scholars for its clown's want of taste, now stands in the most prestigious editorial series in Shakespeare studies, edited with the full apparatus of collation and commentary. The circle of the German reception closes with a click: the players' version, the improvers' versions and Schlegel's version are all, at last, documents in one archive, and the criticism that once ranked them by fidelity now studies them as a single phenomenon, the manufacture of a German Shakespeare across four centuries.

The institutions persist in the same temper. The Gesellschaft founded at Weimar in 1864 continues, and its Jahrbuch, published by Kröner of Stuttgart since 2019, runs as a peer-reviewed annual with thematic articles in German and English, reviews of the year's German Shakespeare productions and reports of the society's work. The theatre review is the telling survival: the journal still sends its critics to the German stages to record what the nation's directors are doing with the plays, as it has done since the 1860s, so that the archive of the German stage encounter grows by a volume a year. Paulin's history of the critical reception to 1914, published by Cambridge in 2003, gave the whole story its modern scholarly form, and the studies on which this article has drawn, Conrad on Caroline's manuscripts, Westlake on the Jahrbuch, Engler on the translation quarrel, are themselves products of the archive's openness. A tradition confident enough to publish its own receipts has nothing left to fear from its slogans. The German Romeo of the modern scholarship is not a possession but a case file, complete, dated and cross-referenced, and the case file is the tradition's final answer to Gundolf: what Germany made of the play can be shown, item by item, and what can be shown needs no spirit to explain it.

The openness is measurable in the tradition's own instruments. The Jahrbuch's annual bibliography of Shakespeare writing, a fixture since the journal's first decades, turned the German production of criticism into a census any scholar could check; the catalogues of the Gesellschaft's library, printed in the same volumes, did the same for the society's shelves. A modern historian auditing the tradition therefore works from the tradition's own ledgers, which is why Paulin's history can be as unsparing as it is precise: the receipts for the failures survive beside the receipts for the triumphs, the proclamation of 1864 filed next to the editions it celebrated. For this play the audit trail is unusually complete, since every temper the tradition passed through left a Romeo document behind it, the clown's script for the players' age, Weiße's reworking for the improvers, Schlegel's verse for the restorers, Delius's apparatus for the philologists, Rothe's versions for the quarrel and the Studienausgabe for the settlement. Six documents, six Germanys, one story from Verona. Few foreign plays in any literature can be re-performed, in their second language, as a documentary sequence of that kind, and the possibility is itself the tradition's achievement: Germany did not merely keep the play, it kept the evidence of having kept it.

No other national reception of the play shows the alternation so cleanly, because no other nation built so much of its literary self-consciousness on a foreign dramatist and then kept the receipts.

## Verdict: What German Criticism Made of the Play

The shape of the German reception is a long act of adoption, performed in four tempers. The wandering players adopted the story and felt free to rebuild it. The Enlightenment critics adopted the author as a weapon against French rules and felt free to prune him. Schlegel adopted the play as a poem and rebuilt it so faithfully that the rebuilding became the German original. The institutions adopted the whole estate, translation, text and interpretation together, and administered it for a century, until the tradition's own scholars audited the administration and recovered the difference between the play and its German monument. Each temper left the play larger than it found it, and each left a characteristic blindness for the next temper to correct: the players lost the verse, the improvers lost the irregularities, Schlegel lost the city, the institutions lost the heretics, and the correctors, Schücking at their head, risked losing the rapture that had made the enterprise worth conducting.

On the brief's standing complication, the claim of Shakespeare as a German classic, the verdict of this article is a partition. The claim is true as a statement about cultural fact, attested by Paulin's history and by the timeline above: by the measures of translation, edition, performance, schooling and organized study, the dramatist functioned in German culture as a classic of that culture, third on the shelf the celebrants themselves described. The claim is false as a statement about essence, wherever it appears, in the tercentenary's proclamation of an inborn Germanic nature or in Gundolf's philosophy of affinity, because the documentary record shows adoption all the way down: English actors, a Saxon translator, his wife's fair copies, a count and a professor's daughter completing the canon uncredited, a Bonn philologist editing the English text. Germany's Romeo and Juliet is not a birthright recovered. It is a masterpiece of sustained attention, and it is worth more, critically and morally, as the second thing than as the first.

The case carries a methodological moral for any reader of reception history, and this article states it as its closing claim. A national tradition's talk about a foreign masterpiece is evidence about the nation, not about the masterpiece's blood, and it becomes criticism only when it can be cashed in documents: dated translations, named translators, printed editions, founded societies, kept yearbooks. By that test the German talk about this play is the richest criticism any nation produced about it outside England, because the German paper trail is the longest and the most honestly preserved, receipts and manifestos together. The cliché the series brief sets against the record assumes an English play with foreign admirers. The German archive shows something else: a play that lived a second, fully documented life in another language, acquiring there a second text, a second criticism and a second set of institutions, without ceasing to be the first thing. Romeo and Juliet has two homes. The German one has better records.

What German criticism distinctively emphasized, across all four tempers, was wholeness. The players assumed the story whole enough to survive their cuts; Schlegel proved the poem whole by his organic test; Ulrici and Gervinus sought the governing idea that makes the whole intelligible; Delius secured the whole text; the Gesellschaft kept the whole canon in print and in repertory; Clemen traced the whole development of its image-life. The final Chorus asks for the story to be heard entire, "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3.309-310), and no national criticism took that instruction closer to the letter than the German. Its reward is the strongest foreign afterlife the play possesses. Its warning is written in its own archive: wholeness, administered too long, becomes custody, and custody must periodically be broken by the tradition's own hands, as Schücking and Rothe and Clemen broke it, for the play to go on living in the language that adopted it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch German timeline, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch German timeline is the table in this article listing the dated stations of the play's German reception, from Morhof's first printed mention of Shakespeare in 1682 to the bilingual Studienausgabe of 1974, each with the change the station made to the play's German life. The pattern it exposes is an alternation of making and keeping. A century of players, adapters and prose translators made the tragedy performable and readable in German; the single year 1797 remade it definitively in Schlegel's essay and verse translation; a century of societies, editions and yearbooks then kept the remaking in print, in school and on stage; and a final century of critics, translators and editors audited the keeping and corrected it. The timeline's argument is that the German distinction lies in the continuity of the record, station succeeding station without intermission across wars and regime changes, and that every claim made for the tradition, including the claim of national possession, can be tested against the dated entries.

### Q: When did Shakespeare's name first enter German print, and in what company?

The name first entered German print in 1682, in Daniel Georg Morhof's Unterricht von der Teutschen Sprache und Poesie, where Shakespeare appears in a list of English poets, mentioned in the train of discussion of Dryden's dramatic theory. The company matters more than the date. The playwright arrived in German letters as a catalogue entry inside a poetics handbook, known by report rather than by reading, one name among several English names in a literature whose own drama was being rebuilt on French models. For two generations the name circulated in that condition, cited without acquaintance, while the Leipzig school of Gottsched treated the English stage as a warning. The gap between the mention of 1682 and the first sustained criticism in the 1740s is the measure of the German delay, and it throws the later German intensity into relief: the culture that was slowest to read the plays became, within a century and a half, the culture that organized their study most completely.

### Q: Who wrote the first sustained German criticism of Shakespeare, and whom did he set beside him?

Johann Elias Schlegel wrote it, in his Vergleichung Shakespears und Andreas Gryphs of the 1740s, and he set Shakespeare beside Andreas Gryphius, the Silesian dramatist of the previous century whom German letters counted its classical tragedian. The comparison was a rehabilitation in form. By measuring the Englishman against a national classic rather than against French rules, the elder Schlegel could concede the charges of crudity and obscurity while arguing that the command of character placed Shakespeare with the ancients as a model for admiration. The family sequel gives the essay its piquancy: the critic was the uncle of August Wilhelm and Friedrich Schlegel, so that German Shakespeare criticism begins inside one family's two generations, the uncle weighing the dramatist against Gryphius in the 1740s and the nephew, fifty years later, rebuilding the dramatist's German text and supplying the reading that ruled for a century. Between the uncle's comparison and the nephew's translation lies the whole first act of the German reception.

### Q: What did Wieland's prose versions omit, and what did the omissions cost this play?

Wieland omitted whatever offended or puzzled him, cutting passages he judged coarse and smoothing passages he judged obscure, and he rendered the whole in prose, so that the Germany of 1762 to 1766 read a Shakespeare pruned by the taste the plays were busy refuting. For this play the cost was formal and therefore structural. The tragedy thinks in verse contracts: the sonnet the lovers compose between them at the feast, the rhymed exchanges of the balcony scene, the prose of servants and Nurse set against the verse of their betters. Prose dissolved the skeleton. What survived was a striking story with striking speeches, actable and readable, in which the difference between the lovers' shared forms and the city's noise could no longer be heard. Eschenburg's revision of 1775 to 1782 restored accuracy without restoring measure. The verse, and with it the play's way of meaning, waited for Schlegel in 1797, whose founding principle was that the form is part of the sense.

### Q: Why did Weiße's Romeo und Julia, rather than any translation, rule the German stage for two generations?

Because Weiße gave the German stage the play it was equipped to want. His Romeo und Julia of 1767, built heavily on Garrick's English adaptation and claimed by its author as an original work, recast the tragedy as a bourgeois drama for the new national theatre movement: sentiment centred, irregularities disciplined, the action fitted to companies and audiences formed by the prose decades. Scholars of its circulation describe it as the most successful German play of the eighteenth century; it toured from the capitals to Hungary and Transylvania, persisted in the provinces into the 1820s, crossed into French in Mercier's translation and reached Moscow in the 1790s. A faithful text could not have done that work in 1767, because no faithful German text existed and no audience had been trained for one. Weiße's rule ended only when Schlegel's verse gave actors a better instrument and the Romantic campaign gave audiences a new expectation, and even then the reworking lingered in the provinces long after the capitals had converted.

### Q: Why does the Benda and Gotter Singspiel of 1776 let the lovers live?

Because the operatic convention of the lieto fine, the happy ending, governed the Singspiel stage, and Friedrich Wilhelm Gotter's libretto obeyed it. His Romeo und Julie, set by Georg Benda and first performed at the Ekhof Theatre in Gotha on 25 September 1776, strips out most of the comedy, discards Mercutio's world, makes Julie the strongest figure in the piece, and rescues her in the vault: she wakes before Romeo's dagger falls, and the work closes on the lovers' duet of joy. The ending is not a failure of nerve but a genre's contract, the same contract that had governed operatic treatments of tragic fable across Europe. The version held the stages of the Habsburg lands until Bellini's opera displaced it in 1836, and its music lived inside the German Shakespeare story at the highest level: Caroline Schlegel's letters record the role of Juliet from Gotter's opera sung nearly entire in a Jena house in 1797, while her husband's verse translation, in which the lovers die on schedule, was in the press.

### Q: What did Goethe proclaim on 14 October 1771, and what did the day declare?

Goethe proclaimed the day a Shakespeare festival. At Frankfurt on 14 October 1771 the twenty-two-year-old organized a private jubilee for the English dramatist, declared the date William's day, and delivered the speech Zum Schäkespears Tag, hailing a theatre of unbounded nature against the prison of the French rules. The day declared a changing of the guard in German letters. A generation formed on Gottsched's French foundations announced, in festival form, its transfer of allegiance to the northern genius Herder was about to theorize, and it announced the transfer socially, as a feast among friends rather than a treatise among scholars. The form stuck. German Shakespeare devotion kept the festival habit for two centuries: the tercentenary celebrations of 1864, the founding of the Gesellschaft at Weimar on the birthday itself, the monument of 1904. Goethe's own judgement cooled with age, and his essay of 1813 weighs the plays with a classicist's reservations, but the Frankfurt day fixed the social shape of the German claim before the claim had any institutions.

### Q: What did Eschenburg add to Wieland's Shakespeare between 1775 and 1782?

Eschenburg added completeness and correctness. Johann Joachim Eschenburg revised Wieland's twenty-two plays, emended their text against the English, and supplied the remainder of the canon, so that by 1782 German readers possessed the whole dramatic works in a uniform prose version of respectable accuracy. The addition was infrastructural rather than poetic, and its importance is easy to miss. Schröder's Hamburg company, the model stage of the era, worked from Eschenburg's texts; professors lectured from them; the reading public's Shakespeare for a generation was Eschenburg's Shakespeare. Prose accuracy, however, entrenched the prose condition: the standard text of the German Enlightenment confirmed audiences in hearing the plays as dramas of situation and sentiment whose verse was an English accident. When Schlegel's verse versions began to appear in 1797, they were therefore not competing with an absence but displacing an incumbent, and the completeness of Eschenburg's achievement measures the scale of Schlegel's. The incumbent had given Germany the plays entire, and the challenger still won, on form.

### Q: What evidence did Hermann Conrad publish in 1911 about Caroline Schlegel's hand in the translations?

Conrad published the manuscript evidence that Caroline Schlegel, the translator's wife, had performed the editorial labour of the canonical versions. Examining the surviving manuscripts for the Deutsche Revue in 1911, he showed that for most plays August Wilhelm Schlegel neither prepared the printer's copy nor read the proofs: Caroline made the fair copies from his drafts, chose among the variant readings he habitually left standing unmarked on the page, and entered improvements of her own, recognizable in her handwriting across the manuscripts of the plays he studied. The exceptions were the Henry VI plays and probably Richard III, prepared after the couple's separation in 1801. The finding changed the translation's mythology without diminishing its quality. The German Romeo und Julia, treated for a century as one man's mirror of Shakespeare, reached print through two judgements, and the second judgement belonged to a woman the title page never named, an erasure the completion of the corpus would repeat with Dorothea Tieck.

### Q: Why did the Schlegel-Tieck Romeo keep its prestige after the Voss family's rival version appeared?

Because the Voss version arrived after the position had closed. Johann Heinrich Voss, the Homer translator, with his sons published a complete Shakespeare in nine volumes between 1818 and 1829, a rendering with its own fidelity and its own pedigree, and it could not displace the Romantic text in school, study or stage repertory. The Cambridge History's verdict on all such successors is that the romantic translators had done their work so well that later productions could hold only a subordinate and supplementary value. Priority explains part of the resistance and saturation the rest: Schlegel's founding plays had been absorbed into curricula, acting editions and the reading memory of two generations, so that a German audience's inner ear for the lovers' voices was tuned to his cadences. A rival translation was no longer competing with a book but with a formation. The prestige was tested again by Bodenstedt's handsome edition of 1867 and by the twentieth century's organized attacks, and the pattern held: the canonical Romeo could be supplemented, glossed and fought, but not replaced.

### Q: Which plays did Dorothea Tieck translate for the completed edition, and why is this play not among them?

Dorothea Tieck translated Timon of Athens, Cymbeline, Coriolanus, Macbeth, The Winter's Tale and The Two Gentlemen of Verona, together with the Sonnets, for the edition her father completed between 1825 and 1833. Romeo and Juliet is not among them because August Wilhelm Schlegel had translated it in 1797, in the founding instalment of his versions, before Dorothea was born. Her share of the corpus is nonetheless among its distinctions: her Macbeth became the most admired of the completion's versions and has been republished on its own, and readers from Marx onward quoted her Timon. The title pages tell a poorer story. Tieck referred to his daughter only as his young friend, in the masculine form; Baudissin's name was added to the title page after the first edition; Dorothea's never was, and her later translation of Cervantes appeared under her father's name as well. The corpus Germany calls Schlegel-Tieck is a four-handed work, and the recovery of the two women's names, hers and Caroline Schlegel's, is the modern scholarship's amendment to its record.

### Q: What did Gervinus mean by calling Shakespeare a German poet in 1849?

Gervinus meant that naturalization had done its work. In the Shakespeare volumes of 1849 and 1850, the historian Georg Gottfried Gervinus wrote that the dramatist had become a German poet almost more than any of the native writers, and the sentence, in its own moment, reported saturation rather than ancestry: the plays, in Schlegel's German, filled the theatres, the schools and the common reader's shelf more completely than the works of most German authors. Gervinus was a liberal historian conducting a moral and historical reading of the canon, and his sentence belongs to his method, a judgement about cultural function. Posterity misread it as a title deed. Detached from 1849 and quoted through the decades of the national claim, the observation hardened into a doctrine of ownership that Gervinus's own seriousness would not have signed. The article's adjudication keeps the two apart: as a report on what translation and institution had achieved by mid-century, the sentence is accurate and important; as a claim about what Shakespeare is, it confuses the history of a reception with the nature of its object.

### Q: Why was Weimar, rather than Berlin, the city where the Shakespeare Society was founded in 1864?

Because Weimar was the claim's home address. The Deutsche Shakespeare-Gesellschaft was founded there on 23 April 1864, the tercentenary of the birth, in the small Thuringian capital where Goethe and Schiller had lived and worked, where Goethe had directed the court theatre and Schiller's Macbeth adaptation had been staged, and where the German classical heritage was administered as a civic cult. Berlin had the larger universities and the royal theatres, but Weimar had the symbolism the founders required: the foreign dramatist was to be enrolled in the national pantheon at the pantheon's own seat, a third classic beside the two whose houses stood in the same streets. The choice also fitted the society's scholarly temper; Nikolaus Delius, a co-founder and for years its chairman, gave the enterprise its philological programme from Bonn, while Weimar supplied the aura. The sequel kept the logic: the Jahrbuch began in 1865, the revised Schlegel-Tieck followed under the society's imprint in 1867, and in 1904 Otto Lessing's monument rose in the Ilm park, minutes from the Goethe and Schiller houses.

### Q: What exactly did the tercentenary speakers claim about Shakespeare's nature in 1864?

They claimed that he was not foreign at all. In the formulation preserved from the founding celebrations, Shakespeare was proclaimed not a foreign poet but one whom England must share with Germany on account of his inborn Germanic nature. The sentence deserves exact attention because it marks the moment the labour claim changed into a blood claim. Everything the German tradition had actually done by 1864, the translation, the editions in preparation, the stages and schools, was an achievement of work, performed by named people over a documented century. The proclamation reassigned the credit to descent: the plays belonged in Germany because their author's spirit was Germanic before it was English. No evidence was offered, because the claim's function was not evidentiary but ceremonial, the consecration of the new society's project. This article's verdict follows the documents against the ceremony. The proclamation is a fact of the reception and belongs in its history; as a proposition about a Stratford glover's son it is empty, and the tradition's later embarrassments, Gundolf's philosophy of affinity at their head, are the proclamation's children.

### Q: What did Gundolf argue about Shakespeare and the German spirit in 1911?

Friedrich Gundolf argued that the affinity between Shakespeare and German culture is at least as great as, and perhaps greater than, the affinity between Shakespeare and his own country. Shakespeare und der deutsche Geist, his Habilitation of 1911, written out of Stefan George's circle, whose Jahrbuch für die geistige Bewegung he had edited in 1910 and 1911, recast the documented reception as a meeting of spirits: the German culture that had translated, staged and studied the plays for a century and a half had done so, on his account, because the plays' Geist was German in essence and recognized its kin. The book is learned, and its assembly of the reception's materials remains useful; its thesis inverts its own evidence. The record Gundolf himself quarried shows adoption by labour, not recognition by blood, and the century after him, in which his own works were banned by the regime of 1933 on account of his Jewish descent, supplied the grimmest possible gloss on grounding cultural possession in spirit and descent. Paulin's modern history reaches the German-classic conclusion by the labour route and discards the affinity metaphysics entire.

### Q: What was Delius's quarrel with Tieck's Shakespeare criticism in 1846?

Delius quarrelled with its foundation. In Die Tieck'sche Shakespearekritik of 1846, Nikolaus Delius examined the critical writings of Ludwig Tieck, the Romantic settlement's presiding editor, and found them conducted without the one discipline the new age demanded: knowledge of the original text. Tieck's authority on the plays, on Delius's showing, rested on translations, adaptations and theatrical memory, the poet's instruments, while the words Shakespeare wrote went unexamined in their early editions. The pamphlet was the young philology's declaration of war on the Romantic criticism that had canonized the plays, and it announced the programme Delius fulfilled in his Elberfeld edition of 1854 to 1860, the English text established from the quartos and the Folio with German commentary. The quarrel's fairness is arguable, since Tieck's labours on the corpus had made the philologist's audience, and the completion's prefaces are not empty of textual sense. Its effect is not arguable. After 1846, German Shakespeare criticism argued from edited evidence or explained why not, and the centre of the tradition moved from the poet's study to the professor's seminar, where Delius himself took the first German chair of English studies.

### Q: What did Hans Rothe attack in the Schlegel-Tieck text, and why did theatres listen?

Rothe attacked its distance from the world of the theatre. In his own new translations and his critical writings of the early twentieth century, Hans Rothe charged the canonical version with speaking the literary German of 1800, a language of the study and the schoolroom that companies could no longer put living on a stage. The theatres listened because the charge named their own fatigue. Balz Engler's survey of the translation history records that stages had begun to tire of the Schlegel-Tieck version, whose language belonged to another age and which represented the bourgeois educational tradition from which the modern stage wished to break; Weimar-era directors, in William Grange's account, were busy dismantling the dramatist's status as a cultural icon in production after production. Rothe's remedy, wholesale replacement by versions cut for theatrical effect, persuaded the playhouses more than the study, and the settlement the tradition reached was partition: the canonical text kept the reading public and the prestige stage while new versions multiplied beside it, an argument Engler shows still running at full heat in the 1970s.

### Q: How did Levin Ludwig Schücking propose to correct Romantic character criticism in 1919?

Schücking proposed to correct it with history. In Die Charakterprobleme bei Shakespeare of 1919, Englished in 1922 as Character Problems in Shakespeare's Plays, the Leipzig professor attacked the subjective character analysis descended from the Romantics, which treated the dramatis personae as souls available for psychological biography, and demanded two controls in its place: the conventions of the Elizabethan stage, which determine what a figure is doing in a scene, and the probable attitudes of the original audience, which determine what the scene could mean. His Sociology of Literary Taste of 1923 extended the programme from the plays to their publics. Applied to the Veronese tragedy, the correction dissolves Schlegel's lyric absolutes into a legible social machine of feud, marriage negotiation and civic edict, and it overshoots in the familiar way of corrections, since convention-knowledge cannot by itself account for the balcony scene's surplus of imagined life. The direction of German study after him is nevertheless his. The universities went historical, and the rhapsodic mode survived mainly in the schools the Gesellschaft had built.

### Q: Where did Wolfgang Clemen rebuild German English studies after the war, and what did he found in 1964?

Clemen rebuilt them from Munich, where he held the chair of English and in 1964 founded the Munich Shakespeare Library, a research collection that grew into one of the major holdings of Shakespeare scholarship outside Britain. The founding was characteristic of its founder and of the tradition this article has traced: the answer to catastrophe was infrastructure. Clemen's authority rested on his study of the imagery, begun as a Munich doctorate in 1936 and published in revised English form as The Development of Shakespeare's Imagery in 1951, which arranged the plays as a growth from iterative, decorative imagery toward imagery fused with action, and gave the Veronese tragedy its own chapter in the early sequence. Where Schlegel had heard the play's image-world as one fused impression and Spurgeon had counted its images in England, Clemen asked what the images were doing and how their doing developed. The library and the book are one gesture in two media, the provision of the means by which a shattered scholarly culture could check claims again.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch German timeline to argue that the German claim rests on labour rather than blood?

An essay should use the InsightCrunch German timeline as an audit trail, marching the claim past the dated stations and asking, at each, whose hands did the work. The wandering players' cuts, Borcke's alexandrines of 1741, Wieland's prose of the 1760s, Weiße's reworking of 1767, Schlegel's essay and translation of 1797, Caroline Schlegel's fair copies, Baudissin's twelve plays and Dorothea Tieck's seven in the completion of 1833, Delius's edition of 1854 to 1860, the Gesellschaft's founding of 1864 and the Jahrbuch's run from 1865: every entry names agents, dates and artefacts, and not one entry requires a Germanic essence to explain it. Against that record the essay can then set the two blood documents, the tercentenary proclamation of an inborn Germanic nature and Gundolf's affinity thesis of 1911, and show them arriving late, after the work was done, as ceremonies laid over a labour history. The conclusion writes itself from the table's own shape: Germany's Shakespeare is a German classic the way a building is a city's classic, because generations built, kept and repaired it, and the timeline is the set of receipts.
