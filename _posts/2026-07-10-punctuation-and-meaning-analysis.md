## The Verdict First: Whose Punctuation Does a Reader Actually Read?

The punctuation of Romeo and Juliet, the commas and colons and stops that tell a reader where one thought ends and the next begins, is the least authorial layer of the printed play. The words descend from a manuscript of the 1590s through the second quarto of 1599; the pointing descends from printing houses, from three centuries of editors, and from the modern grammatical system that no Elizabethan writer used. That is the verdict this study defends: the pointing on a modern page is an overlay, applied first by compositors who set type by ear and habit, then by editors who regularized the marks into a syntax the first audiences never saw, and it deserves to be read the way a translation is read, as a skilled mediation that mostly serves the text and sometimes silently replaces it.

![An early printed page of the play, its lines pointed with the light stops and commas of the first editions - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The claim is not that editors have vandalized the play. Repunctuation was unavoidable, because the system the marks belonged to has died: a comma in 1599 measured a pause for the voice, while a comma in a modern edition marks a joint in the grammar, and printing the old marks under the new understanding would mislead more readers than it served. The claim is narrower and harder. At a small number of passages, a mark does not merely pace the sense but chooses it, deciding whether Death or Romeo takes Juliet to the wedding bed, whether Romeo addresses his own lips or a personified power at his death, whether Juliet's last sentence falls in one motion or in three hammered beats. At those passages the reader is not reading the playwright's pointing, for none survives. The reader is reading an editor's argument, printed in the least visible form an argument can take, as a comma.

Two doctrines have governed how editors handle this inheritance. The first, descending from W. W. Greg's rationale of copy-text, holds that an editor should take the pointing of the chosen copy-text, here the second quarto, as the default fabric of the page, correcting only where sense fails, because the early marks stand closest to the author's papers. The second, defended in practice by the Oxford Shakespeare under Stanley Wells and theorized in his Re-Editing Shakespeare for the Modern Reader, holds that modern readers are best served by modern pointing throughout, since the copy-text's marks answer questions a modern reader is not asking. Between the doctrines stands the evidence assembled in this study: the second quarto's pointing is demonstrably presswork, variable within a single scene, and yet the system behind it, rhetorical pointing, encodes delivery information that grammatical pointing discards. The verdict reached below is that Greg's doctrine wins on honesty and loses on legibility, that Wells wins on legibility and loses on disclosure, and that the editions a reader can trust are the ones whose textual notes expose every place where a mark has been made to choose a sense.

Punctuation escapes suspicion for a reason no other layer of the text shares: it is learned before it can be questioned. A reader meets this play first in a schoolroom or on a bedroom shelf, in an edition whose pointing has the same typographical authority as its words, and the marks settle into the memory as part of the lines themselves, so that a comma misremembered feels like a word misremembered. By the time that reader is equipped to ask whose comma it is, the comma has been quoted in essays, set in examinations, and spoken on stages for years. The words enjoy a permanent audit; every generation of scholarship collates them, and a changed word in an edition is an event. The marks enjoy no such audit, because the culture of reading assigns them to the category of presentation rather than content, the way a frame is assigned to a painting. This study's first contention is that the assignment is an error with a history: the marks became presentation during the same centuries in which they changed their system and their makers, and the frame, examined closely, turns out to have been painted by the framers.

The stakes are easy to underestimate because punctuation feels like plumbing, beneath interpretation. The history of this play's text says otherwise. The one universally known fact about its most famous textual crux, the word printed runawayes in Juliet's Gallop apace speech at 3.2, is a pointing fact: the quarto sets no apostrophe, and every reading of the line ever offered, the runaway Cupid, the runaway eyes, the runaways of night, begins with an editor deciding where an apostrophe should stand. That crux has its own study in this series, and it appears here only as the limiting case that proves the rule. What follows examines the whole fabric of the play's pointing rather than the single famous tear in it: what the first printed texts actually show, whose hands made the marks, which system they belonged to, the passages where a mark chooses the meaning, and the reckoning of how much of what a reader reads is interpretation wearing the disguise of punctuation.

## What Did Punctuation Look Like in the Play's First Printed Texts?

A reader who opens the first quarto of 1597 or the second quarto of 1599 meets a page pointed far more lightly than any modern edition, and pointed to a different purpose. The available marks were the comma, the semicolon, the colon, the full stop, the question mark, the parenthesis, and the note of admiration that became the exclamation mark. They appear sparingly. Whole speeches run on commas where a modern text would set semicolons and periods; sentences that a modern editor breaks into three are allowed to flow in one long period, their joints marked by colons of varying weight; question marks appear at questions of fact and vanish at questions of rhetoric. The pointing is also uneven within a single speech, because it was applied at the case, by workmen setting type line by line, and it answers to the rhythm of a voice reading aloud more faithfully than to the architecture of the sentence being built.

The lightness is clearest at the level of the whole speech. Juliet's great invocation over the coming night at 3.2 runs in the early texts as a single accumulating period, clause banking on clause behind commas and the occasional colon, the grammar subordinated to the swell of the voice. A modern edition of the same speech sets semicolons at the clause joints, a full stop or two at the turns, and quotation-level clarity about which clause governs which. Neither text is wrong about the words. They differ about what punctuation is for. The quarto's pointing assumes a reader who will breathe the speech and find its sense in the breathing; the edition's pointing assumes a reader who will parse the speech and find its sense in the parsing. Between those assumptions lies the entire history traced in this study.

The Friar's entrance speech at 2.3 shows the system at full stretch, and a modern reader who compares the quarto page with an edition will learn the difference faster there than anywhere else in the play. The speech is a single meditation of nearly forty lines, moving from the grey-eyed morn through the earth's womb and tomb to the basket of herbs and the doctrine that nothing is so vile it lacks a special good. In the second quarto the meditation is pointed as a meditation: long reaches of comma-bound clauses, colons at the great balances where the thought turns from nature to man, full points reserved for the moments where a sententia seals itself and the voice may rest. The sentences do not so much end as arrive, repeatedly, at positions of rest inside a continuing act of attention. A modern edition must, for its readers, do something else: it breaks the meditation into sentences of digestible length, sets semicolons at the balances, promotes two or three of the sententiae into free-standing paragraphs of sense, and produces a text that can be studied clause by clause at the cost of the trance the quarto's pointing preserves. Both texts print the same words in the same order. They are different experiences of the same mind because their marks belong to different theories of what reading is: an act of sustained voicing, or an act of analysis performed in silence. The speech also exposes the practical editor's dilemma in its purest form, since no modern edition can print the quarto's version without a note confessing that its readers will founder, and no modern edition can print its own version without performing, silently, the conversion this study describes.

### Did the first printed texts of the play use exclamation marks and dashes?

Scarcely, and the scarcity is the point. The note of admiration existed in the period's type cases and appears in the early texts of the play only in a handful of places, while the dash as a mark of interruption is effectively absent from the quartos altogether.

The exclamations that stud modern editions, at Juliet's recognitions, Capulet's rages, the laments over the bodies, and the dashes that break off interrupted speeches are editorial supplies, added by hands of the eighteenth century and after, and a reader who feels their force is feeling an editor's staging of the line, not a survival from the first printed page.

The same lightness governs the play's second accidental system, its lineation, which stands to pointing as a sister: the early texts set verse as prose and prose as verse in places, and the study of lineation and lost verse in the quartos, treated in [the companion study of lineation and lost verse in the quartos](/2026/07/03/lineation-lost-verse-quartos/), pursues that half of the accidentals. Pointing is the half that governs the sentence rather than the line, and its neglect has a simple cause. A relineated passage announces itself on the page; a repunctuated one does not. The modern reader sees the same words in the same order and reasonably assumes the same text, while the marks that construe the words have been changed wholesale, silently, and in every edition differently.

Two marks on the early page repay a closer look, because modern readers systematically misjudge both. The question mark in the quartos marks interrogative shape, not interrogative force, and it comes and goes by a logic no modern reader would predict: questions of fact carry it reliably, while the play's great rhetorical questions, the laments' demands on heaven, the Friar's probing of Romeo in the cell, may carry a full stop or a colon instead, as if the mark were reserved for sentences expecting an answer and withheld from sentences performing an emotion. The effect on a modern eye is a repeated small surprise, speeches that read as strangely composed at exactly the moments of highest feeling, until the logic is grasped: the mark belongs to grammar's simpler half, the sentence's form, while the force was the voice's business, supplied by the reader or actor the text trusted. The parenthesis works the opposite way, as a mark of the voice the modern page has largely forgotten. In the early texts parentheses lower the voice, fencing matter spoken under the breath, in confidence, qualification, or aside, and John Lennard's study of the mark in early modern verse showed it functioning as a kind of volume control built into the line. The two quartos of this play differ in pointing character as they differ in origin, the second quarto's marks generally the more considered, the first's the more theatrical in distribution, but both trust the voice with work the modern page reassigns to the marks themselves, and that transfer of trust is the deep event beneath every particular difference this study records.

One further feature of the early page matters for everything that follows. Early modern pointing was not one system but a transition caught mid-passage. M. B. Parkes, in Pause and Effect, the standard history of Western punctuation, describes the long shift by which marks that had served the reader's voice, guiding delivery in a culture of reading aloud, were reassigned to serve the sentence's grammar, guiding the eye in a culture of silent study. The play's first editions stand near the start of that shift, the work of a print trade that still pointed largely for the ear while beginning to point for the eye. The mixture explains the quartos' inconsistency better than carelessness does: two systems overlapped in the same printing house, sometimes in the same speech, and compositors moved between them without noticing a border that historians have drawn only in retrospect.

## Rhetorical or Grammatical? The Two Systems Fighting over Every Line

The distinction the brief for this study insists on, between rhetorical and grammatical punctuation, is not a nuance laid over the evidence; it is the evidence's organizing fact. Grammatical pointing, the modern system, maps the sentence's syntax: commas fence off subordinate clauses, semicolons join independent clauses of equal weight, colons announce consequences and lists, full stops close completed structures. Its loyalty is to the construction, and a well-pointed modern sentence can be diagrammed from its marks alone. Rhetorical pointing maps delivery: its marks proportion pauses, lift emphasis, and mark the turns where a speaker gathers or spends breath. Its loyalty is to the utterance, and a rhetorically pointed sentence is a set of instructions to a voice. The same mark does different work in the two systems, which is why copying early marks into a modern text does not preserve early meaning: a rhetorical comma, transcribed faithfully, will be misread by every modern eye as a grammatical one.

The period's own grammarians describe the rhetorical system in so many words. Ben Jonson's English Grammar, published in 1640 in the folio of his works, treats the stops as rests proportioned for the reader's breath, the comma the shortest pause, the semicolon longer, the colon longer again, the period the full rest that closes a sentence's circuit. Charles Butler's English Grammar of 1634 proportions the pauses in the same spirit. These are not descriptions of a syntax notation; they are a metronome. A text pointed on Jonson's scheme tells a reader how long to hold each joint open, and the sense is expected to emerge from the held and released breath the way sense emerges from a sung phrase. When Percy Simpson opened Shakespearian Punctuation in 1911 with the claim that the pointing of the early Shakespeare texts is rhetorical in precisely this sense, he was recovering the grammarians' own account of what their marks were for.

Simpson's book remains the foundation every later account builds on or pushes against. Surveying the First Folio's pointing across the canon, he argued that its system, light, pause-based, indifferent to modern clause geometry, was coherent enough to be a system at all, and that its coherence pointed back through the copy to the author's own habits, since a dramatist writing for voices would point for voices. The Folio's lighter stops fall where a speaker breathes; its colons balance antitheses the way a rhetorician balances them, at the hinge of a period; its relative scarcity of full stops inside long speeches lets a sentence accumulate the pressure that a grammatical pointing would have vented at the first completed clause. Simpson's readings of individual passages retain their force: again and again, a Folio pointing that looks negligent by grammatical standards proves exact by rhetorical ones, catching an emphasis or a suspension that the modern repunctuation, busily clarifying the syntax, has flattened.

Parkes's history explains how the grammatical system won. Print standardizes; schools teach; and across the seventeenth century the teaching of grammar in the Latin-trained classroom made syntactic pointing the mark of an educated page, while the expansion of silent private reading removed the voice whose needs the rhetorical system served. By 1700 a lightly pointed text looked not rhetorical but defective, and the editors who inherited this play inherited that judgement with their grammars. The sequence matters for the verdict forming here: the repunctuation of the play was not a series of individual editorial decisions so much as the local effect of a civilizational change in what punctuation was, and the modern editor who points grammatically is not imposing a private system but obeying the only system the culture still reads. Which is precisely why the interpretation the marks perform has become invisible: a whole civilization changed its mind about the stops, and then forgot that the stops had ever meant anything else.

### Can a comma change what a line means, or only how it sounds?

It can change the meaning outright, because in the passages that matter the comma is not pacing a settled sense but assigning one. A comma decides what a phrase attaches to, whether words stand in apposition or in address, whether a clause explains its neighbour or opposes it.

Where the early texts leave such a joint unmarked or marked for the voice alone, the modern editor's comma becomes the only grammar the reader is given, and the reader construes the line by it without knowing a choice was made. Sound is where pointing begins; sense is where its choices end.

The rhetorical system also explains a feature of this play's verse that grammatical pointing persistently misrepresents, its long periodic sentences. The Friar's counsel and Juliet's invocations are built as periods in the rhetorical sense, their clauses suspended across line endings until a late main verb or a closing couplet discharges them. George T. Wright, in Shakespeare's Metrical Art, demonstrated that the verse line is itself a timing instrument, its endings and caesuras marking units of attention that cooperate with, and sometimes override, the pointing. A heavy grammatical stop set mid-period by an editor instructs the eye to close a structure the ear is still holding open; the line fights its own punctuation. Rhetorical pointing rarely fights the verse, because both were made for the same organ. When modern editions complain, in their notes, that a quarto colon seems misplaced, the frequent truth is that the colon is keeping time in a system their comma cannot hear.

None of this requires the rhetorical system to be the author's system in any simple sense, and the next section takes up the question of hands. What the rhetorical analysis establishes is prior and more important: whatever the early marks are, they are not failed attempts at modern pointing, and an edition that corrects them into grammar is not restoring an intended clarity but translating between systems, with the gains and losses translation always carries. The Friar's antithesis at 2.3, "Virtue itself turns vice, being misapplied; / And vice sometimes by action dignified," wears a semicolon in modern texts that diagrams its balance like a hinge. The quarto's lighter pointing leaves the two clauses to be balanced by the voice, in performance, at whatever tilt the speaker chooses. The semicolon is clearer. It is also a decision, foreclosing tilts the copy left open, and clarity of that kind is interpretation by another name.

One further feature of the rhetorical system deserves record, because the modern overlay handles it worst of all: the shared line. This play's verse constantly divides a single pentameter between two speakers, at the feast, in the orchard, at the parting, and in the shared sonnet of 1.5 two speakers divide not a line but a whole form, quatrain answering quatrain across the boundary of their meeting. A line shared is a sentence shared, and its pointing is a problem in joint ownership: the mark at the handover, comma, stop, or nothing, instructs the second speaker whether the first speaker's thought continues through the new voice or ends for a new one to replace. The quartos point these handovers with the same light hand as everything else, trusting the actors' rehearsed sense of the exchange to time the joints; the sonnet at the feast flows across its speaker changes with scarcely a mark to show where Romeo ends and Juliet begins, because the form itself, rhyme answering rhyme, is the timing. Modern editions, whose readers must see the joint to construe it, point the handovers grammatically, setting stops that clarify the syntax while suggesting a pause the verse forbids, since a shared line is metrically one breath passed between two mouths. Actors ignore those stops as a condition of playing the scenes at all. The handover is the pointing problem in miniature: a mark that serves the eye's analysis and betrays the ear's event, printed by editors who know it will be disobeyed, in a text that cannot show both truths at once.

## A Natural History of the Marks: Five Signs and Their Careers in This Play

The argument so far has treated punctuation as a system, but systems are made of individual marks, and each mark on the play's page has its own biography, its own date of birth, its own rise and demotion. Reading those biographies against this play's text is the quickest way to see why the modern overlay misleads where it does: the marks did not merely change their management between 1599 and the present edition; several of them changed their jobs.

The comma is the oldest worker on the page and the most promoted. Descended from the virgule of medieval manuscripts, it entered print as the lightest of rests, a flick of pause for a voice in motion, fit for lists, addresses, suspensions, and the small joints of a period still under construction. In the quartos of this play the comma does nearly everything: it separates the items of Mercutio's catalogues, suspends Juliet's clauses while her sentences gather, fences the vocatives of the tomb speeches, and marks a hundred joints that a modern text would distribute among four different signs. The modern system has promoted the comma into the chief fence of syntax while demoting its pause to near zero, so that the mark a first reader felt as a breath is now read as a bracket. When an editor retains a quarto comma, the shape survives and the instruction reverses: what told the voice to hesitate now tells the eye merely how to parse. No mark better deserves the study's central caution, that fidelity to the sign can be infidelity to the signal.

The semicolon is the youngest mark in the case and the most revealing. It was devised in Venice in the 1490s, in the circle of the printer Aldus Manutius, appearing in Pietro Bembo's De Aetna as a deliberate hybrid, stronger than a comma and weaker than a colon, for a prose learning new subtleties of suspension. A century later, when Creede's men set this play, the mark was still settling into its value, and the quartos use it sparingly and inconstantly, sometimes where a modern hand would set a comma, rarely where a modern hand would set the semicolons that now lattice the play's great arguments. The consequence is a quiet scandal at the centre of the modern page: the mark that does the heaviest logical work in a modern edition, articulating the name speech into premises and conclusions, balancing the Friar's antitheses into a diagram, is the mark the copy-text trusted least, a newcomer whose duties were still being negotiated while the play was new. The semicolon lattice readers admire as the speech's logic is an editorial architecture built from the period's least settled sign.

The colon is the demoted aristocrat of the system. In rhetorical pointing it stood near the top of the hierarchy of rests, the balance point of the period, the hinge at which a sentence's first half, fully stated and fully suspended, is answered by its second: the mark of antithesis, consequence, and formal correspondence. This play's rhetoric runs on such hinges, the Friar's paired clauses on virtue and vice, the Prince's paired sentences of pardon and punishment, the Chorus's paired destinies, and the quartos' colons stand at those joints doing ceremonial work. The grammatical era cashiered the colon to its modern duties, introducing lists, explanations, and quotations, worthy work that has nothing to do with balance, and modern editions frequently replace the quarto's hinge colon with a semicolon or a full stop, clarifying the syntax while dissolving the correspondence the colon existed to celebrate. Of all the marks, the colon's demotion has cost this play most, because the play thinks in balances, and its first pointing punctuated the balances as events.

The question mark and the parenthesis complete the five. The question mark, as the earlier section noted, certifies form rather than force in the early texts, and its career in the editions has been a long inflation of force: editors extend it to every rhetorical demand, so that speeches the quartos leave composed arrive on the modern page visibly agitated, their questions marked as cries. The parenthesis travelled the opposite road. A working mark in the early texts, the lowered voice of the aside and the qualification, it suited a drama built from confidences, overheard plans, and speeches with a second audience inside them, and the quartos fence such matter with it as a matter of course. Modern pointing, committed to the sentence's surface grammar, retains parentheses grudgingly and often dissolves them into commas, flattening the two-level utterance, the speech and its whisper, into a single level of address. Lennard's history of the mark demonstrated how much early modern verse encodes in that lowered register; the editions' treatment of it in this play, mostly silent normalization, erases a channel of meaning that operates by volume rather than by words. Five marks, five careers, and one moral between them: the overlay is not a transparent sheet laid over the early text but a different instrument, tuned to different work, and it plays the play differently in the places where the tunings diverge.

## Who Held the Pen? Copy, Compositors, and the Author's Sparse Hand

Between the playwright's manuscript and a modern page, the pointing of this play was made and remade at least four times. The second quarto of 1599 was printed in Thomas Creede's house for Cuthbert Burby, from copy that Greg and the editors who follow him identify as the author's foul papers, the working draft with its revisions still standing in it, as the doubled dawn quatrain at the join of 2.2 and 2.3 famously attests. The first quarto of 1597 had already passed through the shop of John Danter and Edward Allde, set from copy of a theatrical cast whose exact route remains disputed. The Folio text of 1623 was then reset in Isaac Jaggard's house from the quarto line of descent, the third and fourth quartos, so that its pointing is quarto pointing at two removes, re-applied by the Folio workmen Charlton Hinman identified as compositors B and E. At every transfer, pointing was re-executed, because in the early print trade the marks were not copied as data. They were part of what a compositor did with his hands as he set a line: he read a phrase of copy, held it in memory, and pointed it as he composed the type, by the habits of his house and his own schooling in the rhetorical system.

The evidence for compositorial agency is not speculative. Hinman's study of the Folio's printing established that its compositors can be distinguished by their spelling and their pointing habits alike, some setting heavier stops, some lighter, some favouring the colon where another man set a comma, and that the distribution of these habits follows the men's stints through the volume rather than any feature of the copy. D. F. McKenzie's account of the early modern printing house generalized the point: the book is a social product, and its accidentals record the conditions of manufacture as faithfully as they record anything about an author. For this play the consequence is measurable in kind if not in count. A speech that crosses a boundary between two compositors' stints, or between formes worked at different speeds, can change its pointing character mid-course, thickening or thinning for reasons that have everything to do with the working week and nothing to do with the rhetoric of the scene.

What, then, did the compositors start from? The best surviving evidence for how a dramatist of the milieu pointed his own pages is the manuscript of Sir Thomas More, whose three added pages in the hand widely attributed to Shakespeare, Hand D, show a pointing as light as anything in the quartos: commas doing nearly all the internal work, full points reserved for the ends of speeches or the completion of a thought, the verse line itself left to carry the timing. If those pages are his, and the attribution, though argued, is the scholarly centre of gravity, then the author's own practice was a skeleton pointing, a set of cues a knowing reader, actor, scribe, or compositor was expected to flesh out. The foul papers behind the second quarto would on this evidence have been pointed in the same spare hand. Creede's men did not override a dense authorial pointing; they completed a sparse one, under time pressure, each in his own habit. The second quarto's pointing is therefore best described as a trade completion of an authorial skeleton, authorial in its occasions, professional in its execution, and personal to no one.

If the second quarto's copy was indeed foul papers, its pointing carried one more layer of accident that editors rarely picture. A working draft is not a fair surface: it carries deletions, interlineations, and second thoughts fitted into margins, and its sentences were pointed, where they were pointed at all, for an audience of one who knew what he meant. The doubled dawn quatrain, which stands in the quarto in two versions at the join of the balcony scene and the Friar's cell, differs between its versions in small verbal particulars, and the pointing of such a passage in the copy would have been the pointing of a man revising, not of a man presenting: marks made at different passes, in different tempers, some belonging to the discarded shape of the sentence and some to its successor. A compositor meeting that surface does what his trade requires: he tidies it into one consistent fabric, and the tidying is invisible because consistency is what type does best. The playhouse adds its own possible link in the chain. The company's book, the licensed manuscript from which performances were mounted, would have carried a book-keeper's working marks, and if any theatrical copy stands behind the first quarto, its pointing passed through that professional filter too, a filter concerned with entrances, properties, and the practicable, not with the niceties of rhetorical proportion. At every hypothetical link the same law operates: the closer a copy stands to use, the more its pointing belongs to the user, and the author's sparse skeleton recedes behind completions made for purposes, rehearsal, recollection, manufacture, that were never his.

### Why did printers, rather than the playwright, end up controlling the stops?

Because pointing belonged to the compositor's craft, not to the author's delivery of copy. A manuscript reached the printing house pointed lightly, in the expectation that the men at the case would complete the marks as part of setting the type, and the playwright neither saw proofs nor held any right in the printed book.

The company owned the play; the stationer owned the copy; the house owned the stops.

Trevor Howard-Hill's work supplies the necessary counterweight before the case hardens into dogma. Studying Folio texts set from transcripts by the scribe Ralph Crane, Howard-Hill showed that a copyist's pointing could be systematic, distinctive, and transmitted into print with real fidelity, and he cautioned against the assumption that all early pointing is compositorial static. The caution matters and limits the scepticism this study has been building: pointing in the early texts is not noise, and Simpson's rhetorical system is genuinely there to be read. But for this play the counterweight cannot restore what it might restore elsewhere, because the Folio's copy here was not a careful scribal transcript but printed quartos, themselves set in Creede's and Danter's shops. The chain of custody for the marks runs authorial skeleton, compositorial completion, reprint, compositorial re-completion. At no link in that chain does a mark carry a warranty.

The mechanics of the printing house left their fingerprints on the marks in ways a reader can still detect. A quarto was set by formes, sheets imposed so that pages fell in sequence only after folding, which means the compositor worked through the copy out of order, in blocks, sometimes two men dividing a gathering between them; and copy was cast off, measured in advance so that each block began and ended at an appointed word, which squeezed or stretched the setting at the block's edges. Pointing suffers under both regimes. A man setting out of order, meeting a sentence whose beginning he set last week or will set tomorrow, points the fragment in front of him and cannot point the period it belongs to; a man pressed for space at a forme's end economizes in marks as he economizes in spacing. Stop-press correction, the period's proofreading performed while the sheets were already printing, altered marks along with words, so that surviving copies of the same edition can differ in their pointing at a given line, a fact with an uncomfortable corollary that editors absorb silently: there is no single document called the second quarto's pointing, only the pointing of the copies and formes an editor happened to collate. The first quarto's route adds a further filter. Whatever its copy was, prompt-book, reported text, or some braid of the two, its pointing passed through hands concerned with performance or recollection before it reached Danter's case, and a text whose marks have already survived one act of theatrical transmission arrives at the compositor doubly mediated. None of this makes the early pointing worthless. It makes it evidence of a kind different from what readers assume: evidence of how the play was handled, voiced, and manufactured, in which the author's intentions are one strand among several and rarely the thickest.

Greg's doctrine, understood against this chain, reveals both its wisdom and its strain. The rationale of copy-text asks the editor to reproduce the copy-text's accidentals not because they are certainly the author's, for Greg knew they were not, but because they are the least arbitrary choice available: better the second quarto's trade-completed pointing, a period artifact with a documented provenance, than the editor's own pointing imposed piecemeal under the disguise of correction. The strain shows precisely at punctuation, the one accidental with semantic reach. Spelling can be followed mechanically, since one spelling rarely changes a sense, but a pointing followed mechanically prints the marks of a dead system for readers trained in a living one, and fidelity to the mark becomes infidelity to the effect. Every editor of this play has therefore broken Greg's doctrine at the pointing while honouring it in the spelling, and the interesting question is not whether they break it but whether they say so. The period itself supplies the control experiment for the whole argument, in the person of the grammarian already cited. Ben Jonson, whose English Grammar proportions the stops for the breath, supervised the printing of his own folio Works in 1616 with a notorious author's care, correcting, arranging, and presenting his plays and poems as literature under his own hand. The pointing of that volume is correspondingly denser, more systematic, and more evidently governed than the pointing of any Shakespeare quarto: it is what early modern pointing looks like when an author treats it as part of the work. Set beside it, the quartos of this play show the opposite condition at every feature, lightness where Jonson is full, inconsistency where he is regular, silence where he is emphatic. The contrast is the cleanest evidence available that the play's pointing is not authorial pointing imperfectly transmitted but trade pointing competently supplied, since the age demonstrably knew how to print an author's marks when an author insisted on them, and no one insisted for the Chamberlain's Men's most popular tragedy. The sonnets' quarto of the same era, its pointing erratic in ways no supervising poet would have passed, tells the same story from the other side of the canon: where the author did not supervise, the house pointed, and the house pointed as houses do.

One property of pointing completes the account of its transmission, and it is the property that makes the whole subject easy to miss: pointing errors propagate exactly as verbal errors do, but they conceal themselves better. The third quarto of 1609 reprinted the second, with a corrector's hand at work on the text; the fourth reprinted the third; the Folio printers set their copy from that line of descent, and at every reprinting the marks were executed afresh by new workmen reading old pages. A misprinted word advertises itself, since nonsense draws the eye, and a corrector or a later editor stands a chance of catching it. A misconstrued comma advertises nothing, because a wrongly jointed sentence usually still makes a kind of sense, the kind the pointing itself supplies, and it can travel down a century of reprints gathering the authority of repetition at every stage. Several of the pointing decisions examined in the case file below have exactly this biography: a mark set by a compositor construing on the run, inherited by the next edition because it was there, regularized by the next because it had been inherited, and finally defended in a note, three centuries on, as the reading of the tradition. Tradition, in pointing, is often only the name for an accident that learned to repeat itself. The editor who breaks such a chain performs the bravest act punctuation allows, and the editor who declines to break it should at least know, and say, that a chain is what is being preserved.

The case files that follow show what is at stake when they do not.

## The InsightCrunch Pointing Case: Where One Mark Chooses the Sense

The table below, the InsightCrunch pointing case, gathers the passages of this play where the pointing is not plumbing but interpretation: where the early texts leave a joint unmarked or marked for the voice, where modern editions must set a mark that construes the line, and where the mark they set decides something a reader will take for the play's meaning. Each row states the early pointing, the pointing editions characteristically supply, and the decision the supplied mark performs. The pattern the table exposes is consistent: the editorial mark is almost always defensible, often almost certainly right, and never the author's. Its danger lies not in error but in invisibility.

| Passage | The early pointing | The pointing editions supply | What the mark decides |
|---|---|---|---|
| Juliet, 3.2: "And death, not Romeo, take my maidenhead" | The second quarto prints no commas around "not Romeo" | Commas bracketing "not Romeo" as a correction | That Death substitutes for Romeo in the sentence; unpointed, the line momentarily reads as two subjects yoked to one verb |
| Romeo, 5.3: "lips, O you / The doors of breath, seal with a righteous kiss" | The early texts leave the attachment of "the doors of breath" unmarked | Commas folding "the doors of breath" into apposition with "lips" | Whether Romeo addresses his own lips, completing the address to his eyes and arms, or turns from them to Death personified |
| Juliet, 5.3: "This is thy sheath; there rust, and let me die" | The second quarto sets a single comma after "sheath" | A semicolon or a full stop after "sheath" | Whether the death falls in one flowing motion or in three separated beats, assertion, command, and surrender |
| Juliet, 4.3: "No, no; this shall forbid it. Lie thou there" | A lighter stop in the second quarto after the doubled refusal | A sharpened semicolon or colon before "this" | How hard the pointing presses "this" toward the knife laid on the bed, the object that is to forbid the marriage |
| Capulet, 3.5: "Out, you green-sickness carrion! out, you baggage!" | Plainer stops in the early texts | Exclamation marks through the tirade | Whether the father's abuse plays as operatic fury or as cold contempt wearing a loud voice |
| Juliet, 1.5: "My only love sprung from my only hate" | The couplet closed with plainer pointing in the early texts | An exclamation mark at the recognition | Whether the line lands as a cry torn from the speaker or as a sententia she composes and understands |
| Juliet, 2.2: the name speech, "'Tis but thy name that is my enemy" | Commas and colons in rhetorical proportion through the argument | A lattice of semicolons and full stops diagramming the steps | Whether the speech flows as persuasion from a girl thinking aloud or stands as a logic proof with premises and a conclusion |
| The Prince, 5.3: "Some shall be pardon'd, and some punished" | A colon or lighter stop before the closing couplet in the early texts | Colons, semicolons, and full stops in varying editions | Whether the final couplet explains the sentence, "For never was a story of more woe," or stands sealed from it as an epitaph |

The first case is the cleanest. Juliet's line at 3.2 closes the great night speech: if Romeo is dead, as the speech has been imagining, then "death, not Romeo, take my maidenhead." The sense is not in doubt among editors, and the bracketing commas state it perfectly, lifting "not Romeo" out of the clause as a substitution. But notice what the unpointed quarto line does that the pointed line cannot: it lets the grammar stumble, for the half-second in which "death not Romeo" reads as a pair of takers before the mind corrects itself, and in that stumble the line enacts the confusion it describes, the bridegroom and the grave exchanging places faster than syntax can police them. The commas are the right editorial decision and a real loss, both at once. That doubleness is the study's whole subject in miniature.

The second case decides an address. Dying over Juliet's body, Romeo speaks to his own frame in a rising triad: "Eyes, look your last! / Arms, take your last embrace! and, lips, O you / The doors of breath, seal with a righteous kiss." The editions point "the doors of breath" into apposition with "lips," and the parallel structure supports them: eyes, arms, lips, each organ summoned in turn, the lips glossed as breath's doors at the moment they are to be sealed. Yet the early texts leave the attachment open, and an open attachment permits the flicker of another reading, in which "O you" wheels away from the body toward the Death who has been addressed throughout the scene, and the kiss seals a bargain with him. Editors who print the apposition commas are almost certainly right about the speech's architecture. They are also deciding, invisibly, that the dying man speaks only to himself, and a reader who has never seen the joint unmarked will never know the line could lean toward the personification it has been courting since "Death, that hath suck'd the honey of thy breath" twenty lines before.

The third case is about tempo, which is to say about the grammar of a death. "O happy dagger! / This is thy sheath; there rust, and let me die." The second quarto's comma lets the sentence run in a single motion from recognition to act: the sheath named, the rust commanded, the death accepted, one breath. The semicolon that editions print saws the motion into beats, and a full stop saws harder, so that Juliet's end becomes ceremonial, a rite performed in stages, the tempo of a girl arranging her own monument. Actors feel the difference in the body, and directors choose between editions on the strength of such marks more often than textual notes admit. What no edition can claim is that the playwright chose the tempo. The tempo on the page is the tempo of Creede's shop, revised by the tempo of the edition's house style, and the actress's Juliet dies at the speed an editor set.

The aubade at the opening of 3.5 offers a case the table had no room for, and it may be the most quietly consequential of all. The lovers' dispute over the lark and the nightingale is a contest of assertions and retractions, Juliet first denying the dawn, then, terrified for Romeo's life, insisting on it: "It is, it is: hie hence, be gone, away!" Romeo's answering speech accepts the reversal and builds his consent into a formal period: "Let me be ta'en, let me be put to death; / I am content, so thou wilt have it so." The semicolon an edition sets after "death" is doing legal work, dividing the speech into the sentence pronounced and the condition that ratifies it, so that Romeo's surrender reads as a contract executed in two clauses, the penalty accepted and then the signature appended. The quartos' lighter pointing leaves the consent less articulated, a single exhalation in which the acceptance and the condition blur as they do in speech, and the difference between the two pointings is the difference between a Romeo who drafts his own death warrant and a Romeo who simply stops resisting. Criticism has produced both Romeos in quantity, the self-dramatizing contractor and the exhausted boy, and while temperament and production explain much of the split, the semicolon stands at the head of the contractual reading like a clerk's initials on a deed. No reader who meets the speech in one edition suspects that the other Romeo is a punctuation mark away.

A ninth case, kept out of the table because it concerns a habit rather than a sentence, completes the case file: the pointing of questions in Juliet's potion speech at 4.3. The speech is an interrogation the heroine conducts against herself, what if the mixture fails, what if the Friar means her harm, what if she wakes before Romeo comes, and its movement is the movement of a mind trying its terrors one by one. The early texts point those questions inconstantly, some carrying the interrogation point and some a plainer stop, as if the asking were sometimes a grammatical event and sometimes merely the next item in a catalogue of fears recited to keep the dark at bay. Modern editions regularize the speech into a battery of question marks, every terror formally interrogative, and the regularization decides the speech's psychology: a girl cross-examining her situation, lucid and procedural, rather than a girl reciting her dreads in the flatter voice of someone talking herself through a rite. Both Juliets are playable and both are argued in the criticism, which quotes the speech in its pointed form. The question marks that make her an interrogator are, in the proportion the table has taught, an editorial contribution to her character.

The remaining rows extend the same finding across the play's registers, and three of them deserve their paragraphs. Take the name speech first, because it is the passage where the modern lattice does its most admired work. "'Tis but thy name that is my enemy; / Thou art thyself, though not a Montague. / What's Montague? It is nor hand, nor foot, / Nor arm, nor face, nor any other part / Belonging to a man." In a modern edition the sentence architecture is drawn for the eye: the semicolon after "enemy" poses the thesis, the full stop after "Montague" closes the concession, the catalogue of parts is comma'd into a list whose cumulative grammar drives at the conclusion the speech will draw about names and things. The quarto's pointing, commas and colons in rhetorical proportion, declines to draw the architecture, because for its first readers the architecture was an event of the voice: the girl thinks the thought at the speed of speech, premise tumbling into example, and the persuasion lies in the tumbling. The diagrammed version is easier to teach and is quoted in every classroom; it also converts an act of thinking into a proof already thought, and the conversion is performed entirely by marks no early text authorizes. An essay that praises the speech's inexorable logic is, in part, praising an editor's semicolons.

Capulet's tirade at 3.5 shows the exclamation mark working as a volume knob on character. "Out, you green-sickness carrion! out, you baggage! / You tallow-face!" arrives in modern editions fully exclaimed, and the pointing instructs the reader to hear shouting from the first word. The early texts point the abuse more plainly, and plain pointing holds open a possibility the exclamations close: that the horror of the scene is not noise but control, a father speaking at conversational pressure while he disposes of his daughter's life, the menace lying in the evenness. Productions divide on exactly this, the shouting Capulets against the quiet ones, and the division follows the editions more faithfully than it follows any stage tradition: a company working from an exclaimed text shouts, and discovers the scene's brutality in volume; a company that strips the marks finds the brutality in calm. The early page, by declining to instruct the feeling, leaves the actor the decision the modern page makes on the actor's behalf. Which is better theatre is a genuine question; whose decision it is, is not.

The Prince's colon, finally, shows a single mark governing the play's last impression. The closing speech distributes pardon and punishment, and then the couplet arrives, "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo." The mark before it determines the couplet's office: a colon keeps it inside the judgment as the reason the judgments stand, a full stop sets it outside as the epitaph under which the court disperses. Editors have printed both, and the two plays that result end differently, one in a magistrate's justification, one in a chronicler's sigh, with the same words in the same order. In each case, here and across the table, an editor chose, and chose well or badly on grounds a reader can assess, but the choosing was the editor's, executed in a medium the reader has been trained to overlook. Add the limiting case of the apostrophe at 3.2, the runawayes whose pointing has generated two centuries of commentary, and the pattern completes itself: from the most famous crux to the least noticed comma, the pointing of this play is a record of decisions, and the decisions are posthumous.

## How the Eighteenth Century Repointed the Play

The overlay acquired its modern character in the eighteenth century, when the play passed from the printing house to the editor's study and punctuation became criticism. The editors whose sequence is traced in [the history of editing the play from Rowe to Malone](/2026/06/05/editing-rowe-to-malone-history/) worked in a culture whose grammarians were completing the reassignment Parkes describes, the conversion of the stops from measures of breath into markers of syntax, and they regularized the play's pointing as they regularized its spelling, its lineation, and its stage directions, in the confidence that the quartos' marks were negligence awaiting correction. Nicholas Rowe's text of 1709, set from the late quartos, already points the play more heavily than anything printed in the 1590s; Alexander Pope, a poet editing a poet, repunctuated with a versifier's ear and a systematizer's impatience; Lewis Theobald, whose Shakespeare Restored of 1726 founded modern textual criticism on Pope's errors, understood that pointing was evidence, and his corrections repeatedly turn on construing a passage by its stops. By the time Edmond Malone closed the century, the play wore a pointing that was grammatical in design, editorial in authority, and wholly posthumous in fact.

Two features of the century's work deserve isolation, because both survive in every modern edition. The first is the exclamation mark's promotion. Where the quartos had admitted the note of admiration sparingly, the eighteenth-century editors distributed it generously, and the nineteenth multiplied it further, until speeches that the early texts deliver at a level pressure, Juliet's recognitions, the laments in the tomb, Mercutio's dying raillery, arrived on the modern page pre-scored with cries. The exclamation is the least innocent of marks, since it does not construe a sentence but instructs a feeling, and its inflation across the play's editorial history is a history of editors deciding, generation by generation, how much of the grief and fury a reader should be told to hear. The second feature is the dash. Absent from the quartos as an instrument, the dash entered Shakespeare editing as the sign of interruption, of the aside, of the thought that breaks itself, and this play, whose dialogue lives by interruption, the Nurse cut off, Mercutio cut off, Juliet cutting herself off in mid-plea to her mother, gathered dashes the way a shore gathers weed. Each dash is an editor standing in the wings, holding up a placard that says the speech breaks here. The placard is usually right. It was never in the copy.

Two editors of the century mark the turn from correction toward evidence, and both matter to the pointing. Samuel Johnson, whose edition of 1765 gave the play its most famous preface, treated the text with a conservative's suspicion of novelty and yet repunctuated as freely as his predecessors where the sense, in his judgement, required a joint the quartos had not marked; his notes show a mind construing passage by passage, and where Johnson construes by pointing, the pointing is criticism of the highest authority wearing the dress of typography. Edward Capell, working in the same decades, was the first editor to collate the quartos systematically for the text, and collation changed pointing's status: a mark that had been negligence to be corrected became a reading to be recorded, the earliest step toward the modern apparatus in which a pointing variant can be cited like a verbal one. The variorum tradition that followed, gathering the editors' choices into a single crowded page, had the unintended virtue of making the divergence visible; a reader of the great variorums watches Pope, Theobald, Johnson, and Steevens point the same sentence four ways and learns the study's lesson from the furniture of scholarship itself.

The nineteenth century then did two things whose effects still govern the page. The Cambridge edition of 1863 to 1866, edited by William George Clark and William Aldis Wright from a fresh collation of the early texts, issued the play in a pointing that was conservative by intention and modern in system, and its one-volume offspring, the Globe Shakespeare of 1864, carried that pointing into virtually every home, school, and stage in the English-speaking world for the better part of a century. When the world quoted this play between the Civil War and the Second World War, it quoted, in the main, the Cambridge-Globe marks: the semicolons in Juliet's arguments, the exclamations at the recognitions, the stops at the deaths are, in a great proportion of instances, the decisions of two Cambridge editors working at the height of the grammatical system's confidence. The variorum enterprise, crowned for this play by Horace Howard Furness's New Variorum edition of 1871, worked the other way, amassing beneath the text the record of what every editor had done, pointing included, until the page's foot became a parliament of marks. Between the Globe's silent standardization above the line and the variorum's noisy archive below it, the nineteenth century bequeathed the modern reader both conditions of the present problem: a pointing so widely shared it feels like the text, and an apparatus so full it proves the sharing is a convention.

## How the Modern Editions Point the Same Lines Differently

The modern scholarly editions of the play all begin from the second quarto and all print different punctuation, which is the single most instructive fact in this study. Brian Gibbons, in the Arden second series edition of 1980, worked closest to the copy-text's accidentals, modernizing the spelling while treating the quarto's pointing as a fabric to be preserved where it could be construed, on Greg's principle that the least arbitrary pointing is the earliest. Jill Levenson's Oxford edition of 2000, prepared under the Oxford Shakespeare's modernizing programme, repunctuated more freely toward modern grammatical norms, on the principle Wells had argued: that a modern reader construes by the modern system, and an edition that prints rhetorical marks for grammatical eyes manufactures difficulties the author never made. René Weis, in the Arden third series edition of 2012, took a mediating practice, retaining more early pointing than Oxford while recording his departures in fuller textual notes than either predecessor. G. Blakemore Evans's New Cambridge edition and the Folger Shakespeare of Barbara Mowat and Paul Werstine each strike the balance at further points, the Folger frankly prioritizing the reader's and the actor's immediate construal. The full comparison of these editions, their layouts, audiences, and textual policies, belongs to [the comparison of the modern editions, Arden, Oxford, and Folger](/2026/06/12/modern-editions-compared-arden-oxford/); what matters here is the shape of their disagreement about marks.

For the disagreement is not random, and its map is the honest core of the subject. Where the pointing construes syntax that no reading disputes, the editions agree, because grammar leaves one reasonable answer and the quarto's mark usually already gives it. Where a mark must choose, the editions divide, and they divide along the doctrines their editors hold: Gibbons keeping the quarto's lighter stop and letting the sentence flow at the risk of ambiguity, Levenson setting the disambiguating mark and accepting the foreclosure, Weis setting the mark and flagging the foreclosure in the notes. A reader who collates three editions at the eight passages in the pointing case table will watch the doctrines operate like weather systems over the same terrain. The lines do not change. The weather does.

### What does an editor do when two pointings of the same line both make sense?

The honest ones fall back on a declared rule and then confess the exception. The rule is the copy-text: keep the second quarto's pointing wherever it can be construed, so that the choice is the document's and not the editor's taste.

Where the quarto's mark cannot be construed by a modern reader, or where two construals stand open and the edition must print one, the editor chooses, prints the choice, and records the alternative in the textual notes, where the confession belongs. An edition that repunctuates silently at such a joint has not avoided interpretation; it has concealed it.

The Wells and Marcus quarrel gives this practice its theory. Stanley Wells, defending the Oxford modernization, argued that an edition is a performance of the text for its own time, that spelling and pointing are the notation of that performance, and that refusing to modernize them privileges the document over the play. Leah Marcus, in Unediting the Renaissance, answered from the documents: the smoothing operations of editing, repunctuation prominent among them, manufacture the coherent author readers then attribute to the page, and the marks an editor sets will be quoted for a century as the playwright's emphases, his pauses, his intentions. The adjudication this study reaches splits the verdict by function. Wells wins on the reading text: a modern edition that printed the second quarto's pointing raw would be a facsimile with pretensions, misleading exactly the readers it claimed to respect, since the marks' meanings have migrated. Marcus wins on the apparatus: because repunctuation is interpretation, the places where it chooses a sense must be exposed in the notes with the same prominence given to a changed word, and editions that record their emendations of words while passing over their emendations of stops in silence are keeping two sets of books. The editions of this play sit at various points on that scale of candour, and a reader choosing among them is choosing, among other things, how much invisible interpretation to accept on trust.

The divergence can be watched at the case-file passages, where the editions' doctrines cash out into different marks on the same words. At the happy dagger's sentence the editions divide between the semicolon's ceremony and stops of differing weight, none printing the quarto's comma without comment, each thereby setting the tempo of Juliet's death at its own editorial pulse. At the doors of breath, the apposition commas command a majority but not a unanimity, and the minority pointings, which let the address hang toward Death, are defended in their editions' notes with an ingenuity that proves how much argument a comma can be made to carry. Weis's Arden text is the most instructive of the modern witnesses at such joints, not because its choices are always the most persuasive but because its notes most often show the working, naming the quarto's mark and the reason for leaving it; a reader trained on such notes learns to hear the second text running beneath the first, the document beneath the edition. The Folger text, at the other end of the scale of annotation, makes the opposite wager, that a reader or actor is best served by a page whose pointing has been settled thoroughly and silently by editors who know the evidence, and the wager is honest in its way: the Folger's pointing is a performance text, and like a promptbook it does not apologize for directing. What no modern edition supplies is the experience the first quarto's buyers had, of a pointing that trusted them to voice the sense out of a light sprinkling of rests. That experience is recoverable now only by leaving the editions for the facsimile, which is why the facsimile's new availability, the subject of the reckoning below, matters more than any single editorial doctrine.

One modern development complicates the reckoning helpfully. Digital texts and the old-spelling and facsimile resources that accompany them have made the early pointing consultable by any reader in a way no previous century allowed, so the overlay, though still the only pointing most readers will read, is no longer the only pointing a reader can check. The scholarly consequence is a slow transfer of the burden: editors may repunctuate as boldly as their doctrine requires, because the document against which the reader checks them is a click away, and the editions that thrive under that condition are the ones whose notes behave as if the checking had already happened.

The Oxford text's afterlife deserves a note of its own, because it shows how an editorial pointing becomes a culture's pointing. The Oxford Shakespeare's principles, carried into Levenson's edition of this play and into the Norton Shakespeare that adopted the Oxford text for the university market, placed the play's repunctuated lines before more students than any rival version, and the Norton page, clean, modern, and assured, teaches its pointing with the same silent authority as the school texts below it in the market. A generation of essays has accordingly quoted the Oxford-Norton commas as the play's joints, and more than one published argument about the shape of Juliet's reasoning rests, at the level of the marks, on decisions taken in Oxford in the 1980s and 1990s under a programme their makers defended in print and their readers rarely consult. The programme was honourable and its defence is on record; the propagation is the problem, since quotation strips the defence away and leaves the decision wearing the author's name. Arden's rival text propagates the same way through the other half of the syllabus. Between them, two pointings, made within living memory by named scholars working to declared doctrines, now constitute what the educated world means by the punctuation of Romeo and Juliet, and the second quarto's marks, the only ones with any claim to the period, are read chiefly by the specialists who edit the editions.

Below the scholarly editions lies the stratum where most readers actually meet the play, the school texts and mass-market paperbacks, and their pointing is the most thoroughly modernized of all and the least documented. A school edition must make the sentences parse for a fourteen-year-old at sight, so its pointing is the boldest grammatical overlay in the tradition, its exclamations the most generous, its dashes the most frequent, and its textual notes the briefest, often nonexistent. The result is a readership paradox that the scholarly debate about Greg and Wells rarely faces: the editions whose pointing performs the most interpretation circulate in the millions, while the editions that confess their pointing in full apparatus circulate in the thousands, and the play as the culture holds it, quoted, remembered, and examined on, is pointed by the silent stratum. The popular quotation websites extend the stratum's reach, reproducing one edition's text, pointing included, as the play itself, without naming the edition; a sentence lifted from such a source carries an editor's commas into essays and wedding speeches under the playwright's name. None of this is a scandal that better information would end, since the silent stratum is silent because its readers want a play, not a bibliography. It is, however, the true scale of the phenomenon the doctrines govern: for every reader who checks a pointing in an apparatus, a million read the pointing as the author.

## Pointing for the Ear: Punctuation, the Verse Line, and Performance

Everything so far has treated punctuation as a problem of the page, but the play was built for the ear, and the pointing question returns in performance wearing different clothes. A modern actor works from a modern edition, which means the actor's first score for breath, emphasis, and turn is a grammatical pointing laid over verse that was timed by other means. The verse itself is the older score. As the account of [blank verse in Romeo and Juliet and how it works](/2024/12/06/blank-verse-romeo-and-juliet/) sets out, the pentameter's line endings, its caesuras, and its pattern of end-stopped and enjambed movement tell a speaker where energy gathers and where it spills, independently of any mark a printer set. Wright's metrical analysis gives the relation its precise form: in Shakespeare's verse the line is the primary unit of attention, and pointing that cooperates with the line's own articulation reinforces the delivery system the verse already contains, while pointing that fights it, heavy syntactic stops set against a driving enjambment, forces the actor to choose between the grammar on the page and the momentum in the line. Experienced actors choose the line, and in choosing it they silently repunctuate, restoring in the voice something closer to the rhetorical pointing the compositors were completing than to the grammatical pointing the edition supplied.

The rhetorical system, heard this way, was a performance technology before it was a reading one. A text pointed by Jonson's proportions told the first actors, and the first readers who voiced the book aloud, where the sentence meant its weight to fall and how long each suspension should be held before the sense resolved. The Friar's sententiae, balanced clause against clause across a colon, are built to be weighed by a voice at exactly the joint the rhetorical colon marks; Juliet's accumulating periods are built to be ridden, breath managed across subordinate clauses until the main verb lands like a handrail at the stair's end. Grammatical pointing serves these speeches well enough in study, where the eye can climb back up the sentence and reassign the clauses, but it undersells them in the theatre, where the sentence happens once, at speed, and the audience construes by ear or not at all. Directors who mark up a modern text, striking out stops, restoring colons, pencilling breaths against the printed marks, are doing in rehearsal what this study describes in the study: treating the edition's punctuation as a first interpretation, to be kept where it plays and replaced where it does not.

The first actors, it should be added, never worked from an authorially pointed text either, at any link of the chain this study has traced. A player learned his role from a part, the roll containing his own speeches and the cues that triggered them, copied out by a scribe from the company's book; the pointing in a part was therefore scribal pointing at best, made for one voice's use, and the full text from which the part was copied was itself the book-keeper's working document, marked for the practicable. Performance in the play's own century was thus already a negotiation with non-authorial marks, and the notion that stripping away the editions would restore the actors to the playwright's pointing mistakes the archaeology: there was never a moment, in print or in the playhouse, when the pointing that governed a performance of this play came from the hand that wrote the words. What the editions added was not mediation, which was always there, but invisibility, the modern page's habit of presenting its mediated marks as if they were the text's own skin.

The play's prose exposes the same truth from the other side. Verse pointing is disciplined by the line, which gives the compositor a grid and the editor a skeleton of pauses already implied by the metre; prose has no grid, and the pointing of the prose scenes in the quartos, the servants' opening sparring, Mercutio's flights, the Nurse's expeditions, wanders accordingly, set by the compositor's ear for the joke's timing with nothing to check it. Modern editions regularize that prose pointing into grammar, and the regularization has a cost a verse passage rarely pays: prose comedy lives by its pointing, the comma that delays the punch word by half a beat, the stop that lets a non sequitur land flat. When an editor reparses the Nurse's syntax into tidy subordination, the tidiness is visible in a way a verse emendation is not, because the audience's laugh arrives or fails to arrive at exactly the joint the mark governs. The prose pointing is the freest in the early texts and the most rewritten in the modern ones, and the distance between the two is a fair index of how much interpretation a full pointing represents when nobody is watching it happen.

Performance history adds its own layer of pointing, because the marks that governed playing were for centuries neither the quartos' nor the editions' but the promptbook's. A prompt copy is a working text annotated for production, cut, re-cued, and marked with the warnings and emphases a company found necessary, and the emphases in surviving promptbooks of this play constitute a shadow punctuation, applied in pencil and ink by the people who had to make the sentences work at speed before an audience. The shadow system clarifies what the printed systems obscure: pointing in use is always somebody's decision about the next three seconds of sound. The recorded performances of the modern era extend the line into a new medium with the same structure. A narrator reading the play for an audio edition breathes where the edition's commas tell the breath to fall, lengthens at its semicolons, and lands its full stops as cadences, so that a modern pointing, devised for the silent eye, is converted back into a rhetorical score by the act of voicing, three centuries after the rhetorical system died, with the grammatical marks doing the rhetorical work as best they can. The fit is imperfect in exactly the places this study has mapped, the enjambed periods and the hinge colons, and a listener who knows the early pointing can hear the narrator negotiate the misfits in real time.

A last performance consequence concerns the marks that print emotion. The exclamation, distributed by editors with the generosity traced above, functions in the theatre as an instruction to raise the voice's pressure, and a text exclaimed at every recognition teaches a company to shout a play whose early pointing keeps returning it to the level of speech. The laments over Juliet's body in 4.5, the tomb scene's addresses, the Prince's closing judgment all play differently at the quartos' pressure than at the modern page's, quieter and therefore, in the judgement of many productions that have experimented with the early pointing, heavier. The experiment cannot be conclusive, since no company can unhear the modern text it trained on, but its recurring result supports the study's thesis from the stage side: the pointing is a performance, layered over the play by its transmitters, and like any performance it can be renewed by going back to the earliest recoverable score and asking what its marks were for.

## How Much of the Punctuation Is Interpretation? The Reckoning

The brief for this study demanded a verdict on quantity: how much the punctuation we read is interpretation. The honest answer distinguishes the fabric from the joints. As fabric, nearly all of it is posthumous. The spelling of a modern text advertises its modernization openly, since no reader mistakes modern spelling for the author's, but the pointing conceals an equal modernization behind marks that look identical to the early ones, comma for comma and colon for colon, while meaning something systematically different by them. Of the marks on a modern page, the great majority descend through the eighteenth century's grammatical regularization and the modern editions' house practices, not from anything set in 1597 or 1599; and even the minority that reproduce the second quarto's stops reproduce them under a new system, where the same comma instructs a different construal. In that strict sense the punctuation a reader reads is interpretation throughout, a continuous editorial performance running beneath the words like a figured bass.

At the joints, where a mark chooses a sense, the answer sharpens. The passages collected in the pointing case are few, eight in a play of some three thousand lines, with the apostrophe crux at 3.2 standing beyond them as the case where a single mark has supported a library. Fewness is not unimportance. The eight passages include the heroine's two most analyzed sentences before her marriage and at her death, the hero's dying speech, the father's great tirade, and the judgment that closes the play, which is to say the joints cluster where the play is most itself. A reader's Romeo and Juliet, the one quoted in essays and carried in memory, is pointed at exactly those places by editors. The cliché trusts the punctuation because punctuation is where readers have been trained not to look for an author. Restoring its editorial origin does not dethrone the editors, whose choices are mostly good and sometimes demonstrably right. It reclassifies them: from transmitters of the text to its first interpreters, and their marks from part of the evidence to part of the argument.

### Which is safer to trust at a crux: the pointing every edition shares, or the pointing only one prints?

The shared pointing, and the reason is diagnostic rather than deferential. Where Arden, Oxford, Cambridge, and Folger set the same mark, the mark almost always construes a syntax no reading disputes, and its authority is the grammar itself. Where the editions divide, the divided mark is a flag planted on an ambiguity the early texts left open.

The reader should treat that choice as one editor's argument, weigh the alternative in the textual notes, and know that the sentence at that point has two legitimate shapes. Divergence is not a defect in the editions; it is their most honest page, the map of where interpretation lives.

The play's most famous punctuation mark makes the point in two strokes of type. "O happy dagger!" is quoted everywhere with its exclamation, on posters, in anthologies, in the titles of essays, and the exclamation has become so completely the line's property that quoting it without the mark looks like a misprint. Yet the exclamation is an editorial gift: the early texts point the address with nothing like the modern cry's force, and the dagger speech, as the case file showed, runs in the second quarto at a level, almost level, pressure, the recognition and the act carried in one motion of syntax. The cry was added by the tradition, generation by generation, each editor finding the line a little more exclamatory than the last text had left it, until the accumulated emphasis hardened into what readers take to be the author's own punctuation and, from the punctuation, the heroine's state of mind. An entire popular psychology of Juliet, transported, hysterical, operatic, leans at this line on a mark she was given by editors. The cooler Juliet of the quarto's pointing, who names the sheath and completes the act in a single grammatical breath, is available to any reader who checks, and almost no reader checks, because the exclamation has done its work too well to be suspected.

The reckoning has one further consequence, for criticism rather than for editing. Critics quote the play constantly, and quotation reproduces the quoting edition's pointing as faithfully as its words, so that arguments about emphasis, abruptness, hysteria, or calm in a speech are regularly built, in part, on marks an editor supplied. A reading of Capulet that leans on the violence of his exclamations, a reading of Juliet's recognition that leans on its cry, a reading of the Prince that leans on the finality of his full stop before the couplet: each stands partly on typographical evidence admitted without examination, the one class of evidence in Shakespeare studies that is quoted daily and sourced almost never. The discipline proposed below is therefore not a bibliographer's luxury. It is the minimum hygiene for any argument that quotes a pointed line as proof of how the line feels, since how the line feels is, at the joints, how an editor pointed it.

The practical discipline follows from the verdict and is not onerous. A reader working closely with any passage of this play should check its pointing the way its words are checked, in the textual notes where the edition records departures from the copy-text, and at the case-file passages should look at the second quarto's own pointing, in facsimile or old-spelling text, before accepting the modern mark's construal. A class or a seminar can make the divergence map tangible in an hour: one speech, the editions side by side, the quarto page open, the variant marks tabulated and argued over, and students who assemble such a comparison as a presentation, through a tool such as the [ReportMedic PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), learn faster from the exercise than from any summary that the comma is a claim. The words of the play are the playwright's, and scholarship guards them fiercely. The stops are an inheritance of printers, grammarians, and editors, and they repay the same fierce attention, because at the joints of the verse they are not recording the meaning. They are casting the deciding vote on it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch pointing case, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch pointing case is this study's table of eight passages in the play where the punctuation performs an interpretation rather than merely pacing one: Juliet's substitution of Death for Romeo at 3.2, the attachment of "the doors of breath" at 5.3, the semicolon in the happy dagger sentence, the stop before "this" in the potion speech, Capulet's exclaimed tirade, the exclaimed recognition at the feast, the semicolon lattice of the name speech, and the colon before the closing couplet. The pattern it exposes is uniform across the eight: the early texts leave each joint unmarked or marked for the voice, a modern edition must set a mark that construes the line, and the mark it sets decides an attachment, a tempo, or an address that readers then take for the play's own meaning. The case is a map of where editing becomes authorship by other means.

### Q: Who supplied the punctuation in the second quarto: the playwright or the printing house?

The printing house, completing a skeleton the playwright supplied. The second quarto of 1599 was set in Thomas Creede's shop from copy identified as the author's foul papers, but pointing in the early trade belonged to the compositor's craft: the workman read a phrase of copy, held it in memory, and set the stops as he composed the type, by his house's habits and his own. The author's hand, to judge by the sparse pointing of the manuscript pages attributed to him, supplied commas and occasional stops and expected the system to be completed by others. The second quarto's pointing is therefore a trade completion of an authorial skeleton, variable within the book, and authoritative for the occasions of the marks rather than for their execution.

### Q: What did a comma, a semicolon, a colon, and a full stop each tell an early reader about the pause to take?

Each mark named a proportioned rest for the voice. In the rhetorical system the period's grammarians describe, the comma marked the shortest pause, a brief catch of breath within an unfinished sense; the semicolon marked a longer rest, where a clause had gathered weight but the sentence had not turned; the colon marked a longer pause still, characteristically at the hinge of a balanced period where the second half was about to answer the first; and the full stop marked the complete rest, the close of the sentence's circuit. Ben Jonson's English Grammar proportions the stops in exactly this way, as rests for the reader's breath. The marks were a metronome before they were a map of syntax, and their first readers used them to time a voice, not to diagram a construction.

### Q: Why is the pointing of the early printed texts called rhetorical rather than grammatical?

Because its loyalty is to the utterance rather than to the construction. Grammatical pointing, the modern system, maps syntax: it fences subordinate clauses, joins equals with semicolons, and closes completed structures with full stops, so that a sentence can be diagrammed from its marks. Rhetorical pointing maps delivery: it proportions pauses, lifts emphasis, and marks the turns where a speaker gathers or spends breath, so that a sentence can be performed from its marks. The early texts of the play point rhetorically, allowing long sentences to accumulate behind commas and colons where a grammatical pointing would have set stops and semicolons at the clause joints. The distinction matters practically, because a rhetorical comma copied into a modern edition is read by modern eyes as a grammatical one, and faithful transcription of the mark can mistranslate its instruction.

### Q: What did Percy Simpson argue about Shakespeare's punctuation in 1911, and where has his case worn thin?

Percy Simpson, in Shakespearian Punctuation of 1911, argued that the pointing of the early Shakespeare texts is a coherent rhetorical system, light, pause-based, and indifferent to clause geometry, and that its coherence carries it back through the printing house to the author's own practice, since a dramatist writing for voices would point for voices. His passage-by-passage readings remain formidable: again and again a Folio pointing negligent by grammatical standards proves exact by rhetorical ones. The case has worn thin at the transmission, not at the system. Compositor studies, above all Charlton Hinman's work on the Folio's workmen, showed that pointing habits vary by compositor and stint in ways no authorial copy explains, and for this play the copy line runs through two print shops before the Folio, so the rhetorical system Simpson recovered is real, while the hand he assigned it to remains unproven.

### Q: What do the commas modern editions print around "not Romeo" decide in Juliet's wedding-bed line?

They decide the substitution the line performs. Juliet, imagining Romeo dead, closes her night speech with "I'll to my wedding bed; / And death, not Romeo, take my maidenhead." The second quarto prints no commas around "not Romeo"; modern editions bracket the phrase with commas, lifting it out of the clause as a correction, so that Death is grammatically installed in Romeo's place as the subject of the taking. The commas are almost certainly right about the sense, and they foreclose what the unpointed line permits for a flicker of a moment: a misreading in which death and Romeo stand yoked as joint subjects, the bridegroom and the grave exchanging places faster than syntax can police them. The editorial mark chooses the clean substitution and sacrifices the productive stumble, and readers who meet the line only in its pointed form never learn that a choice was made for them.

### Q: What does the semicolon editors set after "sheath" change in Juliet's last speech?

It changes the tempo, and therefore the grammar, of her death. "O happy dagger! / This is thy sheath; there rust, and let me die" falls in most modern editions into three separated beats: the assertion that the dagger has found its sheath, the command that it rust there, the surrender to death. The second quarto sets a single comma after "sheath," and the sentence runs in one motion from recognition to act, the naming, the commanding, and the dying carried on a single breath. The semicolon makes the death ceremonial, a rite performed in stages by a girl arranging her own monument; the quarto's comma makes it continuous, an act completed before it can be reconsidered. Neither tempo is the author's provable choice. The one on the page belongs to Creede's shop as revised by the edition's house style, and it directs every performance that trusts the text.

### Q: How does the pointing of "the doors of breath" decide whom Romeo is addressing as he dies?

It decides whether his last address stays inside his own body or wheels outward to Death. Romeo's dying speech summons his frame in a triad: "Eyes, look your last! / Arms, take your last embrace! and, lips, O you / The doors of breath, seal with a righteous kiss." Editions point "the doors of breath" into apposition with "lips," and the parallel structure supports them, eyes, arms, and lips each addressed in turn. The early texts leave the attachment unmarked, and unmarked the phrase can lean the other way, with "O you" turning from the body toward the Death who has been personified throughout the scene, so that the kiss seals the bargain with him. The apposition commas are very likely right; they are also an invisible decision that the dying man's last words are spoken only to himself, and a reader who has never seen the joint open will never know it could close differently.

### Q: What does the word "this" point at in Juliet's "No, no; this shall forbid it," and why does the pointing matter?

It points at the knife. In the potion speech at 4.3 Juliet works through the terrors of the vault, checks herself with the doubled refusal, and resolves that if the mixture fails she will not be married to Paris in the morning: "No, no; this shall forbid it. Lie thou there," the last words addressed to the knife she lays beside her on the bed. The pointing matters because the deictic "this" has no noun to carry it, and the strength of the stop an edition sets before it governs how firmly the reader's eye is thrown from the pronoun to the object on the stage. A light stop lets "this" float toward her resolve or her will; the sharpened semicolon or colon most editions print pins it to the blade, making the sentence a piece of stage business as much as a thought. The mark does the pointing, in every sense.

### Q: How lightly did the hand attributed to Shakespeare punctuate the Sir Thomas More pages?

Very lightly, and the lightness is the best surviving guide to the author's own practice. The three pages of the manuscript play of Sir Thomas More written in the hand widely attributed to Shakespeare, Hand D, carry a pointing in which commas do nearly all the internal work, full points appear mainly where a speech or a completed thought ends, and the verse line is left to carry the timing that a modern text assigns to semicolons and stops. The sample is small and the attribution, though it holds the scholarly centre of gravity, has been argued over; but nothing in the surviving dramatic manuscripts of the milieu contradicts it. Dramatists pointed in skeleton. The fleshing out of the stops, into the rhetorical completions of the quartos and then into the grammatical systems of the editions, was the work of scribes, compositors, and editors, which is why the pointing of the play cannot be quoted as the author's.

### Q: How much of the punctuation in a modern edition of the play survives from the second quarto?

Less than the page suggests, and none of it unchanged in meaning. Even an edition that professes to follow the copy-text's accidentals, on the principle of Brian Gibbons's Arden edition, modernizes the pointing wherever the quarto's marks would mislead a grammatical reader, and the freer Oxford practice of Jill Levenson's edition repunctuates toward modern norms as a matter of programme. What survives is a selection: the stops at joints where the quarto's rhetorical mark and the modern grammatical mark happen to coincide, mostly the clear sentence boundaries. And survival of the mark is not survival of the instruction, since a comma retained from 1599 is read by a modern eye as a syntactic fence where its first reader took a catch of breath. The honest summary is that a modern text's pointing is a modern composition with period quotations embedded in it.

### Q: Where do the dashes printed in modern editions come from, when the early texts barely use the mark?

They come from the editors, and chiefly from the editorial centuries after the quartos. The dash as an instrument of interruption, of the aside, and of the self-breaking thought is effectively absent from the play's early texts; it entered Shakespeare editing as the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries developed a typography of the speaking voice and needed a sign for the speech that stops mid-course. This play, whose dialogue lives by interruption, gathered the mark fastest: Mercutio cut off by Romeo, the Nurse cut off by Juliet, Juliet breaking off her own plea in her mother's chamber. Each dash is an editor's placard announcing that the speech breaks at that joint, and the placard is usually right about the drama. It remains an editorial insertion with no warrant in the copy, staging printed where the early texts trusted the actors to find the break unaided.

### Q: Why does the pointing of the play's prose scenes wander more freely than the pointing of its verse?

Because the verse line disciplines the pointing and prose has no such discipline. A compositor setting verse works inside a grid of line endings that already imply a skeleton of pauses, and an editor repunctuating verse can lean on the metre to check the marks. Prose offers no grid: the servants' opening sparring, Mercutio's flights, and the Nurse's expeditions run at whatever length the joke or the narrative requires, and the quartos' pointing in those passages is the compositor's ear for timing, set phrase by phrase with nothing to contradict it. It is correspondingly the most freely rewritten pointing in modern editions, which parse the prose into tidy subordination the early texts never imposed. The cost is peculiar to comedy, where the laugh lives at the joint the mark governs: reparsing the Nurse's syntax alters the placement of her punch words, and the tidiness is paid for in timing.

### Q: What happens to a speech's open ambiguity when an editor has to print one pointing and no other?

It collapses, silently, into the reading the editor chose. Print is a single-valued medium: a compositor's modern heir cannot set two commas in one place, so where the early texts leave an attachment open, an address undecided, or an ambiguity standing between two construals, the edition must point one way, and the other way ceases to exist for every reader who does not consult the textual notes. The happy dagger's tempo, the doors of breath's attachment, the substitution in the wedding-bed line all reach most readers pre-collapsed. This is not an argument against editing; an unreadable text serves nobody. It is an argument for the apparatus: the places where pointing has collapsed an ambiguity are precisely the places an edition owes its reader a note, recording the early pointing and the alternative it excludes, with the same prominence given to a changed word.

### Q: Where do scholarly editions record the pointing they have changed, and how should a reader use those records?

In the textual notes, the collations printed at the foot of the page or in an appendix, where each departure from the copy-text is keyed by act, scene, and line to the reading of the early witnesses. Practice varies in candour: some editions record pointing changes that affect sense while passing silently over routine repunctuation, others aspire to record every substantive departure and treat weighty pointing among them, and the reader has to learn each edition's threshold from its textual introduction. The records are used by working the passage in both directions: read the modern line with its editorial pointing, then read the note, restore the quarto's mark in the mind, and construe the line again. Where the sense moves, the reader has found one of the joints where the edition is an interpretation. Where the sense holds still, the pointing was plumbing after all.

### Q: Why can a heavy editorial stop fight the verse line it stands in?

Because the line is itself a timing instrument, and a stop instructs a pause the line has not earned. George T. Wright's analysis of Shakespeare's metrical art shows the pentameter working as the primary score of attention: line endings, caesuras, and the pattern of end-stopped and enjambed movement tell a speaker where energy gathers and where it spills forward. A full stop set mid-line by a grammatical editor closes a structure the verse is still carrying open, and a strong stop at a line ending where the syntax runs on instructs the eye to halt at the exact point the metre instructs the voice to continue. Juliet's accumulating periods suffer most: pointed heavily, they parse beautifully and ride badly. The actor resolves the fight in favour of the line, silently repunctuating in delivery, which is one more proof that the pointing on the page is an interpretation competing with the verse's own, not a transcription of it.

### Q: What did Ben Jonson's grammar teach readers that the stops were for?

It taught them that the stops were rests for the breath, proportioned like the rests in music. Jonson's English Grammar, published in 1640 in the folio of his works, treats punctuation under the art of reading: the comma marks the shortest pause, a brief suspension where the sense is unfinished; the semicolon holds longer; the colon longer again, at the balance point of a period; the full stop closes the sentence and lets the breath out entirely. Charles Butler's grammar of 1634 proportions the pauses in the same spirit. Nothing in the account concerns the diagramming of clauses; the marks answer the question of how long a reader should hold each joint open so the sense arrives as utterance. Readers of the play's first editions brought exactly this training to its pages, which is why those pages can be pointed so much more lightly than a modern text: the voice, governed by proportioned rests, did the work the modern comma lattice does for the eye.

### Q: How does the colon before "For never was a story of more woe" change the work the closing couplet does?

It decides whether the couplet explains the judgment or replaces it. The Prince's closing speech ends its sentences of pardon and punishment and then delivers the play's epitaph: "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo." The early texts set a colon or a lighter stop before the couplet, and editions divide among colon, semicolon, and full stop. With a colon, the couplet arrives as the reason for the sentence, the warrant the Prince offers for his distribution of pardon and punishment: hear this story, and the judgments explain themselves. With a full stop, the couplet stands sealed off as pure epitaph, offered after judgment is complete and explaining nothing. The difference is the difference between a magistrate justifying a verdict and a chorus pronouncing over the bodies, and it hangs on the single mark an edition chooses to print at the joint.

### Q: Why do old-spelling and facsimile editions matter to anyone studying the play's punctuation?

Because they are the only places the evidence survives in usable form. A modernized edition has already made every pointing decision on the reader's behalf, and its page cannot show what the second quarto's marks were at the joints where the modern marks differ; the evidence is recoverable only from the early page itself, in a facsimile of the quarto, in an old-spelling edition that reproduces the copy-text's accidentals, or in a digital image of the surviving copies. Lukas Erne's edition of the first quarto as a document in its own right belongs to the same recovery. Study of the pointing therefore begins one shelf away from the reading text, and the transfer of the early texts into freely available digital facsimiles has changed the study's economics: checking an editor's comma against Creede's comma, a labour once confined to great libraries, is open to any reader who suspects a joint of having been decided invisibly.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch pointing case to argue that punctuation is interpretation?

Begin from the table's pattern rather than from the general claim. Select two or three cases of different kinds, an attachment case such as the doors of breath, a tempo case such as the happy dagger's semicolon, a substitution case such as the commas around "not Romeo," and show for each that the early texts leave the joint open, that editions must close it to print at all, and that the closure decides something readers attribute to the play. Then widen to the doctrines: Greg's copy-text defence of the early accidentals, the Oxford modernizing programme and Stanley Wells's case for it, and Leah Marcus's warning that invisible repunctuation manufactures the author readers think they are quoting. The essay's verdict should be the study's: punctuation in this play is a continuous editorial performance beneath the words, mostly serviceable, occasionally decisive, and always argument where it matters most.
