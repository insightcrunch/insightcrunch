The upper stage and the discovery space are the two features of the Elizabethan playhouse that Romeo and Juliet tests hardest, and the play returns a split verdict on them. The upper level passes every test the texts can set: the dialogue measures its height, the early quartos place a window in it, and two of the tragedy's defining scenes cannot be played without it. The discovery space passes a weaker test. One direction in the First Quarto, and one only, proves that a curtained enclosure stood at the back of the stage and that Juliet's bed stood inside it; beyond that single proof, the curtained recess fades into inference, and the deep inner room of older theatre history fades further still, into a conjecture the play never asks for. That graded result is the argument of this article. The height is the building's. The curtain is the building's, used once and used decisively. The room is the scholars'.

![The upper stage and discovery space of the Elizabethan playhouse - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The distinction matters because the two features are habitually drawn as a pair, two halves of one facade, as though the evidence for each were equally firm. It is not. A playhouse is a machine for showing, and its parts earn belief in proportion to what the surviving record forces a sceptic to concede. For the upper stage, the record forces nearly everything: a playing level above the main platform, reached from inside the tiring house, fronted by an opening that could serve as a window, and high enough that a man below must speak of climbing to reach it. For the discovery space, the record forces one effect, concealment and revelation behind a curtain, and then falls silent about the architecture that produced the effect. An honest account of the play's first staging has to hold those two grades apart scene by scene, because the scenes themselves hold them apart. The window scene spends the proven feature lavishly. The potion scene spends the half-proven one exactly once. The tomb scene, the place where a discovery space is most often claimed, spends neither, and is carried instead by properties carried in men's hands. This article grades the evidence for each feature, scene by scene, against the quartos, the building contracts, the one surviving drawing of a playhouse interior, and the reconstructions that have had to turn the argument into timber. Its verdict can be stated in advance: the architecture shaped the writing at the points where the architecture is certain, and the writing declines to certify the architecture at the points where it is not. The play is a witness that knows the difference between what it used and what it merely touched, and the criticism of the play should learn the same discipline.

## What Is the Upper Stage, and What Is a Discovery Space?

The terms need fixing before the evidence can be weighed, because the quarrel this article adjudicates is largely a quarrel about what the words should be allowed to mean. The upper stage is the playing area at the first-floor level of the tiring-house facade, the wall of doors and openings that closed the rear of the platform in every purpose-built London playhouse. Actors reached it from inside the tiring house, the building's dressing and storage block, and appeared there at a window or along a gallery front, visible over the main stage and under the roof that covered it. The discovery space, in the strict sense this article will enforce, is a curtained opening at ground level in the same facade, behind which a person or a property could be concealed and then revealed by drawing the curtain. Neither term is Elizabethan. The players who used these features left no name for the recess, and the phrase discovery space is a modern label for an effect their stage directions occasionally record. That modernity is not a defect in itself, but it licenses a carelessness the older scholarship fell into: once the recess had a name, it acquired a size, a depth, a furniture of its own, until some accounts describe an inner stage, a second acting area sunk behind the facade with room for whole scenes to be played inside it, curtained off from the platform like a stage within the stage. The evidence will not carry that room. What it carries is an opening.

### Was the window built into the playhouse, or carried on as scenery?

The window was built into the playhouse, a permanent opening in the tiring-house facade at the upper level, dressed by use rather than by carpentry. Nothing in the record suggests a frame carried on and struck between scenes; everything suggests a fixture, one aperture in a wall of apertures, which became a casement when the dialogue called it one.

The same aperture served as a stretch of city wall or a castle battlement in other plays of the repertory. Its permanence is the point: the playwright wrote toward a hole in a known wall, and trusted the words spoken around it to furnish the room behind it.

The terminology quarrel has a datable modern history. Richard Hosley, in a Shakespeare Survey paper of 1959 titled "The Discovery-Space in Shakespeare's Globe", proposed that the tiring house of the first Globe resembled the facade drawn at the Swan playhouse in 1596, and that its wide stage doors, perhaps seven or eight feet across and hung to open outward, could themselves disclose the space behind them. A discovery, on Hosley's account, needed no separate chamber at all: open a broad door, or hang curtains across the opened doorway, and the tiring-house interior nearest the facade became visible as a shallow discovered place. Hosley was careful to separate that modest mechanism from the inner stage of the older reconstruction drawings, and the care was needed, because the inner stage he rejected had been drawn large for decades. The Internet Shakespeare Editions, surveying the same ground for the reconstructed Globe, states the case against the large version plainly: early reconstructions assumed an inner stage where intimate action took place behind a curtain, but the evidence argues against it, not least because the highest-paying spectators, seated where they could least afford a blocked view, would have seen none of the action supposedly played inside it. The deep room, on this showing, is a Victorian inheritance, the proscenium stage of the nineteenth century projected backward onto a platform that had no use for it. Between Hosley's wide door and the Victorian room stands the position this article will test against the play: a curtained central opening, purpose-made, shallow, used sparingly, and proved for this play by exactly one direction. The definitions fixed, the evidence can be graded.

The period's own vocabulary, where it survives, points the same way, toward cloth and thresholds rather than rooms. A traverse was a curtain that ran across an opening; an arras was a hanging, the tapestry behind which a man might hide, and through which, in another tragedy of the repertory, a hidden listener is killed; within was the directions' ordinary word for the unseen interior of the tiring house, the place from which voices call and from which figures enter as if from inside a dwelling. None of these words names a chamber. Across the repertory, the directions speak of figures discovered, of beds and tombs named as properties rather than as places, of curtains drawn and hangings opened, in a dialect that consistently describes an effect at the facade's surface. When the First Quarto of this play writes within the curtains, it is using the house dialect exactly: a preposition of concealment and a plural of cloth. Readers who translate that dialect into architecture should translate with care, and a careful translation yields hangings at an opening, and no more.

## The Evidence Problem: A Contract, a Drawing, and a Silence

No Elizabethan playhouse facade survives, and no builder's elevation of one was ever drawn for the record. What survives is a scatter of unlike documents, each eloquent about something adjacent to the question and silent about the question itself, and the first discipline of the subject is to keep their grades separate. At the top of the scale stand the building contracts, legal instruments that specify dimensions because money turned on them. Beside them stands one drawing, the view of the Swan playhouse made by Johannes de Witt in about 1596 and preserved in a copy by his friend Aernout van Buchell, the only picture of the inside of a public playhouse to come down from the period. Below these stand the stage directions of the printed plays, evidence of practice rather than of carpentry, and below those the archaeology of excavated foundations, which has recovered footprints and stage positions at the Rose and a fragment of the Globe while leaving every facade unexcavated and unknown. A claim about the upper stage or the discovery space is worth exactly the grade of its best document, and the documents are unevenly distributed between the two features. The contracts and the drawing are generous about levels and doors. They are silent about curtains. The directions are generous about one curtain in one play. They are silent about dimensions. The whole quarrel lives in that unevenness.

The richest single document is the contract drawn up on 8 January 1600 between Philip Henslowe, Edward Alleyn, and the carpenter Peter Street for the Fortune playhouse in Cripplegate. Street had built the Globe for the Burbage consortium the year before, and the Fortune contract leans on that building as its model at every turn: the Fortune's stage and tiring house were to be made in the proportions of the Globe's, the stairs and divisions contrived as they were contrived in the Bankside house. The contract fixes the Fortune's frame at eighty feet square outside and fifty-five within, in three storeys of twelve, eleven, and nine feet, and it fixes the stage at forty-three feet in length, extending in breadth to the middle of the yard, with a shadow or cover over it and a tiring house behind it fitted with glazed windows and lights. Translated with due caution to the Chamberlain's Men's houses, this document proves the class of building this play inhabited: a platform of known generous size, a roof over its rear portion, and a tiring house whose stacked storeys put a floor, and therefore a playing level, directly above the stage doors at a height set by the first storey's twelve feet. What the contract never mentions is a discovery space. It itemizes gentlemen's rooms, twopenny rooms, stairs, conveyances, windows, and iron pikes along the lower storey, and no curtained recess appears. That silence cuts both ways and must be read honestly in both directions: a contract specifies what a carpenter is paid to build, and a curtain across an existing opening is a hanging, not a building work, arguably beneath the document's notice. Silence in the contract is not proof of absence. It is proof only that the recess, if it existed, was not architecture expensive enough to itemize, which is itself a fact about its likely scale.

The second contract, drawn for the Hope playhouse in 1613, adds a different kind of information: it shows the fittings in motion. The Hope rose on the Bankside site of the Bear Garden under the same theatrical management, and its contract specifies a stage that could be carried away entire, so that the yard could revert to the baiting of bears, together with a cover over the stage carried without posts rising from the platform. A removable stage is a fact of the first importance for the discovery question, because it proves that the playing surface and its furniture were carpentered assemblies whose configuration could change with the building's business, and it makes the portable fitting inherently plausible: a trade accustomed to stages that came up and went down would not regard a hung curtain or a traverse as a solemn architectural commitment. The Hope's postless cover carries the complementary lesson for the upper works, since it shows the shadow over the stage being re-engineered between one house and the next, which means the region above the platform was understood by the builders as working machinery and not as a fixed pediment. Between them, the contracts describe a building culture of adjustable timber, in which the permanent facts are the frame, the yard, the galleries, and the tiring house, and nearly everything the actors touched was negotiable. The discovery space, on the contracts' showing, belongs among the negotiables. That placement does not diminish it. It explains the documents' silence, and it explains why the plays, and only the plays, can testify to its use.

The drawing is the second document, and it is the sceptics' strongest exhibit. De Witt's Swan shows the platform, the two columns carrying the stage roof, the tiring-house facade with its doors, and above the doors a gallery running across the facade, occupied by seated figures. The upper level is therefore not a conjecture: a contemporary drew it, in a playhouse of exactly this play's years. Yet the drawing shows no curtained central opening at stage level, no recess, no traverse. Its facade offers doors and the gallery above them, and if the Swan was typical, then any discovery at the Swan was made through or behind those doors, as Hosley's model requires. Two cautions govern the drawing's use, both carried by the series' account of [Romeo and Juliet on the Elizabethan Stage](/2027/08/06/romeo-juliet-elizabethan-stage/), which grades the original staging evidence for the play as a whole. The drawing depicts the Swan, a house of the Admiral's Men's orbit, not the Theatre or the Curtain where the Chamberlain's Men played this tragedy; and it is a copy of a sketch, made by a visitor more interested in the building's classical dignity than in its working fittings, so that its omissions may record inattention rather than absence. A curtain is precisely the kind of fitting a dignity-minded sketch would drop. The drawing thus proves the upper level and fails to disprove the recess, a split result that mirrors the whole evidence problem in miniature.

The third class of evidence, archaeology, contributes a floor plan and a rebuke. The excavations of 1989 uncovered much of the Rose playhouse and a small portion of the Globe, enough to fix stage positions and yard dimensions and to correct several confident reconstructions, but the tiring-house facades stood where later building has destroyed or sealed the ground, and no spade has tested the question this article asks. What archaeology teaches is methodological: the parts of these buildings that survive are the parts that were buried, and everything above ground level is inference built on the contracts, the drawing, and the plays. The plays are therefore not a supplement to the architectural record. For the upper works of the playhouse, they are the architectural record, and they must be read with a lawyer's care, direction by direction and quarto by quarto, because a stage direction is a tradesman's note that something was done, from which the thing that made it possible has to be deduced.

The two early quartos of this play illustrate the lawyer's problem in its sharpest form, because they are different kinds of witness. The Second Quarto of 1599 announces itself on its title page as newly corrected, augmented, and amended, and its copy stands close to the playwright's own papers; its directions are few, formal, and concerned with entrances and exits rather than with the furniture of effects. The First Quarto of 1597 is shorter by some eight hundred lines, its verse often garbled where the Second is sound, and the prevailing account of it makes it a reported text, a version carried out of the theatre by memory and set down from performance. For the words of the play, the First Quarto is the witness to cross-examine hardest. For the business of the play, the relation reverses: a text that descends from performance remembers what was done, and its directions have the character of a prompt tradition rather than of a study. This is why the bed within the curtains, standing in the First Quarto alone, cannot be waved away as a bad-quarto corruption. Corruption garbles what a reporter mishears; it does not invent the furniture a scene was played with, and a direction that solves a staging problem, how to end a scene on a motionless body and clear the stage for the next, has the stamp of the playhouse on it. The lawyer's rule for this article follows: the Second Quarto governs what the play says, the First Quarto testifies to what the stage did, and where the two diverge on a matter of staging, the divergence itself is the evidence, marking the line between the text's requirements and the company's practice. The next three sections read this play's directions in that spirit, taking the features in the order of their certainty: the window above, the chamber aloft, the curtained bed, and then the tomb, where the certainty runs out.

## The Window Above: What Act 2 Scene 2 Actually Requires

The orchard scene is the most famous use of the upper stage in English drama, and its evidence is unusually complete, because for once the dialogue and the directions testify together and can be checked against each other. The directions first. In the First Quarto of 1597, Juliet's entrance is marked at a window: the quarto's wording gives the scene its fixture in plain terms, Enter Juliet at a window. The Second Quarto of 1599, the fuller and more authoritative text, prints its directions more sparingly at this point, and the word above that stands in modern editions of the scene, Enter Juliet above, at a window, is an editorial completion, assembled by later editors from the First Quarto's window, from the dialogue's insistence on height, and from the parallel direction the Second Quarto itself supplies for the dawn scene of Act 3. The editorial bracket is not a scandal; it is the honest sign of a composite. But it matters for the architectural argument, because it means the upper stage in Act 2 Scene 2 rests on three legs of different strengths: a First Quarto direction naming a window, a Second Quarto dialogue that cannot be played at ground level, and a Second Quarto direction elsewhere that uses the word aloft. The full textual anatomy of these directions belongs to the series' study of [Stage Directions in the Early Texts](/2026/06/26/stage-directions-early-texts/); the architectural consequences are drawn here.

The dialogue is the strongest leg, and it deserves to be read as a specification. Romeo opens the scene by looking up: "But, soft! what light through yonder window breaks? / It is the east, and Juliet is the sun" (2.2.2-3). A window is named before Juliet appears at it, and it is placed by the speech in the upper air of the stage picture, the east of a stage whose sun is a girl at a casement. When the lovers speak, the verse keeps surveying the vertical distance it has to cross. Juliet, told that Romeo has scaled the garden, states the measurement as a threat: "The orchard walls are high and hard to climb, / And the place death, considering who thou art, / If any of my kinsmen find thee here" (2.2.66-68). Romeo answers in the same units: "With love's light wings did I o'erperch these walls; / For stony limits cannot hold love out" (2.2.69-70). The scene's poetry of ascent is not decoration on a staging; it is the staging, transcribed. A Juliet at ground level, behind a garden fence of canvas, would make liars of both speeches in the hearing of an audience standing close enough to judge the distance with its own eyes. The platform theatres placed actor and spectator in one shared daylight and one shared space, and the scene exploits that sharing: the yard could look from Romeo's face up to Juliet's and measure the gulf the play is about. The upper stage is the only feature of the building that the orchard scene strictly requires, and it requires it without qualification. Whatever else in the facade is conjecture, the level itself, with an opening in it from which a figure could appear, withdraw, and return, is proved by this scene as firmly as anything in theatre history is proved.

### How far above Romeo did Juliet stand?

Far enough that the distance reads as a wall, not a step. The Fortune contract's first storey stands twelve feet high, and the floor of the upper playing level sits at roughly that height above the platform; the precise sill height for the Theatre or the Curtain is nowhere recorded, and any figure offered as a measurement is invented.

What the documents license is a scale, not a number: a full storey of vertical separation, a girl framed over a man's head by the height of a room, near enough for whispers to carry in a quiet yard and far enough that no hand can reach her. The scene's dramaturgy needs exactly that interval, the reach of a voice and the denial of a touch.

The scene also requires the upper level to be a working space and not a mere frame, and its pattern of exits proves the point. Juliet appears, speaks, is called away, returns, is called away again, and returns a second time before the final parting, a rhythm of advance and withdrawal that the Nurse's voice conducts from inside the house: the Nurse calls within, and the direction within is itself architectural evidence, fixing a speaking position inside the tiring house from which a voice could reach the window's occupant and the audience together. For that rhythm to play at speed, the window must open off an interior the actress can actually move in, with a path between the casement and the room behind it short enough for the returns to land as interruptions rather than as journeys. The upper level, in other words, was not a shelf. It was the upper floor of a working building, connected to the tiring house's stairs and chambers, and the scene's construction, its quick alternation of presence and absence at the window, is unintelligible unless the connection existed. Here the inference runs from effect to structure with unusual safety, because the alternative, an actress scaling a ladder behind the facade in view of nobody and in haste, contradicts the tempo the text imposes. The verdict on Act 2 Scene 2 can be carried in one sentence: the words demand height, the First Quarto names the window, the pattern of returns demands an interior behind it, and no element of the three demands a balcony, a gallery used as a stage floor, or any name the period did not use. The separate question of what later centuries built onto that window is owned by the series' staging study, [There Is No Balcony: Staging Act 2 Scene 2](/2027/12/31/no-balcony-staging-act-2-scene-2/), and is touched here only where the architecture is concerned: the building offered a window in a wall, and everything richer is furnishing.

## The Second Height: The Chamber Aloft in Act 3 Scene 5

The upper stage earns its keep a second time at the tragedy's hinge, and the second use is evidentially precious because it comes from the Second Quarto itself. The dawn parting of Act 3 Scene 5 opens with the lovers together in Juliet's chamber after the wedding night, and the Second Quarto's staging of the scene places the chamber aloft; the First Quarto, characteristically blunter about the mechanics, sends Romeo down at the scene's end in the direction He goeth down, the descent performed in view. Two quartos, two habits of notation, one architecture: the chamber from which Romeo descends is the window's room of Act 2, reached by the ladder of cords the Nurse carried on in Act 3 Scene 2 and vacated by climbing down its outside. The play's geography closes like a hand. The height that kept the lovers apart in the orchard is the height that shelters them for one night, and the same vertical interval then expels Romeo into exile as the day breaks. A modern stage must build this; the Elizabethan stage merely reopened it. The window that had framed a girl out of reach frames a wife at dawn, and the audience that had measured the distance in Act 2 measures its collapse and restoration in Act 3, when Romeo stands below a second time and the farewell is spoken across the gap that the marriage briefly abolished.

Between the two heights stands the ladder of cords, the facade's one moving part, and it deserves a sentence in an architectural account because it converts the building's fixed interval into a passage the lovers can use. The cords are commissioned in Act 2, carried on by the Nurse in Act 3 Scene 2 as a property in her lap, and put to their work between the scenes, so that Romeo's ascent to the chamber happens, like most of the tragedy's decisive actions, offstage and by arrangement; what the audience sees is the descent, the lover let down at dawn from the window he could not reach unaided in Act 2. The property's full history belongs to the series' study of the play's stage properties; the architectural point is narrower. A facade whose levels can be connected by a purchasable length of cord is a facade understood by its users as a system, wall and window and rope together, and the play's second use of the upper stage is unintelligible without the humble technology that made the first use's prohibition escapable. Buildings in this theatre are not backdrops. They are equipment, and the equipment includes the means of defeating the building.

Juliet's line at the window, "Then, window, let day in, and let life out" (3.5.41), compresses the whole architectural argument into a single apostrophe. The window is addressed as a working part of the house, a thing that opens and shuts, admits and excludes, and the line is spoken to the fixture itself at the moment the fixture resumes its first function of separation. It is worth pausing on how little scenery such writing needs and how much building it assumes. The speech does not describe the window, because the window is present; it does not explain the descent, because the descent is happening; the poetry spends itself on what the architecture cannot show, the equivalence of daylight and death, and leaves the architecture to be itself in silence. That economy is the signature of a playwright writing for a known house. Shakespeare had the Chamberlain's Men's facades in his ear the way a composer has an instrument's range in his hand, and Act 3 Scene 5 is written inside the range of the upper stage as fluently as Act 2 Scene 2, with the additional refinement that the second scene remembers the first. The two scenes are a pair of variations on one architectural fact, and the fact's certainty, resting on both quartos and on the dialogue's own surveying, is the firmest ground in this entire subject. Whatever is concluded about curtained recesses below, the level above carried the play's two most consequential interviews, and any reconstruction that treats the upper stage as an occasional convenience rather than a principal playing area has the play's own weighting against it.

### Did any spectator sit where Juliet stood?

Possibly, and the possibility disciplines the imagination. The gallery crossing the facade in de Witt's drawing is occupied by seated figures, and the contracts speak of gentlemen's rooms among the frame's divisions, so the upper level was not reserved to actors by any law of the building: parts of the over-stage space could serve privileged spectators.

What no document does is assign this play's window to such a room, and the room over the stage in some houses was a place from which to see and be seen. The prudent conclusion is that the facade's upper storey was a mixed territory, playing space and seating sharing one floor behind one wall, and that Juliet's window was an opening in a wall whose other openings might hold an audience. The scene loses nothing by the admission and gains its true period flavour: she appeared at a window in a working building, not on a dedicated balcony built for her scene alone.

## Within the Curtains: The Bed of Act 4 and the One Firm Proof

At ground level the play's evidence changes character completely, and the change is the heart of the subject. For the lower facade there is no dialogue that surveys a recess, no pair of quartos agreeing on a fixture, only a single stage direction in a single early text, and the discipline of this article is to give that direction its full weight and not one dram more. In the First Quarto's version of the potion scene, Juliet, alone in her chamber, drinks the Friar's distilled liquor with the words "Romeo, I come! this do I drink to thee", and the quarto directs: She falls upon her bed, within the curtains (4.3). The direction does not appear in the Second Quarto or in the Folio, whose texts end the scene without it, and that distribution is the first fact to hold steady: the clearest evidence for a discovery space in the whole of Romeo and Juliet stands in the quarto that scholars treat as the reported, performance-derived text, the version closest to what was done on a stage and furthest from the author's papers. A sceptic may discount the First Quarto's words where they garble the verse. A direction that describes a piece of stage business, in a text whose whole character is that of a performance remembered, discounts badly. The bed was within curtains. The curtains were a fitting of the stage. On this point the record is not inference but report.

Richard Dutton, in Shakespeare's Theatre: A History, reads the direction with the economy it deserves and draws from it the model this article adopts. The bed, on his showing, stood at the front of the curtained space, so that Juliet could fall directly onto it and the curtains could fall shut to end the scene; she is found there two scenes later, when the household comes to wake her for Paris and the discovery of her apparent death is made by opening what had closed over her. Nothing in the sequence requires other characters to enter the space, and Dutton is explicit that the discovery space, wherever it appears in the period's directions, was used sparingly and held little action inside itself: it was a device for showing and hiding, not a room for playing in. That reading converts the First Quarto's direction into a precise architectural minimum. Somewhere in the facade at stage level there was an opening wide enough to frame a bed, hung with curtains that could close across it, with just enough depth behind the hangings for the bed to stand and a girl to lie on it unseen. Everything beyond that minimum, the depth of the recess, its permanent or improvised construction, its use in other scenes of this play or other plays of the repertory, is further inference, to be graded as such and not smuggled into the certainty of the bed.

### What closed behind Juliet when she drank the potion?

A traverse, a curtain drawn across the opening, and the scene closed with it. The bed stood at the front of the curtained opening with Juliet fallen across it; the hangings fell shut over her, and the platform stood clear for the brief scene of wedding preparations that follows.

Two scenes later the same hangings opened on the same bed for the Nurse's discovery, which is why the potion scene and the waking scene must be read as one architectural event in two acts: the curtain that ends Act 4 Scene 3 is the curtain whose reopening starts the catastrophe of Act 4 Scene 5, and the audience's knowledge travels through the closed cloth while the household's ignorance gathers outside it.

The Second Quarto's silence about the curtains is the counterweight, and it must be given its due rather than explained away. The fuller text, printed from copy far closer to the playwright's hand, manages Act 4 without the direction, and a staging built on the Second Quarto alone could thrust the bed forward onto the platform, discover Juliet by the Nurse's approach rather than by a curtain's opening, and lose nothing the words demand. The two quartos therefore certify different things. The First certifies that on some stage, in the play's first years, a curtained enclosure held the bed. The Second certifies that the play as its author left it does not depend on that enclosure. Both certifications can be true together, and their conjunction is the correct statement of the evidence: the discovery effect was available to the company and was used for this play's most secret action, the feigned death, whose whole meaning is concealment; but the effect was a resource, not a foundation, and the text was written to survive its absence on a bare platform with a bed carried on. That is why the discovery space, as a claim about this play, must be stated in the grammar of occasion rather than of structure. The upper stage is structural; the play leans its weight on it twice. The curtained opening is occasional; the play touches it once, in one quarto, for the one action in the tragedy that is, in the strictest sense, a hiding. The fit between the effect and the action is so exact that it argues design rather than accident, a playwright reaching for the facade's one secret place at the one moment his plot becomes a secret. Design of that kind does not require a deep room. It requires a curtain that works.

## The Tomb: A Door Forced Open, Not a Room Revealed

The tomb is where the discovery space is most often claimed for this play, and it is where the claim is weakest, a combination that has distorted the whole debate and that the evidence, read cold, corrects. The inference runs that Juliet laid in the Capulet vault must have been discovered as she had been discovered in her bed, the curtain opening on the tomb as on the chamber, and the inference has a surface logic: the play ends with a concealed interior breached, a body displayed, a tableau assembled. Richard Dutton, who gives the bed its full evidentiary value, marks the tomb inference at its true grade in words this article adopts as a model of the discipline: the text is far less clear, and the discovery-space tomb is an inference that may be drawn but may not be quoted as the play's direction. No early text directs a curtain at the tomb. No direction in either quarto places Juliet's body inside an enclosure that opens. What the texts direct instead is a sequence of properties and forcings on the open platform, and the property plot, the list of things carried on in men's hands, is the firmest ground this scene offers, firmer than any room that has been drawn behind it.

Read the scene's warrants in order. Paris comes to the vault before Romeo, and his business is placed outside it: he strews flowers at the tomb, "Sweet flower, with flowers thy bridal bed I strew" (5.3.12), and the strewing is done at the monument's face because Paris has brought no tool to open it and no intention of entering; he withdraws to hide when he hears Romeo approach, and the hiding, like the strewing, is platform business. Romeo arrives equipped, and the equipment is the scene's architectural statement: a torch, a mattock, and a crow of iron, the tools of a man who means to force a door that does not open for him. His speech over the vault is the forcing transcribed into verse, "Thou detestable maw, thou womb of death, / Gorged with the dearest morsel of the earth, / Thus I enforce thy rotten jaws to open" (5.3.45-47), and the First Quarto makes the business explicit in its direction: Romeo opens the tomb. Opens it: the verb of a door attacked from outside, not of a curtain drawn from within. The vault in the early staging is a front, a face with a door in it, and the tragedy of the scene is played at that face and then in the open, as Paris challenges, the fight spills across the platform, and Romeo carries Paris's body in, an entrance through the forced door that takes the action inside only in the sense that the platform's fiction relocates, the oldest convention of the bare stage. What the audience must see at the end is not an interior but an assembly: the bodies laid in view, the Watch, the Friar with his lantern, crow, and spade, the Prince, the fathers, the whole city gathered around a display that the closing speeches address as a spectacle, "See what a scourge is laid upon your hate" (5.3.292). A discovery space a few feet deep could not hold that assembly, and the text never asks one to. It asks for a door that resists, tools that open it, and room in the open air for Verona to stand around its dead.

What stood for the vault on the platform is a question the property discipline can narrow even where it cannot close it, and narrowing is worthwhile, because the candidates are few and their grades differ. Three means exhaust the period's resources. The first is a property monument, a framed structure set at the platform's rear with a door that could be barred and forced; the Admiral's Men's surviving property inventory of 1598 lists a tomb among its holdings, which proves that the repertory's stages possessed such objects as stock and that a tomb could be a thing a company owned rather than a room a building contained. The second is a facade door dressed for the night, the central opening hung not with a curtain but with the vault's painted face, so that the forcing is done at the tiring-house wall itself and the interior Romeo enters is the fiction an audience grants any doorway. The third is the curtained opening with its hangings drawn aside and its shallow depth black within, which concedes the discovery space a place in the scene while denying it the curtain that defines its effect. The texts do not choose among the three, and this article does not pretend to choose for them. What the texts exclude is the fourth candidate, the curtained revelation, in which the vault stands open by the drawing of cloth from inside. Every surviving warrant, the strewing outside, the named tools, the spoken forcing, the First Quarto's direction, describes work done at a resisting face by a man standing outside it. The monument's material is unrecorded. Its behaviour is fully recorded, and behaviour, in a theatre of properties, is the architecture that matters.

### Where were the bodies during the Prince's closing speech?

In the open, on the main platform, in full view of the yard and of the city the scene assembles around them. The closing movement is a public exhibition, fathers and Prince and Watch disposed about the lovers' bodies as they pronounce the sentences that end the feud, and its staging requirement is width, not depth.

The breadth in question is the platform's own, which the Fortune contract measures at forty-three feet across its front. Editors who imagine the bodies withdrawn into a curtained tomb interior make the Prince lecture a curtain. The early texts give him a crowd, a dawn, and the dead in the midst of it, and the discovery space has no part in the play's most public ten minutes.

The tomb therefore returns the opposite verdict from the bed, and the opposition is instructive. For the bed, one direction proves a curtain and no dialogue supports or resists it. For the tomb, the dialogue and the property plot together describe a staging that does not use a curtain, and no direction supplies one. The honest conclusion is not that the tomb could not have been managed with the curtained opening on some occasion, since a company with the fitting available might reach for it; the conclusion is that the play's own instructions describe something else, a forced door and a forward tableau, and that scholarship serves the play badly when it upgrades a possible convenience into a textual fact. This article's ruling follows the documents: the discovery space is proved for the potion scene by the First Quarto, permitted as conjecture elsewhere, and refused for the tomb, where the mattock and the crow are the architecture. The pattern that emerges across the three test cases is the tragedy's own pattern of concealment. What is hidden in this play is hidden by people, by doors forced and letters miscarried and potions drunk behind a curtain at the one moment the plot itself goes into hiding; the building is asked to keep exactly one secret, and it keeps it in Act 4, not in Act 5.

## The Quarrel Adjudicated: Hosley's Door, the Sceptics' Facade, Gurr's Recess

Three positions divide the scholarship, and the play's evidence, graded as the preceding sections have graded it, is sufficient to judge among them. The first is the large claim, the inner stage of the older reconstruction tradition, in which a deep curtained chamber behind the facade serves as a second playing area for interior scenes. Against it stand the sightline argument, the silence of the contracts, the absence of any such chamber from the one drawing, and, for this play, the positive testimony of the directions: no scene in Romeo and Juliet asks for action played inside an enclosure, and the one enclosure the First Quarto certifies is used for a body lying still. The large claim is rejected. The second position is the sceptical minimum, in which the facade offered doors and the upper level and nothing else, and every discovery in the repertory was managed by a door opened, a hanging improvised for the day, or a property carried on. This position has the drawing and the contracts on its side and Hosley's wide-door mechanism as its strongest form, since Hosley himself derived the discovery from the doors the documents prove. Its weakness is the First Quarto of this play. A bed within curtains, fallen on and closed over and reopened two scenes later, is an arrangement of some deliberateness, and while a hung curtain across an opened doorway could produce it, the direction's calm assumption that the thing exists and will be understood presses toward a fitting the company possessed rather than a contrivance assembled for one scene. The third position, associated above all with Andrew Gurr's account of the Shakespearean stage, occupies the ground the evidence actually marks out: a curtained opening in the facade, placed midway between the stage doors, with a shallow recess behind it hung with hangings, used occasionally and for little action, accessible from the tiring house and serviceable also as an entrance. Gurr's formulation, that the discovery space at the Theatre was seemingly used quite sparingly, matches this play's usage with embarrassing precision: one play, one curtain, one scene and its echo.

The adjudication, then, runs as follows. Hosley wins on mechanism and loses on nothing that matters: his insistence that the discovery space be distinguished from the inner stage is the founding act of the modern subject, and his wide-door hypothesis remains possible for playhouses whose facades are known only from the drawing. The sceptics win on the burden of proof: every claim for a permanent, purpose-built recess must carry the First Quarto's direction on its back, since no contract and no drawing will help it, and claims that exceed what one direction can carry, for tombs and studies and prison cells across the repertory, should be discounted accordingly. Gurr wins on the balance for the Chamberlain's Men's houses, because the position that fits all the documents at once is his: a shallow curtained opening, midway in the facade, used sparingly, exactly the fitting that a bed could be set into at the Theatre in the middle 1590s and that a company could also do without when a bed was carried on instead. René Weis, editing the play for the Arden Third Series, supplies the editorial form of the same discipline, printing the early directions with their grades visible, keeping the Second Quarto's wording where the Second Quarto speaks and marking editorial completions as completions, so that a reader can see the difference between what the texts say and what the theatre must have had. Jill Levenson, in the Oxford edition, likewise treats performance as evidence to be weighed rather than scenery to be assumed. The editors and the historians converge, from opposite ends of the subject, on the rule this article has applied throughout: the playhouse is to be reconstructed from the plays outward, feature by feature, and no feature may borrow the certainty of another. Height is certain. The curtain is attested once and probable as a fixture. The room is a mirage generated by the curtain's success, and the mirage should be allowed to evaporate.

Alan Dessen's census supplies the discipline that all three positions must answer to, and it deserves its own hearing because it governs how much architecture any single direction can be asked to certify. In the Dictionary of Stage Directions in English Drama, 1590 to 1642, compiled with Leslie Thomson, Dessen gathered the period's directions entire, and the class that modern commentary calls discoveries proves on counting to be small, miscellaneous, and dominated by stillness: figures found seated, bodies found laid out, objects revealed where they stand. His editorial writings draw the moral that a discovery is an event in the audience's knowledge rather than a description of a place, and that the event was produced across the period by heterogeneous means, a curtain drawn, a door opened, hangings parted, at need a figure simply entering to be found where the fiction requires him. Applied to this play, the census cuts in both directions with impartial effect. It strengthens the bed, since a still body revealed at a curtain's opening is the census's central case, the very type of the period's discovery. It weakens the tomb, since the vault scene carries no discovery direction in either quarto, and on Dessen's showing the absence is evidence, because the repertory's bookkeepers recorded discoveries when discoveries were staged. And it rebukes every diagram, this article's table not excepted, whenever neat rows tempt a reader to forget that the period's own word for the thing was a verb. Figures were discovered. The space is a modern noun, and nouns should be kept on short rations.

One further witness deserves a hearing before the verdict, because it speaks for the sceptics at their strongest. The company toured, playing in halls, inn yards, and great houses where no facade existed at all, and the play had to survive those rooms; a company whose tragedy depended on a deep discovery chamber would have carried a crippled text on the road. The touring argument proves portability, and portability proves that nothing in the play was structurally dependent on a recess, a conclusion this article has already reached from the Second Quarto's silence. What touring cannot prove is that the home stage lacked the fitting or declined to use it when the play was home. London companies used their home architecture opportunistically, as the First Quarto's curtain shows this one doing, and trimmed the effect away on tour as readily as they trimmed a dance or a fight. Opportunism, not dependence, is the word the evidence supports, and it distributes the honours justly among the quarrelling positions: the facade was poorer than the old drawings made it and richer than the strictest scepticism allows, and this playwright, of all the company's writers, was the one who tested both its riches and its poverty most searchingly, which is why his tragedy has become the test case for the whole dispute.

## The Control Group: What Other Plays Ask of the Same Facade

The grading applied to this play gains its credibility from the repertory around it, because the same facade served the whole company, and the other plays' demands show what the building was routinely expected to do. For the upper level, the control cases are unambiguous. In Richard II, the king appears on the walls of Flint Castle to parley with the challengers drawn up below, and the scene's language is the orchard scene's language turned to politics, height as legitimacy, until the king agrees to surrender the height itself: "Down, down I come, like glistering Phaethon" (Richard II, 3.3), spoken as he descends to the base court to submit at ground level. A king's appearance on walls, in a play of the same company and the same years, is not a conjectural use of the upper stage; it is the level's ordinary employment, and it confirms that the facade's upper floor was built and staffed for figures of consequence to appear, to speak at length, and to negotiate across a vertical interval. In Antony and Cleopatra, a decade later, the monument scene asks far more: the dying Antony must be hauled bodily up to Cleopatra's monument by the women above, a feat of stage labour performed in full view and joked about in the verse as it happens, "Here's sport indeed! How heavy weighs my lord!" (Antony and Cleopatra, 4.15). The upper level in that scene bears not a voice at a window but the full weight of a wounded man lifted on ropes, which settles any doubt that the level was a working floor of genuine capacity rather than a decorative frame. Against the repertory's extremes, the tragedy under discussion makes a modest requisition: a girl at a window, twice, and a descent by a ladder of cords.

For the curtained discovery, the control case is The Tempest, where the First Folio's direction for the last act has Prospero discover Ferdinand and Miranda playing at chess: two figures, seated, still, revealed at the moment of reconciliation, the discovery functioning precisely as the census of directions says discoveries function, a held picture produced for the audience's knowledge. The Tempest belonged to the same company in its later years and played both at the amphitheatre and in the indoor hall, and its discovery was portable between a great open yard and a candlelit room, which instructs again: whatever produced that revelation travelled light, and cannot have been a chamber of masonry pretensions. Set beside these controls, the evidence of Romeo and Juliet falls into proportion. The play's upper demands are lighter than the repertory's proved upper demands. Its one discovery is of the census's central type, a still body on a bed behind a curtain. Its tomb carries no discovery direction, and no control case supplies one for it. The control group is therefore the sceptics' gift to the moderate position: it proves the building capable of everything this article claims for it, and it shows the claim stopping, as the article's grading stops, exactly where the period's paperwork stops.

The facade's portability across the company's own history supplies a last control. From 1609 the King's Men, as the Chamberlain's Men had become, wintered at the indoor Blackfriars, a hall playhouse converted from a friary's upper rooms, where the same repertory played to candlelight in a room a fraction of the Globe's size. The Blackfriars stage kept the family's grammar in miniature: doors in a facade, an upper level, a capacity for discovery, music housed in a room of its own over the stage. Romeo and Juliet in that room, an old play by then and a staple, would have had its window at the same relative height, its bed behind the same kind of curtain, its tomb forced on the same kind of platform, with the audience near enough to hear a whisper at the casement. The indoor evidence cannot be backdated into proof about the Theatre of 1595, and this article does not so misuse it; its value is directional. The features the graded record certifies are exactly the features that survive translation into the company's second, smaller, later house, while the features the record refuses, the deep interior and the discovered tomb, are the ones no house of the company, indoors or out, ever had occasion to build. A grammar that persists across both of the company's homes, in plays written for the first and revived in the second, is likelier to be the grammar the first home actually had.

One further control is negative, and its negativity is the point. The play makes no demand at all on the machinery above the stage cover, the hut and heavens from which gods and thrones descended in other plays of the repertory; no direction in either quarto calls for a descent, a flight, or a figure lowered, and the tragedy's single supernatural machinery is a dream and a premonition, both spoken. A playwright ransacking a new building for effects would have found the descent gear as available as the window; the author of this play reached for the window twice, the curtain once, and the heavens never, because his tragedy's cosmology works horizontally, by messages that miscarry and doors that open too late, and vertically only between a girl's window and a vault. The silences of the text are as graded as its directions: the absence of the heavens is proof that the writing selected among the building's resources rather than consuming them, and selection, repeated across a career, is the strongest evidence that the selected features were really there to be chosen.

## What Reconstructions Had to Decide: Timber as Argument

A scholarly position can hold its ambiguities in suspension; a building cannot. The reconstruction of Shakespeare's Globe on Bankside, opened in 1997 a short distance from the original site, had to decide in oak and plaster everything the documents leave open, and its decisions constitute a distinct kind of evidence: not proof of what stood in 1599, but a demonstration of what a coherent playhouse requires when the documents are followed as far as they go and the gaps are filled by the discipline of use. For the upper stage, the reconstruction followed the drawing and the contracts into a gallery level crossing the facade, with the window of the orchard scene played from that level, and playing there has confirmed in practice what the dialogue argues in theory: the height is right, the sightlines from the yard hold, and the scene's alternation of appearance and withdrawal runs at the tempo the text imposes because the level is a genuine floor with the tiring house behind it. For the discovery space, the reconstruction made the Gurr choice made visible: a central opening in the facade, of a width to serve as a principal entrance, hung with a traverse curtain, behind which a shallow space can conceal and reveal. The Internet Shakespeare Editions describes the adopted solution in exactly those terms, a central doorway, perhaps of double width, curtained, serving both as an entrance for major characters and as a discovery space, and notes that early assumptions of a deep inner stage were set aside in the designing. The building, that is, embodies the adjudication this article has reached from the texts, and the agreement is worth something: reached independently, by carpenters answering to audiences, the same shallow curtained opening emerges as the most the evidence will build.

The agreement is worth something, and not more than something. The standing discipline of the series' reconstruction criticism applies here in full: a reconstruction tests functions and cannot recover a first performance, and every fitting it builds is a hypothesis that has learned to stand up. The rebuilt Globe's upper level proves that the window scene plays supremely well from a storey of height over a platform, which was never in doubt; it cannot prove that the Theatre's facade of 1595 disposed its window where the Bankside building disposes a window of its own. Its curtained opening proves that a bed can be discovered from a shallow recess with complete theatrical sufficiency, which supports Gurr against the inner-stage tradition; it cannot prove that the Chamberlain's Men owned such an opening rather than hanging a curtain across a doorway when the potion scene required it, as Hosley's cheaper mechanism would allow. The indoor Sam Wanamaker Playhouse, the reconstruction's Jacobean sibling, sharpens the same lesson from the other side, since its smaller, candlelit room rearranges the relation of facade to audience entirely and shows how much of the discovery effect is an effect of distance and light rather than of carpentry. What reconstructions genuinely add to the record is a laboratory of sufficiency: they mark the minimum that works. On the evidence of Bankside, the minimum that works is a real upper floor and a curtained central opening of modest depth, and the match between that laboratory minimum and the graded documentary record is the strongest external corroboration the moderate position possesses. The indoor and outdoor experiments alike leave the tomb where this article's reading of the properties left it: played forward, around a forced door and displayed bodies, because no reconstruction has found an interior that improves on the platform for the tragedy's closing assembly. The relation of the whole question to the play as it plays on a stage of any period, with nothing behind the actors but a wall and a curtain, is carried by the series' companion study of [Verona on a Bare Elizabethan Stage](/2028/02/18/verona-on-a-bare-stage/), to which this architectural account is the foundation and the foil.

A second reconstruction sharpens the point from across the Atlantic and across the indoor tradition. The American Shakespeare Center's Blackfriars Playhouse in Staunton, Virginia, a recreation of the Jacobean indoor house where this play also lived in the repertory's later years, had to make the same elections in a smaller and more intimate room: an upper level over its stage, a curtained discovery at its centre, doors disposed for the traffic of households and watchmen. Playing the tragedy in that room demonstrates the portability of the graded facade, since the window, the curtain, and the forced door all function at hall scale with the audience on three sides and almost within reach, while the features the record refuses, the deep interior and the discovered tomb, remain as unnecessary indoors as out. Two reconstructions on two continents, one outdoor and one in, converge on the same minimum because the documents leave them the same minimum to converge on. Convergence of that kind is the laboratory's real product: not a photograph of 1595, but a narrowing of the possible, until the possible and the documented are hard to tell apart and the undisciplined versions stand exposed by their superfluity.

## The Sound From Above: The Musicians' Station

One tenant of the upper regions remains unexamined, and the play's treatment of the musicians is a final exercise in grading, because here the texts fix an effect and leave its machinery wholly unrecorded. Music sounds at the tragedy's hinges: at the Capulet feast, where the dancing follows the serving men's clearance and the Second Quarto's direction has music play while the guests dance, and again, inverted, in the house of mourning, where the hired musicians of Act 4 Scene 5 stand idle with their instruments while the household laments, and fall to haggling with Peter over tunes they will not be paid to play. The feast's music is architectural in one strict sense: it must come from somewhere that is not the dancing floor, it must be audible across a crowded platform and a yard of two thousand hearers, and it must stop and start at the scene's convenience. The period's playhouses possessed an obvious somewhere. The room over the stage, in the later indoor houses expressly a music room, and the gallery crossing the facade in de Witt's drawing, offered the band a raised station from which sound falls naturally onto a stage and yard, and reconstructions of the public playhouses have generally seated their musicians in that upper region, in or behind the facade's gallery, where the drawing itself shows figures disposed like an audience that might as easily be a consort. For the indoor theatres of the next reign the over-stage music room is solidly attested; for the amphitheatres of the 1590s the station is an inference from acoustics and analogy, and the texts of this play neither confirm nor forbid it.

### Where did the feast's music come from?

The texts do not say, and any answer more definite than that is furnishing. What the play fixes is audibility and timing: music plays, the guests dance, and the sound must arrive from off the floor it accompanies, at the scene's convenience and at a volume that fills a yard.

The period's usual solution, and the one the reconstructions adopt, places the band in the upper region of the facade, the gallery level over the stage, from which sound carries down into the yard; the play's own papers certify the effect and leave the station to that reasonable inference.

The mourning scene is the control that keeps the inference honest. When the play wants its musicians visible, it puts them on the platform at ground level, named, costumed, and quarrelsome, because their scene is about labour and wages and requires bodies in the room with the corpse. The feast, by contrast, wants music without musicians, a sound whose source the revellers never acknowledge, and the difference between the two scenes is a small lesson in the facade's economy: what the audience must see, the platform supplies; what it must only hear, the building supplies from its hidden storeys, whether from a gallery of players, a room behind the facade, or the tiring house itself. The discovery-space debate is usually conducted as a question about sight, but the playhouse was equally a machine for managing the unseen sources of sound, voices within, music above, and the graded verdict is the same in the acoustic register as in the visual. The effect is certified. The machinery is inference. The room is unproved. A criticism that can hold those grades apart for a curtain can hold them apart for a consort, and the habit of holding them apart is, in the end, the whole method this article has been practising.

## How the Architecture Wrote the Scenes

It remains to draw the consequence the brief for this article demands a verdict on: not merely whether the features existed, but how they shaped the writing. The claim that a playwright composes inside the affordances of a known building is easily made and must be made precisely, because its loose form, that the theatre determined the play, explains nothing and insults both parties. The precise form is visible in the distribution of the play's demands. Shakespeare asks the building for height twice, at the two scenes where his subject is the distance between the lovers, first imposed by the feud and then briefly annulled by the marriage; the requests are large, central, and structurally identical, the second scene quoting the first's geometry to measure what has changed. He asks the building for concealment once, at the scene where his subject becomes a secret kept from a household, and the request is small, occasional, and dispensable, as the Second Quarto's text proves by dispensing with its record. He asks the building for nothing at all at the tomb, where his subject is exposure, the feud's secrets dragged into the open before the assembled city, and he writes the forcing of the vault as manual labour with named tools precisely because there the play's world offers no mechanism but the hand. The architecture and the dramaturgy are not cause and effect; they are a matched pair of vocabularies, the facade's grammar of height, hiding, and opening supplying the physical syntax for a tragedy whose meanings are height, hiding, and opening. A playwright with a different house would have written different emphases; this playwright, with this house, wrote a tragedy whose whole action can be diagrammed as a movement from a window to a curtain to a forced door.

The play names its own machine once, at the threshold, and the naming belongs in any account of the writing and the building. The Chorus of the Prologue promises the audience "the two hours' traffic of our stage" (Prologue, line 12), and the possessive is exact: the stage is the company's, a known platform with known fittings, and the traffic it carries for two hours is the whole of Verona, streets and orchards, chambers and cells and vaults, laid successively on one floor and in one facade. A playwright who introduces his tragedy by naming its stage is not hiding the machinery; he is advertising the terms on which the fiction will be built, and the terms are architectural from the first line. Everything the Prologue's audience is asked to imagine, it is asked to imagine as disposition of that stage and that facade, height for the window, cloth for the hiding, doors for the comings and goings of a divided city. The writing that follows keeps the contract with unusual fidelity, and the fidelity is measurable: count the play's demands, as the diagram counts them, and the demands the building could not certainly meet are the demands the play, on inspection, never made.

The vertical pattern completes itself in the play's last architectural gesture, which is a promise to build. The fathers, standing over the bodies in the open air where the platform has displayed them, close the feud by commissioning monuments: Montague will raise Juliet's statue in pure gold, and Capulet answers that Romeo's shall stand as rich beside his lady. The city that could not keep the lovers apart in its orchards or together in its vault ends by fixing them in its permanent architecture, raised, displayed, and still, the upper stage's logic translated into civic stone. It is the play's final comment on its own staging. Every appearance the tragedy has made at a height, the girl at the window, the wife at the casement at dawn, has been temporary, interruptible, conducted under the threat of a voice calling within; the statues promise the same elevation without the interruption, a discovery that no curtain will ever close. That the promise is also a failure, gold substituting for the living height the building actually provided, is the tragedy's last irony about architecture: the playhouse's perishable timber served the lovers better than Verona's permanent gold will, because the playhouse let them move, answer, withdraw, and return, and the monument lets them only stand. The building shaped the play, and the play ends by judging buildings.

## What New Evidence Could Move the Grades

A graded argument should state its own conditions of surrender, and this one can. The curtain's grade would rise from probable fixture to proved architecture on any one of three discoveries: a carpenter's account or building contract for a Chamberlain's Men's house that itemizes a traverse, a curtain rod, or a central opening; a Revels or household record describing the potion scene's staging at court or in a great house with the fitting named; or an excavation that recovered a facade's footings with a central recess of the requisite width. The tomb's grade would rise on a single direction, in any early text or promptbook fragment, that opens the vault by discovery rather than by force. The upper stage's grade cannot rise, being proof already, and could fall only if the Swan drawing were shown to be a fantasy and the dialogue's surveying explained away, two revolutions no sober critic expects. Naming the missing documents is not pedantry. It is the difference between a position and a temperament: the moderate facade this article defends is held because the record, as it stands, supports exactly that much building and no more, and the defence expires the day the record changes. Scholarship on the early stage has been embarrassed repeatedly by certainties that a single afternoon's digging or a single unexamined manuscript dissolved, and the grades printed in the diagram are offered in the knowledge that they are the record's grades, dated by the record's present state, rather than the building's eternal ones. The excavation of the Rose in 1989 stands as the standing warning: a few weeks of trowel work corrected the size, the shape, and the stage position of a playhouse that generations of scholars had drawn with confidence, and nothing in the facade debate is so secure that a trench across a tiring-house footing could not reorder it. The discipline is not a counsel of despair. It is the reason the grades exist, so that when the record moves, the argument can move with it band by band instead of collapsing entire.

## The Verdict: Proven Height, Probable Curtain, Unproven Room

The evidence, graded without favour, supports a facade of three certainties kept apart. First, the upper stage existed and was a principal playing area: a floor over the stage doors at a full storey's height, fronted by a window from which Juliet appears in the orchard scene and from whose chamber Romeo descends at dawn, connected to an interior from which the Nurse's voice calls and to which Juliet withdraws and returns at speed. This is proved by the dialogue's own measurements, by the First Quarto's window, by the Second Quarto's aloft chamber, and by the class of building the Fortune contract describes. Second, a curtained enclosure at stage level existed at the Theatre in the play's first years and held Juliet's bed through the potion scene and its discovery: this is proved, for the effect, by the First Quarto's direction alone, and the fitting that produced the effect was most probably the shallow curtained central opening of Gurr's account, used sparingly and for stillness rather than action. Third, the discovery space as a room, an inner stage in which scenes were played or in which the tomb was set, is unproven for this play and refused by its texts: the tomb is forced from outside with mattock and crow, its business is strewing, fighting, and display on the open platform, and the closing assembly needs the platform's width, not a recess's depth. The architecture shaped the play exactly where those grades say it could. It gave the writing its vertical axis, its one true hiding place, and its final lesson in the difference between a door that opens and a door that must be broken. Everything else that has been built behind that facade in four centuries of reconstruction, the deep rooms and curtained interiors, is the work of hands other than the playwright's, and the play, read at the grade of its documents, declines to certify it.

The consequences of holding the grades run outward in two directions, and both are practical. For editors, the graded facade prescribes a typography: directions the quartos print stand unbracketed; completions drawn from the sister quarto stand marked as imports; completions drawn from the critic's imagination of the building stand marked as what they are, so that a student meeting Enter Juliet above, at a window can see the sentence's three strata at a glance and learn the subject's method from the page's punctuation. For directors, the grades prescribe a budget of invention. Height is not an effect to be designed but a condition to be honoured, and a staging that levels the orchard scene spends the play's capital; the curtain is an effect the record licenses once, at the play's one hiding, and a staging that curtains its tomb as well as its bed has doubled the licence and halved its meaning; the forced door is an obligation, the one place where the text forbids ease. A production that opens its vault with a draw of cloth has not found a prettier solution to a staging problem. It has declined the problem the playwright set, which is the difference between a door that opens and a door that must be broken, between a city whose secrets are administered and a city whose secrets are kept by force until force breaks them. The architecture of the first stage was modest: a floor above, a curtain at need, a door that resisted. The play built on it is not modest at all, and the disproportion is the final proof of the method. Greatness in this theatre was never a function of the room behind the facade. It was a function of knowing, to the inch and to the occasion, what the facade could honestly do.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch architecture diagram, and what does it grade?

The InsightCrunch architecture diagram is this article's table of the playhouse features that Romeo and Juliet uses, set against the documents that certify each feature and the grade of certainty each document earns. It grades evidence rather than merely listing features, because the subject's chronic error is the migration of certainty from proved fittings to conjectured ones. In the diagram the window, the height, the chamber aloft, and the voice within stand as proof; the curtained bed stands as proof in one quarto; the shallow curtained opening stands as probable; and the tomb imagined as a discovered interior stands as unproven, beside the deep inner stage, which stands as rejected. A reader who absorbs the grades can test any claim about the early staging of the play by asking which band of the diagram the claim belongs to and what document it cites.

### Q: What is a discovery space in an Elizabethan playhouse?

A discovery space is a curtained opening at ground level in the tiring-house facade, behind which a person or property could be concealed and then revealed by drawing the curtain. The term is modern; the players had no such phrase, but their stage directions record the effect, in this play most plainly when the First Quarto sends Juliet to fall upon her bed within the curtains at the end of Act 4 Scene 3. In the account this article adopts, the space was shallow, a recess behind hangings rather than a room, used for stillness and revelation rather than for played action. Andrew Gurr places the opening midway between the stage doors, accessible from the tiring house and serviceable as an entrance. Its function was epistemological before it was architectural: it let a play keep a secret in full view of the audience and produce it at the chosen moment.

### Q: What is the difference between a discovery space and an inner stage?

The difference is size, function, and evidence. A discovery space, in the disciplined sense, is a shallow curtained opening used to conceal and reveal: a bed, a body, a figure standing still. An inner stage, in the older reconstruction tradition, was a deep second playing area sunk behind the facade, curtained like a proscenium, inside which whole interior scenes were supposedly played. The first is attested by directions such as the First Quarto's bed within the curtains in this play; the second is attested by nothing of the period, neither contract, drawing, nor direction, and it fails the sightline test, since the best-paying spectators would have seen least of the action it supposedly housed. Richard Hosley's paper of 1959 founded the modern subject by separating the two, deriving discoveries from wide stage doors or a curtained opening and refusing the room. This article follows that separation throughout.

### Q: Does the architecture argument change if the play was staged at the Theatre rather than the Globe?

Very little, because the argument is built from the class of building, not from one address. The play belongs to the middle 1590s and to the Chamberlain's Men, whose homes in those years were the Theatre and the Curtain in Shoreditch; the Globe on Bankside, built in 1599, inherited the play as a going concern. All three houses were timber-framed public playhouses of the same family, platforms before galleried yards with a tiring-house facade behind, and the Fortune contract of 1600, which models its stage on the Globe's and in turn reflects the practice of the whole generation of houses, describes the family's common anatomy. The features this article grades, the upper level and the curtained opening, belong to that common anatomy rather than to any single building's peculiarities. What changes from house to house is only the confidence of detail: dimensions quoted from the Fortune are a scale for the family, not a measurement of the Theatre's facade.

### Q: How tall was the tiring-house facade behind the stage?

The only facade dimensions fixed by a surviving contract are the Fortune's, and the Fortune was modelled on the Globe, so its figures give the scale of the family rather than the measure of this play's own houses. The contract sets the frame in three storeys of twelve, eleven, and nine feet, with the stage extending to the middle of the yard and a cover over its rear part; the first storey's twelve feet set the height at which the upper playing level's floor stood over the platform. The tiring house behind the stage carried glazed windows and its own stairs and divisions. No contract survives for the Theatre or the Curtain, and the second Globe's facade is known no more precisely. The honest answer is therefore a scale, not a figure: a wall of two playing levels and a further storey rising over a platform, tall enough to make a girl at its window unreachable and a man below her an intruder in a garden.

### Q: Where in the tiring-house wall do scholars place the discovery space?

Two placements divide the scholarship, and the difference is a measure of how little the documents fix. Andrew Gurr places a purpose-made curtained opening midway between the stage doors, with a shallow recess behind it hung with hangings and reached from inside the tiring house, so that the facade offers three ground-level apertures: two doors and a central discovery. Richard Hosley's earlier model needs no third aperture at all: the stage doors themselves, wide and double-hung, opened outward to disclose the tiring-house space nearest the facade, and hangings could be fitted across an opened doorway when a discovery was wanted. The rebuilt Globe on Bankside adopted the central-opening solution, building a doorway of principal width with a traverse curtain. What no responsible placement proposes is depth: on every surviving model the discovered place is a few feet of shadow behind the facade's line, a threshold dressed as an interior.

### Q: Did the original Juliet play the window scene from a gallery, or from a window set into the facade?

From a window set into the facade, on the evidence of the words themselves. The First Quarto's direction is Enter Juliet at a window, and a window is an opening in a wall, not a floor walked upon; the Second Quarto's dialogue confirms the fixture by naming it before she appears, when Romeo asks what light breaks through yonder window. The gallery, by contrast, is the audience's structure, the tiered seating that rings the yard, and also the name sometimes given to the upper level's floor across the facade, which is where the confusion enters. Juliet stood at an aperture in the tiring-house wall at the upper level, framed by it, able to withdraw through it into the room behind; she did not walk a gallery in view like a spectator taking air. Modern productions that give her a railed platform are furnishing the aperture, a legitimate liberty that should not be mistaken for the period's fixture.

### Q: How many times does Juliet withdraw from the window and return in Act 2 Scene 2, and what does the pattern require of the architecture?

Twice called away, she returns twice, after her first appearance and before the final parting, and the First and Second Quartos agree on the rhythm even where their directions differ in phrasing, the fuller text marking her returns with Enter Juliet again. The pattern is an architectural specification disguised as a love scene. Each withdrawal is prompted by the Nurse's voice from within, each return must land within a few lines, and the whole alternation plays at the speed of an interrupted conversation rather than of journeys through a house. The window therefore opened off an interior immediately behind it, with the actress's path between casement and room measured in steps, and the upper level functioned as a floor of the tiring house and not as a decorative frame. A staging that strands its Juliet on a projecting balcony with no door at her back falsifies the scene's mechanics, however faithfully it reproduces its picture.

### Q: What does the Nurse's voice calling "within" prove about the playhouse?

The word within, in the directions and in the scene's management, proves that the facade was a working wall with a staffed interior behind it, and that the audience was expected to read sound through the wall as place. When the Nurse calls and Juliet answers that she comes, the unseen room behind the window acquires a population and a geography: there is a chamber, a household, a summons that can interrupt a lover's interview at any moment, all of it built acoustically out of one voice and one aperture. The proof matters because it extends the upper level's certificate beyond the visible. A window that merely framed a head could be a shallow contrivance; a window whose occupant negotiates with a voice from a room the audience cannot see implies connected interior space, stairs, and doors in regular use. The Elizabethan facade was not a screen. It was the front of a building, and the play leans on its buildingness every time a voice comes through it.

### Q: Why does the Second Quarto's silence about the curtains matter?

Because it separates what the company did from what the text requires, and confusing those two is the root error of the whole discovery-space debate. The curtained bed direction stands only in the First Quarto, the performance-derived text; the Second Quarto, printed from copy close to the author's hand, ends the potion scene without it, and its staging can be managed with a bed thrust forward and a discovery made by approach. A critic who builds the discovery space into the play's foundations is therefore building on the quarto the discipline trusts least for the author's intentions, while ignoring the quarto it trusts most. The correct inference is double: the curtained effect was available and was used, since the reported text records it; and the play was written to survive without it, since the authoritative text does without it. Occasional resource, not structural necessity, is the grade the two quartos together impose.

### Q: Was Juliet's bed thrust onto the main stage, or placed behind the curtain?

The First Quarto answers for its own staging: the bed stood within the curtains, which is to say at the front of the curtained opening, placed there before the scene or drawn into place behind the hangings, so that Juliet could fall across it at the speech's end and be closed away. Richard Dutton's reconstruction of the sequence gives the bed that forward position within the space and the curtains the scene's last action, falling shut over her; two scenes later the same hangings open to discover her to the Nurse. The thrust-bed alternative belongs to the Second Quarto's staging, where no curtain is recorded and a bed carried forward onto the platform serves as well. Both stagings are period-possible, and the texts preserve one each. What the documents exclude is the bed discovered deep inside a room, since the First Quarto's business, fall and closure and reopening, all happens at the curtain's line, the threshold between the platform and its one secret.

### Q: How was the vault door opened if the tomb was not a discovery space?

By force, in the open, with the tools the texts name. Romeo arrives at the vault carrying a torch, a mattock, and a crow of iron, and his speech over the monument performs the forcing as he does it, enforcing the tomb's rotten jaws to open; the First Quarto states the business outright in the direction Romeo opens the tomb. The vault's face on the early stage was a front with a door, whether that door was a property monument brought on, a facade door dressed for the night, or the curtained opening repurposed and uncurtained, and the scene's meaning lives in the resistance of that door and the labour of breaking it. A discovery, by contrast, opens from inside, by the company's choice and at the curtain's ease. Everything in the scene's verse and property plot insists on the opposite experience: entry as violence, against a closure that does not yield willingly, which is why the mattock and the crow, not the curtain, are the tomb's true architecture.

### Q: Why does Paris strew flowers outside the tomb rather than inside it?

Because the text keeps him outside, and the detail is a small proof with large consequences. Paris comes to the vault with flowers and sweet water for Juliet's bridal bed, and he performs the rite at the monument's face; he carries no mattock and no crow, makes no attempt to enter, and withdraws to hide nearby when he hears Romeo approach. Richard Dutton draws the inference the detail supports: the tomb's interior is not a standing set into which characters wander, since the one mourner whose business is tenderness conducts it at the threshold, and entry belongs exclusively to the man who comes equipped to force it. A discovery-space tomb, opened by a curtain and entered at will, would dissolve the distinction the text is careful to draw between the strewer outside and the breaker within doors. The flowers at the face of the vault are the play's own annotation on its staging: the inside of this tomb is reached by violence or not at all.

### Q: What part did the two stage doors play in the play's geography?

The doors were the horizontal counterpart of the window and the curtain, the facade's means of dividing the platform into the play's places without scenery. Verona in this tragedy is a set of thresholds: Capulet's house against the street, the street against the Friar's cell, the cell against Mantua and the vault, and the alternation of the two stage doors, one conventionally felt as the way into the house and the other as the way out to the city, lets those places succeed and oppose each other at walking pace. The First Quarto's directions lean on the doors for the play's crowd movements, entries of households and watchmen whose opposed arrivals stage the feud as traffic. Nothing in the doors' work is conjectural; the drawing shows them, the contracts assume them, and every play of the repertory runs on them. They are the facade's proved ground level, beside which the curtained opening's single attestation stands in instructive contrast.

### Q: How does the upper level change the meaning of "It is the east, and Juliet is the sun"?

The architecture turns the speech from a compliment into a stage direction the building obeys. Romeo speaks the lines looking up at a window in which light has appeared, at a girl framed a full storey over his head, in a yard where the afternoon sun is genuinely in the sky behind the audience; the metaphor's vehicle, a sunrise, is being enacted by the facade, which lifts Juliet into the heavens of the playhouse as the verse lifts her into the heavens of the conceit. Played at ground level, the lines describe a fancy. Played at the upper window, they describe the scene, and their exaggeration shrinks to reportage: she is above, she is radiant against a dark opening, and looking at her hurts and dazzles like looking east. The upper stage is thus not an illustration of the poetry but one of its authors, and the certainty that the level existed is part of what the most famous couplet in the play is made of.

### Q: What did the rebuilt Globe build above and behind its stage, and what does that choice admit?

Above its stage the rebuilt Globe built the upper level as the drawing and the contracts prescribe it, a gallery floor crossing the facade from which the window scenes are played; behind its stage front it built a central opening of principal width, hung with a traverse curtain, to serve as a grand entrance and as the discovery space. The choice admits, in timber, the exact state of the evidence. For the level, the documents converge, and the building could follow them with confidence. For the opening, the documents diverge, offering Hosley's wide door, Gurr's central recess, and the sceptics' bare facade, and the reconstruction had to elect one hypothesis and make it stand, knowing that election is not proof. The Bankside facade is therefore best read as an argument with a roof on: it demonstrates that the moderate position can be built and played, which is a real addition to knowledge, and it cannot demonstrate that the Chamberlain's Men's facade was the one built, which no reconstruction can.

### Q: Could the discovery space have been a temporary curtained booth rather than a built recess?

It could, and the possibility is the sceptics' strongest reserve position. Companies toured to halls and inn yards where no facade existed, and the period knew the traverse and the hanging arras as portable fittings; a curtain strung across a frame or between the stage posts could manufacture a discovery anywhere, and several scenes in the repertory read as though a pavilion or booth, rather than the building, supplied the enclosure. For this play the possibility cuts the claim down to its defensible size. The First Quarto proves a curtained effect at the Theatre or wherever its performance was played; it cannot prove whether the curtain hung in a built recess of the facade or in a fitting assembled for the season. Gurr's permanent shallow opening and the portable booth produce identical evidence and identical theatre. The honest verdict therefore attaches to the effect and the habit, a curtained concealment the company could command, and declines to choose the carpentry that the record leaves genuinely open.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch architecture diagram to argue that the play was written for its building?

Begin from the grade column rather than from the feature column. An essay arguing that the play was written for its building is strong exactly where the diagram's grades are high, so build the case on the upper level: the dialogue that surveys the height, the First Quarto's window, the Second Quarto's aloft chamber and the descent, the pattern of withdrawals that requires an interior behind the casement. Concede the middle band gracefully, presenting the curtained bed as the building used opportunistically for the plot's one act of hiding, and let the concession purchase credibility. Then turn the diagram's lowest band into the argument's edge: a playwright exploiting a known house asks it only for what it certainly has, and the tomb's reliance on carried tools and a forced door shows the writing declining to presume the one fitting the record cannot certify. The building shaped the play most visibly where the play refuses to overdraw on it.

### Q: What is the strongest evidence that the upper stage existed, and the weakest link in the discovery-space case?

The strongest evidence for the upper stage is the play itself, corroborated by the drawing: a contemporary picture of the Swan shows the gallery over the stage doors, and this play's dialogue, in scenes whose staging the two quartos independently support, requires a window a full storey above the platform with an interior behind it. Documents of three kinds, picture, contract, and text, converge on the level. The weakest link in the discovery-space case is its dependence on a single class of witness: no contract itemizes a recess, the drawing shows none, and the entire structure rests on stage directions, of which this play contributes exactly one, in one quarto. Directions record effects rather than fittings, so the recess as architecture is an inference from an effect that a hung curtain could produce by several means. The case for a curtained capability is sound; the case for a built room is the weak link, and it should be loaded with nothing heavier than a bed.

### Q: Why do the fathers' statues matter to the architecture argument?

Because the play ends by translating its staging into building, and the translation judges both. Over the bodies displayed on the open platform, Montague promises to raise Juliet's statue in pure gold and Capulet answers with Romeo's, set rich beside his lady: the lovers who appeared all evening at the playhouse's perishable heights, at a window and a casement, are to be fixed at a city's permanent height, elevated, displayed, and discovered to every passer-by with no curtain ever to close. The statues are the upper stage made monumental and the discovery made perpetual, and the fathers offer them because display is the only reparation their world knows. For the architecture argument the moment is the play's signature on its own evidence: a tragedy staged by height and revelation closes by imagining height and revelation in stone, and finds them wanting, since gold can hold the pose the timber framed but cannot hold the voices that made the pose matter.
