Lineation is the quietest decision on every page of Romeo and Juliet, and one of the most consequential: the division of the play's words into verse lines, the choice of where one pentameter ends and the next begins, is made afresh by every text that transmits the play, and the early quartos of 1597 and 1599 do not always make it well. Their compositors ran verse lines together, split single lines into fragments, and in places set whole stretches of pentameter as prose, so that the metre a reader scans in a modern edition is partly a transmitted inheritance and partly an editorial reconstruction. The verdict argued here is precise rather than alarmist. The skeleton of the verse descends intact from the second quarto, whose lineation follows authorial copy for the great majority of lines; editorial repair clusters at identifiable joints, where the printing house or a reporting actor broke the measure; and at a small residue of famous moments the recovery is conjecture, honestly labelled or not, where two competent editors divide the same words differently and the reader is trusting an ear rather than a document. How large each of those three territories is, and how a reader can learn to see the borders between them, is the subject of the pages that follow.

The problem begins with a distinction most readers never need until it fails them. A verse line is a unit of composition: ten syllables, roughly, disposed in five iambic feet, whether or not any page ever printed it as such. A printed line is a unit of typography: whatever a compositor fitted between a margin and the edge of his measure before the type ran out. In a well-made book the two coincide so completely that the difference stays invisible. In the early quartos of this play they repeatedly come apart. A compositor short of space set verse as prose and let the words run to the margin; a compositor whose copy was a reported text, remembered rather than read, set down lines as the memory had delivered them, broken at the joints of recall rather than of metre; and the narrow quarto page forced long lines to turn, their tails dropping to a second typographic line that looks, to an unwary eye, like a short verse line of its own. Every modern edition of the play is a negotiation among these accidents. The words are, with famous exceptions, Shakespeare's; the lines they stand in are a collaboration among the playwright, his copyists, two printing houses, and four centuries of editors.

## What Is Lineation, and Why Does It Govern the Sound of the Verse?

Lineation governs sound because the pentameter is a contract between the line and the voice. A line ending is a small event: it promises a cadence, suspends or completes a phrase, and tells the speaker where the measure breathes even when the syntax runs on. Divide the same sentence at different points and the emphasis migrates. Put the break after a verb and the verb lands like a blow; run on past it and the blow dissolves into fluency. This is why the textual question is not pedantry. When the second quarto sets a passage of Mercutio's as prose, it is not merely untidy: it instructs the reader's inner ear to stop counting, and the speech's hurtling couplets and catalogues flatten into chatter. When an editor restores the lineation, the speech stands up again in measure, and the wit turns out to have been riding on rhythm all along.

The stakes rise at the joins between speakers. Shakespeare's dialogue constantly splits single pentameters between two voices, a device scholars call the shared line: one speaker supplies the first half of the measure, the second completes it, and the verse itself enacts the speed or intimacy of the exchange. A shared line exists only if the printer or editor presents it as one. Print the two halves as separate short lines and the page inserts a silence the verse did not write; a hesitant gap opens where the composition built a hinge. Print two genuinely separate short lines as one, and a deliberate pause, the held breath before a death or a recognition, is papered over. Lineation, in short, is where bibliography becomes prosody. The study of how the verse works, the metrical contract the lovers' speeches keep and break, has its own treatment in the account of [Blank Verse in Romeo and Juliet: How It Works](/2024/12/06/blank-verse-romeo-and-juliet/), which takes the measure itself as its subject; the present study takes the page as its subject, and asks who drew the lines the measure lives in.

A reader can feel the difference at the play's most famous threshold. The lovers' first exchange at the feast is a sonnet, fourteen lines shared between two speakers in alternating quatrains, and its lineation descends through every early text without disturbance: Romeo's "If I profane with my unworthiest hand" (1.5) opens a structure whose joints every witness respects, because a sonnet's architecture advertises its own divisions. Set against that stability the opening of the balcony scene, where the same compositorial tradition handles rapid dialogue rather than formal verse, and the divisions grow arguable. The contrast is the article's first lesson. Lineation fails where the verse is doing something the page finds hard: moving fast, changing speakers, or swelling past the measure. It holds where the form is self-announcing. Editors have known this in practice for centuries; the scholarship of the last hundred years has given the knowledge a method, and the method a set of quarrels.

Those quarrels matter because the alternative to editorial judgement is not purity. A reader who demands the quarto's lineation exactly as printed is not reading Shakespeare unmediated; that reader is reading Thomas Creede's printing house, its apprentices, its space calculations, and in the first quarto's case an actor's memory, all unmediated. The question is never whether a mediating intelligence stands between the reader and the author. The question is whether the mediation is intelligent, documented, and honest about its own limits. Lineation is the test case because it is everywhere: a disputed word troubles one line, but a disputed principle of division troubles every rapid exchange in the play.

## How the Quartos Lay Verse on the Page: Copy, Compositors, and the Narrow Measure

A quarto of the 1590s is a small book, its page a fraction of the folio's, and its type area narrower than the verse it had to carry was long. The second quarto of Romeo and Juliet, printed by Thomas Creede for Cuthbert Burby in 1599, sets a play of some three thousand lines into that confined space, and the confinement leaves fingerprints on the lineation. A pentameter in the roman type of the period usually fits the measure; a pentameter swollen by a long word, an alexandrine reaching to twelve syllables, or a line the copyist had already crowded, does not. The compositor's remedy was the turned line: the overflow syllables are carried to a second typographic line, set indented beneath the first or tucked above it, so that one verse line occupies two printed lines. The convention was understood by its first readers. It is a trap for modern ones, and for the earlier editors who occasionally mistook a turn for a division and bequeathed the error to their successors.

### Why does a quarto page's narrow measure shape the verse it carries?

Because the compositor must fit a fixed count of syllables into a fixed width of type, and the page wins every argument the copy cannot afford. Long lines turn, crowded passages compress, and verse at the foot of a full page can be set as prose outright, so the physical book silently redraws the poem's architecture.

The deeper pressure was economic, and its name is casting off. Before setting began, the copy was counted and apportioned among compositors and gatherings, so that each workman knew how much manuscript had to fill how many pages. The estimate was exact arithmetic performed on inexact material, and when it failed, the failure landed on the text. Copy that ran long had to be squeezed: abbreviations multiply, stage directions crowd their speeches, and verse, whose white space is expensive, can be run on as prose to reclaim the line-end margins a whole page at a time. Copy that ran short had to be stretched, with spaced type and generous leading. The second quarto of this play was set from copy the scholarship identifies as the author's own working papers, the foul papers whose unfinished state the text betrays elsewhere in duplicated and unrevised passages; such copy, blotted and interlined, was the hardest of all to cast off accurately, and the wonder is less that its lineation sometimes buckles than that it holds as steadily as it does.

Behind the casting off stands the copy itself, and the two quartos had different copies with different diseases. The second quarto's copy was authorial and messy. The first quarto's copy, printed by John Danter and Edward Allde in 1597, was something else again: a text assembled away from the author's papers, on the dominant scholarly account reconstructed from the memories of actors who had played in the piece, and its lineation is the lineation of recall, a subject that earns its own section below. Even the Folio, which might have reset the standard, inherited the quarto tradition for this play, taking its text from the line of quartos that descends from the second, so that the great collected volume of 1623 transmitted the earlier books' divisions along with their words. For Romeo and Juliet there is no early witness that stands outside the quarto tradition and audits it. The editor's entire evidence for how the verse was lined is the evidence of two small books, one careful and one corrupt, and a folio that copied the careful one's descendants.

It helps to name the agents precisely, because "the quartos mislineate" conceals three separate culprits. The author may mislineate, drafting a line his own metre had not finished deciding, and foul papers preserve such hesitations. The scribe or book-keeper who prepared copy may mislineate, misreading a crowded page. The compositor may mislineate, through haste, misjudgement of his measure, or a casting-off failure he is silently repairing. Each culprit leaves a different signature, and the signatures matter to the editor deciding whether a strange division is a corruption to be healed or a datum to be preserved. An author's irregular line is evidence about composition; a compositor's is evidence about a printing house; confusing the two builds literary arguments on a workman's emergency. Much of the scholarship surveyed in this article is, at bottom, the discipline of telling those signatures apart.

## Verse Set as Prose: The Second Quarto's Most Visible Failure

The most startling lineation failure in the play's textual history is also the easiest to see. In the second quarto, a stretch of Mercutio's Queen Mab speech in 1.4 stands on the page as prose: the words run margin to margin in undivided blocks, and the fairy catalogue that every modern reader knows as verse appears in the copy-text of all modern editions wearing the dress of a prose anecdote. Editors restore the divisions, and the restoration is one of the least controversial in the play, because the words fall back into pentameters with almost no forcing, proper names and all, as if the lines were merely waiting for their white space to be returned to them. The passage is treated here at the length its function in this argument requires, as the type specimen of verse set as prose; its full collation across the early texts, variant by variant, is the business of the study of [The Queen Mab Speech Across the Quartos](/2026/05/08/queen-mab-speech-across-quartos/), which follows the speech through every witness.

Why would a printing house commit so visible an act? Two explanations divide the scholarship, and the division is instructive. On the first, the fault is the compositor's: casting off had failed at that gathering, space ran short, and prose setting, which abolishes the expensive white space at every line end, was the standard emergency economy. On the second, the fault is the copy's: the foul papers at that point were themselves disordered, the verse at the foot of a manuscript page written on in prose-like continuity or so interlined that the compositor followed what he saw. The evidence cannot finally convict either suspect, but it can weigh them. The first quarto, corrupt as it is, divides the same speech as verse, and a reported text's memory is of the spoken play, where the passage is verse in the ear whatever the papers held. Verse stands behind both witnesses; prose stands in only one, at exactly the sort of place where space pressure bites. The economy explanation carries the verdict, with the disordered-copy explanation retained as the honest caveat: the case teaches the method even where it withholds certainty, because it shows an editor reasoning from the shape of an error to its cause, and grading the conclusion as probable rather than proved.

### How many kinds of prose does the play contain, and which kind is an accident of print?

The play contains prose by design, in the mouths of servants, clowns, and the Nurse in her great reminiscent vein, and prose by catastrophe, where verse has collapsed into it on the page. Only the second kind is an accident. The editor's first task is telling them apart, since repairing the first kind would vandalize the play's social music.

The designed prose is itself the control experiment. Mercutio dies in prose: "No, 'tis not so deep as a well, nor so wide as a church door; but 'tis enough, 'twill serve" (3.1) stands unversified in every early witness, and no competent editor has ever tried to lineate it into pentameters, because its prose is the meaning, the wit's flat refusal of the verse world's consolations. The Nurse's long act 1 recollection of Juliet's weaning rambles in prose in the quartos, and its ramble is characterization. Against these certainties the Mab block convicts itself: it scans, it rhymes at its joints in the manner of the surrounding verse, and its companion passages in the same speech stand as verse in the same book. Prose in this play is a register the author commands; where it appears without register's reason, wearing verse's fingerprints, the page and not the poet is speaking. The traffic runs in both directions across the tradition: the first quarto even presents as verse one speech the second quarto gives as prose, a reminder that the boundary was porous in both printing houses, and that lineation evidence, handled carelessly, can be made to prove anything.

One more pressure deserves naming, because it operates silently in every crowded quarto: verse costs more paper than prose, and paper was the most expensive material in the book. A publisher who had bought the copy of a long play and a printer estimating his gatherings both knew the arithmetic. The second quarto advertises itself on its title page as "newly corrected, augmented, and amended," a longer text than its predecessor, and length was the selling point and the production problem in one. That the augmentation survived at all, at the price of a prose block here and turned lines there, is the material history of the text in miniature. The verse readers admire was expensive to print, and some of its lineation is the scar tissue of its cost.

## The Borderlands: Where Verse and Prose Change Places

Between the designed prose and the collapsed verse lies a borderland of passages where the two registers trade places across the witnesses, and the borderland is where lineation evidence is at its most delicate. The first quarto presents as verse one speech that the second quarto prints as prose, a fact recorded in the scholarship of the short text and worth pausing over. If prose-setting were always the printing house's economy, the traffic would run one way, from verse down into prose as space tightened. A speech travelling the other way, acquiring lineation in the corrupt witness that the authoritative witness denies it, breaks the simple theory. Three explanations compete. The reporting actors may have remembered the speech in the measure in which it was spoken and imposed verse on words their copy, the prompt tradition behind the second quarto, had genuinely held as prose; actors verse-ify in recall as naturally as compositors prose-ify in haste. The speech may stand at a register boundary the author himself had left undecided in foul papers, its status an open question the two transmissions answered differently. Or the second quarto's prose may itself be the accident, and the first quarto's verse the survival, in which case the borderland contains a passage where the corrupt witness corrects the good one on a matter of layout.

Editors decide such cases on the local evidence: whether the words scan without forcing, whether the speaker's register elsewhere in the scene supports the elevation, whether the passage's rhetoric is doing verse's work of pattern and climax or prose's work of bustle and information. The decision is recorded as an emendation either way, and the case stands in this article as the emblem of the borderland's lesson. The verse-prose boundary in the early texts is evidence, but it is evidence with a margin of error, and an editor who treats every prose block as collapsed verse will eventually lineate a passage the author wrote flat, manufacturing pentameters out of the Nurse's chatter and calling the manufacture recovery. Restraint at the border is the same virtue as boldness at the Mab block, applied in the opposite direction: the evidence, not the tidiness, governs.

The borderland has a geography. It clusters where scenes change register rapidly, in the Mercutio scenes where wit moves between prose banter and verse flight within a page, around the servants and musicians whose prose frames the verse of their betters, and at the openings of scenes, where a compositor settling into his stint is most likely to misjudge his copy's intentions. It thins to nothing in the verse's ceremonial interior, the Prince's judgements, the Friar's expositions, the lovers' set speeches. An editor approaching a doubtful passage first asks where on that map the passage stands, and lets the geography set the presumption before the scansion is even attempted. Geography is not proof. It is the prior probability that keeps proof honest.

## The First Quarto's Broken Lines: Lineation as the Record of a Memory

The first quarto of 1597 is roughly a third shorter than the second, and its lineation is the visible geology of its making. Where the second quarto's lines were drawn by a compositor looking at written copy, the first quarto's were drawn, on the prevailing account of its origin, by men remembering a performance: and memory does not store verse in lines. Memory stores sense, cue, and gesture; it keeps the phrase that got the laugh and the image that carried the scene, and it joins them with improvised connective tissue that scans approximately and divides wherever the breath of recollection happened to fall. Read the first quarto's verse with that origin in mind and its oddities organize themselves. Lines run together where two pentameters have fused in recall into one long utterance. Lines split at sense-pauses that the metre does not recognize, because the remembering actor paused there night after night. Speeches that the second quarto gives at full metrical stretch arrive compressed, their arguments intact and their measures collapsed, like a tune hummed by someone who knows the melody and not the barring.

Juliet's invocation at the opening of 3.2, the "Gallop apace" speech, shows the two transmissions side by side: the second quarto's version moves in ordered pentameters, while the first quarto's is shorter, its lines differently jointed, the sense surviving the metre. That speech's variants have their own dedicated comparison in the study of ['Gallop Apace': The Variants Compared](/2026/05/15/gallop-apace-textual-variants/), which settles what can be settled about its famous crux; for the lineation question the speech is one exhibit among many, and the exhibit's lesson is general. A reported text garbles lineation in a characteristic direction: toward the sense and away from the measure. A foul-papers text garbles it in the opposite direction, preserving measure through passages whose sense the author had not finished revising. An editor who knows which witness is speaking can weigh a strange division accordingly, trusting the second quarto's joints and suspecting the first quarto's, except where the first quarto's very roughness certifies contact with the stage.

### Where does relineation most often happen in modern editions of the play?

At three kinds of joint: where a quarto sets verse as prose and the block must be divided afresh; where rapid dialogue throws half-lines between speakers and the halves must be joined or left standing; and where the first quarto's compressed version disagrees with the second's divisions and the editor must choose a witness.

The full collation of the two quartos, the enterprise of laying these thousands of small disagreements side by side until a pattern convicts one witness or the other, is conducted in the comparison of [Q1 vs Q2: What the Differences Reveal](/2026/04/10/q1-vs-q2-romeo-and-juliet-differences/), and its findings frame everything claimed here. Lineation is one strand of that comparison, but a privileged one, because it is the strand where the first quarto's testimony is most nearly worthless and most nearly priceless at once. Worthless, because an actor's memory is no authority for where an author's line ended. Priceless, because where the first quarto's lineation agrees with the second's against probability, two independent transmissions have converged on the same joints, and the convergence is the strongest external evidence the play offers that a division is authorial. Editors lean on such convergences constantly, often without remarking them. The balcony scene's exchanges, where the lovers trade half-lines at speed, stand in both quartos divided at essentially the same points, and that agreement is a large part of why no modern edition relines them: the memory and the manuscript, arriving by different roads, point to the same verse.

There is a subtler gift in the first quarto's broken lines. They record where the play's first audiences, in the person of its own performers, felt the verse's joints to be. A division made by memory is a division made by the ear, and the ear of a man who had spoken the lines nightly is evidence about the verse's performed shape that no document of the study can supply. Lukas Erne's edition of the first quarto, which prints that text as a document in its own right rather than as a corruption to be mined, makes the point by its mere existence: the short text's lineation is data about performance, even where it is noise about composition. The mature editorial position holds both truths in their separate jurisdictions. For the constitution of the text, the second quarto's lineation rules. For the history of the play in the theatre, the first quarto's broken measures are a primary source, and relining them away in a documentary edition would destroy the evidence it exists to present.

## The Balcony Scene's Joints: A Passage Under the Lens

No passage tests an editor's lineation judgement more continuously than the balcony scene, 2.2, where the verse is at once the play's most famous and its most conversational, arias dissolving into exchanges and exchanges stretching back into arias without a seam the reader can trust the page to show. Consider the scene's physiology. Romeo's opening soliloquy, "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks?" (2.2), descends in settled measures; the copy's divisions and every edition's agree, because a single voice in meditation presents no interface at which corruption breeds. The moment Juliet appears and the scene becomes a duet, the interfaces multiply. Speeches shrink to fragments. "Ay me!" (2.2) is a line of two syllables that editors print standing alone, an exclamation whose missing feet are filled by Romeo's listening below and the audience's held breath; joined to anything, it would vanish, and with it the scene's first signal that its verse will now be traded rather than declaimed.

As the exchange quickens, the half-lines come in volleys. The parting sequence is the anthology case: Juliet is called within, returns, is called again, and the verse fractures under the pressure of the door she keeps almost closing. "A thousand times good night!" (2.2) has already served this article as the type of the eloquent short line; its companion fragments in the same passage show the witnesses behaving with unusual unanimity, both quartos fracturing at the same joints, and the unanimity is the editor's charter to leave the fractures alone. Where the scene's verse is whole again, in the great central declarations, "My bounty is as boundless as the sea" (2.2) and its surrounding measures, the divisions are whole in both witnesses too. The balcony scene thus contains its own demonstration of the article's thesis in miniature. The lineation is authorial where the drama is formal or solitary, collaborative where the drama is rapid and shared, and at the shared joints the collaboration was ratified by two independent transmissions before any editor touched it.

The scene also supplies the cautionary counterweight. Precisely because its exchanges are famous, they have attracted editorial improvement across the centuries, small regularizations of division in the eighteenth-century tradition that modern collations have since reversed in favour of the quartos. Each reversal is a small parable of the conservative turn: an editor's ear, however distinguished, is one reader's rhythm, while the quartos' agreement is an event in the text's history. Where the two conflict at a joint in 2.2, the modern settlement follows the event. That settlement is the practical form of the verdict the final section defends for the whole play: the divisions are the author's until the documents, not the taste of a later age, relieve him of them.

## Shared Lines: When Two Speakers Complete One Pentameter

The shared line is the point where lineation stops being a convenience and becomes an interpretation of the drama's physics. When Juliet, at the feast, caps Romeo's plea with "You kiss by th' book" (1.5), the line she answers is left hanging: "Give me my sin again" runs to six syllables, her reply to five, and the two fragments together complete a single pentameter, wit answering desire inside one measure. Editors divide on the presentation, and the division is a stage direction in disguise. Joined as one line, the exchange snaps shut without a seam, the kiss's choreography unbroken. Standing as two short lines, a beat of appraisal opens between plea and verdict, and Juliet gains a fraction of a second in which to judge the man she is about to love. Neither page is wrong about the words. They are offering different performances, and the difference was manufactured entirely by line breaks.

### Why do editors indent the second half of a shared line?

The indented continuation is the modern page's way of showing that a speech completes a measure begun by another voice, rather than starting a measure of its own. The convention keeps the pentameter visible to the scanning eye while preserving the change of speaker, so the reader sees the hinge and the joint in a single glance.

The device saturates the play's great duets. The balcony scene's leave-taking trades fragments as the Nurse's calls interrupt from within: Juliet's "A thousand times good night!" (2.2) is a half-measure left deliberately incomplete, its missing feet occupied by the offstage summons and her own divided attention. The aubade in 3.5 builds an entire scene on the hinge, the lovers completing each other's measures as they argue about whether the bird was the nightingale or the lark, the verse's joinery performing the union the plot is about to dissolve. In the tomb, Romeo's "Thus with a kiss I die" (5.3) falls short of the measure by four syllables, and the deficit is the point: the line dies before its time, as he does, and editors who print it standing alone, as all responsible editions do, preserve a rhythmic event no relineation could improve. These cases establish the spectrum on which editorial judgement works. At one end stand half-lines whose completion across a speech boundary is certain, because sense, speed, and both quartos' practice converge. At the other stand short lines whose incompletion is the meaning. Between them lies the disputed middle where the quarrels of the next section live.

The stakes are easiest to hear in performance. A joined line instructs the second actor to come in on the beat, riding the first speaker's measure without a gap; a separated short line licenses a pause, and pauses in this play are load-bearing, marking the instants where feeling outruns language. Directors work from editions, and editions work from compositors, and so a decision taken by a workman in Creede's shop in 1599, or corrected by an editor at a desk in 1980, reaches the stage as timing. George T. Wright, whose Shakespeare's Metrical Art remains the fullest modern account of what the verse line does, insists that the line is the unit in which Shakespeare thought rhythmically, and that every division is therefore a claim about how the verse means. The claim of this article is narrower and prior: before the divisions can be interpreted, they must be attributed, and a large share of the play's divisions were made by men who were not interpreting anything, but fitting type to a page or a part to a memory.

## Short Lines and the Pauses Editors Erase

Every editor of the play inherits a temptation: the short line looks like an unfinished job. A speech of six syllables stands on the page with white space at its right hand, and the tidying instinct reaches for the next speech to complete the measure. Sometimes the instinct is right, and the completion recovers a shared line the printer had split. Sometimes it vandalizes the text, because the short line was the meaning: a pause written into the verse, the silence in which a character hears, decides, or dies. "O happy dagger!" (5.3) runs to five syllables before Juliet's "This is thy sheath" begins a new measure; an editor who reached forward to fill the gap would join the dagger to its sheath in a single breath and strike the held instant in which she looks at the blade. No editor does this, because the case is obvious. The quarrel is over the hundreds of cases that are not obvious, where a short line might be a hinge or a held breath, and where the choice remakes the tempo of a scene.

Wright's metrical scholarship gives the conservative position its theory. On his account, Shakespeare's verse thinks in lines, and the short line is one of its expressive resources: a measure deliberately left incomplete, its missing feet supplied by stage action, by a look, by the audience's own startled arithmetic. A partisan might call this the doctrine of the eloquent gap; Wright's own prose is drier and stronger, grounding the short line's legitimacy in the statistical profile of the verse as a whole, which Marina Tarlinskaja's metrical studies of the canon independently confirm: the verse of this play tolerates a known proportion of short and shared lines as part of its design, and the proportions differ between scenes in ways that track the drama. A passage whose short lines cluster where the action quickens is exhibiting the design; a passage whose lines collapse only where a compositor's page was full is exhibiting the printing house. Statistics cannot decide a single case, but they can convict a pattern, and pattern evidence is what moves a relineation from taste to argument.

### Why does metre alone fail to settle a disputed line division?

Because the pentameter is a contract rather than a mould: syllable counts bend around elisions, feminine endings, and proper names, and a determined editor can scan any division into regularity or out of it. Metre supplies a presumption, never a proof, and must sit in judgement alongside sense, speech rhythm, and the witnesses' agreement before a line is moved.

Against Wright's presumption stands the working editor's experience, and its most distinguished local voice is Brian Gibbons, whose Arden edition of 1980 relines the copy-text wherever its divisions defeat the measure, and defends the practice in his collations with a craftsman's patience. Gibbons's implicit argument deserves stating at full strength. The second quarto's lineation is not sacred; it is the output of a commercial printing house working from difficult copy, and where that output produces lines no metrical theory can accommodate, lines that stumble in the mouth and mislead the eye, the probability that the compositor and not the author drew them is overwhelming. To print them unchanged is not fidelity but fetishism, the worship of an accident. The conservative rejoinder, practised by G. Blakemore Evans in the Cambridge edition, keeps the copy-text's divisions unless they are impossible, and prints the conservatism as a matter of evidence law: an editor who relines on a hunch destroys the datum every future reader would need to judge the hunch wrong. Between Gibbons's ear and Evans's evidence law the whole modern practice of the play's lineation is negotiated, edition by edition, line by line, and the disagreement is genuine because both parties are right about different risks: the risk of printing a compositor's stumble as Shakespeare's rhythm, and the risk of erasing Shakespeare's pause as a compositor's stumble.

The adjudication offered here sides with Wright and Evans on the presumption and with Gibbons on the exception. A copy-text division stands unless two independent witnesses convict it: the metre must refuse it, and the sense or the stage must refuse it too. Where only the metre objects, the line is probably an expressive irregularity, of which this play, the most lyrically experimental of the early tragedies, is full. Where metre and sense object together, as in the prose-set block of the Queen Mab speech, the division is a printing accident and repair is restoration, not conjecture. The rule is conservative but not inert, and its real function is to sort the play's thousands of divisions into the vast majority no one should touch and the small, locatable minority where an editor's ear is the only instrument available. How small that minority is, and what hangs on it, the comparison table and the verdict now measure.

The table's deepest row is the second. The feast sonnet matters to the lineation debate out of all proportion to its fourteen lines, because it proves the transmission could carry lineation perfectly when the verse's form made the divisions unmissable. The sonnet is the experiment's control: same compositors, same printing houses, same decade, and the joints arrive exact. It follows that the failures elsewhere are not a general fog of corruption through which the whole text swims. They are local accidents with local causes, a failed casting off at one gathering, a reporting actor's breath at another, and they yield to local repair. A scepticism that treats all editorial lineation as conjecture must explain away the sonnet; a confidence that treats all quarto lineation as authorial must explain away the prose block in 1.4. The evidence supports neither extreme, and the discipline's actual practice, at its best, is the refusal of both.

Note what the table does not contain: any passage where editors have rewritten the words. Lineation disputes are disputes about arrangement, and arrangement only, conducted over a verbal text whose authority is comparatively secure. That is precisely what makes the problem philosophically interesting. The play offers a case where the author's words are known, the author's divisions are partly lost, and the gap between the two is filled by a disciplined guesswork whose rules the next section states. The verse readers admire is real. The question is how much of its visible shape is a recovery, and the answer, when the table is read whole, is: the load-bearing shape is transmitted, and the recovery is concentrated, locatable, and at its best honestly signposted.

## The Folio's Inheritance: How 1623 Froze the Quartos' Lines

The First Folio of 1623 might have been the tradition's chance to start again. For this play it was not, because the Folio's text of Romeo and Juliet descends from the quarto line: its copy was a quarto of the second quarto's descent, the third quarto's line as later quartos transmitted it, and its compositors set the divisions they found. The consequence for lineation is a quiet catastrophe of authority. Agreements between the Folio and the modern text prove nothing about the author, since the Folio is a copy of a copy at this point in the stemma; and disagreements, the places where the Folio's compositors divided differently from their quarto copy, are the work of Jaggard's printing house, valuable as evidence of how the text was handled and worthless as evidence of how it was written. Charlton Hinman's analysis of the Folio's printing identified the workmen by their spelling habits, and for this play Compositors B and E share the stint; their lineation habits are part of the record of transmission, a stratum to be read through, not a voice to be obeyed.

What the Folio did give the play, it gave at the level of the book rather than the line: the dignity of the collected volume, the act divisions whose history belongs to another study in this sequence, and eventually, through the through-line numbering Charlton Hinman's edition made standard, the reference system by which scholars cite the text's smallest units. The irony is structural. The system for citing lines descends from the one early edition whose lineation for this play has no independent authority at all. Through-line numbers count the Folio's printed lines, prose and verse together, so that the very grid laid over the play for scholarly reference memorializes a derivative page. Editors live with the irony because the grid is indispensable, but the lineation student should register it: the play's lines are cited by a numbering drawn from the witness least entitled to draw them.

The Folio's real lineation legacy is negative and enormous: it froze. Once the collected edition existed, the quarto tradition's divisions acquired the momentum of print, reprinted from edition to edition through the seventeenth century's later folios, each resetting its predecessor's page. A compositor's expedient of 1599 could thus enjoy a century of unexamined life, and the eighteenth-century editors who finally began to question the divisions inherited them wearing the Folio's prestige. Breaking that momentum took the collation revolution described in a later section, and even now the default settings of the play's text, the places where no editor has thought to look, are the places where 1599 and 1623 agree for no better reason than descent. Lineation history is largely the history of that default being audited, passage by passage, at the speed of scholarship.

## Where Lineation Holds: Sonnets, Soliloquies, and the Set Speech

Against the failure map, the success map deserves equal precision, because the verdict on editorial recovery depends on how much of the play never needed recovering. The sonnets stand first: the two Chorus prologues and the lovers' feast exchange transmit their fourteen-line architectures intact through every witness, their lineation guaranteed by a form whose joints are structural. Rhyme assists the transmission throughout the play. The Friar's opening soliloquy in 2.3, "The grey-eyed morn smiles on the frowning night" (2.3), moves in couplets whose closures announce every line ending twice over, once in metre and once in sound, and its divisions descend unchallenged. Wherever Shakespeare writes in couplets, the lineation is nearly self-defending, and a compositor would have to work to lose it.

The soliloquy proper shows the same resilience for a different reason: length of utterance without interruption. Juliet's potion speech in 4.3, "Farewell! God knows when we shall meet again" (4.3), runs for dozens of lines in a single voice, and its divisions descend from the second quarto essentially untouched, because a sustained paragraph of verse presents the compositor with a simple task, copying measures in sequence, and the foul papers behind the copy had themselves settled the divisions in the act of composition. Romeo's final speech in the tomb, down to its famous truncated close, tells the same story: set speeches travel well. The pattern generalizes into a rule the whole article relies on. Lineation corrupts at interfaces: where speakers change, where registers switch between verse and prose, where a scene crowds its page, where a memory replaces a manuscript. It holds in the verse's open water. The play's most monumental passages are its most textually secure, and its most fragile are its quickest, which is why the editorial problem, small by count, sits so close to the drama's living tissue.

A further control deserves mention, the Chorus's second prologue, whose sonnet in the first quarto is absent altogether while the second quarto prints it complete. The contrast isolates the variables neatly. Where the short text lacks a passage, no lineation question arises from it; where the long text has the passage, the divisions are the copy's; and the editor's task in such a place is the plain one of faithful transmission. Most of editing is that plain task. The contested joints this article anatomizes are the residue, the places where transmission failed in interesting ways, and their interest should not be mistaken for their frequency. A map of the play's lineation drawn to scale would show a calm interior and a narrow, fascinating coastline, and the coastline is where the next sections walk.

## Compositors at Work: Stints, Skeletons, and the Shape of a Gathering

To judge a compositor's lineation fairly, the modern reader has to reconstruct his working day. He stood at a case of type with a portion of manuscript copy in front of him, copy he might hold for a day or a week depending on how the book had been cast off and divided. His unit of work was the page, but his unit of payment and fatigue was the stint, the continuous stretch set by one pair of hands before another workman took over, and stints begin and end in the middle of scenes, speeches, even sentences, wherever the division of copy happened to fall. At a stint boundary the risk to lineation doubles: the incoming compositor inherits his predecessor's decisions about measure and spacing without inheriting his knowledge of the copy's habits, and a passage that crosses the boundary can change its divisional character mid-speech for no reason but a change of hands. Bibliographers hunt stint boundaries precisely because anomalies cluster at them, and some of the play's local lineation oddities sit suspiciously where other evidence, spelling, type damage, the state of the skeleton forme that carried the page's headline and furniture, suggests a new workman took up the copy.

The formes through which a quarto sheet passed impose their own geography on the text. A quarto sheet carries eight pages, printed in two formes of four, and the pages of a gathering are not set in reading order: the compositor sets the pages that will be imposed together, leaping through the copy in a sequence dictated by the folding, which means his sense of the text's continuity is repeatedly broken by the mechanics of imposition. Verse tolerates this worse than prose, because verse's continuity is its meaning at the level of the line, and a man setting pages out of order, returning to a speech after an interval spent on another part of the scene, is a man likely to lose the measure's thread at exactly the joints where the modern editor later finds the thread lost. None of this excuses the errors; all of it explains their distribution, and explanation is what converts a suspicious division from a mystery into a diagnosis. The second quarto's compositors were, by the standards of the trade, careful men working on a prestigious reprint; the wonder this article keeps returning to is not their failure rate but their success rate, the thousands of lines that crossed formes, stints, and gatherings with their divisions intact.

Behind the compositors stands one more shadowy workman, the scribe of the copy itself. The foul papers behind the second quarto were the author's own, but between author and printing house a manuscript could pass through hands that regularized, transcribed, or annotated it, and each passage through hands is another chance for divisions to shift. Stage directions in the second quarto crowd and wander in ways that suggest copy in motion; lineation, less visible to a transcriber than words, could drift silently in the same passage. The editor's genealogy of error must therefore allow four generations: the author's pen, the possible transcript, the compositor's case, and the press's own corrections and corruptions. A strange line division in 1599 may be a fossil from any of the four, and assigning it to the right generation is half the science this article describes.

## The Promptbook's Shadow: How a Theatrical Copy Would Have Lined the Verse

One road not taken haunts the whole inquiry. The copy behind the second quarto was the author's papers, not the theatre's book, and a promptbook, the licensed manuscript from which the company performed, would have carried its own lineation, made by a book-keeper whose interest in divisions was practical: the book had to be readable at speed, in poor light, by a man following the performance with one ear and cueing entrances with the other. Prompt copy tends toward clarity over fidelity; it expands, marks, and regularizes, and its verse divisions serve the prompter's eye rather than the poet's ear. No such book survives for this play, and the first quarto, whatever its making, is the nearest surviving relative of the performed text, which is why its divisions, drawn from performance rather than from any manuscript, fascinate the scholarship out of proportion to their authority. The performed play had joints, felt nightly by the actors; the first quarto's broken measures are the only fossil of those joints, and a fossil, however distorted, is evidence of the living shape.

The contrast teaches a final methodological refinement. When the second quarto and the first agree in a division, two genealogies converge: the study and the stage, the pen and the breath. When they disagree, the editor must ask which jurisdiction the passage belongs to. A set speech belongs to the study; its divisions are compositional facts, and the second quarto's word is final. A rapid exchange belongs partly to the stage; its divisions are also performance facts, and the first quarto's testimony, while never decisive, is admissible in a way it is not for the soliloquies. Weighing witnesses by jurisdiction rather than by a single global ranking is the mark of the mature editing this play now receives, and lineation, the feature most sensitive to the difference between a page and a voice, is where the weighing matters most.

## How Editors Relineate: Method, Record, and the Limits of Conjecture

Editorial relineation begins from a classification problem that W. W. Greg's theory of copy-text made famous. Greg divided a text's features into substantives, the words themselves, which an editor chooses among witnesses on critical judgement, and accidentals, the spelling and punctuation, which follow the copy-text's authority. Lineation refuses the division. It looks like an accidental, a matter of layout; it behaves like a substantive, altering rhythm, emphasis, and at the joins of speeches the very distribution of sense. The mature practice treats it as a substantive wearing accidental's clothing: the copy-text's divisions are the default, every departure is an emendation, and every emendation is owed a defence in the collation notes. René Weis's Arden edition of 2012 and Jill Levenson's Oxford edition of 2000 both work under that discipline for this play, recording their relinings where a reader can audit them, and the record is the difference between scholarship and taste. A relined passage with a note is an argument; a relined passage without one is an assertion, and the history of the play's editing contains assertions enough.

### What does the second quarto's page reveal about its copy at the joins of speeches?

That the copy behind it was a working manuscript rather than a fair copy prepared for readers: its joins are the author's own, made at the speed of composition, mostly exact and occasionally crowded or unresolved where revision had passed through. The page preserves a writer dividing his verse as he wrote it, not a scribe tidying the divisions afterward.

The working method of a relineation, stripped to its steps, runs as follows. The editor first establishes what the witnesses actually print, resisting the edited texts through which the quartos are usually read. Then the passage is scanned in each available division, and the scansions are compared for metrical cost: a division that requires three forced elisions loses to one that requires none. Then sense is weighed, because a line ending is a small act of punctuation and a division that strands a verb from its object across an implausible pause convicts itself. Then the witnesses are weighed against each other, the second quarto outranking the first on composition, the first outranking the second on nothing except the stage. And finally the decision is recorded, so that the next editor inherits the problem rather than the conclusion. Researchers who attempt this weighing for themselves, with the quartos' divisions and three modern editions open at once, discover why the professionals keep parallel-text notebooks and collation files; a private research archive such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/) exists for exactly this species of work, the holding of rival versions side by side until their pattern declares itself.

The limits of the method are as important as the method, and the brief for this article rightly insists on them. Conjecture enters at the point where the witnesses fail together, and they do fail together: both quartos descend, by different roads, from performances and papers neither of which survives, and there are divisions, particularly in the rapid prose-verse borderlands of the Mercutio scenes, where the author's intention is simply unrecoverable and two editions divide differently with equal warrant. The honest response is not despair but labelling. An edition that relines silently in such a place teaches its readers a false confidence, and a criticism that builds an interpretation on a conjectural division builds on sand it has not inspected. The practical rule for readers follows directly. Interpretations may rest on the words anywhere. Interpretations that rest on a line break should check whose line break it is: the second quarto's, the first quarto's, or a named editor's recorded decision. The check takes a minute and is the cheapest rigour in the study of this play.

There remains the question of scale, the counting question, and it deserves a straighter answer than it usually gets. Nobody has relined this play wholesale, and nobody needed to. The second quarto's divisions serve, unaltered, for the overwhelming run of the text; the standard editions' collations record their departures in dozens, scattered at the pressure points this article has mapped, not in hundreds and not evenly. Set against the whole, editorial lineation is a small correction. Set against the passages readers quote, memorize, and build readings on, it is anything but small, because the pressure points and the famous moments are the same places: the speeches that strain the measure are the speeches the culture kept. The verdict therefore has to be given in two currencies at once, the count and the weight, and the final section gives it.

## A Short History of Relining the Play: Regularizers, Collators, and the Conservative Turn

The eighteenth century edited the play's lineation with a free hand and a clear conscience. Nicholas Rowe's edition of 1709, the first edited Shakespeare, took its text from the late folios and adjusted the verse to Augustan expectations of smoothness, and Alexander Pope went further, treating irregular measure as evidence of corruption or interpolation and handling the lines accordingly. The regularizers were not vandals; they were applying the best metrical theory of their age, which held the pentameter to be a stricter instrument than the Elizabethan stage had practised. Their error was the mirror image of the facsimile fetish: where the conservative trusts the page because it is old, the regularizer corrects the page because it is rough, and roughness in this play is often the signature of the genuine. Juliet's broken measures in the potion speech and the lovers' fragmentary exchanges in the aubade survived the century in the text, but they survived as tolerated blemishes, and more than one passage emerged from the period's editing smoother and worse.

The correction came from collation. Lewis Theobald's generation established the method of deciding readings by documentary evidence, and Edward Capell's systematic return to the early quartos gave editors, for the first time, the second quarto's page as a working standard rather than a rumour behind the folios. From Capell forward, the history of the play's lineation is the history of the copy-text's rehabilitation: each generation's edition stands closer to Q2's divisions than its predecessor's, and the departures require progressively better paperwork. The nineteenth century's great variorum and Cambridge traditions regularized the recording of variants, so that a relining could be checked against the witnesses from the foot of the page. The twentieth century completed the turn with Greg's theory and the new bibliography's printing-house analysis, which together supplied what the regularizers lacked and the collators wanted: a principled account of which features of a printed page deserve trust. The conservative presumption this article adjudicated, copy-text lineation stands until metre and sense jointly convict it, is the settlement that history produced. It is not timidity. It is the scar tissue of two centuries of editors learning, in both directions, what a line break costs.

## Lineation as Evidence: What a Division Can Prove About Copy

Lineation is not only a problem to be solved; it is a witness to be examined, and some of the surest knowledge about the play's transmission arrives through its divisions. The principle is the bibliographer's oldest: shared errors prove shared descent, and lineation errors are errors of a particularly fingerprint-like kind. When the Folio reproduces the second quarto tradition's divisions at points where those divisions are unlikely to have been invented twice, descent is demonstrated as cleanly as by any verbal variant, and the stemma of the play's texts, quarto to quarto to folio, rests partly on such agreements in arrangement. Conversely, a passage where the first quarto's divisions agree with the second's against the odds is evidence that the reporting memory had hold of something written, or that both transmissions converge on a performed shape stable enough to impress itself on paper twice.

Compositor study draws on the same evidence from the other side. Workmen have habits at the joins of lines as they do in spelling: a preference for turning lines under rather than over, a way of handling a speech that begins mid-measure, a tendency to crowd or to spare. Where the analysis of spelling has identified stints in the Folio, the analysis of lineation supplements it, and in the quartos the divisions can corroborate a casting-off reconstruction, showing where a compositor began to fear for his space before the verbal text confesses it. The prose block in the Queen Mab speech is the play's locus classicus for this kind of reasoning: its position and extent are data about the gathering in which it stands, and bibliographers read it the way geologists read a fault line, as the record of a pressure the surface conceals elsewhere.

There is a discipline of restraint to observe here, and the scholarship has not always observed it. A single odd division proves almost nothing; compositors nod, memories slip, and any individual anomaly can be accident without ancestry. Lineation evidence convicts in patterns or not at all. The aubade's consistent half-line exchanges across both quartos are a pattern. One turned line in the Friar's soliloquy is weather. Editors who respect the distinction can use divisions as documents; editors who do not build genealogies on noise, and the play's textual history contains a few such structures, cited here as a warning rather than a tour.

## Lineation in Performance: The Beat, the Breath, and the Actor's Page

The final jurisdiction of lineation is the stage, where divisions stop describing the verse and start directing it. An actor working from a modern edition receives the editor's lineation as a set of rhythmic instructions, most of them silent. The shared line says: do not wait. The short line says: here is a space, to be filled with the action the verse declined to name. Verse-speaking pedagogy in the modern theatre, the tradition associated with the Royal Shakespeare Company's rehearsal rooms, builds on exactly these signals, teaching actors to ride the measure's completions and to honour its gaps rather than papering them with business. When the pedagogy and the bibliography are set side by side, an uncomfortable dependence appears. The actor's beat at the feast, the held instant before the dagger, the unbroken hinge of the aubade: each descends from an editor's decision about a compositor's page, and where the decision was conjecture, the production is performing the conjecture with total conviction. Performance cannot pause to footnote. It can only choose its edition knowingly, which is the stage's version of the reader's rule about checking a line break's parentage.

Directors who work with the first quarto in rehearsal report the reciprocal discovery: its broken lineation, worthless as authority, is eloquent as a record of a company solving the play at performance speed. The compressions fall where a touring cast would compress, the joints at the breaths of men who had to get through the scene nightly, and a production that studies those joints learns something about the verse's performed skeleton that the perfected page hides. The two quartos thus divide the theatre's inheritance between them. The second supplies the text the actors speak. The first supplies the earliest surviving evidence of how speaking it felt, its divisions drawn by the only editors who ever worked from inside the event, the players themselves. Lineation, which began this inquiry as a printer's problem, ends it as the point where bibliography and performance history are the same subject seen from two sides.

## Elision and the Syllable Count: The Small Tools of the Trade

Every lineation judgement in this article has quietly depended on counting syllables, and the counting is less mechanical than it looks, because Elizabethan verse contracts and expands its words to fit the measure in ways the printed page only partly records. "Ta'en" sheds a syllable by apostrophe; "e'en" and "o'er" wear their contractions openly; but the quartos leave many elisions unmarked, trusting the reader's ear to swallow a syllable in "heaven," to compress "spirit" toward one beat, or to let a proper name, Verona, Capulet, Montague, occupy the metrical space its music requires rather than the space its letters claim. The editor who would convict a quarto line of being a syllable too long must first decide how the line was meant to be pronounced, and that decision is a judgement about verse practice, not an act of arithmetic. Marina Tarlinskaja's statistical profiles of the canon matter here precisely because they replace the single editor's ear with the distribution of the whole practice: a line type that occurs in two percent of the play's secure lines is a licence the verse grants itself, and a line type that occurs nowhere else is a corruption asking for repair.

The feminine ending is the commonest licence and the one most often mistaken for error by beginners: the eleventh, unstressed syllable that closes some of the play's most famous lines, "To be, or not to be" being the canon's type specimen from another play, while this play's own "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks?" shows the measure's ordinary strength without it. In the lineation wars the feminine ending matters because a line of eleven syllables shared between speakers can be misread as a shared pentameter plus a fragment, or a true pentameter with a feminine close misread as an alexandrine's beginning. Each misreading generates a false relineation, an editor dividing where the author merely breathed. The discipline the scholarship has built around elision and licences is, at bottom, a humility mechanism: it defines how irregular a line may be before irregularity becomes evidence, and it keeps the threshold high enough that the author's variety survives the audit.

Proper names deserve their own sentence in the small-tools inventory, because Verona's families carry names whose syllables bend. Juliet is two beats or three as the line requires, Romeo expands and contracts, and Mercutio's name, four syllables in the dictionary, rides the verse like a wave the metre has learned to absorb. An editor ignorant of the names' flexibility will find false faults all over the lovers' scenes. An editor who knows it will find the true faults standing out in higher relief, the lines no licence can save, and those, concentrated at the interfaces this article has mapped, are the legitimate quarry of relineation.

## What Relineation Does to Meaning: Emphasis, Syntax, and the Moving Break

A line ending is a stress the syntax did not order. Whatever word stands at the end of a pentameter receives a small promotion, the ear's salute to the measure's completion, and moving a break therefore redistributes emphasis through a sentence as surely as moving a spotlight redistributes a stage. The point is abstract until a case makes it bleed. In Capulet's rage in 3.5, the verse cracks into fragments as the old man's syntax cracks: "Hang thee, young baggage! Disobedient wretch!" (3.5) arrives in blows, and the editions that preserve the quarto's fractured disposition preserve the blows. A regularized version of the same words, smoothed into even measures, would report the same fury in a calmer voice, which is to say it would report a different fury. Lineation here is not the clothing of the meaning but part of its musculature, and the editor who moves a break in such a passage is revising the performance of the emotion.

Syntax makes the same point from the grammarian's side. A break that falls at a clause boundary reads as a pause the sentence endorses; a break that falls mid-phrase, enjambment's territory, reads as overflow, the thought outrunning the measure. The quartos and the editions disagree in places about which of these a given passage performs, and the disagreement changes the psychology an attentive reader infers. Juliet in the balcony scene, on one disposition, thinks in balanced periods, her measures and her sentences closing together like doors. On another, her thought spills past the line ends in the hurry of feeling, and the measures strain to contain her. Both Juliets are built from identical words. The lineation chooses between them, and since no witness can be cross-examined, the choice must be argued from the run of the scene and the habits of the witnesses, which is to say it must be argued as criticism wearing bibliography's clothes. This is the deepest reason the subject repays a full study rather than a textual footnote: the smallest unit of the page turns out to be a unit of interpretation, and every reader of the play has been reading interpretations at the joints of every rapid scene, mostly without knowing the joints were there.

## Facsimiles and Diplomatic Texts: Reading the Quarto Page Directly

The modern reader who wants to inspect the evidence at first hand is better served than any previous generation. Charlton Hinman's facsimile of the First Folio put the great book's pages, with their turned lines and prose blocks, into ordinary libraries, and the second quarto's pages circulate in facsimile and in digital reproduction through the major research libraries, so that the crowded measures of 1599 can be examined at leisure rather than taken on trust. At the scholarly pole, Lukas Erne's edition of the first quarto prints the short text diplomatically in spirit, its lineation honoured as the document's own, so that the memory's joints can be studied as data. Between the facsimile and the diplomatic edition, the reader possesses the full laboratory: the witnesses as they were, and the modern editions as arguments about them.

Working in that laboratory changes a reader's relation to the edited text in one durable way. The modern page stops looking like the play and starts looking like a verdict about the play, which is what it is: a series of judgements, mostly inherited, mostly right, and at the contested joints openly provisional. That is not a loss of innocence but a gain of accuracy, and it is available to any reader willing to spend an hour with the quarto page of the Queen Mab speech or the balcony exchanges. The divisions will never look innocent again. They will look like what they have always been, the record of a poem's passage through hands, some inspired, some hurried, all human, and the poem's survival of the passage is the closest thing textual scholarship has to a proof of strength.

## Why This Play Suffers Most: Lyric Density at Conversational Speed

It is worth asking why the lineation problem presses harder on this play than on its fellows, because the answer explains the map of failures this article has drawn. Romeo and Juliet is the most lyrically saturated of the early tragedies: sonnets in the prologues and at the lovers' meeting, arias of oxymoron and invocation, a heroine whose speeches repeatedly rise into formal lyric, and all of it moving at conversational speed, the scenes built from exchanges rather than orations. Lyric verse advertises its joints, as the sonnets prove, and is safe. Conversational verse hides its joints in the traffic of voices, and is exposed. This play contains more of both than any comparable text of its decade, set cheek by jowl, so that a compositor or a reporting actor passes from the safest verse in the canon to the most fragile within a single scene, and the failure rate varies accordingly. The feast sequence is the emblem: a sonnet transmitted perfectly, framed by the prose of servants and the verse of the dance, three registers on facing pages, each with its own divisional fate.

The play's speed compounds the exposure. Its famous compression of time has a metrical analogue: speeches shorten as the plot accelerates, the last acts trade in fragments, and fragments are the natural prey of lineation error, since a fragment's status, hinge or held breath, must be judged rather than merely copied. A slower play gives its compositors long measures to copy and its editors few decisions to take. This play gives both the opposite, and so its textual history concentrates in miniature the whole problem of editing Shakespeare's verse: an author whose line was an expressive instrument of unusual subtlety, transmitted by mechanisms tuned for coarser work. The editors who have served the play best are those who understood that the subtlety and the speed are the same fact, and that relining the verse is therefore not the repair of a text but the continuation of a performance, undertaken with documents instead of voices.

## The Classroom's Page: Lineation as Most Readers Meet It

Most readers meet the play's lineation in a school or paperback edition whose divisions arrive without any visible parentage, and the meeting shapes the reading more than the reader knows. The classroom page numbers the verse in fives and tens down the margin, a grid drawn from the editorial tradition that makes citation easy and makes the divisions look like facts of nature, surveyed rather than decided. A student who learns the aubade from such a page learns its hinges as givens; the possibility that the hinge was an editor's judgement, or a compositor's, does not arise, because nothing on the page suggests that a judgement occurred. The anthology tradition compounds the effect by excerpting: the Queen Mab speech circulates in collections as a self-standing poem, its restored pentameters gleaming, its prose incarnation in the copy-text unmentioned, so that the most famous passage of mislineation in the play is universally known in its repaired form and almost never known as a repair.

This is not an argument against clean reading texts, which serve their purpose honourably. It is an argument for one hour of dirt under the fingernails somewhere in a reader's education: one passage, the Mab block or the balcony joints, read in the quarto's disposition and then in the edition's, until the difference between a text and a transmission becomes a felt thing rather than a doctrine. The play survives the hour improved, because its verse, seen once as a recovered thing, is never again mistaken for a printed inevitability. Teachers who build that hour into the study of the play report the same discovery the scholarship made across two centuries: the students stop asking whether the editors should have intervened, and start asking the only question that matters, whether they intervened well.

## A Note on Numbers: What Counting the Lines Can and Cannot Show

The argument of this article has repeatedly appealed to scale, dozens of interventions against thousands of lines, and the appeal deserves its own scrutiny before the verdict rests on it. Counting lineation interventions is possible only against a chosen copy-text and a chosen edition, and the count moves with the choices: set the conservative Cambridge text beside the second quarto and the departures are few; set an eighteenth-century text beside it and they multiply past counting, because the older editing relined as a matter of taste rather than of record. The modern count is therefore a statement about modern practice as much as about the quartos, a measure of how far the conservative settlement has disciplined intervention. What does not move with the choices is the distribution. Under every counting regime the interventions cluster at the same addresses: the prose borderlands, the rapid exchanges, the joins of scenes and stints. Distribution, not totals, is the robust evidence, and it is distribution that the comparison table in this article was built to display.

Counting has a second limit, subtler and worth naming in a study that has used numbers as rhetoric. A lineation intervention at a quiet joint, some exchange of servants in a transitional scene, and an intervention at the feast or the tomb count identically in a tally and weigh altogether differently in the culture. The verdict's two currencies, count and weight, are not a rhetorical flourish but a confession about the instrument: the play's text is not a uniform field in which every line matters equally, and any statistic that treats it as one will lie while telling the truth. The honest summary is the one the evidence supports in both currencies at once, small by count, load-bearing by weight, and it is with that summary in hand that the final reckoning can be made. Readers who distrust all such summaries on principle are invited to perform the count for a single act, the second, against a facsimile of the second quarto: the labour of an hour, and the shortest road to the calibration this article defends, since the act contains a sonnet's certainty, a balcony scene's negotiated joints, and a parting's fragments, the whole problem in one sitting.

## The Editor's Ear: Taste Dressed as Method, Method Disciplining Taste

Running beneath every argument in this article is an instrument no bibliography can calibrate: the editor's ear. Scansion is presented as measurement, and at the level of the syllable count it is; but the decisions that matter are taken one level up, where two divisions both scan, both construe, and the choice between them is made by a rhythm felt in the body of a reader who has lived with the play for years. Alexander Pope had such an ear and trusted it past the evidence, smoothing the verse toward his century's music and demoting what resisted. Brian Gibbons has such an ear and distrusts it in public, showing in collation after collation the documentary path by which each relining was reached, so that a reader who rejects the conclusion can still use the evidence. The difference between the two editors is not talent. It is whether the ear submits its verdicts to audit.

The audit metaphor deserves pressing, because it explains the modern settlement better than any doctrine. An edition is a set of accounts: the copy-text is the opening balance, every emendation a transaction, the collation the ledger in which transactions are justified. Lineation entries in the ledger are peculiarly revealing, because they expose the editor's working theory of the verse at the exact points where theory was needed. Read the collations of the Arden, Oxford, and Cambridge texts of this play consecutively and three temperaments emerge from the small print: an interventionist who relines where the measure limps and argues the limp; a conservative who lets divisions stand that a stricter ear would move, preferring a documented oddity to an elegant guess; and a mediator whose edition tries to teach the reader to hear both texts, the quarto's and the editor's, at the contested joints. None of the three is the villain of the story. The villain, where the story has one, is the silent edition, the reading text that relines without a ledger and leaves its purchasers believing that line breaks grow on trees.

For the reader, the practical skill is learning to hear the difference between a division and a decision. A division is what the witness prints; a decision is what an editor did about it, and the distance between the two is the exact size of the editorial recovery in that place. The skill is acquired the way all textual skills are acquired, by exposure: an hour with the quartos' facsimiles, an hour with a parallel text, a deliberate passage through one scene in three editions, watching the joints move. After that exposure the smooth modern page reads differently forever, not with suspicion but with gratitude properly addressed: to the author for the verse, to the compositors for its survival, and to the editors, named and auditable, for the recovery of the parts the survival lost. The verdict that closes this article distributes that gratitude in its measured proportions.

## How Much of the Verse Is Editorial Recovery? The Verdict

The count first, because the count disciplines the rhetoric. Of the play's roughly three thousand lines, the divisions a reader meets in a modern edition descend from the second quarto without editorial interference in the overwhelming majority of cases: the soliloquies, the set speeches, the sonnets, the great verse paragraphs of the Friar and the Prince stand lined as Creede's compositors set them from the author's papers, and no editor has found reason to move them. Editorial recovery concentrates in three territories. The first is the prose collapse: blocks where the printing house abandoned lineation altogether, of which the Queen Mab passage is the great instance, where recovery is total within the block and certain in its result, because the words scan only one way. The second is the hinge: the shared and short lines of rapid dialogue, where the witnesses often agree and editions intervene in handfuls of places, dividing or joining on the adjudicated presumption that a copy-text division stands until metre and sense together convict it. The third is the first quarto's shadow: passages where the short text's divisions differ from the long text's, and the editor, following the second quarto, is in effect voting for the manuscript's joints against the memory's, a vote cast so routinely that its scale hides inside every parallel-text comparison.

By weight, the verdict reverses the count's comfort. The recovered and adjudicated divisions sit at the play's most trafficked crossings: the Mab catalogue that every anthology prints, the feast exchange where the lovers meet, the partings and deaths where short lines carry the silences. A reader's experience of the verse, the scanned and memorized experience on which the play's reputation as a poem rests, is disproportionately an experience of passages an editor has touched. This is not a scandal; the touches are documented, the documents are public, and the alternative, a facsimile fidelity that prints a compositor's emergency as a poet's rhythm, would be the greater falsification. But it is a fact worth carrying into every reading. The pentameters are Shakespeare's. Their disposition on the modern page is a collaboration across four centuries, and the collaborators include two printing houses, one company's worth of remembering actors, and a line of editors from the eighteenth century's regularizers to the present scholarship's evidence lawyers.

Three practical consequences follow for anyone who reads, teaches, or performs the play. First, quote editions, not websites: a quotation's lineation is part of its text, and a text that names no editor has silently chosen one. Second, distrust any interpretation that leans its full weight on a single line break until the break's parentage is known; the collation notes of the Arden, Oxford, and Cambridge editions answer the question in a minute. Third, and most hopefully, learn to read the quartos' pages themselves where they survive in facsimile and in Erne's edition of the short text: the broken measures of 1597 and the crowded measures of 1599 teach the verse's resilience better than any smooth page can, because they show what the poem survived. The verse was never the fragile thing in this history. The fragile thing was the line, the drawn boundary between one breath and the next, and the record of its breaking and recovery is the record of the play's passage from a company's repertory to a culture's possession. Lineation is where that passage left its clearest tracks, and reading them is reading the play's biography written in its own smallest units.

One further consequence belongs to the writers of the next editions, digital ones, in which the page's constraints that caused the whole problem have vanished. An electronic text can present the quartos' lineation and the editor's lineation as layers rather than rivals, letting a reader toggle between the disposition of 1599 and the disposition of the chosen edition, and watch the joints move. Such an edition would not abolish editorial judgement; it would display it, converting the silent decisions of the print tradition into visible ones and returning to every reader the audit that print reserved for scholars. Until that edition is standard, the discipline this article prescribes remains the manual version of the same virtue: know whose lines are on the page, and read them in that knowledge. The verse can bear the scrutiny. It has been bearing it, through worse centuries than this one, since a compositor in Creede's shop first fitted it to a narrow page and got nearly all of it right. That near-success is the ground on which the whole inquiry stands, and the recovery of the small remainder is the scholarship's honourable, unfinished, and well-documented business.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch lineation comparison, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch lineation comparison is the table in this article that sets eight passages' presentation in the early quartos against their presentation in modern editions, naming what each division does to the verse. The pattern it exposes is a map of pressure: editorial repair is total where a printing house collapsed verse into prose, absent where a formal structure like the feast sonnet announced its own joints, and genuinely contestable only in the narrow band of shared and short lines where speakers trade fragments of a measure. The comparison's purpose is calibration. It shows that editorial recovery in this play is concentrated and locatable rather than general, and that the famous passages and the fragile passages are largely the same passages.

### Q: What does mislineation mean in an early printed play text?

Mislineation means that the printed page divides the verse at points other than the ones the verse's own measure establishes: lines are split where no line ends, joined where a line should end, or abandoned altogether when verse is set as prose. The words may be perfectly transmitted while their arrangement is corrupt, which is what makes mislineation insidious: a reader can trust every word on the page and still be reading a rhythm the author never wrote. In the quartos of this play the causes are identifiable, a compositor's space failure, a narrow measure forcing turned lines, or a reporting actor's memory joining and splitting at the breaths of recollection rather than at the joints of the pentameter.

### Q: Why would a compositor set verse as prose in a quarto?

A compositor set verse as prose because prose is cheaper in space: it abolishes the white margin at every line end and lets the words fill the page completely. The occasion was usually a failure of casting off, the advance estimate by which copy was apportioned to pages; when the copy for a gathering ran longer than its allotted space, something had to give, and lineation, invisible to a casual buyer, gave before the words did. A disordered stretch of manuscript copy could produce the same result innocently, where the verse on a crowded foul page was hard to distinguish from prose. The prose block in the second quarto's Queen Mab speech is the play's great instance, and editors restore its pentameters because the words fall into measure with almost no forcing.

### Q: What is a turned line in a quarto, and how can it mislead a modern reader?

A turned line is a single verse line too long for the page's measure, whose overflow syllables the compositor carried onto a second typographic line, usually indented beneath the first. The quarto page was narrow, and a pentameter swollen by long words or an alexandrine's extra feet often could not fit its allotted width. The convention was transparent to the book's first readers, who understood the indentation as continuation. It misleads modern readers, and trapped some early editors, because the turned tail looks exactly like a short verse line of its own, inviting a pause the verse never wrote and, in a critical edition prepared carelessly, hardening into a false division transmitted to later texts.

### Q: How can a reader tell whether a passage in a modern edition has been relineated?

A reader can tell by consulting the edition's collation or textual notes, where scholarly editions record every departure from the copy-text's lineation as an emendation. The Arden, Oxford, and Cambridge editions of this play all keep such records, typically in a collation at the foot of the page or in a textual appendix, listing the quarto's disposition against the editor's. A reading text without notes gives no such signal, which is why quotations drawn from unnamed sources are unreliable in their divisions as well as their words. The practical test takes a minute: find the passage in the edition's apparatus and look for a lineation entry. Where none exists, the division is the second quarto's own.

### Q: What evidence do editors use to decide where one verse line ends and the next begins?

Editors weigh four kinds of evidence in sequence. Metre comes first: a proposed division is scanned, and one requiring forced elisions or impossible stresses loses to a cleaner rival. Sense follows, since a line ending behaves like punctuation and a division stranding a verb from its object across an implausible pause convicts itself. The witnesses are weighed next, the second quarto's divisions outranking the first quarto's on composition while the first quarto's agreement can corroborate a joint from the stage side. Finally the statistics of the verse's licences, its tolerated rates of feminine endings and short lines, set the threshold at which irregularity becomes evidence of corruption rather than of design.

### Q: Why does the first quarto's lineation look so much rougher than the second quarto's?

The first quarto's lineation is rougher because its copy was not a manuscript but, on the prevailing scholarly account, a reconstruction from the memories of actors who had performed the play. Memory stores sense, cue, and gesture; it does not store verse in lines. The remembering actors joined pentameters where the sense ran on, split them where their own nightly breathing paused, and compressed speeches until the argument survived and the measure collapsed. The second quarto, set from the author's working papers, shows a compositor's occasional failures against a background of authorial order. The first shows recollection's failures against a background of performance, which is why its roughness is evidence about the stage even while it is worthless as authority for the study.

### Q: Can mislineation change the meaning of a speech, or only its rhythm?

Mislineation changes meaning as well as rhythm, because a line ending redistributes emphasis through everything around it. The word at a line's close receives the ear's salute to the completed measure, so moving a break promotes one word and demotes another within the same sentence. A break at a clause boundary reads as the sentence's own pause; a break mid-phrase reads as overflow and hurry, and whole interpretations of a character's state of mind have been built on the difference. Capulet's rage in 3.5 is the standing example: its fractured disposition in the quartos performs a fury that a smoothed, regularized lineation would report in a calmer and therefore different voice.

### Q: What happens to a shared line when an editor prints its two halves as separate short lines?

A silence appears that the verse did not write. The shared line is a hinge: the second speaker completes the first speaker's measure, and the completion instructs the ear, and in performance the actor, to take no pause at the change of voice. Printed as two short lines, the same words acquire a gap at exactly the joint where the composition built continuity, and the exchange slows, hesitates, sometimes changes character altogether, from the snap of wit to the caution of negotiation. At the feast, Romeo's "Give me my sin again" and Juliet's "You kiss by th' book" (1.5) can be paginated either way, and the two pages describe two different temperatures of the same flirtation.

### Q: What does George T. Wright argue is lost when editors join short lines into shared pentameters?

Wright argues that the short line is one of the verse's expressive instruments, a measure deliberately left incomplete whose missing feet are supplied by action, by a look, or by silence, and that joining it to a neighbour's speech erases the event it creates. In Shakespeare's Metrical Art he treats the line as the unit in which the playwright thought rhythmically, so every editorial join is a claim that a rhythmic event was an accident. What is lost, on his account, is not tidiness but information: the pause before a recognition, the held instant before a stab, the beat in which feeling outruns language. His statistical allies confirm that this play's verse licenses short lines at rates that track the drama, which makes wholesale joining a falsification of a pattern rather than the repair of a series of accidents.

### Q: How much of the play's lineation descends from the second quarto rather than from editorial repair?

Nearly all of it, by count. The set speeches, soliloquies, sonnets, and great verse paragraphs stand in modern editions lined as Thomas Creede's compositors set them from the author's papers in 1599, and the standard editions' collations record departures in dozens of places, not hundreds. The qualification is weight: the repaired and adjudicated divisions cluster at the play's most quoted crossings, the Queen Mab catalogue restored from a prose block, the feast exchange's closing hinge, the fragments of the partings and deaths. A reader's lived experience of the verse is therefore disproportionately an experience of passages an editor has touched, even though those passages are a small minority of the whole. Both figures are the honest answer, and the article's verdict keeps them together.

### Q: If two editions divide the same speech into different lines, which lineation should a quotation follow?

The quotation should follow the edition the writer is quoting, cited by name, and where the division itself is under discussion, the second quarto's disposition is the court of first resort. Editions differ at contested joints because the evidence underdetermines the answer, and neither page is simply wrong; but a quotation silently mixing one edition's words with another's lineation manufactures a text that no witness and no editor ever produced. The scholarly convention is to quote one edition faithfully, record significant lineation variants in a note where they matter, and treat the quarto's division as the datum any argument must start from. For classroom purposes the named edition on the course list governs, precisely so that a class shares one set of decisions.

### Q: Why do some quarto speeches run two verse lines together as one long line?

Run-together lines arise from the same pressures that produce the other failures, acting on the measure from the opposite side. A compositor saving space could abolish a line ending and let two pentameters run on as a single typographic line, reclaiming the margin at the suppressed joint; on a narrow quarto page such a doubled line then needed turning, and its tail could masquerade as a further line. A reporting memory produced the same shape by a different road, fusing two measures where the sense ran continuously and recollection felt no joint. Editors detect the fusion by the doubled caesura and the twenty-syllable span, and divide the line again at the point where sense, rhyme, or the parallel witness marks the seam.

### Q: How does lineation change the speed of the lovers' exchanges in performance?

Lineation is the page's tempo marking. A shared line instructs the second actor to enter on the beat, completing the measure without a gap, so joined half-lines accelerate an exchange to the speed of thought; separated short lines license pauses, and the same words proceed at the speed of deliberation. In the balcony scene and the aubade the lovers' scenes live on this difference, their intimacy performed by the verse's joinery: where the measures interlock, the two voices behave as one instrument, and where the measures fracture, under the Nurse's interruptions or the approach of morning, the world's wedge enters the scene. Directors take the tempo map from the edition on the rehearsal table, which is why an editor's decision at a joint in 1599, or its correction in a modern collation, reaches the stage as timing.

### Q: What did the First Folio inherit from the quartos in matters of lineation?

The First Folio inherited the quartos' lineation almost entire, because its text of the play descends from the second quarto's line through the later quartos, and its compositors set the divisions their copy showed them. The Folio therefore has no independent authority over the play's divisions: its agreements with the second quarto prove descent, not corroboration, and its disagreements are its own printing house's work. What the great volume added was momentum and machinery rather than judgement, a century of reprinting that froze the quarto dispositions into a default, and eventually the through-line numbering by which scholarship cites the text. The irony is permanent: the reference grid for the play's lines is drawn from the one early edition whose lineation is wholly derivative.

### Q: Why does the feast sonnet's clean transmission matter to the lineation debate?

The feast sonnet is the debate's control experiment. The same compositors, printing houses, and decade that produced the prose block in 1.4 and the fused lines elsewhere transmitted the lovers' fourteen-line exchange at 1.5 without a disturbed joint, because a sonnet's architecture announces its own divisions and rhyme double-locks every ending. The sonnet therefore disproves both extreme theories at once. Against the sceptic, it shows the transmission could carry lineation perfectly, so editorial repair is not guesswork over a general fog. Against the facsimile purist, its very perfection throws the failures elsewhere into relief as local accidents with local causes. Calibrating confidence by the sonnet, neither trusting every quarto joint nor doubting every editorial one, is the discipline the whole question requires.

### Q: What role do elisions play in making a quarto line scan as pentameter?

Elisions are the verse's compression system, and they decide whether a line counts as regular, short, or corrupt. Words like heaven, spirit, and the play's proper names expand and contract under metrical pressure; the quartos mark some contractions with apostrophes, as in "ta'en," and leave many more unmarked for the reader's ear to perform. An editor judging a suspicious line must first decide how its words were pronounced, since a line that overflows in spelling may fit the measure exactly in speech. Statistical profiles of the canon, notably Marina Tarlinskaja's, define the licences the verse grants itself, and a division that looks faulty until an elision is allowed, or sound until an impossible elision is required, changes sides accordingly.

### Q: What is the difference between a lineation error in the author's copy and one made in the printing house?

The difference is jurisdiction over the evidence. A lineation error in the author's foul papers, a line left crowded, doubled, or half-revised where revision had passed through the manuscript, is evidence about composition, and an editor who heals it erases a datum of the play's making. An error made in the printing house, a division lost to a failed casting off, a turned line mistaken for a break, a stint boundary's change of hands, is evidence about a book's manufacture, and preserving it as if it were rhythm falsifies the poem. The two errors can look identical on the page, which is why editors reason from signatures: foul-paper errors cluster where the argument of a passage was still in motion, printing errors where the page was full, the stint changed, or the measure ran out.

### Q: Can a compositor's lineation habits reveal which copy a quarto was printed from?

Yes, in patterns, though never from a single instance. Compositors have habits at the joins of lines as they do in spelling: preferences in turning lines, in crowding or sparing at page ends, in handling a speech that begins mid-measure. Where those habits shift at a point in a book, a change of workman or a change of copy may be diagnosed, and lineation anomalies corroborate the diagnoses that spelling evidence begins. More powerfully, lineation agreements in error between two books demonstrate shared descent, since the same unlikely division is not invented twice; the Folio's reproduction of the quarto tradition's joints is part of the proof that its text of this play is derivative. A lone odd division proves nothing, since workmen nod and memories slip, but a run of them is a fingerprint.

### Q: What does the lineation evidence finally prove about the verse readers inherit?

The lineation evidence proves that the verse readers inherit is genuine in its substance and collaborative in its shape. The pentameters are the author's; their disposition on the modern page descends mostly from the second quarto's faithful transmission, repaired at locatable joints by editors whose work is recorded and auditable, and at a small residue of famous moments settled by judgement where the documents run out. Nothing in the evidence supports either complacency, the belief that the printed line is a fact of nature, or despair, the belief that the verse is an editorial invention. It supports attention: a reader who knows where the joints are, and whose hands set them, reads the same words with a truer sense of what in them is bedrock and what is well-built scaffolding.
