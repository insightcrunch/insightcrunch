The 1991 economic reforms in India are usually narrated as an emergency answered at speed: a new government formed on 21 June 1991 found the treasury nearly empty, borrowed from the International Monetary Fund on hard conditions, devalued the rupee twice inside three days, and rewrote industrial policy inside five weeks. Every element of that narration is documented, and the narration still misleads. It explains why the reforms happened in July 1991. It cannot explain how a programme of that breadth, drafted to that level of detail, was executable by a minority government that had held office for a single month. Programmes of that breadth are not written in five weeks. They are written across a decade, in committee rooms and ministry files, and then they wait for a political moment permissive enough to carry them. The argument of this article is that the 1991 reform was the institutional consummation of a decade-long centralisation of economic decision-making that had been running inside the Indian state since the end of the 1970s, and that the balance of payments crisis of 1991 supplied the legitimation for the programme rather than its cause.

## The 1991 Economic Reforms as Crisis Response: The Standard Account and Its Evidence

The standard account begins with arithmetic, and the arithmetic is not in dispute. India's foreign exchange reserves fell through the first half of 1991 until, by June of that year, they stood at about $1 billion, a sum covering less than two weeks of imports for an economy that imported most of its crude oil. The fiscal deficit of the central government had reached 8.4 percent of GDP in 1990-91 on the figures the incoming Finance Minister himself presented to the Lok Sabha on 24 July 1991. Inflation measured by the wholesale price index was running in double digits. The Gulf War of August 1990 to February 1991 had raised the oil import bill and interrupted remittances from Indian workers in Kuwait and Iraq. Non-resident Indian deposits, a financing source the state had cultivated through the 1980s, were flowing out. Credit rating agencies had downgraded Indian paper, and commercial borrowing on tolerable terms had closed.

The political scaffolding that might have managed an earlier correction had also collapsed. V.P. Singh's National Front government fell in November 1990. Chandra Shekhar's minority government, kept in office by Congress support from outside, lost that support in March 1991 and resigned, leaving a vote-on-account in place of a budget and an election in place of an economic policy. In January 1991 the Chandra Shekhar government had drawn about $1.8 billion from the IMF under the first credit tranche and the compensatory financing facility, a transaction that bought weeks rather than room. Rajiv Gandhi was assassinated on 21 May 1991, in the middle of the general election, and the Congress that emerged from that election on 244 seats was a party in mourning, led by a man, P.V. Narasimha Rao, who had been preparing to retire from active politics months earlier.

The gold operations of May and July 1991 remain the most legible symbol of the emergency, and the standard account treats them as proof of desperation. In May 1991 the Chandra Shekhar government transferred 20 tonnes of confiscated gold to the State Bank of India, which sold it to the Union Bank of Switzerland under a repurchase arrangement that raised roughly $200 million. In July 1991 the Reserve Bank of India pledged 46.91 tonnes of its own gold stock to the Bank of England and the Bank of Japan, raising roughly $405 million more. Chartered aircraft carried the metal out of Mumbai at night. When the shipments became public knowledge, the political reaction crossed party lines: the government stood accused of mortgaging the national honour, and former Reserve Bank Governor S. Venkitaramanan later recalled Rajiv Gandhi's private defence of the logic, that gold which could not serve the national purpose in a difficulty served no purpose at all.

### Why did India pledge gold abroad in 1991?

India pledged gold because reserves covered less than two weeks of imports and lenders had shut their doors. In May the State Bank of India sold 20 tonnes of gold to the Union Bank of Switzerland under a repurchase pact, and in July the Reserve Bank pledged 46.91 tonnes to the Bank of England and the Bank of Japan.

Against this background, the standard account continues, the Rao government's actions of June and July 1991 read as improvisation under duress. The rupee was adjusted downward in two steps, on 1 July and 3 July 1991, by a cumulative 18 to 19 percent against the currencies of India's major trading partners. A trade policy package followed on 4 July 1991 from the Commerce Ministry of P. Chidambaram. On 24 July 1991 Manmohan Singh presented the 1991-92 budget to Parliament, and the same day the government laid the Statement on Industrial Policy before both Houses, ending industrial licensing for all but a short list of industries and opening 34 high-priority industries to automatic foreign investment approval up to 51 percent equity. In October 1991 the IMF approved a standby arrangement of about $2.3 billion, and in December 1991 the World Bank extended a structural adjustment loan of $500 million. On the standard reading, the sequence is a chain of forced moves, each link forged by the creditor's condition and the calendar of maturing debt.

This reading has serious scholarly custody. It is the reading of the participants' first drafts, including Manmohan Singh's own budget-day framing of an economy in crisis requiring correction, and it organises the dominant reference treatments, which file 1991 under crisis and response. It also has the virtue of matching the visible chronology. Governments that possess a settled programme and freedom of timing do not usually pledge the central bank's gold in the same month they announce their economic philosophy. The crisis-response reading deserves to be taken at its strongest before it is displaced, and its strongest form is this: whatever papers existed in ministry files, nothing would have moved without the gun of default at the government's head, so the crisis is the cause and the papers are footnotes.

## Speed as Evidence: The Problem the Standard Account Cannot Solve

The difficulty with the standard account is a single stubborn fact, and the fact is speed. Between Rao's swearing-in on 21 June 1991 and the Industrial Policy Statement of 24 July 1991, thirty-three days passed. In those thirty-three days the government prepared and executed a two-stage devaluation, which required modelled estimates of trade elasticities and a decision on the basket; a trade policy package that replaced a discretionary import-licensing regime with EXIM scrips and began decanalisation; a full Union budget carrying a fiscal correction of nearly two percentage points of GDP, a peak customs duty reduction from above 300 percent to 150 percent, and a fertiliser price increase of 40 percent; and an industrial policy statement that redrew the boundary between the state and private capital more completely than any document since 1956. Each of these instruments is a drafting exercise measured in months inside any functioning bureaucracy. Executed together, in sequence, without a single instrument contradicting another, they describe a programme that existed before the government did.

The personnel record makes the same point in a different register. The men who executed the programme were not assembled in June 1991. Manmohan Singh had been Governor of the Reserve Bank from 1982 to 1985 and Deputy Chairman of the Planning Commission from 1985 to 1987. Montek Singh Ahluwalia had served in the Prime Minister's Office as an economic adviser and special secretary, then as Commerce Secretary, and had put the entire reform sequence on paper in May 1990 in the memorandum that the economist Ashok Desai later nicknamed the M document. C. Rangarajan had been Deputy Governor of the Reserve Bank through the decade and would become its Governor in December 1992. The execution team of 1991 was the drafting team of the 1980s, promoted. A crisis can explain why a government reaches for such men. It cannot explain why such men were already there, holding a decade of convergent drafts.

There is a third strand of evidence, quieter than the other two, and it concerns what happened after the emergency passed. A programme improvised under a creditor's deadline should be expected to decay once the deadline recedes, the way wartime controls decayed after 1945. The 1991 programme did the opposite. The Securities and Exchange Board of India received statutory authority in 1992. The Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System arrived in March 1992, the unified market-determined exchange rate on 1 March 1993, and current account convertibility through acceptance of the IMF's Article VIII obligations in August 1994. The National Telecom Policy followed in 1994, the Marrakesh agreement closing the Uruguay Round was signed in April 1994, and India entered the World Trade Organization as a founding member on 1 January 1995. The insurance opening began with the Malhotra Committee of 1993 and reached statute in the IRDA Act of 1999. Successor governments of opposed ideology, the United Front, the Bharatiya Janata Party led National Democratic Alliance and the Congress led United Progressive Alliance, governed inside this architecture and extended it. Improvisations do not behave this way. Consummations of a prepared design do.

The rest of this article assembles the preparation decade in order: the IMF Extended Fund Facility negotiated at its opening, the fiscal and industrial papers of the middle 1980s, the personnel deployments that concentrated drafting authority, the committee layer that codified the external-sector blueprint, the M document that wrote the sequence down in full, and the political decisions of Rao that made execution survivable. The crisis-response reading is then weighed against the consummation reading on the same record, with the strongest version of each stated before judgment. The claim defended throughout is precise: the BoP crisis determined the timing of the 1991 reform and supplied its political cover, and the substance of the reform had been determined earlier, by a decade of centralised preparation inside the Finance Ministry and its satellite institutions. Timing and substance are different variables, and the standard account's error is to let the first swallow the second.

## The First Draft: The 1980 IMF Extended Fund Facility and the Grammar of Conditionality

The preparation decade opens with a transaction that the standard account of 1991 rarely connects to it, because the transaction belongs to a different government's biography. In 1979 and 1980 the second oil shock doubled India's petroleum import bill, and the Janata experiment's successor, Indira Gandhi's returned Congress government of January 1980, confronted a payments position that domestic instruments could not repair. India went to the IMF for the largest arrangement the Fund had then approved for any member. Negotiations ran through 1980 and 1981, and in November 1981 the Fund approved an Extended Fund Facility of SDR 5 billion over three years. The loan's size matters less than its structure. An EFF was not balance-of-payments first aid. It was a medium-term programme in which the borrowing government committed to a schedule of structural measures, on trade, on subsidies, on public enterprise pricing and on the exchange rate, in exchange for disbursement in tranches.

The EFF taught the Indian state a grammar it had not previously spoken fluently: the grammar of the programme document, in which a government states its intentions as quantified commitments with dates, and a creditor verifies performance against them. Finance Ministry officials drafted the letters of intent. The Reserve Bank built the monitoring tables. When India terminated the arrangement early in 1984, having drawn about SDR 3.9 billion and declined the final tranches from a position of improved reserves, the official moral drawn in New Delhi was that India had used the Fund without surrendering to it. The institutional moral, visible only in retrospect, was different. A cadre of officials had written, negotiated and operated a structural adjustment programme from the inside. When the same grammar reappeared in the standby arrangement of October 1991, the Indian side of the table was staffed by men who had drafted its vocabulary a decade earlier.

Atul Kohli's political-economy framework in Poverty Amid Plenty in the New India (2012) supplies the wider setting for this opening move. Kohli reads the Indian state after 1980 as a state that had settled its governing alliance in favour of organised business and had begun, under Indira Gandhi's second tenure, to reorient from a rhetoric of redistribution toward a pro-business growth strategy pursued within the old control apparatus. The EFF fits that reading precisely. It was not liberalisation. It was a state that still licensed, canalised and administered prices deciding to manage its external account with creditor-grade documentation, and centralising that management in the Finance Ministry and the Reserve Bank as it did so. The controls stayed. The locus of decision moved upward and inward, toward the small set of institutions that spoke to Washington. That movement, repeated across the decade, is the centralisation this article tracks: not yet a change in what the Indian state did to the economy, but a change in where and by whom economic decisions of consequence were drafted.

The same years furnish a smaller, concrete exhibit. The 1980s saw the Finance Ministry normalise the medium-term fiscal document as an instrument of government. Annual budgets remained legal events, but the ministry increasingly framed them inside multi-year statements of intent on deficits, tax structure and subsidy discipline, first for the IMF's surveillance and then for domestic use. The habit sounds clerical. Its consequence was strategic. By the end of the decade the ministry possessed both the models and the drafting routines to produce, on demand, a quantified correction programme of exactly the kind Manmohan Singh placed before Parliament on 24 July 1991. The budget of that day was presented as a break. Its paperwork was a routine the ministry had rehearsed for ten years.

## Drafting the Architecture: Fiscal and Industrial Papers of the Middle 1980s

If the EFF opened the decade's education, the middle 1980s supplied its curriculum. Rajiv Gandhi's government, elected in December 1984 with the largest majority in the republic's history, held the political capital for structural change and spent part of it on economic drafting. In December 1985 the government placed before Parliament a Long-Term Fiscal Policy statement, an act without precedent in Indian budgetary practice. A finance ministry that binds itself publicly to a multi-year path for tax rates, deficit reduction and subsidy restraint is a finance ministry rehearsing programme government. The statement's principal authorial figure, on the planning side, was the Deputy Chairman of the Planning Commission, Manmohan Singh, who held that office from 1985 to 1987 and whose committee work on the fiscal structure ran in parallel with the statement's preparation.

### What did the Long-Term Fiscal Policy statement of December 1985 propose?

Laid before Parliament in December 1985, the statement committed the government to a stable, moderate structure of direct taxes over five years, a phased reduction of the fiscal deficit, customs duties restructured toward fewer and lower rates, and restraint on open-ended subsidies. Its novelty was institutional: a finance ministry binding itself in advance to a published multi-year path.

The industrial side of the architecture moved in the same years, by increments that contemporaries registered as relaxations rather than as a programme. The Industrial Policy statements and licensing notifications of 1985 and 1986 delicensed a widening set of industries, raised the asset ceiling below which firms escaped the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices apparatus, and introduced broad-banding, which let manufacturers vary their product mix inside a licensed category without fresh permission. The Abid Hussain Committee on Trade Policies had reported in 1984 with recommendations for replacing quantitative import restrictions with tariffs and for simplifying the licensing of imports, and the decade's trade administration absorbed its logic in stages: the import-export policy of 1985 to 1988 shifted categories of imports to open general licence, and the EXIM policy documents began to read as liberalisation schedules rather than as prohibition lists. None of this dismantled the control regime. All of it drafted the component parts of dismantling, and tested each part at small scale inside the existing law.

The political meaning of these increments deserves one precise distinction. The Avadi inheritance, examined in the founding-era record of [the Avadi rhetoric-practice gap](/2019/02/05/avadi-resolution-socialist-pattern-1955/), was a pattern in which the Congress announced transformative economic intentions and administered continuity. The middle 1980s inverted the pattern. The Rajiv Gandhi government announced modest intentions, modernisation, efficiency, a loosening here and a committee there, and administered the drafting of a transformation it did not announce. Rhetoric and practice traded places. When the transformation finally was announced, in July 1991, it arrived with the peculiar authority of a programme its authors had been operating in miniature for years.

The fiscal side also carried a warning that the decade's drafting could not draft away. The same years that produced the Long-Term Fiscal Policy statement produced the deficits that made 1991 necessary: the combined fiscal deficit of centre and states climbed toward 10 percent of GDP by the end of the decade, financed increasingly by internal debt and by non-resident deposits attracted at premium rates. The preparation thesis does not require the 1980s state to have been prudent. It requires it to have been literate: a state that had learned, in its finance ministry and its central bank, exactly which levers a correction programme would have to pull, in which order, with which estimated effects, because it had spent ten years pulling each lever a short distance and measuring the result. The crisis of 1991 was in part the bill for the decade's borrowing. The reform of 1991 was in part the yield of the decade's drafting. Both inheritances arrived together, and an account that sees only the bill will misread the yield.

## The Personnel of Preparation: Singh, Ahluwalia and Rangarajan Across a Decade

Institutions prepare through persons, and the preparation decade has an unusually legible personnel record. Begin with the Finance Minister of 1991. Manmohan Singh took charge of the Reserve Bank of India as Governor on 16 September 1982, in the middle of the EFF's operation, and held the office until January 1985; the central bank's side of the programme, its external debt tables, its import-cover arithmetic, passed across his desk in those years. From 1985 to 1987 he served as Deputy Chairman of the Planning Commission under Rajiv Gandhi, the office from which the fiscal and industrial papers of the middle decade were coordinated. From 1987 to 1990 he served in Geneva as Secretary-General of the South Commission, an appointment that reads in his biography as an interlude and in this article's argument as a vantage point: the Commission's work on developing-country adjustment placed him inside the international argument about sequencing at the moment the argument was being settled. He returned to India in 1990, served as an adviser to Prime Minister Chandra Shekhar, and was briefly Chairman of the University Grants Commission in the spring of 1991. No Indian finance minister before or since has arrived at the budget carrying that particular sequence of postings, and the sequence, not merely the man, is the evidence: every institution that would execute the 1991 programme had already been one of his offices.

Montek Singh Ahluwalia's record runs in counterpoint. A professional economist recruited into the Prime Minister's Office under Rajiv Gandhi, he served as special secretary to the Prime Minister, moved to the Commerce Ministry as Secretary in 1990, and after 1991 passed into the Finance Ministry, where he served as Finance Secretary in the first reform years. His postings trace the programme's own geography: trade in 1990, finance after 1991, and the Prime Minister's Office before both. The Prime Minister's Office posting matters most for the argument. The PMO of the late 1980s was becoming the clearing house in which Finance Ministry drafts, Planning Commission papers and Reserve Bank positions were reconciled before they reached a cabinet that rarely debated economic structure. Centralisation, in this article's usage, names exactly that migration of the draft: from the line ministries and the cabinet table upward into a secretariat triangle of Finance Ministry, Reserve Bank and Prime Minister's Office, staffed by a circulating cadre who carried the drafts with them from post to post.

C. Rangarajan completes the triangle's third side. Deputy Governor of the Reserve Bank from 1982, through the whole preparation decade, he chaired the committee work that codified the central bank's doctrine on the external account, and his High Level Committee on Balance of Payments, reporting in 1993, supplied the reform government's external-sector blueprint: a market-determined exchange rate reached by stages, current account liberalisation carried to convertibility, restraint on short-term and volatile external borrowing, and encouragement of non-debt-creating flows such as foreign direct investment and portfolio investment. The Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System of March 1992 and the unified rate of March 1993 followed that committee's transition path, and the acceptance of current account convertibility in August 1994 closed it. Rangarajan then served as Governor of the Reserve Bank from December 1992 to 1997, administering the regime his committee had drafted. A brief that dates his payments committee to 1988 compresses this record; the documentary sequence runs from his deputy governorship through the 1993 report, and the compression is noted here because the article's claim depends on the sequence being read accurately: preparation and execution in the external sector were continuous, and they ran through one man's committee room and then his governorship.

Devesh Kapur and Pratap Bhanu Mehta, in their editorial framing of Public Institutions in India (2005), direct attention to exactly this stratum: the performance of the Indian state, they argue, is set less by its constitutional design than by the accumulated capacity of its standing institutions and the personnel who rotate through them. The preparation decade is a case cut to their measure. Nothing in the Constitution changed between 1981 and 1991 to make reform possible. What changed was capacity, concentrated: a Finance Ministry that had learned programme drafting from the EFF, a central bank that had codified its external doctrine, a Planning Commission that had written the fiscal architecture, and a Prime Minister's Office that had learned to reconcile the three. When Rao's government needed a programme in June 1991, it did not commission one. It inherited the capacity that produced programmes, with the drafts included.

One further deployment belongs in this section because it belongs to Rao himself, and it corrects a lazy version of the story in which the Prime Minister merely signed what his officials wrote. Rao had held, across the 1980s, the portfolios of External Affairs, Home Affairs, Defence, and Human Resource Development, and had chaired the Congress's internal deliberations on economic policy; he was among the small set of senior Congress figures who had read the decade's drafts as they circulated. When he formed his government he kept the Industries portfolio in his own hands. The decision reads as administrative tidiness only until the Industrial Policy Statement of 24 July 1991 is placed beside it: the minister who presented the most consequential industrial document since 1956 to the political system was the Prime Minister, carrying it personally because its passage through the party required his weight and no one else's deniability. Personnel, again, was programme.

## The M Document: The Sequence Written Down in Full, May 1990

Every long preparation has a moment when it becomes a text, and for the 1991 reform that moment is dated May 1990. V.P. Singh, returning from a visit to Malaysia struck by the distance East Asia had travelled, asked his economic adviser Montek Singh Ahluwalia to set down what India would have to change to travel it. Ahluwalia produced a paper titled Towards a Restructuring of Industrial, Trade and Fiscal Policies. It proposed, in one connected sequence, the abolition of industrial licensing outside a narrow security list, the replacement of import licensing and canalisation with a tariff regime whose rates were to fall in announced stages, a market-oriented exchange rate policy, fiscal correction anchored on subsidy discipline and tax reform, and a restructured regime for foreign investment. Jairam Ramesh, in To the Brink and Back: India's 1991 Story (2015), delivers the verdict that subsequent commentary has not improved: if there is one single document that contains the economic reform programme of the Rao government and of the governments that followed, it is this paper. The economist Ashok Desai, chief consultant in the Finance Ministry from December 1991, gave the paper its enduring nickname, the M document, for its author's initial.

The paper's first career was a scandal, and the scandal is itself evidence for the consummation reading. In July 1990 the M document leaked and was published in the Financial Express. The V.P. Singh cabinet reacted with a fury that the standard account finds puzzling: the Commerce and Finance Ministries disowned the agenda, the Planning Commission was hostile, and Chandra Shekhar, then a dissident within the Janata Dal, published attacks on the paper as a foreign dictation smuggled into Indian policymaking. Consider what the fury establishes. By July 1990, a full year before the crisis peaked, the complete reform sequence existed on paper inside the Government of India, was known to the cabinet, and was politically indigestible. The objection recorded against it was never that it was unready, unmodelled or incoherent. The objection was that it could not be carried. Preparation had outrun permission, and the paper waited, as its authors waited, for a configuration in which carrying it became possible. Crisis supplied that configuration in 1991. It did not supply the paper.

The M document also disciplines the argument against its most tempting exaggeration. The consummation reading does not claim that 1991 was scripted in detail in 1990, still less in 1981. The M document's sequence was a direction of travel with named instruments, not a draft of the July 1991 texts; the Industrial Policy Statement and the budget were written in 1991, by the men of 1991, under the pressure of 1991. What the preparation decade supplied was everything that makes rapid drafting possible and rapid execution survivable: the analysis that selected the instruments, the estimates that priced them, the legislative and administrative routines that carried them, the external doctrine that sequenced the exchange rate, and a cadre that had already argued the whole programme out internally, against the Planning Commission's hostility and the ministries' disavowals, before a rupee of reserves ran out. The scholarly frame offered by Kapur and Mehta names this correctly as institutional capacity. The crisis-response frame has no word for it, and so treats the most striking property of July 1991, its speed, as an effect of desperation. Desperation explains haste. It does not explain accuracy, and the July instruments were accurate to their models to a degree that haste alone has never produced in any finance ministry.

The record of the founding era supplies the counterpoint that this series has tracked from its first article. The Constitution's makers left economic structure deliberately underdetermined, split between justiciable rights and the directive principles, [the directive-rights ambiguity](/2018/12/23/directive-principles-vs-fundamental-rights/) that let successive governments claim constitutional warrant for control and for liberalisation alike. The control regime built after 1956, whose political logic runs through [the 1969 state-led developmental architecture](/2019/04/01/bank-nationalisation-1969-politics/), was an administrative construction resting on ordinary legislation and executive licensing, not a constitutional settlement. That is why a decade of ministry drafting could prepare its replacement without a constituent moment, and why July 1991 needed no amendment to begin. The preparation was possible because the battlefield was administrative. The M document knew its battlefield, and named it: industrial, trade and fiscal policy, the three fields a government can move by statement, budget and notification while Parliament is still finding its seats.

## June 1991: A Minority Government, a Retired Leader and a Finance Minister Chosen as Armour

The political half of the consummation was assembled in five weeks of 1991, and its assembly is unintelligible outside the post-1989 landscape that [the coalition-era renegotiation framework](/2019/05/15/coalition-era-1989-2014-explained/) describes: single-party majority government had ended in 1989, and every government since had been a negotiation. [The post-1989 institutional context](/2019/05/20/vp-singh-1989-verdict-india/) had already consumed two governments in nineteen months when the 1991 election was called. Rajiv Gandhi's assassination on 21 May 1991, between the first and second rounds of polling, removed the Congress's campaign centre and its presumptive prime minister in a single night. The party that won 244 seats in the completed election was led at the parliamentary party meeting of 20 June 1991 to a compromise figure. P.V. Narasimha Rao, sixty-nine years old, a former chief minister of Andhra Pradesh and holder of most senior cabinet portfolios across two decades, who had not contested the election on health grounds and had spoken of withdrawing to private writing, was elected leader and sworn in as Prime Minister on 21 June 1991.

### How many seats did Congress hold when Rao formed his government in June 1991?

Congress won 244 seats in the 1991 general election, the largest bloc in the Lok Sabha and short of the majority mark of 272. Rao therefore formed a minority government on 21 June 1991, dependent on outside support and abstentions, and carried it toward a working majority through by-elections across the following two years.

The composition of the government announced Rao's method before its policies announced anything. The Finance Ministry went to Manmohan Singh, who held no party office, had never contested an election, and could be presented to every audience, to the IMF, to the Congress Parliamentary Party, to the opposition benches, as a technician executing necessities rather than a politician executing a programme. The insulation was deliberate and it was double. It protected the reforms from the Congress's internal factions, who could attack a budget's incidence but found it harder to attack a professor's arithmetic as factional betrayal. It also protected the Prime Minister, who retained deniability for each instrument while retaining, through his own retention of the Industries portfolio and his chairmanship of the cabinet's economic deliberations, complete control of the programme's pace. Rao's admirers, and his biographers among them, describe a premier who governed the reform the way he governed the party: by deciding privately, announcing minimally, and letting the consequences assemble their own majority after the fact. The description is contested, and the contest matters for the adjudication below. What is not contested is the documentary fact that the method worked on the calendar: a minority government introduced, in its first five weeks, measures that majority governments of the 1980s, including one with 404 seats, had drafted and declined to carry.

The Congress's own ideological inheritance was the terrain on which this method operated, and Rao managed it as an editor manages a text. The party of the Avadi resolution and of bank nationalisation could not be told in June 1991 that its economic creed had been a forty-year error; the founding generation's compromise, in which [the rights-development founding tension](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/) had been left permanently open, allowed the reform to be framed instead as development's next instrument rather than its repudiation. Rao's speeches of the period perform exactly that framing. The public sector was to be made efficient, not denounced. Liberalisation was presented as the removal of controls that had become ends in themselves, in language a delegate of 1955 could parse as administrative reform. The silence in this article's title is that editorial silence: the reforms were carried by a government that declined, as a matter of strategy, to narrate them as a revolution, because a revolution would have required the party's permission, and a correction required only its acquiescence.

The parliamentary arithmetic then had to be survived month by month, and the survival record belongs in any honest statement of the consummation thesis, because preparation inside ministries would have counted for nothing if the government had fallen in its first winter. The government passed its first budget in the monsoon session of 1991 against opposition criticism that stopped short of a deciding division. Through 1992 and 1993 it absorbed the political costs of the securities scandal, the demolition at Ayodhya in December 1992 and the austerity grievances of its own backbenches, and on 28 July 1993 it survived a no-confidence motion by a margin narrow enough to generate a decade of litigation and the Jharkhand Mukti Morcha bribery case. Jairam Ramesh's account of these years, and the memoir record assembled since, converge on one institutional observation: Rao treated each confidence test as a drafting problem, identifying in advance the members and parties whose abstention or support the arithmetic required, and pricing the concessions accordingly. The reforms were never put to the house as a single question. They travelled inside budgets, ordinances and notifications, each carrying its own small majority, a legislative technique that is the parliamentary form of the decade's centralisation: decide in the secretariat, ratify in fragments.

Organised business, the programme's supposed beneficiary, supplied a coda that the preparation thesis predicts and the crisis thesis cannot order. The Confederation of Indian Industry, which had been arguing through the late 1980s for delicensing and trade reform in its own committee papers, welcomed the Industrial Policy Statement as the execution of an agenda Indian industry had helped to draft. The houses that had grown largest inside protection answered differently: by 1993 a group of leading industrialists, remembered as the Bombay Club, was petitioning the government for a level playing field against the foreign entrants the new regime admitted. The split is the signature of a consummation rather than an imposition. A dictated programme surprises its beneficiaries as much as its victims; a prepared one divides them in advance, along lines drawn during the preparation, between the firms that had drafted the future and the firms that had merely inherited the past.

## July 1991: Devaluation, Trade Package, Budget and Industrial Policy in Thirty-Three Days

The execution sequence can be read off the calendar, and the calendar is the consummation thesis in miniature. On 1 July 1991 the Reserve Bank adjusted the rupee downward by about 9 percent against the basket of major currencies. On 3 July it adjusted the rate again, by about 11 percent. On 4 July the Commerce Minister, P. Chidambaram, announced a trade policy package that introduced EXIM scrips, tradable import entitlements earned against exports, simplified advance licensing and began the decanalisation of imports. On 24 July the budget and the Industrial Policy Statement arrived together. Four instruments, four different drafting traditions, one direction, no visible seams. A government improvising under a creditor's gun produces seams: the trade ministry learns of the devaluation from the newspapers, the budget contradicts the industrial statement, the second adjustment surprises the first. The July record shows coordination of a kind that has one plausible source, prior joint drafting by the secretariat triangle whose construction the previous sections traced.

### What did the two-step devaluation of 1 and 3 July 1991 do to the rupee?

The Reserve Bank lowered the rupee in two steps, about 9 percent on 1 July 1991 and about 11 percent on 3 July, a cumulative adjustment of 18 to 19 percent against the currencies of India's main trading partners. The two-step design tested market reaction between moves and denied speculators a single announced target.

The budget of 24 July 1991 rewards close reading as a prepared text. Manmohan Singh set a fiscal deficit target of 6.5 percent of GDP against the 8.4 percent realised in 1990-91, cut the peak rate of customs duty from above 300 percent to 150 percent, raised fertiliser prices by 40 percent, trimmed the list of items reserved for the public sector's investment planning, and framed the whole in the speech's closing quotation from Victor Hugo, that no power on earth can stop an idea whose time has come. The fertiliser increase then supplied the government's first lesson in the politics of its own programme: protests inside Parliament and the party forced a partial softening within weeks, exempting small and marginal farmers from part of the increase. Rao let the concession happen, visibly, at the margin of the programme rather than at its centre. The episode is a small controlled experiment in the article's thesis. The parts of July 1991 that had been drafted for a decade, the duty structure, the deficit path, the licensing abolition, were defended without retreat. The part that had been priced by arithmetic alone, the fertiliser incidence, was renegotiated in public. Preparation and politics divided the programme between them, along exactly the seam the consummation reading predicts.

### Which industries still needed a licence after the 1991 Industrial Policy Statement?

The Statement of 24 July 1991 abolished licensing for all industries except eighteen retained on grounds of security, strategic concern and environmental protection, covering defence production, atomic energy, coal and lignite, mineral oils, railway transport and specified hazardous chemicals among others. Every other industry could be established and expanded without a licence from the Government of India.

The Industrial Policy Statement itself was the decade's drafts made law-like in a single afternoon. Licensing abolished outside the eighteen. The list of industries reserved for the public sector cut from seventeen to eight. The asset threshold that had dragged growing firms into the monopolies apparatus abolished outright. Foreign direct investment approved automatically up to 51 percent equity in thirty-four high-priority industries, a provision that inverted the FERA-era presumption that foreign equity was a concession to be bargained down toward 40 percent. Phased manufacturing programmes, the indigenisation schedules that had governed the automobile and electronics industries, discontinued. Each clause has a genealogy in the papers of the 1980s: the delicensing notifications prefigured the licensing clause, the Abid Hussain Committee's tariff logic prefigured the trade clauses, the Long-Term Fiscal Policy statement prefigured the budget's duty path, and the M document had assembled all of it into one sequence fourteen months earlier. To read the Statement's clauses against the decade's file is to watch a bibliography become a statute book.

## Continuation, Not Improvisation: The Architecture Extended, 1992 to 1999

The post-1991 record supplies the thesis with its control test. If July 1991 had been an improvisation extorted by creditors, the years after the standby arrangement expired should show drift, partial reversal and the quiet reassembly of the control regime by the ministries that had always staffed it, the pattern Indian economic administration had shown after every earlier liberalising episode, including the delicensing wave of the middle 1960s and the EFF years themselves. The record shows the reverse. The Rao government spent its remaining four years extending the architecture along the lines the preparation documents had marked out, and it did so at a pace set by politics rather than by any creditor's calendar.

The capital market came first. The Securities and Exchange Board of India, created administratively in 1988, received statutory authority through the SEBI Act of 1992, and the office of the Controller of Capital Issues, the pricing arm of the old regime, was abolished in the same reform season: the state exited the business of fixing share prices and entered the business of policing disclosure, a jurisdictional migration drafted in the Finance Ministry's committee rooms. Foreign institutional investors were admitted to Indian equity markets in 1992, converting the external account from a debt ledger into a portfolio ledger on the lines the Rangarajan doctrine had prescribed, non-debt-creating flows preferred to borrowing.

The exchange rate sequence followed the committee path with documentary fidelity. The Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System of March 1992 interposed a dual rate, 40 percent of export proceeds surrendered at the official rate and 60 percent traded at the market rate, as a transitional mechanism rather than as a compromise improvised in a panic. On 1 March 1993 the rates were unified into a single market-determined rate. In August 1994 India accepted the Article VIII obligations of the IMF, making the rupee convertible for current account transactions and surrendering, deliberately and permanently, the administrative allocation of foreign exchange for trade. Each step was announced in advance, sequenced in the committee literature, and executed by the same triangle of Finance Ministry, Reserve Bank and Prime Minister's Office. Telecom followed in the National Telecom Policy of 1994, which opened basic services to private entry in principle and restructured the department's commercial functions. The external trade constitution was completed at Marrakesh in April 1994, where India signed the agreement establishing the World Trade Organization, accepting the TRIPS disciplines on intellectual property that the preparation decade's trade drafters had priced into their sequencing since the Dunkel Draft controversies of 1991. Insurance, the slowest file, moved from the Malhotra Committee's appointment in 1993 to the IRDA Act of 1999, which ended the statutory monopolies of the Life Insurance Corporation and the general insurers and created a regulator on the SEBI template.

Political scientists who resist the consummation reading sometimes concede the post-1991 continuity and reassign its cause, arguing that continuity after 1991 proves only that the new regime had created its own constituencies, exporters, portfolio investors, a consuming middle class, whose lobbying then locked the reforms in. The constituency argument is strong and the adjudication below takes it seriously. It does not, however, touch the years 1992 to 1994, when the constituencies were still embryonic and the extensions proceeded anyway, on schedule, from documents. Nor does it explain the direction of the extensions: constituencies defend gains, they do not draft unified exchange rates. The drafting hand visible in the 1992-94 instruments is the preparation decade's hand, still attached to the same men: Ahluwalia in the Finance Ministry, Rangarajan at the Reserve Bank from December 1992, Manmohan Singh at the budget lectern until 1996.

The successor governments complete the control test from the other side. The United Front governments of 1996 to 1998, dependent on Left support in Parliament, presented the 1997 budget of P. Chidambaram that cut direct tax rates to levels the 1991 drafters had treated as a destination, and pushed the insurance bill into Parliament. The National Democratic Alliance government of Atal Bihari Vajpayee, elected in 1998 and again in 1999 on a platform that included swadeshi rhetoric from its own movement's fringes, created a disinvestment ministry, carried strategic sales of public enterprises further than Rao had attempted, passed the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act in 2003 and continued the tariff descent toward East Asian levels. The United Progressive Alliance governments after 2004, led by Manmohan Singh as Prime Minister with the Left in supporting coalition, extended the architecture into a national goods and services tax design and an Aadhaar-linked delivery state while raising welfare commitments the 1991 framework had to learn to finance. Three ideological families governed across these decades. None restored licensing, none reimposed exchange allocation, none repriced capital issues. A creditor's deadline cannot bind a successor government that never signed the letter of intent. A consummated institutional settlement can, because the settlement lives in the ministries, the statutes and the cadre, and outlasts the ministry that announced it.

## Reform by Stealth: The Politics of Never Announcing a Revolution

The silence of the silent reforms was a technique, and it has a scholarly name. Rob Jenkins, in Democratic Politics and Economic Reform in India (1999), described the Rao government's method as reform by stealth: a strategy of advancing liberalisation through administrative discretion, incremental notifications and the deliberate avoidance of ideological proclamation, so that no single decision ever presented the opposition, or the ruling party's own factions, with a target large enough to unite against. Jenkins wrote as an analyst of democratic strategy rather than as an economist, and his account supplies the political mechanism that the preparation thesis requires. Preparation inside ministries is a necessary condition for July 1991. It is not sufficient. A prepared programme still has to pass through a Parliament in which its authors hold 244 seats and through a party whose founding myths it contradicts. Stealth was the transmission belt between the file and the statute book.

The technique had recognisable moves, each visible in the 1991-93 record. Measures were fragmented across instruments, so that licensing abolition travelled in a policy statement, duty cuts in a budget schedule, exchange reform in Reserve Bank circulars, and no vote ever asked the Lok Sabha to approve liberalisation as such. Measures were sequenced from the least to the most visible, trade and licensing before prices, prices before public sector restructuring. Measures were announced as technical corrections to inherited distortions rather than as choices among futures, a framing Manmohan Singh's budget speeches sustain with discipline: the 1991 speech speaks of crisis, correction and compulsion, and reserves its one flourish, the Hugo quotation, for the idea of change in general rather than for any doctrine in particular. And the government's public defence was assigned to a Finance Minister whose biography was itself an argument that expertise, not ideology, was speaking.

The stealth reading and the consummation reading are allies rather than rivals, and stating their relation precisely strengthens both. Jenkins explains how the programme was carried; the preparation thesis explains what there was to carry. A government cannot practise stealth with a programme it has not finished drafting, because stealth depends on the executive's ability to act by notification at the moment of its choosing, which in turn depends on the notifications existing. The decade of centralised drafting and the five weeks of quiet execution are one phenomenon viewed from two floors of the same building: upstairs, a secretariat that had concentrated the drafting; downstairs, a prime minister who had learned, across forty years in the Congress organisation, that the party ratified accomplished facts more reliably than announced intentions.

The opposition of 1991 tested the technique and, in testing it, revealed its limits. The Bharatiya Janata Party, holding 120 seats and rising, attacked the programme from a swadeshi position its economic resolutions of the period articulated as self-reliance against foreign capital, and simultaneously attacked the government for timidity in dismantling controls its own business constituency disliked. The Left parties, the Communist Party of India (Marxist) with 35 seats and the Communist Party of India, attacked the programme as an IMF dictation that mortgaged sovereignty, organised trade union resistance to public sector restructuring, and defended the licensing state as the poor's protection against capital. The Janata Dal's remnants attacked Rao personally and the reforms as a betrayal of the social justice agenda of 1989-90. Each critique had a constituency and a literature. What the three critiques never acquired was a common object, because stealth had dissolved the programme into a hundred administrative acts, each with its own small print and its own expiry of news value. The opposition could win arguments. It could not find the single division in which to win the war, and on the one occasion the war was formally joined, the no-confidence motion of 28 July 1993, the government survived by the arithmetic Rao's method had been assembling for two years.

## Drafted in Delhi: The Creditor's Share in the Programme

The adjudication this article owes its readers turns on a measurable question: how much of the July 1991 programme did the creditors write? The crisis-response reading implies a large share. The documentary record supports a small one, and the discrepancy is testable because letters of intent and programme documents survive on both sides.

The Fund's own paper trail begins before Rao. The January 1991 drawings under Chandra Shekhar, about $1.8 billion across the first credit tranche and the compensatory facility for the Gulf shock, carried the light conditionality of those windows: consultations, not structural benchmarks. The standby arrangement approved on 31 October 1991, after the reforms, committed India to fiscal and monetary targets and to a structural programme whose content, trade liberalisation, industrial deregulation, financial sector reform and public enterprise restructuring, reproduces the M document's table of contents in the Fund's vocabulary. The sequence of drafting matters. The Industrial Policy Statement predates the standby by three months. The devaluation predates it by four. A condition that is met before it is imposed is not a condition in any sense that supports causation; it is a ratification. Indian officials of the period, in memoirs and interviews on the record since, describe the negotiation accordingly: the Fund staff arrived with a template, the Indian side arrived with a programme, and the letter of intent that emerged was negotiated at the boundary where the two overlapped, with the Indian programme supplying the structural content and the Fund supplying the macroeconomic targets and the monitoring calendar. Montek Singh Ahluwalia's memoir Backstage (2020), written from the drafting chair, presents the 1991 programme documents as Indian texts that the Fund endorsed, and even commentators hostile to the reforms have rarely disputed that description of the drafting; their dispute is with the programme's wisdom and its distributional incidence, not with its authorship.

Two exhibits fix the proportions. The first is the two-step devaluation itself. Fund templates of the era preferred a single, decisive exchange adjustment announced with the programme. The Reserve Bank chose two steps, forty-eight hours apart, sized at about 9 and about 11 percent, a design aimed at Indian speculative conditions and at the political management of the headline, and the Fund accepted the Indian design. The second exhibit is negative space: the programme contains no commitment the preparation documents had rejected. The M document and the decade's committee literature had kept capital account opening at the far end of the sequence, behind current account convertibility and banking reform, and the 1991 letters of intent, for all the Fund's institutional preference for faster capital liberalisation, kept it there too. India did not open the capital account in 1991, or on the Fund's later urging through the decade, and the Asian financial crisis of 1997 closed the argument in Delhi's favour in the eyes of most of the drafting cadre. A dictated programme does not exhibit refusals of this size at its centre. A negotiated programme, whose structural text predates the negotiation, does.

What the creditors genuinely supplied should be stated with equal clarity, because the consummation thesis does not need to minimise it. The Fund and the World Bank supplied money at the moment of maximum need, the standby arrangement of October 1991 and the structural adjustment loan of December 1991 together with the January drawings amounting to several billion dollars of breathing room. They supplied a monitoring calendar that Rao's government used, with full knowledge of its utility, as an external commitment device against its own backbenches: a finance minister who can plead a dated obligation to Washington negotiates differently with a parliamentary party than one who pleads only his own judgment. And they supplied blame absorption. The phrase dictation from the IMF, repeated by critics across the decade, was politically functional for the government it attacked, because it let the reforms be simultaneously implemented and disowned. The creditors were the programme's bankers, its calendar and its lightning rod. The evidence does not support them as its authors.

## The Adjudication: Timing Forced, Substance Prepared

The disagreement named at the head of this article can be decided on the record assembled. The crisis-response reading, in its strongest form, holds that the BoP emergency of 1991 caused the reforms: that absent reserves of two weeks, no Indian government of 1991, and certainly not a minority Congress government led by a compromise candidate, would have abolished licensing, devalued twice and halved peak tariffs, and that the papers of the 1980s are the kind of paper every bureaucracy generates without consequence. The consummation reading holds that the crisis determined when the programme was executed and supplied the political permission for its execution, and that the programme itself, its instruments, order, estimates and drafting cadre, was the product of a decade of centralised preparation that the crisis did not create and could not have improvised.

Three tests decide between the readings, and each test has been built from evidence a sceptic can inspect. The first is the speed test. Thirty-three days from swearing-in to industrial policy statement, with a devaluation, a trade package and a full budget interleaved, is a datum the crisis reading must explain by adrenaline and the consummation reading explains by inventory. No finance ministry on record has drafted a coherent four-instrument programme at that tempo from a standing start, and the Indian Finance Ministry of 1991, for all its quality, was not staffed to be the first exception; it was staffed, precisely and unusually, by the men who had spent the previous decade writing the components. The second is the textual test. The M document of May 1990, published in a national newspaper in July 1990 after its leak, contains the reform sequence a year before the standby arrangement existed, and Jairam Ramesh's judgment that this one paper contains the programme of the Rao government and its successors has stood unrefuted in the literature. A cause cannot postdate its effect by fourteen months. The third is the continuity test. The programme's extensions from 1992 to 1999 followed the preparation documents' sequencing, the exchange rate by the Rangarajan transition path, the external account toward the convertibility the committee literature had marked as the destination, and they continued under governments that owed the creditors of 1991 nothing and feared the Congress of 1991 less. Improvisation decays; the record compounds.

The crisis reading retains one province, and the adjudication awards it without reservation: timing. The preparation had been politically indigestible in July 1990, when the M document's leak produced a cabinet fury and no action. Nothing in the record suggests that a Congress government of June 1991, absent the reserves crisis, would have spent its first month abolishing the licensing system its own founding generation had built. The crisis destroyed the veto of the status quo by repricing inaction: default, import compression and a second gold pledge became the visible alternative to reform, and against that alternative the prepared programme became the conservative option, the path of least institutional resistance. Rao's political achievement, on this reading, was to recognise that the repricing had occurred and to move the inventory through the window before it closed, financing the movement with the creditors' money and shielding it with the creditors' reputation for insistence. Legitimating cover is the brief's phrase for this function, and it is exact: cover conceals something that exists. The crisis concealed the programme's origins, letting a decade of contested drafting enter history as six weeks of necessity.

Two scholarly cautions bound the verdict. Kohli's framework warns against reading 1991 as a rupture in the Indian state's social character: the pro-business reorientation he dates from 1980 continued through 1991 and after, and the reform changed the state's instruments more than its alliances, a caution the consummation reading absorbs, since a decade of preparation by the same state is precisely a story of continuity in personnel and alliance beneath a change in instruments. The distributional critique associated with the Left's economists and with scholars of the informal economy warns from the other side: the programme's preparation was concentrated in institutions structurally attentive to organised industry, finance and the external account, and inattentive, by the same concentration, to agricultural pricing, informal labour and the fiscal position of the states. That critique does not dispute the preparation; it indicts its syllabus. The adjudication accepts both cautions as compatible with the verdict, and the next section takes the second as its subject.

## What the Preparation Did Not Prepare

An honest consummation thesis must inventory the gaps in the inventory, and the gaps are not small. The preparation decade had drafted the liberalisation of industry, trade and the external account to the level of clauses. It had not drafted a prudential regime for the financial markets it was about to free. The securities scandal of 1992, in which the broker Harshad Mehta's operations exploited the interbank market's settlement practices to inflate share prices, exposed a regulatory architecture that the reform's own sequencing had outrun: SEBI received its statute in the same season the scandal broke, and the Joint Parliamentary Committee that investigated the episode documented gaps in the Reserve Bank's supervision of the very markets the programme had energised. The drafting cadre's syllabus had priced the benefits of freeing capital markets with more care than the costs of policing them, a syllabus error the Indian state spent the following decade correcting through the SEBI amendments and the dematerialisation of share trading.

Agriculture is the second gap, and it is structural. The M document's triad, industrial, trade and fiscal policy, describes the programme's centre of gravity with a candour its authors perhaps did not intend: the sector employing the largest share of India's workforce appears in the preparation literature mainly as a source of fiscal pressure, through fertiliser and food subsidies, and as a beneficiary of the exchange rate. The fertiliser price episode of July and August 1991, in which the one instrument that touched the farm gate directly was also the one instrument partially withdrawn under protest, marks the boundary of the prepared programme with unusual clarity. Successor governments inherited the boundary. Agricultural marketing, land and input reform remained state subjects administered outside the 1991 architecture, and the growth the architecture produced was correspondingly concentrated in services and organised industry, the pattern that later critics, including Kohli in his assessments of the post-reform decades, identify as the settlement's deepest limitation.

The third gap is federal. Centralisation is this article's explanatory variable, and it was also the programme's constitutional exposure. The Finance Ministry, the Reserve Bank and the Prime Minister's Office could draft for the union subjects, industry, trade, foreign exchange, banking. They could not draft for the states, whose consent governed power pricing, agricultural markets, land and much of the implementation climate into which delicensed industry was supposed to move. The reform's second decade therefore became a negotiation with state governments of every party, fought over value-added tax adoption, power sector losses and investment summits, on terrain the preparation decade had barely mapped. That the programme survived this second front is a fact about Indian federalism's adaptability. That it had to fight there at all is a fact about the preparation's address: a programme drafted in three buildings in New Delhi was complete everywhere except in the country it governed.

## The Verdict of the Record

The namable claim of this article can be restated in its decided form. The 1991 reform was decade-long preparation consummated in weeks; the BoP crisis was legitimating cover, not actual cause. Cause, in the only sense that distinguishes the readings, attaches to the variable that explains the effect's content, and the content of July 1991, its instruments, its order, its estimates, its statutory destinations across the following eight years, was fixed before the reserves ran out, in the EFF's programme grammar, the fiscal and industrial papers of the middle 1980s, the committee doctrine of the external account and the M document's complete sequence of May 1990. The crisis fixed the date. It also fixed the story India told itself about the date, a story of emergency and rescue in which a reluctant state was saved by its creditors' firmness, and that story has served every participant: it flattered the Fund's institutional memory, it excused the Congress's apostasy from its own creed, and it spared the drafting cadre the political price of authorship.

The archival afterlife of the episode has slowly corrected the story, as memoirs by the participants, the publication of the M document's text, and reconstructions assembled from leaked papers and protected interviews accumulated into a record that the crisis frame cannot organise. Students of that afterlife will find the handling of sources instructive in itself, and accounts of [how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) describe one part of the machinery by which such records reach print intact. What the record shows, once assembled, is a state that had been quietly rewriting its economic constitution in committee for a decade, waiting for the week in which rewriting it aloud became the safest available course. Rao's government deserves its reputation for nerve in that week. The decade deserves the authorship.

## The Party Argues With Its Own Past: Congress Between Avadi and 1991

The preparation decade was bureaucratic, but the obstacle it had to clear in 1991 was not. It was a political party whose economic identity had been composed across forty years, and whose internal argument about the reforms is the missing middle term between the ministries' drafts and the government's survival. The Congress of 1991 contained, in senior office, men who had administered the control regime as a creed. Its organisation secretaries had grown up inside a party that treated industrial licensing as the administrative form of socialism and the public sector as the commanding heights of a moral economy. Rao's management of this inheritance began with vocabulary. In his addresses to party forums through 1991 and 1992 the reforms appear as the correction of distortions, the restoration of efficiency and the fulfilment, by new instruments, of the party's old commitment to growth with social justice. The word privatisation was kept away from official resolutions. The phrase commanding heights was not repudiated; it was allowed to lapse, unattended, until its absence from the party's vocabulary stopped being news.

The argument came into the open at the party's Tirupati session in April 1992, the first full organisational reckoning with the programme. The economic resolution debated there carried the dual character Rao required: it endorsed the new industrial and trade policies as the government's settled direction, and it wrapped the endorsement in the inherited language of self-reliance, protection of the poor and suspicion of foreign capital's terms of entry. Senior figures who harboured doubts about the pace and incidence of reform, among them Arjun Singh, pressed their case inside the session's committees and accepted the resolution's compromise in public. The pattern set at Tirupati repeated across the government's life: discontent expressed as concern about implementation, never as a motion against direction; concessions priced at the margin, the fertiliser softening, a slower disinvestment calendar, a committee to examine each grievance; and the centre of the programme, licensing, trade and the exchange rate, kept off the table of internal bargaining entirely because stealth had already converted those questions into administrative facts before the party assembled to discuss them.

The reckoning that stealth deferred arrived after the government's term, in the Congress's long opposition decade, when the party argued retrospectively about whether 1991 had been its rescue or its apostasy. That later argument is beyond this article's evidence scope, but its terms were fixed in 1991-93 by the settlement Rao imposed: the reforms were the party's own act, performed by its most organisationally orthodox leader through its own parliamentary majority arithmetic, and therefore could not be disowned without disowning the last full term the party had won in the century. The settlement held for the same reason the preparation had worked. Authorship had been arranged, patiently, so that denial cost more than ownership.

## The Yield: Reserves, Growth and the External Account After the Window Closed

A programme's authorship can be debated from documents; its consequences enter the statistical record, and the record of the five years after July 1991 is the final exhibit in the adjudication. Foreign exchange reserves, the crisis variable itself, rebuilt from about $1 billion in June 1991 to roughly $15 billion by March 1994 and about $21 billion by March 1995, a reconstruction driven substantially by the portfolio and direct investment inflows the new regime admitted rather than by borrowing. Export growth in dollar terms, stagnant in 1991-92, ran above 20 percent in 1993-94 and 1994-95 as the devalued and then market-determined exchange rate worked through the trade accounts the Abid Hussain logic had modelled a decade earlier. Foreign direct investment, about $100 million in 1990-91 under the bargaining regime the Industrial Policy Statement replaced, rose above $2 billion a year by the middle of the decade under the automatic route. GDP growth, 1.1 percent in the crisis year 1991-92 on the official series, averaged above 7 percent across 1994-95 to 1996-97, the fastest three-year run the republic had recorded to that date.

Each of these series carries caveats that the literature has properly insisted on, and the article's argument does not require the caveats to be waived. Growth in the 1990s was led by services whose regulation the reform touched lightly and by an investment recovery whose causes include the monsoon sequence and the global liquidity cycle. Poverty reduction through the decade was slower and more regionally uneven than the growth acceleration promised, and employment in organised manufacturing stagnated even as output rose, the distributional indictment stated in the adjudication above. The fiscal correction of 1991 partially reversed within three years, as subsidy discipline met electoral arithmetic, and the centre's deficit path became the settlement's unfinished business into the next decade. These caveats discipline any triumphalism about outcomes. They do not redistribute authorship. A programme can be incompletely successful and wholly prepared; indeed the specific pattern of its incompleteness, strong on the external account and industry, weak on fiscal consolidation and agriculture, is the preparation syllabus's pattern, the strengths and gaps of a decade of drafting by the Finance Ministry and the Reserve Bank reproduced in the results. Outcomes carry the signature of the drafting table because they were drafted there.

One more series belongs in the yield, because it measures the variable the crisis story treats as decisive: the opinion of the creditors whose ultimatum supposedly authored the programme. India's credit ratings, cut to speculative grade in 1991, were restored to investment grade by the middle of the decade; the standby arrangement was completed without the drawings becoming a dependency, and India declined to extend the relationship into the further arrangements the Fund proposed for the later 1990s. The borrower that had supposedly been dictated to behaved, within three years, like a client dismissing a contractor whose work was finished. Behaviour of that kind is data. It is the conduct of a government that regarded the programme as its own property throughout, and the creditors' money as the one input it had rented rather than the design it had been handed, a distinction the crisis story cannot accommodate and the consummation story predicts in advance.

## The Ghost of 1966: The Earlier Rehearsal and the Lessons It Filed

The preparation decade had a negative text as well as a positive one, and the negative text is dated June 1966. In that month the government of Indira Gandhi, under pressure from the World Bank and the United States during a drought and an aid suspension, devalued the rupee by 36.5 percent, from 4.76 to 7.50 to the dollar, and paired the devaluation with an import liberalisation package negotiated in Washington. The economics of the package was defensible. Its politics were catastrophic. Export response disappointed, aid promised against the adjustment arrived slowly and with fresh conditions, the opposition across the spectrum framed the devaluation as a surrender dictated abroad, and the Congress carried the wound into the 1967 election, after which the liberalisation was quietly rolled back and the control regime rebuilt thicker than before. The episode entered the administrative memory of the Indian state as the standing proof that reform by foreign instruction, announced nakedly and defended apologetically, destroys the government that attempts it.

The men of the 1980s drafting cadre had been formed inside that memory; several of the decade's institutions were staffed by officials whose seniors had served in 1966, and the lessons were transmitted as craft knowledge rather than as doctrine. Read the design of 1991 against the 1966 template and the design resolves into a point-by-point inversion. In 1966 the devaluation stood alone and bore the whole political weight of the adjustment; in 1991 the devaluation was the smallest and earliest item in a package whose centre of gravity, licensing abolition and the budget, lay elsewhere, so that no single instrument could be isolated as the foreigner's clause. In 1966 the programme's authorship was visibly external, negotiated with the Bank's Bell Mission tradition and defended in Delhi as an obligation; in 1991 authorship was arranged to be Indian in text and in personnel, with the Fund's arrangement following the programme by a quarter and ratifying a sequence already executed. In 1966 the government apologised for its own policy in the idiom of necessity and reaped contempt from both its supporters and its creditors; in 1991 the Finance Minister's Hugo quotation performed the opposite register, presenting the adjustment as an idea whose time had come, indigenous in its arrival even where it was convergent with the international consensus. In 1966 the liberalisation was reversed within two years because it had created no domestic institutional residue, no regulator, no statute, no constituency inside the state; the 1991 programme was built to leave residue at every step, SEBI's statute, the exchange rate's unification, the convertibility acceptance, so that reversal would require a successor government to repeal an architecture rather than merely to neglect a decision.

The 1966 ghost also explains a stylistic feature of the preparation decade that otherwise looks like mere caution: its preference for committee reports, policy statements and fiscal documents over manifestos and white papers of intent. A committee report is a reform that has already happened at the level of expert consensus and awaits only notification; it creates authorship without creating a target. The Abid Hussain Committee of 1984, the fiscal documents of 1985, the Rangarajan committee literature and the M document itself, a paper for internal circulation that entered public life through a leak rather than a launch, all belong to this genre of deniable preparation. The cadre that had watched 1966 did not lack conviction about the destination. It lacked, and deliberately refused to create, a public moment at which conviction could be defeated before it became administration. Rao, who had been a minister in Andhra Pradesh in 1966 and a member of the Congress's national councils through the episode's aftermath, did not need the lesson explained to him in 1991. The silent reforms were silent because the republic's first attempt at the same adjustment had been loud, and had died of the noise.

## The Governor in the Middle: Venkitaramanan and the Reserve Bank's Crisis Craft

One personnel continuity runs underneath the change of government itself and deserves separate statement, because it locates the preparation inside the crisis's worst months rather than only before them. S. Venkitaramanan took charge as Governor of the Reserve Bank in December 1990, under the Chandra Shekhar government, and held the office until December 1992, across the whole arc of the emergency and the first reform year. He came to the governorship from the Finance Secretary's office, which he had held from 1985 to 1989, the very years of the fiscal statements and delicensing notifications this article has described. The man who executed the gold operations and the two-step devaluation of 1991 was therefore not an appointee of the crisis improvising a technique. He was a preparation-decade finance secretary administering, from the central bank's chair, a crisis whose arithmetic he had been compiling for six years.

The Reserve Bank's craft in those months repays attention because it shows preparation operating at the level of instruments rather than documents. Import cover was rationed through cash margin requirements and interest surcharges on import finance imposed in stages from late 1990, compressing the import bill administratively while the policy programme was still being assembled. The India Development Bonds issued through the State Bank of India in October 1991 raised roughly $1.6 billion from non-resident Indians on terms designed inside the central bank, a financing instrument that treated the diaspora as a reserve asset years before that treatment became conventional. The gold operations themselves, for all their political cost, were executed as collateralised borrowing with repurchase, preserving the metal's return, a banker's distinction that a panicked administration would not have troubled itself to draw. And the two-step structure of the July devaluation, sized and spaced by the Reserve Bank's market intelligence, belonged to the same craft tradition. When the adjudication above credits the preparation decade with the programme's accuracy, a share of that accuracy has this address: Mint Street, under a governor whose previous posting had been the preparation itself.

## Why the Crisis Story Won the Memory

A decided adjudication leaves one question standing: if the preparation record is this legible, why did the crisis story capture the textbooks, the anniversary journalism and the reference entries so completely? The incentives of every narrator point the same way, and the point deserves record because it explains the persistence of a framing the documents contradict. For the Fund, the crisis story is institutional self-portraiture: a Fund that rescues is a Fund that matters, and the structural adjustment era's official memory, in Washington as in the borrowing capitals, was composed by the rescuers. For the Congress, the crisis story was the alibi that stealth required after the fact as much as during it: a party that had been forced could reunite, in time, with a party that had chosen, and the forced reading spared two generations of Congress politicians the charge of apostasy that the chosen reading invites. For Rao himself, the crisis story was the final protective colouration of a man who never corrected a useful misapprehension about his own authorship; his public reticence across the decade after 1996, unbroken by any memoir, left the field to the narrators whose interests the emergency frame served. For his Finance Minister, the most prolific narrator of all in speeches across 1991 to 1996, the crisis was both true and useful, the warrant for measures whose deeper genealogy it was never expedient to advertise to the house that had to pass them. And for the textbook form itself, crisis and response is a teachable shape, two dates and a turning, where a decade of committee papers is a syllabus. The preparation record survived in the specialist literature, in Kohli's political economy, in the Kapur and Mehta institutional essays, in Ramesh's narrative reconstruction and in the participants' later memoirs, which is to say it survived exactly where the consummation reading would predict: in the archive, waiting for readers willing to trade a turning point for a paper trail.

## The Speech as Document: Reading the Budget of 24 July 1991 as a Prepared Text

Primary sources argue in their own voice, and the mandatory primary source at the centre of this article's brief, the budget speech of 24 July 1991, rewards being read the way the adjudication has taught: as the surface of a preparation, not as a cry of alarm. The speech is conventionally mined for two sentences, the Hugo quotation at its close and its declaration that India was wide awake, and the mining has obscured its architecture. Read whole, the speech is built like the programme it announced. It opens with the crisis stated in numbers, reserves, deficit, inflation, in the flat register of a balance sheet. It moves to the correction as a system rather than as a list: fiscal consolidation, trade reform, industrial deregulation, financial repair and public enterprise restructuring are presented as interlocking parts of a single design, each part's justification citing the others. It closes in the prophetic register, the Hugo line converting the system into a destiny. A speech improvised to satisfy a creditor does not need the middle movement at all; creditors read letters of intent, not perorations to the Lok Sabha. The middle movement exists because the design existed, complete enough to be explained as a design to a hostile house by a minister who had spent six years, at the Reserve Bank and the Planning Commission, thinking in exactly these connected terms.

The speech's silences argue as forcefully as its sentences. It does not blame the Fund. It does not announce an ideology, coining no doctrine and burying none. It does not promise a timetable it cannot keep, and its quantitative commitments, the deficit path, the duty path, are set at levels the ministry's models, run across the preparation decade against the EFF's aftermath and the fiscal statements of the middle 1980s, had already stress-tested. Compare the document with the reform speeches of the improvisation era in other capitals in those years, finance ministers reading creditor term sheets into parliamentary records with the serial numbers barely filed off, and the Indian text's anomaly stands out: it is a programme speech in a country whose official story insists no programme existed until the emergency demanded one. The standard account needs the speech to be a translation. The paper trail, the decade of drafts in the same vocabulary, the same sequence, the same numbers discipline, supports the simpler description: it was an original, delivered by its principal drafter, in the first person of a government that had finally acquired permission to say in public what its secretariat had been writing in private for ten years.

## The International Moment: The Alternative Model Collapses, 1989 to 1991

One further layer of the record belongs in the adjudication's background, because the preparation decade unfolded inside a world that was itself changing the price of the Indian model. India's external economic constitution after 1956 rested on two supports that felt permanent to the generation that built them: a Soviet market that absorbed Indian exports against rupee payment, providing a soft-currency outlet for goods that could not compete in hard-currency markets, and a domestic consensus, shared across the Congress and much of the opposition, that the licence-permit system was the administrative skeleton of sovereignty. Between November 1989 and December 1991 both supports failed together. The Berlin Wall fell in November 1989. The Soviet Union, India's largest rupee-trade partner and a supplier of oil and defence equipment on concessionary terms, dissolved in December 1991, months after the reform budget, converting a trading relationship built on political allocation into a hard-currency claim the Indian treasury could not service from reserves of $1 billion. The Gulf War had already demonstrated the new exposure in miniature: the oil price spike of August 1990 arrived through markets India did not influence, and the remittance lifeline from Indian workers in the Gulf was interrupted by an evacuation that the Indian state had to finance.

The drafting cadre read this environment earlier than the political class did, and the M document's timing belongs to the reading. Ahluwalia's May 1990 paper was commissioned against the spectacle of East Asian growth that V.P. Singh had witnessed in Kuala Lumpur, and its argument, that India's control regime was the binding constraint on a convergence its neighbours were achieving, gained force each quarter as the socialist alternative's material base contracted. The consummation thesis should absorb this layer carefully. The international moment did not write the Indian programme; programmes resembling it were being written in Finance Ministries from Warsaw to Buenos Aires in those years, and the Indian text's distinguishing marks, its sequencing, its capital account refusal, its stealth politics, remained Indian. What the international moment changed was the audience. A programme that in July 1990 had struck a cabinet as a foreign dictation could be presented in July 1991 as the terms on which a country rejoins a world that had stopped offering the old terms. Legitimation, again: the crisis supplied the domestic half of it, the collapsing Soviet support the international half, and the preparation supplied everything else.

The point can be sharpened by the counterfactual the critics of 1991 themselves proposed. The alternative governments of the period, a Chandra Shekhar administration continued, or a coalition of the Janata Dal and the Left, would have faced the same reserves, the same oil bill, the same dissolved Soviet account and the same creditors. Their programmes, import compression, default brinkmanship, an appeal to non-aligned solidarity, existed as rhetoric and as the record of the Chandra Shekhar months; the January 1991 drawings and the May 1991 gold sale are what the alternative looked like in office, buying weeks at rising prices with no structural exit. The preparation thesis claims only that the Rao government possessed, and its plausible alternatives lacked, a drafted exit. The international record of 1989 to 1991 is the reason an exit had become necessary. The domestic record of 1981 to 1990 is the reason one was available.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the 1991 economic reform?

The 1991 economic reform was the programme executed by the government of P.V. Narasimha Rao in June and July 1991, comprising a two-step devaluation of the rupee on 1 and 3 July, a trade policy package on 4 July, and, on 24 July, a Union budget and a Statement on Industrial Policy that abolished industrial licensing for all but eighteen industries, cut the industries reserved for the public sector from seventeen to eight, and opened thirty-four industries to automatic foreign investment up to 51 percent. It dismantled the licensing core of the control regime built since 1956 and re-founded Indian economic policy on markets, trade and private investment.

### Q: Who was P.V. Narasimha Rao?

Pamulaparthi Venkata Narasimha Rao (1921 to 2004) was Prime Minister of India from 21 June 1991 to 16 May 1996. A Congress veteran from Andhra Pradesh, where he had been Chief Minister from 1971 to 1973, he held the External Affairs, Home, Defence and Human Resource Development portfolios across the 1980s and was known as a scholar-politician fluent in several languages. Elected leader of the Congress Parliamentary Party on 20 June 1991 after Rajiv Gandhi's assassination, he headed a minority government that lasted a full term, and he supplied the political authorship and protection for the 1991 economic reforms while Manmohan Singh administered them as Finance Minister.

### Q: What was the BoP crisis of 1991?

The balance of payments crisis of 1991 was India's near-default on its external obligations. Foreign exchange reserves fell to about $1 billion by June 1991, under two weeks of import cover, after the Gulf War raised oil prices, remittances from the Gulf faltered, non-resident deposits flowed out and credit ratings were downgraded. The Chandra Shekhar government drew about $1.8 billion from the IMF in January 1991, sold 20 tonnes of gold through the State Bank of India in May, and the incoming Rao government pledged a further 46.91 tonnes of Reserve Bank gold in July, while commercial borrowing closed and India came within weeks of defaulting.

### Q: What did Manmohan Singh do as Finance Minister?

Manmohan Singh, Finance Minister from June 1991 to 1996, presented the reform budgets that cut the fiscal deficit target from 8.4 percent of GDP toward 6.5 percent, reduced the peak customs duty from above 300 percent to 150 percent in 1991 and lowered it further in successive budgets, and dismantled exchange and capital-issue controls. His 1992-93 budget introduced the Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System, the 1993-94 budget unified the exchange rate, and his ministry created the statutory framework for SEBI, admitted foreign institutional investors and prepared the tax and financial sector reforms that the preparation decade had drafted and that his five budgets executed in sequence.

### Q: What is the Industrial Policy Statement of 1991?

The Statement on Industrial Policy laid before Parliament on 24 July 1991 is the founding document of the reformed industrial regime. It abolished industrial licensing for all industries except eighteen retained on security, strategic and environmental grounds, reduced the list of industries reserved for the public sector from seventeen to eight, abolished the monopolies legislation's asset threshold for large firms, ended phased manufacturing programmes, and provided automatic approval for foreign direct investment up to 51 percent equity in thirty-four high-priority industries. Presented on the same day as the 1991-92 budget, it converted four decades of discretionary industrial control into a short negative list.

### Q: Was the 1991 reform forced by the IMF?

The reform was financed and legitimated by the IMF but not authored by it. The Fund's standby arrangement was approved on 31 October 1991, three months after the Industrial Policy Statement and the devaluation it supposedly dictated, and its structural content reproduced a programme the Indian Finance Ministry had been drafting since the early 1980s and had written down in full in the M document of May 1990. The IMF supplied several billion dollars of credit, a monitoring calendar the Rao government used as a commitment device against domestic opposition, and a public explanation, compulsion, that shielded the government. Timing was forced by the crisis; substance was prepared in Delhi.

### Q: How long was the 1991 reform in preparation?

The preparation ran for about a decade. Its opening document is the IMF Extended Fund Facility negotiated in 1980 and approved in November 1981, which trained the Finance Ministry in programme drafting. The Long-Term Fiscal Policy statement followed in December 1985, delicensing notifications and trade committee reports through the middle 1980s built the component instruments, and Montek Singh Ahluwalia's M document of May 1990 wrote the complete sequence down a year before the reforms were executed. The personnel matched the papers: Manmohan Singh, Montek Singh Ahluwalia and C. Rangarajan spent the decade rotating through the Reserve Bank, the Planning Commission and the Prime Minister's Office that executed the programme in 1991.

### Q: What was the M document that Montek Singh Ahluwalia prepared in 1990?

The M document is the memorandum titled Towards a Restructuring of Industrial, Trade and Fiscal Policies, prepared by Montek Singh Ahluwalia in May 1990 after Prime Minister V.P. Singh asked him to set down what India needed to match East Asian growth. It proposed the abolition of industrial licensing outside a narrow list, replacement of import licensing with falling tariffs, a market-oriented exchange rate, fiscal correction and a liberal foreign investment regime: the 1991 programme in outline. Leaked and published in the Financial Express in July 1990, it provoked cabinet hostility and was shelved. Jairam Ramesh judged it the single document containing the Rao government's reform programme.

### Q: What was the IMF Extended Fund Facility that India took in 1981?

The Extended Fund Facility approved for India in November 1981 was an IMF arrangement of SDR 5 billion over three years, then the largest the Fund had granted, negotiated after the second oil shock doubled India's petroleum import bill. Unlike emergency drawings, an EFF carried medium-term structural commitments on subsidies, pricing and trade, verified in tranches against letters of intent drafted by the Finance Ministry. India drew about SDR 3.9 billion and ended the arrangement early in 1984 from a position of improved reserves. Its lasting effect was institutional: a cadre of Indian officials learned to write, negotiate and operate a structural adjustment programme from the inside.

### Q: How did the Rao government carry reforms through a Parliament where Congress lacked a majority?

The government fragmented the programme so that no single vote ever decided it. Licensing abolition travelled in a policy statement, tariff cuts inside budget schedules, exchange reform through Reserve Bank circulars, and each fragment assembled its own majority or required none. Rao bought time with by-elections, priced concessions at the programme's margins, a fertiliser softening here, a slower disinvestment calendar there, and defended the centre absolutely. When the opposition finally forced a single deciding question, the no-confidence motion of 28 July 1993, the government survived by a narrow margin its managers had spent two years assembling, and completed its full five-year term.

### Q: What changed for foreign direct investment under the 1991 Industrial Policy Statement?

The Statement inverted the presumption that had governed foreign equity since the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act of 1973, under which foreign shareholding was bargained down toward 40 percent and admitted case by case. From 24 July 1991, foreign direct investment up to 51 percent equity in thirty-four high-priority industries received automatic approval through the Reserve Bank, without prior government clearance, and dividend payments were linked to export earnings only for a defined transition in consumer goods. Majority foreign ownership became the default for the listed industries, trading houses were permitted majority equity to promote exports, and a Foreign Investment Promotion Board handled the exceptional cases the automatic route did not cover.

### Q: Which reforms of the 1990s extended the framework set in 1991?

The extensions followed the preparation documents' sequence. SEBI received statutory authority in 1992 and the Controller of Capital Issues was abolished; foreign institutional investors entered Indian equities the same year. The Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System of March 1992 led to a unified market-determined exchange rate on 1 March 1993 and to current account convertibility in August 1994. The National Telecom Policy of 1994 opened telecom services to private entry. India signed the Marrakesh agreement in April 1994 and entered the WTO as a founding member on 1 January 1995. The Malhotra Committee of 1993 began the insurance opening completed by the IRDA Act of 1999.

### Q: How did the main opposition parties respond to the 1991 reforms?

The opposition attacked from three directions without ever uniting. The Bharatiya Janata Party, with 120 seats, criticised the programme in the language of swadeshi self-reliance and suspicion of foreign capital, while its business constituency pressed the government to dismantle controls faster. The Communist Party of India (Marxist) and the Communist Party of India denounced the reforms as an IMF dictation, defended the public sector and licensing as protections for workers and the poor, and organised union resistance to restructuring. The Janata Dal's remnants attacked the reforms as a betrayal of the social justice programme of 1989. Fragmentation of the programme into administrative acts denied all three a single target.

### Q: What did the 1991 budget do to fertiliser prices, and why was the increase partly reversed?

The budget of 24 July 1991 raised fertiliser prices by 40 percent, the first increase in a decade, as part of its subsidy discipline and deficit correction: fertiliser subsidy had become one of the budget's fastest growing lines, and the preparation documents had marked it for correction. Within weeks the increase met organised resistance inside Parliament and the Congress party, where members argued that the full incidence fell on cultivators in a single season and threatened the government's rural support. The government softened the increase, exempting small and marginal farmers from part of it, and defended every other budget measure without retreat, an early demonstration of Rao's method of conceding at the margin to protect the centre.

### Q: How did the 1991 reforms treat public sector enterprises?

The reforms restructured the public sector's boundary rather than abolishing it. The industries reserved for public sector operation were cut from seventeen to eight, confined to defence, atomic energy, railways and a small strategic list, and the monopolies asset threshold that had constrained private growth was abolished. Sick public enterprises were referred to the Board for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction for restructuring or closure decisions, the government began disinvesting minority shareholdings in selected enterprises to raise revenue and widen share ownership, and a memorandum of understanding system, begun in the late 1980s, was extended to give enterprise managers autonomy against performance targets. Strategic privatisation came later, under successor governments, beyond the 1991 settlement's own terms.

### Q: Who was Montek Singh Ahluwalia and what part did he play in the reforms?

Montek Singh Ahluwalia is an economist who served in the Prime Minister's Office under Rajiv Gandhi as an economic adviser and special secretary, became Commerce Secretary in 1990, and moved into the Finance Ministry after 1991, serving as Finance Secretary in the first reform years. His authorship of the May 1990 paper Towards a Restructuring of Industrial, Trade and Fiscal Policies, nicknamed the M document by Ashok Desai, made him the programme's draftsman before it had a government. After 1991 he administered trade and fiscal components of the sequence he had written, later becoming Deputy Chairman of the Planning Commission from 2004 to 2014, and recorded the drafting history in his memoir Backstage (2020).

### Q: What was the trade policy package announced in July 1991?

The trade policy package announced by Commerce Minister P. Chidambaram on 4 July 1991, three days after the first devaluation step, converted export success into import capacity. Its central instrument was the EXIM scrip, a tradable entitlement earned at a uniform rate against exports, which exporters could sell to importers at a market premium, replacing a thicket of discretionary replenishment licences with a single priced instrument. The package simplified advance licensing for exporters, expanded the list of imports on open general licence, began decanalising imports from state trading monopolies, and aligned the trade regime with the two-step devaluation so that exporters gained from both the exchange rate and the entitlement premium.

### Q: What did Manmohan Singh mean by an idea whose time had come in the 1991 budget speech?

Closing his budget speech of 24 July 1991, Manmohan Singh quoted Victor Hugo: no power on earth can stop an idea whose time has come, and he applied the line to the emergence of India as a major economic power in the world. The quotation did deliberate work. It presented the reform programme, assembled under emergency conditions and carrying measures many in his own party distrusted, as the arrival of a historical moment rather than as a minister's preference or a creditor's instruction. It has since become the most quoted sentence of the reform era, reproduced in textbooks and anniversary accounts as the rhetorical founding act of the new economic policy.

### Q: When did India make the rupee convertible on the current account?

India accepted the Article VIII obligations of the International Monetary Fund in August 1994, the act that made the rupee convertible for current account transactions such as trade payments, travel and remittances. The step closed a sequence set out in the Rangarajan committee's transition path: the dual-rate Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System of March 1992, unification into a single market-determined rate on 1 March 1993, and then convertibility once reserves had been rebuilt. Convertibility meant the state surrendered the administrative allocation of foreign exchange for current transactions. Capital account convertibility was deliberately not accepted, a refusal the preparation documents had built into the sequence from the start.

### Q: Why did Rao keep the Industries portfolio in his own hands?

Rao retained the Industries portfolio when he formed his government on 21 June 1991 because the Industrial Policy Statement was the programme's most contested instrument inside his own party, and its passage required the Prime Minister's personal weight. A separate industries minister could have been lobbied, disowned or made the factional target for the abolition of licensing; the Prime Minister could not. Retaining the portfolio also joined political command to the drafting process, since the statement was being finalised in the same weeks by the secretariat, and it signalled to the bureaucracy and to industry that the Statement carried the government's full authority rather than one ministry's enthusiasm.
