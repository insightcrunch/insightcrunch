The Urban Land Ceiling Act repeal is the strangest repeal in the Indian statute book, because it is a repeal that did not finish. In 1999 Parliament passed a short Act of five sections repealing the Urban Land (Ceiling and Regulation) Act, 1976, the Emergency-era law that had capped how much vacant urban land any person could hold. An ordinary repeal ends a statute on a date. This one ended the ceiling law in two states and the Union territories on a date deemed to be 11 January 1999, and in every other state only on the day that state's own legislature chose to agree, which in one large state meant a wait of more than eight years, and in a few states has meant no agreement at all. A law repealed at the Centre went on being administered, litigated and complied with in parts of India long after Parliament had declared it gone.

![The Urban Land Ceiling Act and its state-by-state repeal under Article 252 - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The reason is constitutional, and it is the whole subject of this article. Land is a state subject. Parliament had no power of its own to impose a ceiling on urban land, and it acquired the power in 1976 only because eleven state legislatures passed resolutions under Article 252 of the Constitution asking Parliament to legislate for them. What consent gave, only consent could take away. Article 252 carries a second clause that receives a fraction of the attention given to the first: an Act passed on the strength of state resolutions may be amended or repealed, for a state that adopted it, only by another Act passed in the same consenting manner. The ceiling law was therefore repealed twice over in form and many times over in fact, once by Parliament in 1999 and then again, state by state, as each legislature passed the resolution that alone could switch the statute off in its territory.

That mechanism matters beyond this one statute, because the ceiling law left behind the largest single residue of unfinished business of any repealed Indian Act. Land that had vested in state governments stayed vested. Exemptions granted over two decades stayed valid. Proceedings that had run for years abated in a day in one state and ran on for years more across a border. Property titles in Indian cities still carry the marks of ceiling proceedings begun in the 1970s and 1980s, and whether a given parcel is touched by the old law depends on a question most buyers never think to ask: not what Parliament did, but what their own state legislature did, and when.

## A repeal that could not travel on its own

Most repeals in this series follow a simple grammar. Parliament enacts a statute under its own competence, discovers in time that the statute has failed or been outgrown, and repeals it, sometimes replacing it with a successor. The ceiling law breaks the grammar at the first step. The subject it governed, land, together with rights in or over land, sits in the State List of the Seventh Schedule, in Entry 18, and the Constitution gives Parliament no general entry under which a ceiling on urban land holdings could be imposed for the states. The preamble of the 1976 Act says this with unusual candour. After reciting the purposes of the ceiling, it records that Parliament has no power to make laws for the states with respect to those matters except as provided in Articles 249 and 250 of the Constitution, and then records the fact that supplied the power instead: resolutions passed by the legislatures of eleven named states under clause (1) of Article 252, asking that the matters be regulated in those states by Parliament by law.

Article 252 is the Constitution's consent mechanism, and it deserves to be understood on its own terms because the repeal story is unintelligible without it. Where the legislatures of two or more states pass resolutions to the effect that a matter in the State List should be regulated by Parliament, Parliament may legislate on that matter for those states. A state that did not pass a resolution at the outset may adopt the resulting Act later, by a resolution of its own. Clause (2) then locks the arrangement: an Act so passed may not be amended or repealed, as respects a state to which it applies, by that state's own legislature at all, and Parliament's power to amend or repeal it runs through the same consenting route. The division of competence that the Seventh Schedule creates, which the article on the [Seventh Schedule and its three lists](/2018/09/15/seventh-schedule-lists-explained/) sets out in full, is therefore not merely bypassed by Article 252. It is voluntarily re-drawn by the states themselves, and once re-drawn it cannot be re-drawn back by either side acting alone.

The namable claim of this article is that Article 252 is a one-way door with a second key. A state that resolves under clause (1) surrenders its exclusive competence over the subject to Parliament, and it cannot legislate the central Act away, however completely its politics change. Parliament, for its part, holds the statute but not the whole of it, because its repeal takes full effect in a consenting state only when the constitutional condition on which the original power rested is worked through again. A state that changes its mind after the Centre has changed its own can therefore keep a repealed central statute alive in its own territory indefinitely, not by defying the repeal but by simply never completing it. The ceiling law is the standing proof. It was dead in Delhi and Chandigarh from January 1999, alive and administered in Mumbai for nearly nine more years, and alive in Kolkata for far longer.

There is a further reason this repeal repays attention, and it connects the article to the argument of the series as a whole. The catalogue of statutes assembled across these articles includes several that Parliament passed and the executive then declined to commence, laws that existed on paper and never operated because no notification brought them into force. The ceiling repeal is the mirror image of that failure, and it is constitutional rather than administrative. Parliament passed a repeal in proper form and lacked the power to make it effective everywhere it was meant to bite. The statute book recorded a death; the land records of several states went on recording a life. That is a distinct mode of legislative failure, the sixth this series has identified, and the only one in which the gap between enactment and effect is written into the Constitution itself.

## What the ceiling law set out to do

The Urban Land (Ceiling and Regulation) Act, 1976 was a product of the Emergency, and its text carries the marks of that origin in its confidence that a distributive problem could be solved by prohibition. It received the assent of the President on 17 February 1976 and came into force at once in the eleven resolving states and the Union territories. Its stated purposes, taken from its own preamble, were to prevent the concentration of urban land in the hands of a few persons, to check speculation and profiteering in urban land, and to bring about an equitable distribution of land in urban agglomerations to subserve the common good. The political logic was the urban counterpart of the agricultural land ceiling laws that the states had enacted over the preceding two decades. If rural land could be capped and the surplus redistributed, urban land, scarcer and appreciating far faster, seemed an even stronger candidate for the same treatment.

The instrument chosen was a ceiling on vacant land. The Act did not cap the value of a person's urban property, or the size of a person's house, or urban land in every form. It capped the holding of vacant land, a defined term that excluded land mainly used for agriculture and land already occupied by a building together with its appurtenant area, and it applied that cap in the urban agglomerations listed in its First Schedule. The Schedule named 64 urban agglomerations, towns and cities with a population above two lakh on the 1971 Census, sorted into four categories by size. Category A comprised the four great metropolitan agglomerations of Delhi, Bombay, Calcutta and Madras. Category B took the other agglomerations with a population of ten lakh and above. Categories C and D descended through progressively smaller agglomerations to the two-lakh floor. The ceiling tightened as the city grew, on the reasoning that land scarcity, and therefore the social cost of one person holding land idle, rose with the size of the agglomeration.

Around that cap the Act built a complete administrative circuit. Every person holding vacant land above the ceiling had to file a statement disclosing the holding. A competent authority, appointed by the state government for each area, examined the statement, prepared a draft and then a final statement of the excess, notified the excess land for acquisition, and on a further declaration the excess was deemed to have been acquired by the state government and to have vested in it free from all encumbrances. The dispossessed holder was paid not compensation at market value but an amount computed by statutory formula and capped at levels that were low even by the standards of 1976. Vested land was then to be disposed of by the state government for the common good, which in the design meant, above all, housing for the poor. The scheme's moral centre was that transfer of value: from the idle holdings of the few to the housing needs of the many, executed by a ceiling, a statement and a vesting declaration.

Two features of the design should be fixed before the record is examined, because both returned to dominate the repeal. The first is that the Act was administered entirely by the states. Parliament wrote the text, but the competent authorities were state appointees, the statements were filed with state machinery, the exemptions were granted by state governments and the vested land passed to state governments. A law that existed by state consent was also operated by state hands, which meant that its vigour varied with the enthusiasm of each administering state from the first day. The second is the exemption power in Section 20, which allowed a state government to lift the ceiling off a particular holding in the public interest or to avoid undue hardship. The exemption was designed as a safety valve. In operation it became a second, discretionary ceiling system running alongside the statutory one, and the litigation it generated, together with the vesting litigation, made the Act one of the most contested property statutes of its generation.

## The constitutional route: consent given in 1976

The eleven states whose resolutions carried the Act were Andhra Pradesh, Gujarat, Haryana, Himachal Pradesh, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Orissa, Punjab, Tripura, Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal. Section 1 of the Act applied it in the first instance to the whole of those states and to all the Union territories, and provided that it would extend to any other state that adopted it by resolution under Article 252(1). Section 1(3) brought it into force in the resolving states and the Union territories at once, and in an adopting state on the date of adoption. The definition of the appointed day in Section 2 tied the Act's operation to the date of introduction of the Bill in Parliament for the original states, and to the date of adoption for states that came in later, so the statute ran from the start on eleven different territorial footings gathered under one text.

The consent did not stop at eleven. In the months after assent, six more legislatures resolved to adopt the Act: Rajasthan on 9 March 1976, Manipur on 12 March 1976, Assam on 25 March 1976, Bihar on 1 April 1976, Meghalaya on 7 April 1976 and Madhya Pradesh on 9 September 1976. The Act thus came to operate in seventeen states and all the Union territories, a reach recorded by the Constitution Bench that later upheld it. One large state stands apart in the story and is worth naming for what it shows about the mechanism. Tamil Nadu did not work through the central Act at all and instead enacted its own statute, the Tamil Nadu Urban Land (Ceiling and Regulation) Act, 1978, which it could repeal or amend by its own authority whenever it chose. The contrast is the cleanest illustration of what Article 252 surrendered. A state with its own ceiling law kept both keys. A state that consented to Parliament's law kept neither the power to amend it nor the power to end it.

### Why did Parliament need resolutions from state legislatures to pass a land ceiling law?

Because land sits in Entry 18 of the State List, Parliament had no competence of its own over it, so eleven state legislatures passed resolutions under Article 252(1) of the Constitution asking Parliament to legislate for them, and the 1976 Act rested entirely on that consent.

The doctrinal point is easily missed because the Act looks like an ordinary central statute. It was printed as Act 33 of 1976, it carried a single text for the whole of its territory, and it was administered as though it were a routine exercise of union power. In truth its authority was borrowed, and borrowed on terms. The Supreme Court settled the competence question early. In Union of India v Valluri Basavaiah Chowdhary, decided in 1979, the Court upheld Parliament's power to enact the law on the strength of the resolutions the states had passed. A few years later, in Maharao Sahib Shri Bhim Singhji v Union of India, decided in November 1980, a Constitution Bench upheld the Act's validity against the wider challenge mounted against its scheme. The litigation that mattered most, however, was never really about competence. Competence was the door through which the Act entered. The battles were fought inside the house, over what counted as vacant land, what was excess, what had vested and what had been exempted, and those battles were still running when the repeal arrived.

It is worth pausing on what the states had actually agreed to in 1976, because the repeal turns on it. A resolution under Article 252(1) is not a request for a model law, and it is not a delegation that the state can withdraw. Once Parliament legislates, the resulting Act occupies the field for the consenting states, and by clause (2) those states lose the power to amend or repeal it through their own legislatures. The state's remedy for a bad bargain is not legislation but a further resolution inviting Parliament to act again. In the confident centralising climate of 1976, with the Emergency in force and distributive land reform a governing slogan, the eleven resolutions were passed with little evident anxiety about the lock-in. The lock-in was nonetheless real, and it was still operating twenty-three years later, when the same legislatures, or their successors, discovered that ending the ceiling required them to pass the mirror resolution and wait for Parliament's repeal to reach them through it.

## How the ceiling worked

The operative heart of the Act was Chapter III, and its machinery repays a careful walk-through, because the repeal's savings provisions can only be understood against the stages through which a holding passed on its way to vesting.

Section 3 stated the prohibition in general terms: except as the Act otherwise provided, no person was entitled to hold vacant land in excess of the ceiling limit in the territories to which the Act applied. Section 4 then fixed the limit itself, graded by the category of the agglomeration in the First Schedule. In a Category A agglomeration the ceiling was five hundred square metres. In Category B it was one thousand square metres. In Category C it was one thousand five hundred square metres, and in Category D, two thousand square metres.

### How much vacant land could one person hold in a metropolitan city under the ceiling Act?

Five hundred square metres. Section 4 of the 1976 Act fixed the ceiling in a Category A agglomeration, which meant Delhi, Bombay, Calcutta and Madras, at five hundred square metres of vacant land per person, with higher limits of one thousand, one thousand five hundred and two thousand square metres in the smaller categories.

The definition of a person did substantial work in that answer, since Section 2 counted an individual, a family, a firm, a company and an association or body of individuals as persons for the Act's purposes, and the ceiling applied to the holding of each. Section 4 also had to solve the problem of the holder whose land lay in more than one category, and it solved it with a conversion table: one square metre in Category A counted as two in Category B, three in Category C and four in Category D, with corresponding ratios between the other categories, so that a scattered holding could be aggregated into a single measure and tested against a single ceiling. A separate provision protected land committed to a sanctioned group housing scheme before the Act commenced, allowing the holder to retain what the scheme required, subject to conditions that included a bar on any one person owning more than one dwelling unit in the scheme.

The machinery then moved through statements. Section 6 required every person holding vacant land in excess of the ceiling limit at the commencement of the Act to file a statement before the competent authority for the area, setting out the location, extent and value of the holdings and specifying which lands the holder wished to retain within the ceiling. The choice of what to retain mattered enormously in practice, since a holder with several plots could nominate the most valuable for retention and surrender the rest, and disputes over that nomination, over the accuracy of statements and over holdings that had never been declared at all ran through the Act's entire life. On the statements, the competent authority prepared a draft statement under Section 8 showing the extent of excess vacant land, served it on the holder, heard objections, and then issued a final statement under Section 9 determining the excess.

Section 10 converted that determination into acquisition. After the final statement was served, the competent authority published a notification in the official gazette declaring the excess land, and after a further declaration the excess was deemed to have been acquired by the state government and to have vested in it free from all encumbrances from the date specified. Vesting under the Act was therefore a statutory event, accomplished by declaration rather than by conveyance, a design that later produced the central question of the repeal litigation: the difference between land that had vested on paper and land of which the state had also taken possession. Sections 10(5) and 10(6) supplied the possession machinery the declaration lacked. The competent authority was to serve a written notice on the person in possession requiring surrender or delivery of possession within the period the section prescribed, and on failure to comply it could take possession, using such force as was necessary. The two-step structure, vesting by declaration and possession by notice, reads as a technicality in the text. After 1999 it decided who kept their land.

The price of acquisition was fixed by Section 11, and its terms explain more about the Act's failure than any other provision. Where the excess land yielded an income, the amount payable was eight and one-third times the net average annual income actually derived from it over the five consecutive years preceding the acquisition notification, computed on the principles in the Second Schedule. Where the land yielded no income, the amount was calculated at a rate not exceeding ten rupees per square metre in a Category A or Category B agglomeration and five rupees per square metre in a Category C or Category D agglomeration. An overall ceiling capped the total amount payable to any one person at two lakh rupees. Set against urban land values even in the late 1970s, these were not prices at which owners surrendered land willingly. A holder of a large plot in Bombay faced a statutory maximum measured in rupees per square metre for land whose market price was already many multiples of that figure, and the rational responses were the ones the record shows: contest the statement, contest the computation, claim an exemption, or simply never file. The Act's design assumed a level of voluntary disclosure that its own price schedule discouraged.

Disposal completed the circuit in theory. Land that vested was to be dealt with by the state government for the common good, and the Act contemplated its use for housing and other public purposes. In practice this last stage was the weakest of all, because disposal required the administering state to survey, plan, allot and build, and much vested land simply lay with the government, neither returned nor used, while the litigation over its taking continued around it. Appeals lay against the competent authority's orders, and the state government held a revisional power, so a determined holder could keep a file moving through the machinery for years. The circuit from statement to vesting to disposal, drawn on paper as a sequence of months, in operation ran in decades.

## The controls that surrounded the ceiling

A ceiling on holding would have been porous if owners remained free to deal with their land as they pleased, so Chapter IV of the Act wrapped the ceiling in a set of transaction controls, and it is those controls, as much as the ceiling itself, that froze the urban land market.

The most consequential was the exemption power. Section 20(1) empowered the state government, by order, to exempt vacant land from the ceiling chapter where it was satisfied that the exemption was necessary or expedient in the public interest, or that the application of the Act to the land would cause undue hardship to its owner, and it could attach conditions to the exemption it granted. Section 21 worked a related release from the other direction, providing that excess land would not be treated as excess where the holder undertook to use it for constructing dwelling units for the weaker sections of society, within the plinth and other limits the Act prescribed. Between the discretionary exemption and the housing-scheme carve-out, a very large share of the land the ceiling nominally captured was released back by decision of the administering government, on terms that varied from state to state and file to file.

### What did the Section 20 exemption power allow a state government to do?

It allowed the state government to lift the ceiling off a particular holding altogether, by order, where it was satisfied that exemption was necessary or expedient in the public interest, or that applying the Act would cause the owner undue hardship, and to impose conditions on the exemption it granted.

The breadth of that discretion is the point on which most assessments of the Act converge. Public interest and undue hardship are standards without edges, and the power to apply them plot by plot placed the real distribution of the ceiling's burden in the hands of the state executive rather than in the text Parliament had passed. Landowners with access and advice sought exemptions as a first resort rather than a last one. The exemption route also generated its own body of litigation, over refusals, over conditions, over exemptions granted to one holder and denied to a comparable neighbour, and over what happened when land exempted for a stated purpose was used for another. When the repeal came, the exemptions posed a problem of their own, because thousands of holdings rested on exemption orders whose validity landowners had arranged their affairs around, and Section 3 of the Repeal Act was drafted to protect them expressly.

Transfers were controlled in two tiers. Section 26 dealt with vacant land held within the ceiling limit: the holder could not transfer it by sale, mortgage, gift, lease or otherwise without first giving written notice of the intended transfer to the competent authority, and where the transfer was a sale, the competent authority held a first option to purchase the land on behalf of the state government at a price calculated under the land acquisition law. If the option was not exercised within sixty days of the notice, the holder was free to transfer to anyone, and where the option was exercised, the sale was to be completed and the price paid within three months. Section 27 dealt with the more valuable case of urban or urbanisable land with a building on it, or a portion of such land: no transfer by way of sale, mortgage, gift, a lease exceeding ten years or otherwise could be made at all except with the previous written permission of the competent authority, which again held a first option to purchase where a sale was proposed and permission was thought grantable. Section 28 closed the circuit at the registry, barring a registering officer from registering a transfer document unless the Section 26 notice had been given and its sixty days had run, or the Section 27 permission was produced.

The construction controls in Section 29 reached beyond landholding into the size of the housing itself, prohibiting the construction of any dwelling unit with a plinth area above three hundred square metres in a Category A or Category B agglomeration, with lower limits in the smaller categories. Read together, the chapters describe a statute that did not merely cap surplus land but supervised the entire life of an urban plot: what could be held, what could be sold, on what notice, with whose permission, and what could be built on what remained. Each control was defensible in isolation as an anti-avoidance measure, since a ceiling without transfer controls invites evasion by sale and fragmentation. Their combined effect, however, was to make every significant urban land transaction in the covered cities pass through a government office, with the delay, uncertainty and rent-seeking that such a design predictably produces. The penalties and offences provisions at the end of the Act, aimed at false statements and contraventions, completed a scheme whose enforcement burden fell on machinery that no state staffed at anything like the level the caseload required.

## The record: a ceiling that captured little and froze much

The verdict on the Act's working is one of the least contested in Indian legislative history, and it is a verdict on figures as much as on impressions. The pipeline from identification to acquisition leaked at every joint. Statements were filed for large areas of land, and competent authorities determined substantial extents as excess. Of the land determined excess, only a fraction was ever notified, vested and taken into possession, and of the land that did vest, only a fraction was disposed of for the housing purposes that justified the scheme. A published performance review of the Act, drawing on state figures, illustrates the proportions in one of the original states: in Andhra Pradesh, of some 7,596 hectares of vacant land within the scheme's view, about 2,466 hectares were acquired and about 1,176 hectares were used to create housing for the poor. The ratios differ from state to state, and the surviving figures vary by source and period, but the shape of the funnel is everywhere the same. The Act was far better at declaring land surplus than at acquiring it, and far better at acquiring it than at building on it.

Three structural causes account for most of that failure, and each was visible in the design from the start. The first was the price. An acquisition price capped at ten rupees a square metre in the largest cities, and at two lakh rupees in total for any one holder, guaranteed that nearly every vesting would be contested by any owner who could afford a lawyer, and urban landowners as a class could afford lawyers. The second was the exemption discretion, which diverted the most valuable holdings out of the pipeline by application rather than by adjudication, and which made the ceiling's incidence depend on access to the administering government. The third was the litigation load. Every stage of the circuit, the statement, the draft statement, the final statement, the notification, the declaration, the computation of the amount, the refusal or grant of exemption, was an appealable or challengeable act, and the files moved slowly through competent authorities, appellate authorities and the High Courts. Land caught in the circuit was land that could not be sold, developed or mortgaged with confidence, whatever the outcome eventually turned out to be.

The market consequence followed from the title consequence. Urban land is supplied to housing through transactions, and the Act taxed transactions with uncertainty at every point: the ceiling itself, the notice and option regime for land within the ceiling, the permission regime for land with buildings, and the standing possibility that a plot carried an undisclosed ceiling history. Owners withheld land from the market rather than expose it to the machinery, developers discounted or avoided plots with any ceiling taint, and the formal supply of developable land in the covered cities contracted. The housing shortage the Act was meant to relieve deepened, a failure it shared with rent control, the other great statutory intervention in urban housing markets, whose parallel history of frozen supply and distorted incentives is traced in the account of [rent control and the model tenancy framework](/2013/12/15/rent-control-model-tenancy-act-india/). Two statutes passed in the name of the urban tenant and the urban poor between them made urban housing scarcer and dearer, which is the central irony of the period's housing law.

The honest counter-reading deserves to be put at full strength, because the brief of this article requires it and because it is right about one important thing. The objective of the Act was not absurd. Urban land in a growing economy appreciates through no effort of its owner, the appreciation is created by the city's collective investment and growth, and the case for capturing some part of that unearned increment for public purposes, and for preventing the speculative withholding of serviced urban land, is a serious case that serious land economists have made in many countries. West Bengal's government, in refusing the repeal long after other states had taken it, stated the point in political form: without such a law, in its view, lower income people would be deprived of land. That position should be recorded as the government's stated reason, neither endorsed nor mocked. What the record condemns is not the aim but the instrument. A ceiling law works only if the state can identify the surplus, acquire it at a price owners will accept or a tribunal can defend, and put it to use faster than the market can route around the controls. The 1976 Act could do none of the three. Its price schedule made acquisition adversarial, its exemption power made the ceiling optional for the well connected, and its disposal stage barely existed as an administrative reality. The failure was a failure of machinery, and the states that resisted repeal were making a coherent point when they said that repeal without a replacement mechanism for supplying land to the poor simply abandons the objective. The repeal's defenders answer, also coherently, that an objective pursued through an instrument that worsens the underlying scarcity is not being pursued at all, and that the first step to a better instrument was to retire the harmful one. Both propositions can be true together, and on the evidence of the record, both are.

It is also worth being precise about the comparison with the acquisition law that ran alongside the ceiling. The Land Acquisition Act, 1894 took land project by project, for a stated public purpose, at a price the courts could examine against market value, and its defects, which were many and eventually fatal, were defects of fairness in particular takings. The framework set out in the guide to [land acquisition law in India](/2013/10/01/land-acquisition-law-india-guide/) shows that regime and its 2013 successor in detail. The ceiling Act was a different and more ambitious device: a standing, universal taking of a defined class of property, at formula prices, administered continuously. Its failure therefore carried a lesson the acquisition law's failure did not. India could operate episodic compulsory purchase, however imperfectly. What it could not operate, at the administrative capacity of the 1970s and 1980s, was a permanent redistribution machine for urban land.

## The Urban Land Ceiling Act repeal of 1999, and the second key

By the late 1990s the policy consensus at the Centre had turned. Urban reform thinking, in the years of economic liberalisation, treated the ceiling as a supply-side obstruction to be removed, and the Union government moved to repeal it. The manner of the moving is the point. The government did not simply introduce a repeal bill with general effect, because it could not. The statement of objects and reasons accompanying the repeal measure recorded the constraint in terms: Parliament has no power to repeal or amend the Act unless resolutions are passed by the legislatures of the states concerned, since the original Act rested on resolutions under Article 252. The repeal was therefore constructed to mirror the enactment. It would take effect at once where consent for repeal existed, and elsewhere only as consent arrived.

The repeal began as an ordinance, the Urban Land (Ceiling and Regulation) Repeal Ordinance, 1999, promulgated on 11 January 1999, and was then enacted by Parliament as the Urban Land (Ceiling and Regulation) Repeal Act, 1999, Act 15 of 1999, which received the assent of the President on 18 March 1999 and repealed and replaced the ordinance. The Act is five sections long, and its first section carries the whole constitutional story. Section 1(2) provides that the Repeal Act applies in the first instance to the whole of the states of Haryana and Punjab and to all the Union territories, and that it shall apply to any other state which adopts it by resolution passed in that behalf under clause (2) of Article 252. Section 1(3) deems it to have come into force in Haryana, Punjab and the Union territories on 11 January 1999, the ordinance date, and provides that in any other state it comes into force on the date of that state's adoption. Section 2 then performs the repeal in a single sentence: the Urban Land (Ceiling and Regulation) Act, 1976 is hereby repealed.

The choice of Haryana and Punjab as the first-instance states is itself instructive. They were the states whose legislatures had moved the resolutions of consent to repeal, so the repeal could bite there from the ordinance date. Every other state to which the 1976 Act applied, including the largest holders of ceiling litigation such as Maharashtra, Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal, stood outside the repeal until its own legislature passed the adopting resolution under Article 252(2). The Repeal Act on its face therefore announced not one commencement but a commencement mechanism, an open invitation to seventeen legislatures to switch off a central statute within their own borders at their own pace. Nothing in the Act set a deadline, and nothing in Article 252 allowed Parliament to set one. The pace was always going to be set by state politics, and state politics, for reasons the next sections take up, moved at very different speeds in different capitals.

### When did the ceiling law actually cease to operate in a given state?

On the date that state's legislature adopted the Repeal Act by resolution under Article 252(2), not on any single national date. Haryana, Punjab and the Union territories were covered from 11 January 1999; every other state was covered only from its own adoption date, years later in several cases.

| Extension to further states | Six more states adopted during 1976, taking the total to seventeen states | State by state through the following decade, Gujarat in 1999 and Maharashtra at the end of November 2007 among the dated instances |
| Position where consent never came | The Act never applied at all, as in Tamil Nadu, which legislated for itself in 1978 | The Act remained in force, administered and litigated, in states that did not adopt the repeal |

One further feature of the repeal's drafting deserves notice before the savings are examined, because it explains the litigation of the following decades. The Repeal Act did not merely switch the statute off going forward. It had to answer, for every state and every parcel, the question of what became of the enormous stock of unfinished ceiling business: statements filed and not decided, excess determined and not notified, land notified and not vested, land vested and not possessed, amounts assessed and not paid, exemptions granted and acted upon. Sections 3 and 4 of the Repeal Act answer those questions, and they answer them by drawing a single bright line through the stock, at the point of possession. Everything on one side of the line survived the repeal. Everything on the other side fell with it.

## What the repeal saved, and what it let fall

Section 3 of the Repeal Act is the savings clause, and its first limb protects the completed acquisitions. The repeal does not affect the vesting of any vacant land under Section 10(3) of the principal Act where possession of the land has been taken over by the state government, or by a person duly authorised by it, or by the competent authority. Land that had passed both gates, vesting and possession, stayed with the state. The former owner acquired no right to its return from the mere fact of repeal, and the many popular accounts that describe vested land as having been handed back in 1999 are wrong for exactly the class of land that mattered most to state governments, the land they actually held.

The second limb of Section 3(1) protects the exemption regime. The repeal does not affect the validity of any order granting exemption under Section 20(1) of the principal Act or any action taken under such an order, and it says so in the strongest form available to a draftsman, notwithstanding any judgment of any court to the contrary. Nor does the repeal disturb any payment made to the state government as a condition of such an exemption. The third limb, Section 3(2), handles the intermediate case with a draftsman's care: where land was deemed to have vested under Section 10(3) but possession had not been taken, and the state government had paid an amount for the land, the land is not to be restored until the amount paid has been refunded to the government. Vesting without possession, in other words, left the owner able to recover the land, but not to keep both the land and the state's money.

Section 4 then abates the unfinished business. All proceedings relating to any order made or purported to be made under the principal Act, pending immediately before the commencement of the Repeal Act before any court, tribunal or other authority, abate. The proviso carves out a single class: proceedings relating to Sections 11, 12, 13 and 14 of the principal Act, the provisions governing the amount payable for acquired land and the machinery around its determination, do not abate in so far as they relate to land of which possession had been taken. The architecture is coherent. Where the state had the land, the money questions could still be fought to a finish, because the taking was complete and only its price remained open. Where the state did not have the land, the entire proceeding died with the statute, and the owner's title emerged from under the ceiling with the file closed by operation of law rather than by any order in the owner's favour.

This account states the general position under the two Acts; whether a particular parcel stands vested, saved or freed turns on its own record of notifications, declarations and possession, and questions of forum and limitation in such matters are unforgiving, so a person whose land is caught in old ceiling proceedings needs advice on the specific file rather than on the statute in the abstract.

The litigation that followed the repeal organised itself, predictably, around the word possession. State governments that had completed the declaration stage under Section 10(3) but had not in fact dispossessed anyone argued that vesting alone saved their acquisitions. Owners argued that the savings clause protected only land genuinely taken over, and that paper vesting, mutation entries and ex parte possession memos were not taking over. The question reached the Supreme Court in State of Uttar Pradesh v Hari Ram, decided in March 2013, and the Court's answer drew the line where the text suggested it should be drawn. Vesting under Section 10(3), the Court held, is vesting of title, not of possession; the two deeming provisions in that sub-section, deemed acquisition and deemed vesting, operate on title and confer no right to de facto possession by themselves. To defeat the owner's claim to the benefit of the repeal, the state had to establish one of three things: voluntary surrender of the land before the Repeal Act took effect in that state, surrender and delivery of peaceful possession following the notice under Section 10(5), or forceful dispossession under Section 10(6). If it could establish none of them, the landowner took the benefit of Section 3 of the Repeal Act, and the proceedings abated. The Court reaffirmed the position the following year in Gajanan Kamlya Patil v Additional Collector and Competent Authority. Hari Ram is, in substance, the judiciary completing the repeal that the savings clause had left half finished: it read the line at possession as a line at actual possession, proved by the state's own record, and it thereby freed a large volume of land that had rested for decades under declarations nobody had acted on.

The exemption jurisprudence ran on a quieter track, largely because Section 3(1)(b) had been drafted to close it down. By protecting exemption orders notwithstanding any court judgment to the contrary, Parliament removed the most obvious line of attack on exemptions granted before repeal, and disputes instead concerned the conditions attached to particular exemptions, whether construction or use had conformed to them, and what a state government could demand, by way of premium or otherwise, from holders who wished to regularise exemption orders after the ceiling itself had gone. Those questions are state-specific in their answers, and they continue to matter commercially in cities such as Mumbai, where exemption orders under Section 20 underpin a significant stock of development. Their common feature is worth noting: they are questions about the consequences of the Act, arising under the Repeal Act's savings, and they will outlast the last living ceiling proceeding, because a saved exemption order shapes the title of the land it covers permanently.

## The long tail: adoption state by state

The adoption map after 1999 fell into three bands. In the first band were the states that moved quickly. Gujarat, an original resolving state in 1976, brought the repeal into force at the end of March 1999, within weeks of the central Act's assent. Through the following four years the repeal spread across the adopting states of the north and centre: by 2003, on the record assembled in studies of the repeal's economic effects, the Union territories and the states of Gujarat, Haryana, Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Orissa, Punjab, Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh had all repealed the ceiling law. These were, in the main, states whose governments accepted the urban reform argument, or whose ceiling machinery had always been comparatively light, or both.

In the second band stood Maharashtra, the state where the ceiling had bitten hardest and where the repeal mattered most. Maharashtra's cities, above all Bombay, renamed Mumbai, held the largest single stock of ceiling-affected land in the country, and the state government resisted adoption for years while its politics weighed the interests of builders, landowners, housing activists and the state's own large holdings of vested and exempted land. What finally moved Maharashtra was not persuasion but money, and the instrument deserves to be named precisely. In December 2005 the Union government launched the Jawaharlal Nehru National Urban Renewal Mission, a large programme of central assistance for urban infrastructure and governance, and it made repeal of the Urban Land (Ceiling and Regulation) Act, 1976 a mandatory reform at the state level for governments seeking Mission funds, one of a package of required reforms that also included rent control reform and the rationalisation of stamp duty. A state seeking Mission assistance had to commit to repeal within a committed timeframe. Maharashtra, with enormous infrastructure projects in Mumbai dependent on central assistance, adopted the Repeal Act by resolution of its legislature at the end of November 2007, more than eight years after the repeal had taken effect in Haryana and Punjab. Contemporary legal reporting described the repeal, accurately, as a precondition for the grant.

The conditionality deserves a moment of the even-handed attention this series requires, because it is the second constitutional interest in the story. On one view, funding conditionality of the JNNURM kind is a legitimate and familiar instrument of cooperative federalism. The Centre raises revenue nationally, states spend it locally, and a Union government that attaches reform conditions to its grants is doing no more than a lender does, with the added democratic warrant that the conditions themselves were public, uniform and directed at laws the Centre had already repealed on its own side. Maharashtra was free to refuse the money and keep the ceiling, and for eight years it effectively did. On the other view, the field in question was one the Constitution assigns to the states, the states' exclusive competence over land had already been qualified once by their own consent in 1976, and a financial lever applied by the Centre to procure the second consent sits uneasily with the spirit of the assignment. A poor state's choice between its infrastructure budget and its legislative autonomy is a constrained choice, and the constraint is deliberate. Both views can be held honestly, and the record supports elements of each: the reform conditions were transparent and general, and they also worked, which is to say they changed the behaviour of governments that argument alone had not persuaded. The ceiling repeal's history is therefore a small case study in how Indian federalism actually moves on a state subject: by resolution where conviction exists, and by conditional money where it does not.

In the third band stood the states that did not adopt. By 2008, published accounts record, every state to which the central ceiling Act applied had adopted the repeal except Assam, Bihar and West Bengal. Andhra Pradesh, long listed among the holdouts, completed its adoption in that period, leaving the three. West Bengal is the documented case, and its position was ideological in the precise sense that it rested on a stated theory of what the law was for. The state government prepared a draft for repeal in 2008 and did not proceed with it, and its stated reason, recorded earlier in this article, was that without such a law lower income people would be deprived of land. A government that had administered the ceiling as an instrument of redistribution for three decades declined to switch it off, and under Article 252(2) its declining was conclusive: the 1976 Act continued in force in West Bengal, its competent authorities continued to function, and property transactions in Kolkata and the other covered agglomerations of the state continued to be tested against a statute that Parliament had repealed in the year the youngest of the litigants then before the ceiling authorities was born. The position of the other non-adopting states is best described structurally rather than by list and date, because adoption notifications and their dates are inconsistently reported across sources, but the structural point is the same for each: the Act applied in those states because they had consented to it or adopted it in or after 1976, and it ceased to be capable of ending there until each legislature passed the second resolution.

The urban setting of the whole story is worth a closing note in this section, because the ceiling operated inside the weakest tier of Indian governance. The Constitution's urban local bodies, whose framework the account of the [74th Amendment and urban local bodies](/2018/11/01/74th-amendment-urban-local-bodies/) examines, plan land use and approve construction in the same cities where competent authorities administered the ceiling, and the two systems were never integrated: municipalities planned over land whose availability a different authority had frozen, and the master plans of the covered cities were drawn around a stock of plots whose legal status awaited the resolution of ceiling files. The rural comparison sharpens the point. The decentralisation examined in the account of the [73rd Amendment and panchayati raj](/2018/10/15/panchayati-raj-73rd-amendment-explained/) proceeded by constitutional amendment, uniformly and at once, because constitutional amendments bind every state on their own force. The ceiling's repeal, proceeding by consent under Article 252, bound no state until it chose to be bound. Two reforms of local India, two opposite mechanics of federal change, and the difference in their fortunes, one uniform and one ragged across two decades, is the difference between the amending power and the consent power in the Indian Constitution.

## Was the repeal an improvement?

The Template this framework supplies asks what the replacement kept, discarded and added, and the ceiling case tests the Template, because there was no replacement statute. What replaced the ceiling was not a new law but a decision, taken state by state, to govern urban land through the ordinary instruments of planning law, municipal regulation and the market. The analysis is still worth running in the Template's three moves, because the state's own savings provisions tell us what the repeal's authors thought was worth keeping.

What the repeal kept, deliberately and by text, was every completed act of the old regime. Vested and possessed land stayed with state governments, which is to say the one part of the distributive programme that had actually transferred land was preserved rather than unwound. Exemption orders stayed valid, and payments made for them stayed made, so the expectations built on the discretionary half of the old system were preserved alongside the acquisitions of its statutory half. Amount proceedings for possessed land stayed alive, so the state's obligation to settle the price of what it held survived the statute under which it had taken the land. The continuity is not accidental. A repeal that had attempted to reverse twenty-three years of vesting and exemption would have created a second generation of injustice to correct the first, compensating some owners twice over and expropriating the allottees and occupants who had taken vested land in good faith. The savings were the repeal's acknowledgement that the past of a bad law is still a past that people have built on.

What the repeal discarded was the entire prospective apparatus: the ceiling itself, the statement and determination machinery for the future, the notice and permission regime over transfers, the plinth controls on dwelling units, and the offence provisions that policed them. What it added, strictly, was nothing, and that absence is the strongest point in the case the resisting states made. No positive instrument for supplying urban land to lower income households took the ceiling's place. The housing response of the following years ran through other channels entirely, through mission funding, through municipal reform, through state housing boards and eventually through subsidy and regulation schemes of a wholly different design, none of which performed the specific function the ceiling had claimed, that of taking the scarcity rent of urban land for public use. Whether that function can be performed at all by a constitutional state with India's administrative capacity is the question the ceiling's career leaves open. The repeal answered the easier question, whether the 1976 instrument performed it, and on the record the answer was not in doubt.

The verdict on the repeal itself follows. As a piece of legislation it was well made: short, structurally honest about the constitutional constraint, and careful in its savings to distinguish the completed from the pending along the one line, possession, that fairness and administrability both supported. As a piece of reform it was incomplete in the manner this article has described, and its incompleteness was not a drafting failure but a constitutional property. Parliament could no more complete it than it could have enacted the original ceiling without consent. For the student of the Constitution, the episode is the cleanest demonstration available that Article 252 consent is a genuine transfer of power and not a formality, because it constrained a Parliament that wanted to repeal just as it had empowered a Parliament that wanted to enact. For the property owner, the practical residue is simpler: the ceiling law's ghost sits in the title history of urban land across India, and it is exorcised state by state, file by file, or, in the states that never adopted the repeal, not at all. Readers preparing examination answers on Article 252 will find the mechanism repays being learnt as a sequence of dated events rather than as an abstract clause, and the two routes tabulated above can be worked through as slides in the [ReportMedic presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) to fix the symmetry between enactment and repeal before testing it against questions.

## The courts and the ceiling: three decades of reading

The judicial history of the Act falls into three phases, and the phases track the statute's own life cycle: challenge to its existence, contest over its machinery, and finally contest over its ending.

The first phase was constitutional. The competence challenge was answered in Valluri Basavaiah Chowdhary in 1979, as described above, and the wider challenge to the scheme was answered by the Constitution Bench in Bhim Singhji in November 1980. The significance of Bhim Singhji for the repeal story is easy to overlook: the Bench's account of the Act's genesis dwells on the eleven resolutions and on the six adoptions that followed within 1976, treating the consent foundation not as a technicality but as the defining feature of the enactment. A court that understood the Act as a creature of state consent was laying down, in advance, the premise from which the repeal's limits would later be deduced. The Act that the Constitution Bench upheld was an Act that belonged, in a real sense, to the states jointly, and no later court had to struggle with the proposition that Parliament's repeal of it was territorially incomplete. The incompleteness was visible in the foundation documents all along.

The second phase was the long war over the machinery, and its characteristic product was not doctrine but volume. High Courts across the adopting states built up large bodies of case law on the working questions: what counted as vacant land where a building stood on part of a plot, how the land appurtenant to a building was to be measured, how a family was to be counted for the ceiling, how holdings through firms and companies were to be aggregated, and how the conversion ratios in Section 4 applied to scattered holdings. One illustration of the depth of that jurisprudence is the Supreme Court's engagement with Section 4(9), the provision governing a person who held vacant land together with land on which a building stood, in the group of appeals decided with State of Uttar Pradesh v L.J. Johnson, which worked through the interaction of the ceiling computation with built-up property across a batch of Uttar Pradesh cases. The point for the repeal is that each of these questions represented files, and the files numbered in the tens of thousands across the adopting states. When Section 4 of the Repeal Act abated pending proceedings, it closed not a handful of appeals but an entire stratum of Indian property litigation, and when it did so at different dates in different states, it closed that stratum at different depths in different places, which is why the repeal's practical meaning cannot be stated nationally.

Within that second phase, the exemption litigation formed a distinct strand, and the Andhra Pradesh litigation over the Hyderabad agglomeration shows its shape. The ceiling in Hyderabad stood at one thousand square metres as a Category B agglomeration, the amount payable for excess land was capped at ten rupees per square metre, and Section 20 stood behind every determination as the route of escape. Cooperative housing societies and landowners who had been refused exemption, or granted it on conditions they disputed, fought the competent authorities through the Andhra Pradesh High Court and beyond for years, and the courts were repeatedly required to restate the elementary architecture of the Act, the prohibition in Section 3, the caps in Section 11 and the discretion in Section 20, in case after case. The pattern repeated in every major covered city. Exemption jurisprudence under the Act is therefore best characterised at the level of propositions, which is how this article has treated it: the power was discretionary but not unreviewable, it had to be exercised on the statutory grounds of public interest or undue hardship rather than on extraneous favour, and a refusal or grant made without regard to the statutory grounds was vulnerable. Proposition-level treatment is not a stylistic preference here. It is the accurate description of a body of law that was made file by file, in state High Courts, over thousands of individual holdings.

The third phase, the litigation of the ending, has been described with the savings provisions above, and Hari Ram stands at its head. What Hari Ram settled, the High Courts applied for years afterwards in the adopting states, in a steady stream of cases where the whole dispute had narrowed to a single evidentiary question: could the state produce a record of actual taking of possession before the repeal reached that state? Panchnamas, mutation entries and possession memos were scrutinised file by file, and the outcomes turned on documents executed, or not executed, decades earlier by officials long retired. There is no better illustration of the series thesis that a statute is its whole record. The Repeal Act's fate was decided not in 1999 and not in Parliament, but in the quality of the possession paperwork that competent authorities had generated under the principal Act in the 1980s and 1990s.

## A single holding, followed through the machine

The machinery is easier to hold in mind if one holding is followed through it from beginning to end, using figures drawn entirely from the provisions already described. Take a holder in a Category B agglomeration, a city of more than ten lakh people, who in 1976 held three thousand square metres of vacant land within the agglomeration. The ceiling for that holder was one thousand square metres. Section 6 required a statement disclosing the whole holding, and in the statement the holder could specify which one thousand square metres were to be retained. The competent authority's draft statement under Section 8 would show two thousand square metres as excess, the holder could object, on valuation, on measurement, on the character of the land, on the claim that part of it was appurtenant to a building, and the final statement under Section 9 would settle the figure the state proposed to acquire.

Then came the holder's real choices, and they were the choices the whole landholding class faced. The first was exemption: an application under Section 20, arguing public interest, perhaps a proposed use, or undue hardship, and if the state government was persuaded, the ceiling lifted and the file closed with conditions. The second was the housing route under Section 21: an undertaking to construct dwelling units for weaker sections on the excess, within the prescribed limits, converting a confiscatory taking into a development obligation. The third was resistance: objections, appeal, revision, writ, while the land sat frozen, unsellable in practice because Section 26 and Section 27 controlled its transfer and any buyer would discount a plot carrying an open ceiling file. The fourth, chosen by many, was silence: no statement at all, in the hope that the machinery, which depended heavily on self-disclosure, would never arrive.

If none of those routes was taken and the state pressed on, the notification under Section 10(1) declared the two thousand square metres excess, the declaration under Section 10(3) vested them in the state government free from encumbrances, and the amount followed under Section 11. For land yielding no income, two thousand square metres at the maximum rate of ten rupees came to twenty thousand rupees, a sum that would not have purchased a small room in the same city, and the overall cap of two lakh rupees did not even come into play. The notice under Section 10(5) then required surrender of possession, and Section 10(6) authorised force if the holder refused. Every stage was lawful, every stage was documented, and the economic substance was a transfer of urban land at roughly the price of the paper it was recorded on. Holders were not imagining the confiscatory character of the scheme when they litigated it for decades, and governments were not imagining the distributive prize when they defended it. The repeal, when it came to that holder's state, applied the possession line to precisely this file: notice served and possession taken, the land stayed with the state and only the amount remained arguable; declaration made but possession never taken, the file abated and the land came back to the holder free of the ceiling, subject only to refunding any amount the state had paid.

That single trace explains a pattern that aggregate accounts often miss. The repeal did not divide landowners into winners and losers by wealth, connection or merit. It divided them by administrative accident, by whether a particular competent authority, in a particular decade, had completed a particular piece of paper. Two identical plots in the same city could emerge from the repeal in opposite conditions because one file had been worked to possession and the other had slept in a record room. Hari Ram's insistence on proved, actual possession, rather than presumed possession, was the judiciary's answer to that arbitrariness: if the line was to be possession, it would at least be real.

## Article 252 in the wider statute book

The ceiling Act is the most instructive use of Article 252 in the Indian statute book, but it is not the only one, and the comparisons sharpen what is distinctive about it. Parliament legislated on state subjects by consent in other fields in the same era. The Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974 was enacted on the strength of resolutions passed by state legislatures under Article 252, its preamble reciting the same constitutional route, and the Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972 likewise rested on resolutions of state legislatures inviting Parliament to legislate, wildlife and water both being fields where the states held the primary entry. The mechanism was, in the 1970s, a standard instrument of national standard-setting on state subjects, resorted to where uniformity was wanted and the Seventh Schedule stood in the way.

The comparisons cut in an interesting direction when the endings are examined. A consent-based statute that works, that creates institutions states value and standards states would struggle to set alone, never has to test the second key. Its repeal clause sleeps. The ceiling Act is the case where the second key had to be turned, repeatedly, in public, over a decade, and where the turning revealed the mechanism's asymmetry. Clause (1) consent, given at a moment of central strength, is easy to gather: in 1976 eleven legislatures resolved within a single political season, and six more followed within months. Clause (2) consent, given state by state at moments of each state's own choosing, is a slow drip spread across parliaments, elections and changes of government. The door opens fast and closes slowly. A constitution designer reading the episode would note that Article 252 contains no provision for a consenting state to withdraw, no sunset, no mechanism by which Parliament can tidy up a consent statute that every government concerned agrees has failed. The tidying took the form it took, an ordinance, a five-section Act, a mission's funding conditions and a Supreme Court judgment about possession, precisely because the text offered nothing tidier.

For the examination candidate, Article 252 rewards being distinguished from its neighbours with care, because the distinctions are exactly what gets tested. Article 249 empowers Parliament to legislate on a State List matter in the national interest on a Rajya Sabha resolution, for a limited period, without state consent. Article 250 empowers it during an Emergency. Article 253 empowers it to implement international obligations regardless of the lists. Article 252 alone rests on the consent of the governed states themselves, and alone carries the consequence that the resulting law can be amended or repealed only through Parliament acting in the like manner, placing the subject beyond the reach of the state legislature that consented. The ceiling repeal is the story that makes that abstract proposition memorable: the state that consented in 1976, discovered it could not legislate in 1990, and found in 1999 that even Parliament's repeal needed its resolution to arrive.

## What the states did with the land they kept

The savings provisions preserved the state's holdings, and a complete account of the repeal has to ask what became of them, because the answer completes the verdict on the original scheme. Vested land in the covered cities was, in principle, a public land bank assembled for housing and public purposes. In practice its fate was governed by the same administrative limits that had governed acquisition. Parcels were allotted in fragments, for housing schemes, for public institutions, for the weaker sections housing that exemption schemes under Section 20 had also promised, and a substantial residue simply remained on government books, its status recorded in revenue registers, its use deferred. Maharashtra's experience, the best documented because the sums and the litigation were the largest, shows the pattern continuing past the repeal itself: exemption orders under Section 20 remained commercially alive after 2007, and the state government in later years moved to levy a one-time premium for the regularisation of exemption orders, a policy that generated its own round of dispute. A repeal that preserves exemptions preserves, with them, a continuing administrative relationship between the state and the exempted holder, conditions to be monitored and premiums to be charged, so that in the exempted sector the ceiling regime did not end so much as convert into a fiscal one.

There is a general lesson in that residue for the design of redistribution statutes, and it is the lesson the series has drawn from several of the statutes in this cluster. The state is usually better at the prohibitory stages of such a scheme than at the dispositive ones. Declaring a ceiling, freezing transfers, vesting title: these are acts of authority that a gazette notification performs completely. Disposing of the acquired asset, building on it, allotting it to the intended beneficiaries, defending the allotment, maintaining the record: these are acts of administration that must be performed continuously for decades. The ceiling Act's career is a thirty-year demonstration of the gap between the two capacities, and the repeal's savings, by preserving the state's dispositive obligations while abolishing its prohibitory powers, left the harder half of the scheme in force after the easier half had gone. Landowners whose plots were freed by abatement recovered them in a market transformed by the intervening decades. State governments that kept their vested land kept, with it, the unfinished business of the 1976 design.

## The drafting of the repeal, read closely

The Repeal Act rewards the close reading its brevity invites, because every one of its five sections is doing constitutional work, and two of them are doing more than a first glance suggests.

Section 1 has been described: application in the first instance to Haryana, Punjab and the Union territories, extension by adoption under Article 252(2), and a deemed commencement on 11 January 1999 for the first-instance territories. The deeming is the section's quiet achievement. The repeal had begun life as an ordinance on that January date, and landowners, competent authorities and courts had acted on the ordinance's footing for the weeks before Parliament enacted the replacement. By deeming the Act to have come into force on the ordinance date in the first-instance territories, Section 1(3) preserved the continuity of everything done in the interval and fixed a single date from which possession, the savings line, would be tested in those territories. Commencement dates in repeal litigation are rarely elegant. This one was, in the territories it covered at once. In the adopting states the same deeming could not operate, and the operative date became the adoption date, which is why the possession inquiry in Hari Ram was run against 18 March 1999, the date the Repeal Act received assent, when the question reached the Court in the Uttar Pradesh appeals, and why practitioners in each adopting state learned to plead their own state's date as the first fact of any ceiling case.

Section 2, the repeal itself, is a single sentence and contains a deliberate choice. It repeals the principal Act without qualification, and leaves the entire work of qualification to Sections 3 and 4. The alternative drafting, a repeal hedged with provisos in the repealing section itself, would have invited argument about whether a given consequence flowed from the repeal section or the savings. The chosen structure makes the analysis mechanical: the statute is gone; the only questions are the savings questions, and they are exhausted by the two sections that follow. Section 3, as set out above, saves vesting with possession, exemption validity, exemption payments, and conditions restoration of vested-but-unpossessed land on refund. Section 4 abates pending proceedings, save for the amount provisions for possessed land. Section 5 repeals the ordinance and saves everything done under it, the standard closing of an ordinance-replacement Act.

What the Act does not contain is as significant as what it does. There is no provision compelling a state to adopt, none could have been effective if written. There is no provision dissolving the competent authorities or disposing of their records, matters left to the administering states, which is why ceiling records survive in state archives and continue to be produced in litigation. There is no provision for the return of statements, the cancellation of notifications in the revenue record, or the cleansing of titles, and the absence of a title-cleansing mechanism is the direct cause of the practical problem examined in the next section: a plot freed by abatement is freed in law, but the revenue and registration records that a buyer searches may carry the ceiling proceedings on their face for years, until someone takes the trouble to have the entries corrected. And there is no replacement scheme, for the reasons the assessment section gave. The Act is a repeal and nothing else, honestly drafted for a constitution that allowed nothing more uniform.

## Reading a title after the ceiling

For the owner or buyer of urban land in a formerly covered city, the repeal's legacy is a set of questions that a competent title investigation asks in a fixed order, and they are worth setting out because they are the form in which most readers will ever meet this statute.

The first question is territorial: did the ceiling Act apply to the city at all, and has the repeal been adopted in that state? Land in a Tamil Nadu city was governed by that state's own 1978 Act, with its own repeal history, not by the central Act or the 1999 repeal. Land in West Bengal stood under the central Act without the shelter of the 1999 repeal for as long as the state withheld adoption. Land in a city below the two-lakh population threshold of the First Schedule was never within the ceiling at all, whatever its later growth.

The second question is staged: if proceedings were ever begun against the plot or its predecessors in title, how far did they travel? A statement filed and never pursued, a draft statement objected to and never finalised, a final statement never followed by notification: all of these abated when the repeal reached the state. A notification and declaration followed by proved possession: the land was the state's, and a private title derived from the former owner after that point is built on nothing. A declaration without possession: the Hari Ram territory, where the answer depends on the possession record and, where the state paid an amount, on refund. An exemption order: saved by Section 3(1)(b), and the inquiry shifts to the order's conditions and whether the use of the land conforms to them.

The third question is documentary: what do the revenue records and the registration record show, and do they match the legal position the first two questions produce? The common practical defect is a mismatch, an old notification or a ceiling remark carried in the record long after the proceeding that generated it abated, deterring buyers and lenders who read the entry without reading the repeal. The remedy is administrative, an application to correct the record on the strength of the Repeal Act and, where possession is disputed, the Hari Ram standard, but it must be sought; no provision of the Repeal Act performs it automatically. This is the precise sense in which the statute still affects titles decades after its repeal in most of India. The law ended on a date. The paperwork ends file by file, at the pace of the people who need it ended.

## The ceiling among the land reforms

A final comparison places the Act in its family. Twentieth-century India legislated land ceilings in two theatres. In the rural theatre, the states acted for themselves under their own competence: zamindari abolition first, then agricultural land ceiling laws of the 1960s and 1970s, state by state, at state ceilings, with state exemptions and state litigation, protected in the constitutional battles of the period by devices including the Ninth Schedule. In the urban theatre, the states could not so easily act in concert, and the political demand of the 1970s was for a uniform national instrument, which is why the urban ceiling arrived by the Article 252 route with a single text for seventeen states. The rural laws were amended, diluted and in places repealed by the same legislatures that had passed them, in the ordinary way. The urban law could be ended only by the double mechanism this article has described. The comparison therefore isolates the true cost of the 1976 choice of route. Uniformity was bought at the price of reversibility. A state that later regretted its agricultural ceiling law could repeal it in a session. A state that regretted the urban ceiling had to petition, in effect, for the Centre to repeal it, and then resolve to accept the repeal, and the states that never completed those steps kept the law.

The family resemblance in outcomes is equally instructive. In both theatres, the ceiling statutes declared far more land surplus than they acquired, generated litigation out of proportion to their redistributive yield, and were undone less by their formal endings than by the slow recognition that the administrative state could not run them. The urban ceiling's distinction is that its formal ending is the most interesting of the family's, and the most instructive for anyone who drafts, studies or litigates Indian legislation: a repeal whose text is five sections long and whose completion occupied a decade, two constitutional clauses, a national funding mission and a Constitution Bench's worth of accumulated doctrine about a single word, possession.

## The argument over the repeal, as it was made

Because this series' stance charter requires contested questions to be put at their strongest from each side, the political argument of 1998 and 1999 deserves a fuller statement than the summary given in the assessment above. The repeal was a live political question, not a technicality, and it was argued in those terms in Parliament, in the state legislatures that debated adoption, and in the urban policy literature of the period.

The case for repeal, as the Union government and the urban reform community made it, rested on the record rather than on ideology, and it is important to the honesty of the case that it did so. Its first proposition was empirical: the Act had not redistributed urban land at any meaningful scale in twenty-three years, and the gap between surplus declared and land acquired and used was so wide that the statute could not be defended as a working instrument of its own stated purpose. Its second proposition was economic: the ceiling, the transfer controls and the litigation cloud together suppressed the formal supply of urban land, raised prices, pushed transactions into informality and penalised exactly the housing construction the cities needed. Its third proposition was administrative: the competent authority machinery consumed state capacity to produce files rather than housing, and its discretion points, above all the exemption power, had become a standing invitation to corruption that no vigilance machinery had succeeded in policing. Its fourth proposition was federal in a constructive sense: housing and land are state subjects in substance, and states that wished to experiment with land pooling, development charges or inclusionary housing should be free to do so under their own planning law rather than under a uniform prohibition written in 1976. On that case, repeal was not the abandonment of the equity objective but the removal of an obstacle to pursuing it by workable means.

The case against repeal, as the Left parties, housing rights organisations and the resisting state governments made it, also rested on propositions that deserve their full force. Its first proposition was about the nature of the failure: the Act had been sabotaged in operation by the very interests it targeted, through exemptions granted by complaisant state governments, litigation funded by those who could afford it, and administrative starvation, and a state that repeals a sabotaged law rewards the sabotage. Its second proposition was about consequence: whatever the Act's failures, its existence placed a ceiling of law over the speculative assembly of urban land, and its removal, with no successor instrument, would be read by the market as a signal that urban land was open for unrestricted accumulation, with predictable effects on prices and on the displacement of the poor from well-located land. Its third proposition was about the destination of vested and exempted land: repeal politics in the states, its critics predicted, would be followed by pressure to release the state's ceiling-acquired land bank and to relax exemption conditions in favour of commercial development, converting a flawed redistribution scheme's assets into a windfall for the development industry. Its fourth proposition was the federal one in the other direction: the manner of the repeal's promotion, culminating in the funding conditions of the following decade, treated state legislatures' judgment on a state subject as an obstacle to be purchased rather than a position to be answered. West Bengal's stated reason for holding out, that without such a law lower income people would be deprived of land, is the compressed political form of the second of these propositions.

The evidence assembled since permits a judgment between the factual parts of those cases, and this article gives it without hedging. On the record of acquisition and use, the repeal case was right and was never seriously answerable: the instrument had failed at its own purpose by an order of magnitude. On the market effects of the ceiling, the repeal case was also right in substance: a control structure that taxes every legal transaction and freezes title suppresses supply, and the supply response in repealing states, visible in the regularisation and development of long-frozen plots, is consistent with it. On the third critical proposition, the destination of the state's ceiling assets, the critics identified a genuine and continuing risk, and the post-repeal disputes over exemption conditions, premiums and the use of vested land show the risk was not imaginary; the savings provisions protected the state's holdings in law, but what a state does with a land bank across decades is a political question the Repeal Act did not and could not settle. On the federal proposition, the two views remain what they were, a disagreement about the proper instruments of a union, which evidence cannot close. A verdict that distinguishes those strands, the factual settled, the predictive partly vindicated, the federal genuinely open, is the accurate one, and it is more useful to every class of reader of this article than a blanket commendation or condemnation of the repeal.

## The exemption sector after repeal

One corner of the afterlife deserves separate treatment because it is where the old Act continues to make law in the repealing states. Section 3(1)(b) of the Repeal Act saved every exemption order under Section 20(1) and every action taken under one, notwithstanding any court judgment to the contrary. An exemption order was never a simple waiver. It was typically granted for a purpose, a specified development, a housing scheme for weaker sections, an institutional use, a layout of a specified character, and it carried conditions about the time within which the purpose was to be executed, the proportion of the land or the built space to be devoted to the weaker sections component, and the transferability of the exempted land. Saving the orders therefore saved the conditions, and saved the state government's power to insist on them, after the ceiling that had made the orders necessary had disappeared.

The consequences unfold in a pattern that recurs across the repealing states. A holder who built in conformity with the exemption holds a clean position: the exemption is valid, the action under it is protected, and the repeal has removed the only cloud, the ceiling itself. A holder who never executed the exempted purpose holds land whose status depends on the state's reading of its own order: the government may treat the exemption as lapsed or liable to cancellation for breach of condition, and the holder will argue that the savings clause froze the position in his favour. A holder who wishes to change the use of exempted land, to build differently from the scheme the exemption sanctioned, needs the state's consent to vary the conditions, and states have learned to price that consent, Maharashtra's one-time premium for the regularisation of exemption orders being the prominent instance. Each of these postures has generated disputes, and the disputes share a structure: they are not ceiling cases, because the ceiling is gone, but they cannot be understood without the ceiling, because every document in the file was created by it.

This is the deepest sense in which the repeal remains unfinished business even in the states that adopted it promptly. A statute can be switched off. A body of administrative decisions made under it, on which purchasers, lenders, occupants and governments have relied, cannot be switched off with it, and the savings clauses of a repeal are the point at which the legal system states, expressly, which parts of the past it intends to carry forward. The 1999 Act carried forward the vesting and the exemptions, the two halves of the old regime's output, and abandoned the machinery and the proceedings. Litigants and legislatures will be working out the consequences of that choice for as long as urban land in the covered cities changes hands, which is to say indefinitely.

## Two clocks: commencement dates and the archaeology of a case

Every statute in this series runs on a clock, and most run on one: a date of assent, a date of commencement, a date of repeal. The ceiling Act ran on many clocks at once, and its repeal added a second set. For the original states, the appointed day was the date the Bill was introduced in Parliament, a date earlier than assent, chosen so that transactions made after the Bill's introduction but before its enactment could not defeat the scheme. For each adopting state, the appointed day was its own date of adoption. Commencement followed the same pattern. A lawyer opening a ceiling file therefore had to establish, before reaching any question of substance, which of several possible statutory timelines governed the land: the territory's appointed day, its commencement date, the dates of the statement, the draft, the final statement, the notification and the declaration, and, after 1999, the territory's repeal date, against which the possession question would be tested. Two files concerning adjoining plots in different states could be governed by repeal dates a decade apart. Two files in the same state could be governed by different outcomes because one holder had filed his statement early and been processed to possession in the 1980s while his neighbour's file had been opened late and was still in the draft statement stage when the repeal arrived.

This multiplicity is more than a practitioner's nuisance. It is the reason the ceiling episode resists every one-sentence summary that Indian public discussion attempts of it, including the sentence that the Act was repealed in 1999. That sentence is true of the statute book at the Centre and false of the law in force in Kolkata for years afterwards, incomplete for Mumbai until the end of 2007, and incomplete in a different way even in Delhi, where the repeal was immediate but the savings kept its consequences in force. Accurate writing about this Act is forced, by its constitutional structure, into the state-by-state and file-by-file mode this article has adopted. The same discipline is the article's practical payload for the researcher: in ceiling matters, the first research task is never the section. It is the calendar.

There is a corresponding lesson for the legislator and the staffer, the first class of readers on this series' map. Consent-based legislation under Article 252 should be drafted, at its birth, with its death in contemplation. A sunset clause negotiated at enactment, a review mechanism, an agreed procedure for coordinated repeal, any of these would have spared the system the improvisation of 1999 to 2007. The 1976 Parliament, legislating in the Emergency's climate of permanence, wrote none of them. The 1999 Parliament, repealing in the liberalisation era's climate of confidence that markets needed no successor instrument, wrote no transitional institution either, no tribunal to wind up the files, no title-cleansing drive, no deadline discipline for the states. The files were left to abate or survive by the operation of two sections and to be sorted out by the High Courts over the following decades. That they were sorted out as coherently as they were is principally the judiciary's achievement, and Hari Ram's possession doctrine is its centrepiece: a single, evidentially workable test, applied uniformly, that converted a ragged constitutional transition into a rule a record room could administer.

The series thesis can be stated for this statute in its final form. The Urban Land (Ceiling and Regulation) Act, 1976 is remembered as a failed instrument of redistribution, and so it was. But its repeal is the more durable contribution to Indian legislative studies, because it demonstrates, with a completeness no hypothetical could match, that in a federation the power to end a law is as distributed as the power to make it, and that a Parliament which borrows its competence from the states borrows the terms of surrender as well. The ceiling was imposed by consent, administered by the consenting, and lifted by consent, slowly, unevenly, and in a few places never. Any future Parliament tempted to solve a state-subject problem by collecting resolutions under Article 252 should be required to read the repeal first.

## The ceiling and the career of the property right

One further piece of context explains the most striking number in the Act, the ten rupees a square metre, and it lies in the constitutional history of property. The ceiling Act was drafted in the long shadow of the compensation wars between Parliament and the Supreme Court. The original Article 31(2) had required compensation for acquisition, the courts had read that word to import a just equivalent, and Parliament had answered with successive amendments, culminating in the Twenty-fifth Amendment, which substituted the word amount for compensation and barred challenge to a law on the ground that the amount fixed was inadequate. Section 11 of the ceiling Act is written in that amended vocabulary. It does not promise compensation. It prescribes an amount, computed by formula, and the formula's parsimony was constitutionally deliberate: the draftsman of 1976 believed, on the state of the text after the Twenty-fifth Amendment, that the adequacy of the amount was substantially beyond judicial correction. Bhim Singhji was argued and decided in that world, and the Act's survival there is unintelligible without it.

Two years after the Act, the ground shifted. The Constitution (Forty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1978 deleted Article 31 altogether and removed the right to property from the fundamental rights, re-enacting it as the constitutional right in Article 300A, under which no person may be deprived of property save by authority of law. The ceiling Act thus lived most of its life in a constitutional order different from the one that had designed its price schedule: its amounts had been fixed on the assumption that adequacy was unreviewable, and they survived into an era in which the operative protection was the thinner, though real, guarantee against deprivation without legal authority. The repeal litigation of the 2000s and 2010s, fought over possession and savings rather than over price, reflects that shift. Owners who in 1978 might have attacked the amount itself instead attacked, two decades later, the completeness of the state's taking. The property right's career, from fundamental right with a compensation guarantee to constitutional right of legality, runs underneath the ceiling's career at every stage, and the examination candidate who grasps the connection holds the two stories in the form in which they are most profitably remembered: as one story, told in the vocabulary of amount.

The connection also disciplines the counterfactual that hovers over the repeal debate. It is sometimes asked whether a ceiling Act with market-value compensation could have worked. The question answers itself on the fiscal side: a state that paid market value for all urban land above five hundred square metres in its largest cities would have committed sums no budget of the period could carry, and an acquisition programme funded at that level would have been a purchase programme rather than a redistribution. The cheapness of the taking was not an incidental defect of the design. It was the design's enabling condition, and it is also the direct cause of the resistance, litigation and non-disclosure that consumed the scheme. Redistribution of a valuable, visible, individually held asset, at administered prices, through self-disclosure, is a formula that the Indian record of the period tested to destruction in the urban theatre just as its rural counterpart was being tested in the agricultural one. The repeal of 1999 closed the experiment. The questions it was built to answer, about unearned increment, speculation and the housing of the urban poor, remain open, and are being answered, where they are answered at all, by instruments of taxation, planning gain and subsidy that owe nothing to the ceiling idea except the caution of its example.

## The definitions that did the quiet work

Statutes of this kind are usually decided in their definitions, and the ceiling Act is no exception. Section 2's definition of vacant land carried the scheme. Vacant land meant land, not being land mainly used for the purpose of agriculture, in an urban agglomeration, and it excluded three things: land on which construction of a building was not permissible under the building regulations in force, land occupied by a building constructed or being constructed on the appointed day with the approval of the appropriate authority together with the land appurtenant to it, and, in areas without building regulations, land occupied by a building constructed or being constructed on the appointed day with its appurtenant land. Each element generated its own jurisprudence. The agricultural exclusion required a test of main use, applied to plots on the urban fringe that were farmed in form and held for development in substance, and competent authorities and courts spent years deciding whether a given parcel at a city's edge was agricultural land outside the Act or vacant land within it, a question on which revenue entries, actual cultivation and the land's situation in the master plan all pulled in different directions. The appurtenant land exclusion required a judgment about how much land around a building was appurtenant to it, and therefore outside the ceiling, and how much was surplus. The construction-prohibition exclusion took the Act's reach outside itself, tying it to the building regulations of each city, so that land frozen by planning law was not double-counted as ceiling surplus.

The definition of family and the treatment of the Hindu undivided family produced another characteristic line of dispute. The Act defined the persons it bound, individuals, families, firms, companies and associations, and the computation of a family's ceiling, the treatment of adult sons and of joint holdings, and the question whether a Hindu undivided family was to be treated as a single person or through its members, were argued across the High Courts with outcomes that mattered enormously to the families concerned, since the difference between one ceiling and several was the difference between keeping a plot and losing it. None of this definitional matter survives in the text of the Repeal Act, which contains no definitions at all. It survives in the files, because the savings and abatement questions cannot be answered for any real plot without first reconstructing how the definitions had been applied to it. The repeal is five sections long. The law it repealed is present, in every contested title, at the full length of its definitions chapter. That disproportion, between the brevity of an ending and the mass of what has to be understood to apply it, is the final practical lesson of the episode, and it generalises: in Indian legislation, repeals are short and afterlives are long.

## The capital and the Union territories

Delhi's experience forms a useful control case against which the states' story can be measured. The capital was a Category A agglomeration with the tightest ceiling of five hundred square metres, and as a Union territory it stood in both the 1976 Act and the Repeal Act at the first instance, without any resolution being needed at either end, since Parliament legislates for the Union territories directly. The repeal therefore reached Delhi completely and at once, deemed in force on 11 January 1999, and the ceiling's long Delhi afterlife has been purely a savings afterlife: vested land retained, exemption orders honoured, unfinished files closed by abatement on a single known date. The contrast with Mumbai, where eight further years of live ceiling law intervened, and with Kolkata, where the law lived on, isolates the variable that explains the whole episode. Delhi's tidier ending was not the product of better administration or weaker interests. It was the product of constitutional status. Where Parliament held the power directly, the repeal was an ordinary repeal. Where it held the power on loan from the states, the repeal was a negotiation. The ceiling Act's twenty-three years and its repeal's decade are, in the end, an extended footnote to that single distinction.

The same control case explains why the Union government's own conduct after 1999 showed no impatience with the slow states. The Centre had its repeal where its power ran. What remained outstanding lay in territories where the Constitution required it to wait, and the only instruments available to shorten the wait were persuasion and, through the Mission, price. It chose price, and the price worked in Maharashtra within two years of the Mission's launch, which suggests that the obstacle in the slow states was never administrative incapacity but political valuation: the ceiling, for all its failures, still signified something in state politics that governments surrendered only when the compensation for surrendering it was denominated in infrastructure funds. A statute that began as an emblem of one era's distributive politics ended as a bargaining counter in another era's fiscal federalism, and the exchange rate was set in Delhi.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What limits did the Urban Land Ceiling Act impose?

The Urban Land (Ceiling and Regulation) Act, 1976 capped how much vacant land any person could hold in the 64 urban agglomerations listed in its First Schedule. Section 4 fixed the ceiling by category of agglomeration: five hundred square metres in Category A, comprising Delhi, Bombay, Calcutta and Madras; one thousand square metres in Category B; one thousand five hundred in Category C; and two thousand in Category D. A person included an individual, a family, a firm, a company or an association. Holdings spread across categories were aggregated using conversion ratios, so one square metre in Category A counted as four in Category D. Land above the ceiling was liable to be acquired by the state government on payment of a statutorily computed amount.

### Q: Why was the Urban Land Ceiling Act enacted under Article 252?

Because Parliament had no power of its own to enact it. Land, with rights in or over it, is Entry 18 of the State List, so a ceiling on urban land was a subject for the state legislatures alone. Article 252(1) of the Constitution allows Parliament to legislate on a state subject where the legislatures of two or more states pass resolutions asking it to do so. Eleven states, Andhra Pradesh, Gujarat, Haryana, Himachal Pradesh, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Orissa, Punjab, Tripura, Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal, passed such resolutions, and the Act's own preamble records them as the source of Parliament's power. The Act applied in the first instance to those states and all Union territories, and later to states that adopted it by the same route.

### Q: Did the Urban Land Ceiling Act achieve its objectives?

On the record, no, except in fragments. The Act was meant to break up concentrated holdings of urban land, check speculation and release land for housing the poor. What it achieved at scale was the identification of excess land on paper. Of the land determined excess, only a fraction was notified, vested and taken into possession, and a smaller fraction still was used for housing. A published performance review records that in Andhra Pradesh about 2,466 hectares were acquired out of some 7,596 hectares within the scheme's view, and about 1,176 hectares were used for housing the poor. The exemption power diverted valuable holdings, the formula price guaranteed litigation, and the freezing of transactions deepened the housing shortage the Act was meant to relieve.

### Q: When was the Urban Land Ceiling Act repealed?

Parliament repealed it by the Urban Land (Ceiling and Regulation) Repeal Act, 1999, Act 15 of 1999, which received presidential assent on 18 March 1999 and replaced an ordinance of 11 January 1999. But there is no single repeal date for the whole country. The Repeal Act was deemed in force on 11 January 1999 in Haryana, Punjab and all Union territories, the territories covered in the first instance. In every other state it took effect only when that state's legislature adopted it by resolution under Article 252(2). Gujarat followed within weeks in 1999; Maharashtra adopted it at the end of November 2007; and a few states did not adopt it at all, so the ceiling law's operative life ended on different dates in different states.

### Q: Why is the Urban Land Ceiling Act still in force in some states?

Because repeal under Article 252 requires the consent of each state to which the original Act applied. Article 252(2) provides that an Act passed on state resolutions can be amended or repealed, for a state, only by Parliament acting in the like manner, which in practice means the state legislature must pass a resolution adopting the repeal. Parliament's Repeal Act of 1999 therefore operated in a state only from that state's adoption. States that never passed the adopting resolution kept the 1976 Act in force, with its competent authorities, statements and proceedings intact. West Bengal is the prominent case: its government stated that without such a law lower income people would be deprived of land, prepared a repeal draft in 2008, and did not proceed with it.

### Q: What happened to land already vested under the ceiling law?

It depends on whether the state had also taken possession. Section 3 of the Repeal Act saved the vesting of vacant land under Section 10(3) of the 1976 Act where possession had been taken over by the state government or the competent authority, so such land stayed with the state and was not restored to the former owner. Where land had been declared vested but possession had never been taken, the owner could recover it, subject to refunding any amount the state had paid. In State of Uttar Pradesh v Hari Ram (2013), the Supreme Court held that vesting under Section 10(3) transferred title, not possession, and that the state had to prove voluntary surrender, delivery of possession after notice under Section 10(5), or forceful dispossession under Section 10(6), failing which the owner took the benefit of the repeal.

### Q: Did the urban ceiling law worsen housing shortages?

The weight of the record says yes. The Act was intended to release land for housing the poor, but its combined controls worked in the opposite direction. The ceiling itself, the notice and first-option regime for transfers of land within the ceiling under Section 26, the permission regime for land with buildings under Section 27, and the litigation cloud over any plot with a ceiling history together discouraged owners from bringing land to the formal market and discouraged developers from touching affected plots. Formal supply contracted while demand grew, prices rose, and transactions moved into informal channels. Very little vested land was in fact built on for the poor. The parallel with rent control, the era's other major housing intervention, is close: two statutes enacted for the urban tenant between them made urban housing scarcer.

### Q: Can Parliament repeal a law passed under Article 252 on its own?

Not with full effect in the states that consented to the original law. Parliament can pass the repealing Act, as it did in 1999, but Article 252(2) structures what that Act can do: the repeal takes effect in a consenting or adopting state through the same consent mechanism by which the original Act reached that state, meaning a resolution of that state's legislature adopting the repeal. Parliament also cannot be compelled by a state to repeal, and a state cannot repeal or amend the central Act by its own legislation at all. The result is a double lock. The Union holds the repealing text; each state holds the key to its own territory. The ceiling law's history, repealed at the Centre in 1999 and operative in places for years afterwards, is the working proof of that arrangement.

### Q: How much land was actually acquired under the Urban Land Ceiling Act compared with the land declared excess?

Far less at each stage of the pipeline. Statements and final statements identified large extents of excess vacant land across the covered cities, but the machinery then leaked heavily: objections, appeals and writs stalled files, exemption applications diverted holdings, and many notifications were never followed by vesting declarations or by possession. Published reviews of the Act's performance consistently record the same proportions in state after state, with acquisition running at a small fraction of the excess determined and actual use for housing at a fraction of that again. The Andhra Pradesh figures are illustrative: about 2,466 hectares acquired against some 7,596 hectares within the scheme's view, and about 1,176 hectares used to create housing for the poor. Precise national totals vary by source and by the year at which they were compiled, and should be treated with care.

### Q: What did the Section 20 exemption power allow a state government to do?

Section 20(1) of the 1976 Act allowed the state government, by order, to exempt any vacant land from the ceiling provisions where it was satisfied that the exemption was necessary or expedient in the public interest, or that applying the Act would cause undue hardship to the owner, and it could impose conditions on the exemption. In practice the power became a parallel, discretionary ceiling system. Owners applied for exemption as a first resort, governments granted or refused plot by plot, and housing schemes for weaker sections were sanctioned under exemption orders across the major cities. The breadth of the two grounds, public interest and undue hardship, left the real incidence of the ceiling to executive discretion. The Repeal Act protected the exemption regime expressly, saving the validity of Section 20(1) orders and everything done under them notwithstanding any court judgment to the contrary.

### Q: What happened to pending ceiling proceedings when a state adopted the repeal?

They abated. Section 4 of the Repeal Act provides that all proceedings relating to any order made or purported to be made under the 1976 Act, pending immediately before the repeal took effect in that state before any court, tribunal or other authority, shall abate. A landowner whose statement, objection, appeal or writ was still pending when the repeal arrived therefore had the file closed by operation of law, and the land stood free of the ceiling without any order in the owner's favour. One exception was carved out: proceedings under Sections 11, 12, 13 and 14 of the principal Act, which concern the amount payable for acquired land, survived in so far as they related to land of which the state had taken possession, because the taking was complete and only its price remained to be settled.

### Q: How did JNNURM funding conditions push states to repeal the ceiling Act?

The Jawaharlal Nehru National Urban Renewal Mission, launched in December 2005, made repeal of the Urban Land (Ceiling and Regulation) Act, 1976 a mandatory reform at the state level for governments seeking Mission assistance for urban infrastructure. States signing a memorandum of agreement with the Union government committed themselves to a reform agenda with timelines, and repeal of the ceiling Act stood on that agenda alongside rent control reform and the rationalisation of stamp duty. The mechanism was fiscal rather than legal: Article 252 prevented the Centre from repealing the Act for a state, but nothing prevented the Centre from pricing its grants. Maharashtra's adoption of the repeal at the end of November 2007, after eight years of resistance, was reported contemporaneously as a precondition for Mission grants supporting major Mumbai infrastructure projects, including the metro and the trans-harbour link.

### Q: Why did West Bengal refuse to adopt the repeal of the ceiling Act?

The West Bengal government's stated reason was distributive: without such a law, it maintained, lower income people would be deprived of land. The state had administered the ceiling for three decades as an instrument of land redistribution, and its governing political tradition treated the ceiling as protection for the poor against speculative accumulation in Kolkata and the other covered agglomerations. A draft for adopting the repeal was prepared in 2008 but was not proceeded with, and the state declined to pass the resolution under Article 252(2) that alone could switch the Act off in its territory. Other governments treated the point as an argument about instruments rather than aims, holding that a law which had frozen supply without redistributing was not protecting the poor in practice. The refusal is recorded here as the government's stated position, which under the constitutional design was conclusive whatever its merits.

### Q: How can a buyer check whether the ceiling Act affects a property?

By asking the questions the repeal makes necessary, in order. First, territory: was the plot inside one of the 64 scheduled urban agglomerations, and has the state adopted the 1999 repeal, and on what date? Land in Tamil Nadu stood under that state's own 1978 ceiling Act instead. Second, stage: were ceiling proceedings ever begun against the plot or a predecessor in title, and how far did they travel? A proceeding that never reached vesting and possession abated on the repeal's adoption in that state. A completed vesting with possession left the land with the state. Third, documents: an exemption order under Section 20, if one exists, was saved by the repeal and its conditions still matter. Revenue records often carry old ceiling entries long after abatement, so the record should be read against the repeal position rather than taken at face value, and a specific property requires advice on its own file.

### Q: How was the amount payable for excess land calculated under the ceiling Act?

Section 11 of the 1976 Act used a formula, not market value, which is why the Act speaks of an amount rather than compensation. Where the excess land produced an income, the amount was eight and one-third times the net average annual income actually derived from the land during the five consecutive years before the acquisition notification, computed on the principles in the Second Schedule. Where the land produced no income, the amount was calculated at a rate not exceeding ten rupees per square metre for land in a Category A or Category B agglomeration and five rupees per square metre in a Category C or Category D agglomeration. A ceiling capped the total amount payable to any one person at two lakh rupees. Set against urban land values, the schedule explains why owners litigated vesting rather than accepting payment.

### Q: What statement did a landowner have to file under Section 6 of the ceiling Act?

Section 6 required every person holding vacant land in excess of the ceiling limit at the commencement of the Act to file a statement before the competent authority for the area in which the land lay. The statement had to set out the location, extent, value and other prescribed particulars of all the vacant land the person held, and to specify which lands the holder proposed to retain within the ceiling limit. That nomination mattered: a holder of several plots could keep the most valuable and surrender the rest. The statement started the administrative circuit. The competent authority examined it, prepared a draft statement of the excess under Section 8, heard objections, and issued a final statement under Section 9, after which the excess could be notified and vested under Section 10. Failure to file, or a false statement, exposed the holder to the Act's penal provisions and to proceedings begun by the authority on its own information.

### Q: Could urban property be transferred freely while the ceiling Act was in force?

No. Chapter IV wrapped transfers in controls. Under Section 26, vacant land held within the ceiling limit could not be transferred by sale, mortgage, gift or lease without written notice to the competent authority, and on a proposed sale the authority had the first option to purchase for the state government at a price calculated under the land acquisition law; only if the option was not exercised within sixty days was the owner free to sell to anyone. Under Section 27, urban or urbanisable land with a building on it could not be transferred at all, by sale, mortgage, gift or a lease longer than ten years, without the previous written permission of the competent authority. Section 28 barred registration of a transfer document unless the notice had been given or the permission produced. Excess land itself was, of course, liable to acquisition rather than sale.

### Q: What is the difference between the urban land ceiling and agricultural land ceiling laws?

The two families of ceiling law differ in authorship, route and reach. Agricultural land ceilings were enacted by the state legislatures individually under their own competence over land, state by state, in the zamindari abolition and land reform era, with ceilings, exemptions and machinery that differed from state to state and could be amended or repealed by the same legislatures. The urban ceiling was a single central text, the Urban Land (Ceiling and Regulation) Act, 1976, enacted by Parliament only because eleven states invited it to legislate under Article 252, and uniform across the seventeen states it reached. That difference in route produced the difference in ending: a state could repeal its own agricultural ceiling law in one session, while the urban ceiling could be ended in a state only by Parliament's Repeal Act plus that state's adopting resolution under Article 252(2).

### Q: Why did Maharashtra adopt the repeal of the ceiling Act only in 2007?

Because Maharashtra's consent, constitutionally indispensable, was politically expensive and was eventually purchased rather than persuaded. The ceiling had bitten hardest in Maharashtra, above all in Mumbai, where the five hundred square metre Category A ceiling, the largest stock of ceiling litigation and extensive Section 20 exemption schemes made repeal a major distributive decision affecting builders, landowners and the state's own vested land. Successive state governments resisted adoption after 1999. The change came with the Jawaharlal Nehru National Urban Renewal Mission, which made repeal a mandatory state-level reform for governments seeking central urban infrastructure assistance. Maharashtra's legislature adopted the Repeal Act by resolution at the end of November 2007, contemporary reporting describing repeal as a precondition for Mission grants behind projects such as the Mumbai Metro and the Mumbai Trans Harbour Link. Eight years separated the repeal in Haryana from its arrival in the state where the ceiling mattered most.

### Q: Why do property titles still show ceiling Act proceedings decades after the repeal?

Because the repeal ended the law, not the paperwork. The Repeal Act abated pending proceedings and saved completed vesting by operation of law, but it contained no mechanism for cleansing revenue records, cancelling old notifications or correcting the entries that ceiling proceedings had generated in the record of rights. A plot freed by abatement when its state adopted the repeal may still carry a ceiling remark in the revenue record until an owner applies to have the entry corrected, and a plot covered by a saved exemption order carries that order's conditions permanently as part of its title history. Buyers and lenders reading the record encounter the entries without the repeal's context, which is why ceiling remarks continue to surface in title searches in formerly covered cities and why establishing the repeal position, the adoption date and the stage the old file reached remains a standard part of due diligence for such land.
