The legislative history of the Right to Information Act, 2005 contains an episode with no close parallel in Indian lawmaking. Three years before the Act was passed, Parliament had already enacted a transparency statute, the Freedom of Information Act, 2002. That statute received the President's assent, entered the statute book, and was never brought into force. No citizen ever filed a request under it, no officer was ever directed to answer under it, and in 2005 Parliament quietly repealed it in the same Act that replaced it. A legislature had passed a law and the executive had simply declined to switch it on. Everything distinctive about the statute that followed, its fixed commencement date, its independent commissions, its penalty on the individual officer, its reach into every state, makes sense only when read against that failure and against the fifteen years of agitation that preceded it, because the Right to Information Act was not drafted in a ministry and handed down. It was assembled from below: from wage records read aloud in Rajasthani villages, from nine state statutes of very uneven quality, from two civil society drafts, and from a parliamentary committee stage that rewrote the government's own bill almost beyond recognition. The [complete guide to the Right to Information Act 2005](/2011/09/01/right-to-information-act-2005-guide/) sets out what the statute requires; this article explains how that text came to exist, who fought over each part of it, and what the passage record reveals about the way laws are actually made in India.

The sequence matters because each stage left a deposit in the final text. The courts supplied the constitutional premise, that a citizen's right to know is part of the freedom of speech and expression, decades before Parliament acted. A rural labour organisation supplied the method, the public audit of official records, and the political pressure. The states supplied working prototypes, some instructive and some cautionary. The Freedom of Information Act supplied the negative lesson, a demonstration that a weak text combined with a commencement clause left to executive discretion could be nullified without a single vote being cast against it. The National Advisory Council supplied an unusual channel through which campaign drafts could reach the Cabinet table. And the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Personnel, Public Grievances, Law and Justice supplied the mechanism by which a bill introduced as a modest central measure emerged as a national statute. To read the Act's sections without this record is to miss why the penalty falls on the officer rather than the department, why the appeal runs outside the department altogether, why the definition of information expressly includes opinions and advices, and why the commencement provision counts days from enactment instead of waiting for a notification that might never come.

## A right to know before there was a statute

The campaign for a transparency law did not begin by inventing a right. It began by pointing out that the Supreme Court had already recognised one. In State of Uttar Pradesh v Raj Narain, decided in 1975, the Court was concerned with the disclosure of official documents in an election dispute, and Justice Mathew's reasoning supplied the sentence the movement would quote for the next three decades: in a government of responsibility like India's, where all public agents are answerable for their conduct, there can be few secrets, and the people are entitled to know the particulars of every public transaction in its ultimate bearing. The proposition was framed in the language of Article 19(1)(a) of the Constitution. The freedom of speech and expression, the Court reasoned, is empty if a citizen may speak but may not know the facts on which speech depends, so the right to know is implicit in the freedom itself.

That reasoning was widened in S P Gupta v Union of India in 1981, the case arising from the transfer of High Court judges, in which Justice Bhagwati held that the concept of an open government is the direct result of the right to know, itself an aspect of the freedom of speech and expression, and that disclosure of information regarding the functioning of government must be the rule while secrecy must be an exception justified only where the strictest requirement of public interest calls for it. The case concerned judicial appointments, not village wage records, but its logic was portable, and campaigners carried it everywhere. The Court returned to the theme in Secretary, Ministry of Information and Broadcasting v Cricket Association of Bengal in 1995, holding the airwaves to be public property and affirming the right of citizens to receive information and ideas through diverse media, and in Dinesh Trivedi v Union of India in 1997 it treated the citizen's right to know about the antecedents and functioning of public figures as a necessary condition of a functioning democracy. Finally, in Union of India v Association for Democratic Reforms in 2002 and People's Union for Civil Liberties v Union of India in 2003, the Court converted the doctrine into an operative electoral rule, directing that candidates for election disclose their criminal antecedents, assets and educational qualifications, and defending the voter's right to that information as fundamental under Article 19(1)(a), even after Parliament attempted to legislate the disclosure duty away.

Two features of this jurisprudence shaped the legislative campaign that ran parallel to it. The first is that the courts had recognised a right without building a machine. A constitutional proposition about Article 19(1)(a) did not tell a villager which office held a muster roll, what fee could be charged for a copy, how many days an official could take to answer, or what happened to an official who refused. The gap between the doctrine and the remedy is precisely the space a statute had to fill, and it explains why the movement, unusually for Indian rights campaigns, concentrated its energy on legislative design rather than litigation: the case law had already been won, in outline, and what was missing was plumbing. The second feature is that the doctrine gave the campaign a constitutional vocabulary with which to answer the culture of official secrecy whose statutory emblem was the Official Secrets Act, 1923. When an official refused to show a wage record, he was not merely being obstructive; on the campaign's reading of Raj Narain and S P Gupta, he was withholding what the Constitution already said belonged to the citizen. The demand for a statute could therefore be presented not as a request for a favour but as the operationalisation of an existing fundamental right, and that framing runs through every draft, memorandum and committee submission the movement produced.

It is worth pausing on what the courts did not do, because the legislative history is unintelligible without it. The Supreme Court never fixed a time limit for answering a request, never created an information commission, never prescribed a penalty, and never ordered the proactive publication of records. Its election-disclosure directions were the exception that proved the rule: a specific, self-executing direction in a defined field. For everything else, only Parliament and the state legislatures could build the regime. The doctrine was the campaign's charter; it was never a substitute for the statute, and the campaigners, many of whom had read the judgments closely, understood the distinction better than most commentators who later credited the courts with delivering the right to information.

## The official mind: the secrecy inheritance the doctrine had to displace

The doctrine of openness developed by the courts had to make its way against an administrative inheritance built, deliberately and over a long period, for concealment. The Official Secrets Act, 1923 supplied the penal pole of that inheritance: a colonial statute, modelled on British legislation of the same era, whose broadest provision made the unauthorised communication of official information an offence in sweeping terms, and whose very title taught generations of officials to classify the state's papers as the state's secrets. Around the statute stood the service rules. The conduct rules governing civil servants prohibited an official from communicating any official document or information to anyone not authorised to receive it, and the prohibition was not a dead letter but a condition of service, breach of which invited disciplinary proceedings. An official asked by a citizen for a record therefore faced an asymmetric risk that no judicial pronouncement about Article 19(1)(a) could neutralise: disclosing cost him something real if his superiors disapproved, while refusing cost him nothing at all. The law of evidence completed the structure, for the Indian Evidence Act armed the state with privilege, permitting the head of a department to withhold unpublished official records relating to affairs of state, a claim the courts supervised but the administration wielded as a routine instrument. S P Gupta itself, the case that proclaimed disclosure the rule, arose from a privilege claim over the correspondence on judicial appointments, which is a fair emblem of the period: the right to know was being declared in the courtroom and denied at the counter, by the same state, in the same years.

Politicians had noticed the gap before the campaign forced the issue, and the record of their noticing is part of the pre history. The Janata government that took office in 1977 carried a promise of open government and spoke of reviewing the Official Secrets Act; the promise lapsed with the government's own early fall. A decade later the National Front government of V P Singh announced, within weeks of taking office in December 1989, that it intended to amend the Official Secrets Act and to legislate a citizen's right to information, and its information and broadcasting minister spoke publicly of opening the government's files to the people. That government also fell, in 1990, before any bill was introduced, and the announcement joined the Assembly promise of Bhairon Singh Shekhawat in a growing archive of unkept transparency pledges on which the campaign of the 1990s could draw. The archive mattered because it shaped the campaign's institutional pessimism. Three separate political formations had declared for openness and none had legislated, from which the National Campaign drew the conclusion that governed all its later drafting: the statute had to be built to survive the indifference or hostility of the government of the day, with its commencement fixed in its own text, its appeals outside the executive and its penalties automatic in form, because a right dependent on the continuing goodwill of its opponents was the Official Secrets culture wearing a new coat.

## Muster rolls: how a wage dispute in Rajasthan invented a method

The organisation at the origin of the statute was founded in 1990 in Devdungri, a village in Rajsamand district in Rajasthan, by Aruna Roy, a former officer of the Indian Administrative Service who had resigned from the service a decade and a half earlier, together with Nikhil Dey and Shankar Singh. The Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan, the organisation of labourers and farmers, was not founded to campaign for a transparency law. It was founded to work on the questions that decided survival in a drought prone district: land, water, the public distribution system, and above all the payment of minimum wages on public relief and development works. It was the wage question that produced the demand for information, and it did so by a logic so direct that it is worth following step by step, because the statute's design is unintelligible without it.

A worker on a drought relief site who was paid less than the statutory minimum wage faced an evidentiary problem before she faced any other kind. Whether she had worked, for how many days, and at what rate were facts recorded in exactly one place, the muster roll maintained by the junior engineer and the panchayat functionaries who ran the work. If the roll recorded full attendance and full payment, her complaint of short payment had no documentary support; if the roll was false, the falsity itself was the offence, since muster rolls across the region were, as the Sangathan's early investigations established, routinely padded with the names of people who had never worked, with entries for work never executed and bills for materials never delivered. The money for the phantom entries was divided among the functionaries who maintained the record. The worker's demand therefore took a form that sounds modest and was in fact radical: show us the record. Let us see the muster roll, the bills and the vouchers, and compare what the paper says with what the village knows.

### Where did the demand for a right to information law begin?

It began with unpaid and underpaid wages in Rajsamand district, Rajasthan, around 1990, where workers on public relief works found that the muster rolls recording their labour were being falsified, and concluded that the right to inspect and copy official records was not an administrative courtesy but the precondition of claiming every other entitlement they had.

The instrument the Sangathan developed to make that comparison public was the jan sunwai, the public hearing. The method, first used in December 1994 at Kot Kirana in Pali district and then repeated across Rajsamand, Pali and Ajmer districts, was simple in form and devastating in effect. Such official records as could be obtained, sometimes through sympathetic officials, sometimes through the offices of elected representatives, were read aloud, item by item, before an assembled village. The people named in the record were present or were known to those present. A muster roll entry recording twenty days of labour by a man who had been working in another state was contradicted on the spot, by his neighbours. A bill for cement that had never reached the village was contradicted by the absence of the structure it was supposed to have built. Officials and elected representatives were invited to attend and answer, and a panel of independent persons of standing presided, so that the hearing resembled a people's court more than a rally. Movement accounts of the early jan sunwais record money being returned publicly by representatives who could not sustain the entries read out against them, and record also the panic the method induced in the local administration, which responded by trying to discredit the hearings, by warning officials against releasing records, and, on occasion, by physical intimidation of activists.

The jan sunwai mattered legislatively for three reasons, and each of them is visible in the statute that eventually emerged. First, it demonstrated that records, not rhetoric, were the terrain on which petty corruption could actually be proved, which is why the eventual Act defines information to include the inspection of works and the taking of samples of material, rights that make little sense in a ministry in Delhi and complete sense in a village where a road exists on paper only. Second, it showed that disclosure had to be a legal entitlement rather than an administrative grace, because every voluntary disclosure on which the early hearings depended could be, and was, withdrawn the moment it embarrassed the disclosing office. Third, it created a constituency. The demand for the right to information in Rajasthan was not an abstraction of good governance discourse; it was the demand of named people who had stood up in hearings and contradicted a government record in front of their neighbours, and who understood exactly what a refusal to disclose protected.

The struggle that produced this method had a preliminary phase worth recording, because it fixed the demand's precise shape. In the early 1990s the Sangathan's members worked on drought relief and development sites across the Bhim and Devdungri area, and the wage disputes were at first pursued through the ordinary channels: complaints to the block office, representations to the district administration, applications for inspection of the muster rolls. The demand at this stage was almost embarrassingly modest. Workers asked to see, and to be allowed to copy at their own expense, the rolls that recorded their own labour. The refusals they met were equally instructive. Officials invoked the secrecy of government records, offered oral assurances in place of documents, and on occasion produced the rolls only after the intervention of a legislator or a senior officer whose protection could not be relied on twice. Each element of the eventual statute answers one of these encounters. The right to inspect and take certified copies answers the oral assurance. The time limit answers the indefinite deferral. The penalty answers the calculation that refusal was free. And the public hearing answered the deepest problem of all, that an individual complaint against a falsified record could always be isolated and worn down, whereas a record read aloud before the whole village could not be argued with at all. The panels that presided over the hearings were chosen with care from outside the local administration, retired officials, teachers, journalists and lawyers of standing, precisely so that the findings could not be dismissed as a faction's theatre. The lineage of the method extends forward as well as back: the statutory social audit written into the rural employment guarantee legislation of 2005, under which village assemblies audit the works executed among them, descends directly from the jan sunwai, a descent that Jean Dreze, an economist who worked within the wider right to work and right to information networks, has traced in detail.

The state government in Jaipur was forced to respond earlier than the Centre. On 5 April 1995 the Chief Minister of Rajasthan, Bhairon Singh Shekhawat, announced in the Legislative Assembly that citizens would be given the right to inspect and obtain copies of records relating to development works. The announcement was not implemented. A year later, on 6 April 1996, the Sangathan began a sit in at Chang Gate in the town of Beawar in Ajmer district, which lasted forty days and became the founding spectacle of the national movement. The Beawar dharna had an unusual feature that contemporary reporters noticed at once: its participants, overwhelmingly rural labourers, were not demanding food, work or wages, the standard currencies of protest, but information itself, the records of their own labour. The slogans carried the whole argument in miniature. Hamara paisa, hamara hisaab: our money, our accounts. And the sentence that the Hindi editor Prabhash Joshi distilled from the protest in a celebrated Jansatta editorial and that thereafter served as the movement's motto: hum jaanenge, hum jiyenge, we will know, we will live. Senior journalists, among them Nikhil Chakravartty and Kuldip Nayar, travelled to Beawar; writers, lawyers and retired officials followed; and the dharna's support was organised from the villages of the Sangathan's own area, which sustained it with contributions of grain, money and people. When the Rajasthan government again responded with assurances and a committee rather than a law, the Sangathan returned to the streets, mounting a further prolonged sit in at Jaipur in 1997, and the demand migrated from the state's politics into the nation's.

## From a Rajasthani demand to a national campaign, and from a campaign to two drafts

The Beawar dharna's most durable institutional product was the National Campaign for People's Right to Information, formed in Delhi in 1996 in the months after the dharna, when the journalists, lawyers, academics, retired civil servants and activists who had converged on Beawar concluded that a demand proven in Rajasthan had to be carried into national legislation. The National Campaign was deliberately not an organisation with a membership and a hierarchy but a coalition, and its composition was itself a strategic fact: it joined the Sangathan's rural base to people who knew the interior of the state from the other side, former secretaries to government, election commissioners, editors and law teachers, so that the draft legislation it produced could not be dismissed as the work of outsiders who did not understand administration. Its working premise, drawn directly from the Rajasthan experience, was that the statute had to be designed around the refusal, not around the request: the central questions were what happened when an officer said no, to whom the citizen could then go, and what it would cost the officer to refuse without cause.

Two drafting efforts of the mid 1990s supplied the textual raw material, and the eventual statute descends from both. The first came from the Press Council of India under its chairman, Justice P B Sawant. In 1996 the Press Council, working with the National Campaign, prepared a draft Right to Information Bill and sent it to the Government of India. The draft's provenance mattered. The press had its own institutional grievance against official secrecy, and a draft carrying the Press Council's imprimatur, prepared under a retired judge of the Supreme Court, framed transparency as a democratic requirement rather than as an agitator's slogan. The draft proposed an enforceable right of access with an appeal lying outside the department that held the record, and it treated the exemptions as matters to be justified rather than as categories to be assumed. The second effort ran inside government itself. In January 1997 the Government of India constituted a Working Group on Right to Information and Promotion of Open and Transparent Government, chaired by H D Shourie, the consumer rights advocate associated with the Consumer Education and Research Centre, which had itself circulated draft information legislation earlier in the decade. The Shourie Working Group reported in May 1997 with a draft Freedom of Information Bill attached to its report.

The two drafts differed in temperament in ways that anticipated the next decade of argument. The civil society draft started from the citizen and built outward, asking what a refusal should cost and who should police it. The official working group draft, whatever its authors' intentions, started from the administration and built cautiously, with a wider field of exemption and a weaker appellate structure, and it is from the official line, not the civil society line, that the Freedom of Information Bill of 2000 eventually descended, diluted further on the way. Campaign accounts are emphatic about this genealogy, and the texts support them: the statute of 2005 resembles the Press Council draft in its architecture of independent appeal and penalty, while the statute of 2002 resembles the more guarded official tradition. It is also worth recording, because later retellings often simplify it, that the drafting credit is genuinely plural. No single individual drafted the right to information in India. A village movement generated the demand and several of the design requirements, a press council draft and a campaign draft gave them statutory form, an official working group produced a rival text, and the final Act was produced by negotiation among all of these and a parliamentary committee besides.

The paper trail of that negotiation, drafts annotated by officials, committee evidence, correspondence between campaigners and ministers, is the primary archive from which this history is written, and its survival is not accidental: the journalists inside the campaign kept records with a professional's instinct for protecting a source and a document alike. The modern form of that discipline, keeping sensitive research material in an encrypted private workspace rather than in an office drawer, is described in [an account of how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/), and it is the same instinct: whoever holds the record holds the argument.

## The press as midwife: newspapers and the making of a public issue

No account of this legislative history is complete without the newspapers, for the campaign was among the first Indian movements to treat the press not as a megaphone to be borrowed at the climax but as a participant to be enlisted from the start. The connection had a doctrinal root. The Supreme Court's right to know cases were, in significant part, press freedom cases: the great challenges to newspaper taxation and to state control of newsprint and telecast rights had taught the courts to reason about information from the citizen's side of the transaction, and cases such as Secretary, Ministry of Information and Broadcasting v Cricket Association of Bengal were argued and won as contests over who controls the flow of public communication. The movement inherited that doctrine and repaid it with copy. The jan sunwai was, among its other functions, a perfectly constructed news event, local, verifiable, peopled by named officials and named labourers, and producing on the spot the returned money and abandoned defences that make a story write itself. Hindi journalism supplied the movement's most famous sentence: the slogan Hum jaanenge, hum jiyenge, we shall know and we shall live, compressed from an editorial by Prabhash Joshi in Jansatta, the Hindi daily he edited, which took the Rajasthan struggle into the political vocabulary of the Hindi belt. Senior figures of the English press, Nikhil Chakravartty and Kuldip Nayar among them, travelled to the Beawar sit-in and wrote the movement into the national conversation, a pilgrimage the campaign remembered and reciprocated when it supported, in turn, the transparency claims of journalists themselves. The relationship had consequences for the statute. A legislature enacting an information law in the full light of a friendly press faced a different politics from the quiet burial of 2002, and a profession that had helped to make the law became, after 2005, among its most determined users, in a feedback loop between the fourth estate and the Act that the source protection practices of the digital era would extend rather than replace.

## The states go first: a laboratory of nine statutes

While the Centre deliberated, the states legislated. Tamil Nadu and Goa both enacted right to information statutes in 1997, Rajasthan and Karnataka followed in 2000, Delhi in 2001, Assam and Maharashtra in 2002, Madhya Pradesh in 2003 and Jammu and Kashmir in 2004. The question of which state was first is genuinely contested between Tamil Nadu and Goa, both of which legislated in 1997, and the honest answer is that the two Acts are of the same year and dissimilar character, so that the title of first depends on whether one counts enactment or quality. What is not contested is the cumulative significance. By the time Parliament took up the subject in earnest, nine state legislatures had already run the experiment of a statutory right to information, and the constitutional geography that allowed them to do so, a federal distribution of legislative competence in which information and the machinery of state administration were open to state lawmaking, is mapped in the series account of [the Seventh Schedule and its three lists of subjects](/2018/09/15/seventh-schedule-lists-explained/). The central Act, when it came, did not have to imagine its machinery. It could inspect machinery already running, badly in some places and instructively in others, and take what had been tested.

The state statutes were far from uniform, and their unevenness is the analytical point. The Tamil Nadu Act of 1997 was regarded by the campaign's own assessments as a cautionary example, encumbered with a long schedule of restrictions, more than twenty by the Commonwealth Human Rights Initiative's count, with no penalty for refusal and an appeal that stayed inside the official hierarchy. A statute can bear the name of the right and withhold the substance, and Tamil Nadu's did much to teach the campaign what to insist on at the Centre. Goa's Act of the same year stood at the other end of the early spectrum and was widely treated as among the better drafted of the first generation, with defined obligations and time limits. Rajasthan's Act of 2000 was the Sangathan's own legislative trophy, extracted by a decade of pressure, and it carried the movement's signature concerns into law, including access to panchayat records and the right to inspect works, though its enforcement machinery remained modest. Karnataka's Act of 2000 was noted in campaign assessments for reaching further than most, including a penalty on officers who defaulted, an idea that had been tested nowhere at the Centre and that the central Act would later adopt as one of its defining features. Delhi's Act of 2001 acquired a significance beyond its text because the capital's resettlement colonies and ration shops gave it an urban constituency: the organisation Parivartan used the Delhi statute to audit ration distribution and public works in east Delhi, demonstrating to a metropolitan audience that the device a Rajasthani village had invented worked equally well against a city administration. Maharashtra's path ran through a weak statute of 2000, replaced by a substantially stronger Act in 2002 after the campaign of Anna Hazare, who then fasted in 2003 to press the state to implement the new Act in earnest; the Maharashtra experience, a strong text extracted from a reluctant administration by sustained public pressure, was the single most cited state precedent in the national debate.

### What did the state laws teach the drafters of the central Act?

They taught by example and by warning. The penalty on a defaulting officer, the fixed time limit for an answer, the external appeal, and proactive disclosure each appeared in a state statute before 2005, while Tamil Nadu's thicket of exemptions and internal appeals showed how a transparency law could be enacted and emptied at once.

The counter evidence must be recorded alongside the claim, because it qualifies the claim without defeating it. Most of the state laws were little used. Application numbers in most states were small, awareness was low, the appellate routes that stayed within government were predictably unhelpful, and in several states the statute existed mainly as a debating point for the campaign. The strongest version of the laboratory thesis is therefore not that the states perfected the right to information before the Centre adopted it, but that they did three things the national drafting process could not have done without them. They proved that a disclosure regime was administrable, that officers could be required to answer within a fixed period without the collapse of government. They generated the design menu from which the central Act's features were selected, each feature carrying a state provenance that its advocates could cite in committee. And they built the political fact that made a central law unavoidable: a citizen of Goa or Karnataka possessed a statutory right against her state government that she did not possess against the Government of India, an inversion that no central government could comfortably defend once it was pointed out.

## Two proofs of portability: the device leaves the village

A sceptic of the mid 1990s could concede the jan sunwai its village power and still doubt that it translated. Records in a Rajasthani panchayat were one thing; the paper empires of a metropolitan administration were another. Two state experiences of the early 2000s answered that doubt on the record, and both were cited constantly in the national drafting debate. The first was Delhi's. The organisation Parivartan, founded in 1999 by Arvind Kejriwal, then a serving officer of the Indian Revenue Service, together with Manish Sisodia and others, took the Delhi Right to Information Act of 2001 into the resettlement colonies of east Delhi and used it as the Sangathan had used the demand in Rajasthan, to obtain the records of public works executed in the residents' own streets and of the ration shops on which their food entitlements depended. When the Delhi administration stalled on releasing records, the campaign escalated to the jan sunwai form itself, and a public hearing held at Sunder Nagri in 2002, at which the accounts of public works and ration distribution were read out before residents, officials and the press, reproduced the Rajasthan dynamic in the capital: entries for works that residents could see had not been executed, and a rationing system whose paper allocations diverged from what the shops had received. The significance of the Delhi episode for the legislative history is that it broke the movement's identification with a single region and a single idiom. The right to information was shown to be a general instrument of urban as well as rural accountability, and its demonstration ground was the national capital, in front of the national press, while the central statute was being debated.

The second proof was Maharashtra's, and it operated on the text as much as on the politics. Maharashtra's first statute of 2000 was, like Tamil Nadu's, a disappointment to its intended users, weak in its machinery and little invoked. Its replacement in 2002, driven through after the sustained campaign of Anna Hazare, was of a different order: it carried time limits for disposal, a structured appeal that ultimately reached the Lokayukta, an authority outside the departmental hierarchy, and it treated default by an officer as a matter with consequences. Hazare's fast in Mumbai in 2003, undertaken to force the state government to implement the new Act rather than admire it, completed the lesson in implementation politics that the Freedom of Information Act was simultaneously teaching at the Centre: enactment is a midpoint, not a conclusion. Campaign leaders have since said that the Maharashtra Act served the national drafting as a working model of what a strong Indian transparency statute looked like when assembled, and its fingerprints, the external appellate anchor and the seriousness about default, are visible in the central text. Between Delhi's proof of use and Maharashtra's proof of design, the committee that examined the Bill in 2005 was not speculating about consequences. It was choosing among demonstrated alternatives.

## The world had already moved: the comparative backdrop

India's delay requires one further piece of context, because the statute of 2005 was late and strong at once, and both facts belong to its history. Access to official information is an old device in a few democracies: Sweden's Freedom of the Press Act of 1766, with its principle of public access to official documents, is generally identified as the first such law anywhere, and the United States enacted its federal Freedom of Information Act in 1966. For most of the twentieth century such statutes were a minority taste, concentrated in North America, Western Europe and Australasia. The position changed after 1989, when the collapse of one party regimes across Eastern Europe made transparency a standard item of democratic reconstruction, and through the 1990s and the first years of the new century access laws spread across every continent, carried by constitutional settlements such as South Africa's, whose constitution of 1996 guaranteed the right of access to information held by the state and whose Promotion of Access to Information Act of 2000 gave it statutory form. By the time the Indian Parliament legislated, several dozen countries had working access regimes, and Commonwealth networks, including the Commonwealth Human Rights Initiative headquartered in Delhi, were circulating model laws and comparative assessments in which India's absence from the list was conspicuous.

The comparative record shaped the Indian drafting in a specific way. The campaign and the committee could inspect two generations of foreign statutes and observe which of their features had performed: the independent information commissioner on the Canadian and British pattern, the personal consequences for default that the weaker regimes lacked, the proactive publication duties that reduced request volumes where they were honestly implemented. India's statute, when it came, was therefore not a pioneer working in the dark but a late adopter able to select, which goes far to explain the paradox that later global assessments, including the international RTI Rating maintained by the Centre for Law and Democracy, have consistently placed the Indian Act among the strongest access laws in the world on the quality of its legal framework. The same assessments note the distance between the framework and its administrative performance, which is the distance this article's later sections keep in view. Lateness, in this one respect, was an advantage the movement knew how to spend.

## The drafting room: two texts, two theories of the citizen

Between 1996 and 2002 India possessed two complete drafts of a transparency law, and the differences between them are the clearest guide to what the contest of 2004 and 2005 was actually about. Both drafts descended from the same frustration and borrowed from the same foreign models, yet they encoded different theories of the citizen's standing against the state. The campaign draft, prepared under the Press Council's chairmanship with the Campaign's participation and descended from the Consumer Education and Research Centre's earlier text, began from the movement's experience: it treated disclosure as an entitlement that had to be armoured, and so reached instinctively for a penalty on the defaulting officer, for appellate bodies outside the departmental chain, for time limits that ran against the administration rather than the applicant, and for duties of proactive publication that would let citizens obtain the ordinary record of government without asking leave at all. The Working Group draft of 1997, and the Freedom of Information Bill that descended from it, began from the administrator's desk: it treated access as a facility the state would provide within its existing structures, and so located appeals inside the administrative hierarchy, set no personal consequence for default, and framed the whole instrument as a freedom, a liberty to ask that the state would honour, rather than as a right the citizen could enforce.

The difference in title, which can look like a copy editor's quarrel, was in fact the argument in miniature, and the literature of the movement is explicit about it. A freedom of information statute, on the campaign's reading, describes what the citizen is permitted to do; a right to information statute describes what the state is obliged to deliver, and obligations require machinery: an officer designated to answer, a clock that runs, an appeal that leaves the building, a penalty that concentrates the mind. Every structural feature contested in 2004 maps onto this fork. When the 2004 Bill omitted the penalty, the campaign did not read an oversight but a philosophy: the bill had been drafted, its critics argued within the National Advisory Council process, as though the Working Group's theory had won in 1997 and only the campaign's vocabulary had survived. The reinsertion of the penalty, the commissions and the wider extent in 2005 was therefore not an accumulation of improvements but the substitution of one draft's theory for the other's, executed through committee amendments. This is also the answer to a question the legislative historian should ask of any celebrated statute: which of the available drafts does the enacted text most resemble? In this case the answer is unusually clear. The Act of 2005 is the campaign's draft, as revised by the National Advisory Council and the standing committee, wearing the authority of Parliament. The Working Group's draft survives only in the negative, as the 2002 Act whose fate taught the final drafters what to avoid.

## The statute that never began: the Freedom of Information Act, 2002

The first central statute arrived by a route that guaranteed its weakness. The National Democratic Alliance government had committed itself to transparency in its agenda for governance, and in July 2000 it introduced a Freedom of Information Bill in the Lok Sabha. The Bill descended from the Shourie Working Group's draft rather than from the Press Council and campaign draft, and it was examined by a parliamentary standing committee, whose consideration occupied the better part of two years. Parliament passed the Bill in December 2002, and the Freedom of Information Act, 2002 received the President's assent in January 2003. There the story stops. The Act's own commencement provision left its coming into force to a date the Central Government would notify, and the notification never came. Draft rules under the Act were still being circulated for comment in 2004. The government that had enacted it left office after the general election of 2004 without having appointed the machinery the Act required or fixed the day on which a single citizen could invoke it, and its successor preferred a new statute to the activation of the old one. Section 31 of the Right to Information Act, 2005 repealed the Freedom of Information Act outright, closing the file on a law that had been in force for zero days.

The manner of its death matters as much as the fact. Non commencement is the quietest instrument of legislative nullification available to an Indian executive, because it requires no debate, no vote and no public defence: a statute can be enacted, assented to and still never govern a single request, the same [notification gap](/2010/03/01/44th-amendment-act-1978-explained/) that left the Forty-fourth Amendment's preventive detention safeguards uncommenced a generation earlier, and the pattern recurs in the [Whistle Blowers Protection Act 2014](/2012/01/01/whistle-blowers-protection-act-2014/), which was likewise passed by Parliament and then left waiting for a commencement that its sponsors did not deliver. The campaign learned the lesson in the most concrete possible form, and the 2005 Act's commencement clause, which counts one hundred and twenty days from enactment and then operates of its own force, is the direct institutional memory of the 2002 Act's fate.

### What did the Freedom of Information Act 2002 leave out?

It left out every mechanism that makes a disclosure right usable. It carried no penalty for an officer who refused or delayed, created no independent commission to hear appeals, allowed appeals to remain within the administration, set no ceiling on the fees chargeable, and exempted even requests said to involve a disproportionate diversion of a public authority's resources.

The textual weaknesses explain why the campaign did not mourn the Act and did not campaign for its notification. Alongside the missing penalty and the missing commission, the 2002 Act's exemption clauses were drawn widely, its coverage was hedged, and its commencement was discretionary, so that even a timely notification would have delivered a right considerably weaker than the one several states already gave their citizens against their own governments. The official case for the Act, made by the government that piloted it, was that a beginning had been made and that a cautious statute suited a cautious administrative culture; the campaign's answer, pressed by the National Campaign and by the Commonwealth Human Rights Initiative's analyses of the text, was that the Act's defects were not the defects of caution but of design, since each of its omissions protected the refusing officer rather than the requesting citizen. Between those two positions lies the drafting contest that the events of 2004 and 2005 resolved, decisively, in the campaign's favour.

The last irony of the 2002 Act is that it lingered visibly for two years after its enactment, an unrepealed reproach. Draft rules under it were still being circulated by the Department of Personnel and Training in August 2004, and the incoming United Progressive Alliance government initially contemplated the course of least resistance, notifying the old Act into force with the promised improvements to follow. The Commonwealth Human Rights Initiative's chronicle of the period records that the National Advisory Council advised against that course: the 2002 Act was to be replaced, not revived, because repair would have left its weakest features, the absent penalty, the internal appeal and the notification habit itself, embedded in the Republic's transparency law. The decision to begin again, rather than to commence and amend, is what makes 2005 a founding rather than a revision, and it explains a feature of the 2005 Act that puzzles first time readers: a repeals and savings clause with real work to do. Section 31 repealed a statute that had never governed a single request, the only major Indian Act of its generation whose entire operative life was a silence between assent and repeal.

## 2004: a new government, an old promise, and a bill that stopped at the Centre

The general election of 2004 changed the political conditions of the campaign in a way no amount of agitation could have engineered by itself. The United Progressive Alliance government that took office in May 2004 governed on the strength of a National Common Minimum Programme, which stated its commitment in a sentence the campaign had effectively dictated: the Right to Information Act will be made more progressive, participatory and meaningful. To oversee the implementation of that programme the government created the National Advisory Council, chaired by the Congress president Sonia Gandhi, and the Council became the channel through which the campaign's drafting work reached the centre of decision. In August 2004 the National Campaign forwarded to the Council a detailed set of suggested amendments to the Freedom of Information Act, 2002; the Council endorsed most of them and sent them forward to the Prime Minister; and the government's law making machinery set about converting the exercise into legislation.

What emerged, however, was not what the campaign had sent. The government decided to proceed by a fresh statute rather than by amending the Act of 2002, and the Right to Information Bill, 2004 was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 23 December 2004. In the form introduced, the Bill applied only to public authorities of the Union Government. State governments, the level at which the citizen encounters the state in the ration shop, the panchayat office, the police station and the land record room, were outside it, left to their own statutes of uneven quality. The Bill's appellate and penalty provisions were also markedly weaker than the package the National Advisory Council had endorsed. The reaction from the campaign was immediate and public: the National Campaign and allied organisations protested that a bill advertised as fulfilling the Common Minimum Programme's promise in fact delivered a narrower right than the promise described, and that a transparency law confined to the Centre would miss the overwhelming majority of the transactions through which the state touches its citizens.

The episode deserves emphasis because it refutes the simplest version of the origin story, in which a sympathetic government simply enacted the movement's draft. The movement's draft went into government and came out diminished, as drafts do, and the story of the Right to Information Act is in significant part the story of how the diminished bill was then restored and enlarged inside Parliament. The government, facing the protest, referred the Bill both to a Group of Ministers for reconsideration and to the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Personnel, Public Grievances, Law and Justice for examination, and it was in those two forums, rather than in the original drafting, that the statute was substantially made.

## A hundred amendments: the committee stage and the remaking of the bill

The Standing Committee stage of the Right to Information Bill is the strongest exhibit in this series for the proposition that parliamentary committees in India, when they work as designed, can rewrite legislation rather than merely annotate it. The committee, chaired by E M Sudarsana Natchiappan, took evidence from a wide range of witnesses, including members of the National Campaign who appeared before it to argue, point by point, for the restoration and extension of the wider scheme. Its report, tabled in the Lok Sabha on 21 March 2005, recommended changes across the Bill's architecture, and most consequentially it supported the extension of the statute beyond the Union Government to the whole of the country. The Group of Ministers, chaired by Pranab Mukherjee, worked through the National Advisory Council's recommendations and the committee's in parallel. When the government returned to the Lok Sabha with the Bill in May 2005, it did so carrying more than a hundred amendments of its own, an extraordinary number for a bill of this size, and the great majority of them moved the text in one direction: outward in coverage and upward in enforceability. The amended Bill was taken up on 10 May 2005, passed by the Lok Sabha on 11 May and by the Rajya Sabha on 12 May, with support that crossed the floor of both Houses, and it received the assent of President A P J Abdul Kalam on 15 June 2005.

The single most consequential of the changes was the extension to the states. As introduced, the Bill would have created a right against the Union Government while the citizen's most consequential dealings, with state police, state land records, state health and education and local bodies, remained governed by the patchwork of state statutes, several of which were feeble and one of which, Jammu and Kashmir's, coexisted with a constitutional position that kept the state outside central legislation of this kind in any event. As passed, the Act extended to the whole of India except the State of Jammu and Kashmir, bound the public authorities of every state, and set up State Information Commissions alongside the Central Commission. The constitutional competence for Parliament to legislate a disclosure regime binding state authorities was debated in the committee stage and in commentary, and the Act proceeded on the footing that it could; the state statutes it overlapped were left in place beside it, with the central Act's override provision resolving conflicts in favour of disclosure. For the campaign, the extension was the difference between a symbol and a statute, and the parliamentary record shows it was won in committee and in the Group of Ministers, not conceded in the original bill.

The texture of the committee's work repays attention, because it explains the confidence with which the final text departs from the bill. The committee did not confine itself to the campaign's witnesses. It heard the government departments that would have to operate the statute, and the record of its consultations shows the security establishment pressing for exemptions broader than the bill contained, the administrative ministries warning of the burden of requests and of the effect of disclosure on the candour of internal advice, and the campaign's witnesses answering, in effect, that each of these anxieties had already been tested against the state statutes and the two drafts of the 1990s, and that the bill's safeguards, the exemption clauses, the third party notice procedure and the protection for action taken in good faith, were the proportionate reply. The committee's composition mattered to the outcome. A committee of the whole Parliament, with members drawn from the opposition as well as the treasury benches, reporting substantially in one direction, deprived the final debate of its usual polarities: the government could present the enlarged bill as Parliament's own work, and the opposition, whose members had signed the report, had little ground from which to resist it. The Group of Ministers performed the complementary function inside the executive, reconciling the ministries to a text several of them had resisted, so that by May 2005 the bill arrived on the floor of the Lok Sabha pre negotiated in both forums that might have destroyed it. Students of Indian legislation will recognise the pattern, and its rarity. Most bills are passed as drafted or not passed at all. This one was, in the strict sense, co authored by its examiners.

## The gap between the bill and the Act

The distance between the Bill of December 2004 and the Act of June 2005 can be measured provision by provision, and the measurement is the analytical heart of this legislative history. The Bill covered the Centre; the Act covered the country. The Bill's appeal structure kept the citizen substantially within the administration she was complaining about; the Act built a two tier appeal, first to an officer senior to the one who decided, and then outside the department altogether, to a Central Information Commission and to State Information Commissions constituted as independent bodies. The Bill treated refusal as an administrative outcome; the Act attached a personal price to it, empowering the commissions to impose a penalty of two hundred and fifty rupees for every day of delay or refusal without reasonable cause, up to a ceiling of twenty five thousand rupees, with the burden of proving that the refusal was reasonable placed squarely on the officer, and with a recommendation of disciplinary action for persistent default. The Bill's disclosure duties were request driven; the Act opened with an affirmative obligation, in the proactive disclosure provisions, requiring every public authority to publish, on its own initiative, the particulars of its organisation, functions, officers, decision making norms, rules, documents and budgets, across a list of seventeen heads, so that the need to ask would itself shrink.

The definition of information was widened into the form the jan sunwai had demanded a decade earlier. The Act defines information as any material in any form, and its illustrative list runs through records, documents, memos, electronic mail, opinions and advices, press releases, circulars, orders, logbooks, contracts, reports, papers, samples and models: a list in which the words opinions and advices quietly settled, in advance, the question whether the reasoning recorded on a government file could be demanded, the question that would explode into the file notings controversy within a year of commencement. The citizen's procedural position was strengthened at every step. An applicant need give no reasons for a request and no particulars beyond what contact requires. The ordinary time limit for an answer was fixed at thirty days, and at forty eight hours where the information concerns the life or liberty of a person. The exemption clauses, though extensive, were fenced with a public interest override, permitting disclosure where the public interest in it outweighs the protected harm, and with a severability provision directing that the non exempt part of a record be released rather than the whole record withheld. The Act overrode the Official Secrets Act and every other inconsistent law to the extent of the inconsistency, reversing the default under which the entire preceding century of Indian administration had operated. And the intelligence and security organisations listed in its Second Schedule were exempted only subject to a carve back: information pertaining to allegations of corruption and human rights violations was not excluded even for them.

### Why does the Right to Information Act fix its own commencement date?

Because its predecessor died of the opposite arrangement. The Freedom of Information Act, 2002 left commencement to a notification that was never issued, so the 2005 Act was made to operate by its own force: a core of machinery provisions at once, and the remainder on the one hundred and twentieth day after enactment, a deadline no ministry could postpone.

Two features of the enacted text mark the limits of the victory, and the legislative history is incomplete without them. The first is territorial. The Act did not extend to the State of Jammu and Kashmir, which retained its own statute of 2004, a consequence of the state's constitutional position at the time; the exclusion lasted until the reorganisation of the state in 2019 carried the central Act into the successor union territories and displaced the state law. A statute celebrated as national was, for its first fourteen years, national with an asterisk, and the asterisk was not the campaign's choice but the Constitution's geography. The second limit is institutional. The Act created the commissions but left their staffing, budgets and the pace of their appointment processes to the very executives they were to police, a dependence whose consequences the implementation record would display. Neither limit was hidden at passage. Both were visible in the text to anyone who read the passage record carefully, which is the general lesson this article presses: the compromises of a statute are usually legible at its birth, in the distance between the bill that was introduced and the Act that emerged.

Several further features of the enacted text belong in this inventory of the gap, because each of them encodes a decision the drafting history explains. The right is conferred on citizens: the Act speaks throughout of the citizen's right, so that the entitlement belongs to Indian citizens rather than to any person, a narrowing from the broadest drafting possibilities that passed with little debate in 2005 and that locates the statute's theory in democratic membership rather than in universal personhood. The fee structure was made deliberately marginal to the right's exercise, with the application fee set low by rule and a complete exemption for applicants below the poverty line written into the Act itself, so that the price of a question could not quietly become its rationing device. The commissions were given more than an appellate docket: they may inquire into complaints directly, including the complaint of a person who has been unable even to file a request, and in deciding appeals they may require the public authority to take remedial steps and may award compensation to a complainant for loss or detriment suffered, powers that convert the appeal from an order for a document into a supervision of the system that withheld it. The Act bars the jurisdiction of the courts over orders made under it, channelling challenge into the constitutional writ jurisdiction instead, a choice for speed and specialisation that the committee accepted. The one discretionary residue the drafters left, inevitably, was the rule making layer: fees beyond the statutory principles, forms and procedural detail were left to the appropriate governments, and the commencement clause was drafted to ensure that this residue could not become a second notification trap, by bringing the machinery provisions, the constitution of the commissions, the appointment of information officers and the proactive disclosure duty, into force at once upon enactment, with the citizen's enforceable right following on the one hundred and twentieth day. The reader comparing the two texts of 2004 and 2005 should attend to that proviso, for it is the bill's epitaph and the Act's foundation in a single sentence: everything needed to operate the statute existed in law from 15 June 2005, and only the right itself was given time to be prepared for.

## The statute's self-description: reading the long title as an argument

Indian statutes rarely argue, but this one does, and its argument is printed at its head. The long title of the Right to Information Act describes an Act to set out the practical regime of the right to information for citizens, to secure access to information under the control of public authorities, in order to promote transparency and accountability in the working of every public authority. The recitals that accompany it then reason in the open: the Constitution has established a democratic Republic; democracy requires an informed citizenry and transparency of information, which are vital to its functioning, to contain corruption and to hold governments and their instrumentalities accountable to the governed; and revelation of information may conflict with other public interests, including the efficient operations of governments, the optimum use of limited fiscal resources and the preservation of confidentiality of sensitive information, so that it becomes necessary to harmonise these conflicting interests while preserving the paramountcy of the democratic ideal. Every contested choice of the passage history is present in that paragraph, in the order in which the statute ranks them. Disclosure is not justified as an administrative convenience or as a concession to the press; it is derived from the structure of democracy itself, which is the Raj Narain and S P Gupta doctrine domesticated into statutory form. Corruption is named as a target of the Act, not merely as the movement's grievance. The competing interests are acknowledged candidly, and then ranked: paramountcy is assigned, in terms, to the democratic ideal, which is the interpretive instruction the commissions and courts have since taken from the text whenever an exemption has been pressed beyond its words. The drafting history explains how such a recital came to be written. A statute produced by the ordinary departmental process states its objects in the passive voice and as briefly as possible, because its authors expect to administer it. This statute was produced by a coalition of a movement, an advisory council and a parliamentary committee, none of whom expected to administer it and all of whom expected it to be resisted, so they wrote their reasons into its forehead, where a future administrator or judge would meet them first. The long title is, in this precise sense, the campaign's last jan sunwai: the record read aloud at the head of the Act, before any file is opened.

## The one hundred and twenty days: building the machine while the clock ran

The interval between enactment on 15 June 2005 and full commencement on 12 October 2005 deserves a section of its own in a passage history, because it is where the statute's designers made their most consequential wager. The wager was that a right announced four months before it could be exercised would generate its own implementation pressure, as public authorities scrambled to designate information officers, publish the seventeen heads of proactive disclosure and constitute the commissions, under the observation of a campaign that had just demonstrated its capacity to embarrass a government in public. The machinery provisions having commenced at once, none of this preparation was voluntary in law: the Central Information Commission had to be constituted, and was, with Wajahat Habibullah, a retired officer of the Indian Administrative Service, appointed the first Chief Information Commissioner in October 2005, and State Information Commissions were to follow across the country. The record of those first months was uneven in ways the passage history should state plainly. Early assessments by the Commonwealth Human Rights Initiative, which monitored the rollout state by state, recorded wide variation: some governments designated officers and issued fee rules promptly, others moved slowly, constituted their commissions late, or left citizens uncertain where a request could even be filed. The Prime Minister presented the statute as the government's own commitment, in public advocacy around its commencement and in the government's own anniversary accounting of it, a claim the campaign allowed to stand because a government that boasts of a law has taken a hostage to its enforcement. What the interval decisively achieved was the prevention of a second 2002. By 12 October 2005 there existed officers to apply to, commissions to appeal to and a date certain in the statute itself, so that the right's arrival was an event in administrative fact and not merely in the Gazette. The contrast with the Freedom of Information Act, whose rules remained in draft circulation while its commencement awaited a decision nobody took, is the whole argument for self executing commencement clauses, stated in two administrative histories rather than in theory.

## The debate: the cases made for the statute and against its reach

A legislative history owes its reader the arguments, not only the outcome, and in this case the arguments were unusually well formed on every side. The government's case, made in Parliament by the United Progressive Alliance and prepared by the Common Minimum Programme, was a case about the character of the Indian state as its citizens actually meet it. A republic in which the file is secret is a republic in which every entitlement, a pension, a ration card, a place in a school, a plot of land, a wage, depends on the unreviewable word of the officer who holds the file, and the distance between the constitutional promise and the administrative reality is filled, predictably, by intermediaries and bribes. The statute was presented as the instrument that closed that distance without creating any new benefit or bureaucracy of distribution, simply by moving the burden of justification: after October 2005 it was the state that had to explain why a record should be withheld, rather than the citizen who had to explain why it should be shown.

The passage through the two Houses in May 2005 is worth reconstructing in its own right, because its manner predicted the statute's subsequent political protection. The Bill was moved in the Lok Sabha by the Minister of State for Personnel, Suresh Pachouri, in the unusual posture of a minister commending to the House a text his own government had just amended more than a hundred times. Governments do not ordinarily advertise the inadequacy of their own drafts; that this one did so, and was applauded for it, signals how completely the committee process had redistributed ownership of the bill. The debate, spread over the taking up of the Bill on 10 May and its passage on 11 May in the Lok Sabha and 12 May in the Rajya Sabha, drew support from across the political spectrum, and its recurring notes are instructive. Speakers for the government presented the Bill as the fulfilment of the Common Minimum Programme and as an anti corruption instrument of a new kind, aimed not at punishing the corrupt after the event but at removing the secrecy in which petty corruption lives. Opposition speakers supported the measure while probing its edges, questioning the exclusion of private bodies that perform public functions, the capacity of the administration to answer within thirty days, and the adequacy of the security exemptions, questions that read in retrospect as a fair map of the Act's subsequent controversies. The Bill passed both Houses without a contested division. A transparency statute that might have been expected to divide a legislature along the line between those who hold files and those who do not instead passed as a consensus measure, and the breadth of that consensus is part of the explanation for what happened a year later, when the executive discovered that amending its own consensus statute against the campaign's opposition was a far harder exercise than enacting it had been.

## The exemptions argument: where the boundary was drawn and why it holds

Every transparency statute is two laws: the law that opens, and the law that closes. The passage history of the exemptions in the 2005 Act repays the same attention as the opening machinery, because the boundary the committee drew in 2005 explains the shape of every disclosure contest that followed. The exempt categories in Section 8(1) track the standard anxieties of a modern state: the sovereignty and security of India and relations with foreign states; information a court has forbidden; matter whose disclosure would breach the privilege of Parliament or a state legislature; commercial confidence and trade secrets whose release would harm a third party's competitive position; information held in a fiduciary relationship; confidences received from foreign governments; matter whose release would endanger a person's life or physical safety or identify a confidential source assisting law enforcement; information that would impede investigation or prosecution; cabinet papers, including the record of deliberations; and personal information without public interest in its disclosure. The campaign's argument in committee was never that these categories were illegitimate in the abstract. It was that each of them had to be fenced with the two devices that convert a list of exceptions into a discipline. The first is the public interest override: even where a clause applies, a public authority may disclose where the public interest in disclosure outweighs the harm to the protected interest, and the commissions have treated that permission as an instruction in the run of cases. The second is the twenty year rule: with the exception of the clauses protecting sovereignty and security, parliamentary privilege and cabinet papers, an exemption ceases to shelter a matter once twenty years have passed since the event it concerns, so that secrecy under the Act is a wasting asset rather than a permanent title. Comparative commentary on access legislation treats these two devices, the override and the sunset, as the markers of a statute drafted against its own exemptions, and their presence in the central text, against the financial and administrative anxieties recorded in the committee's evidence, is among the clearest evidence for the claim that the bill was remade rather than merely improved in 2005. The exemption that generated the hardest argument, the protection of the intelligence and security organisations listed in the Second Schedule, was settled by the carve-back rather than by deletion: information on allegations of corruption and human rights violations must still be disclosed even for those bodies, subject in the human rights case to the Commission's approval, a compromise that preserved the agencies' operational secrecy while denying them immunity from the two charges a democracy can least afford to exempt.

The campaign's case, made in committee evidence and in print, was narrower and more technical, and deliberately so. Its witnesses concentrated on design: on why the appeal had to leave the department, because a department does not reverse itself; on why the penalty had to fall on the officer personally, because a fine on a department is paid out of the same public funds the requester is trying to protect; on why time limits had to be stated in days rather than left to rules, because the Freedom of Information Act had shown what happened to obligations left to executive convenience; and on why the definition had to name opinions and advices, because a decision without its reasoning is an instruction to obey without the means to argue. Read against the enacted text, the committee record shows a campaign that had learned to argue like drafters, and a committee that largely accepted the arguments. The speed of the final passage, two days for the two Houses, is itself evidence: by May 2005 the contest over the text had already happened in the committee room, and the floor stages ratified a settlement rather than negotiating one.

The sceptical case was also made, in Parliament and more forcefully outside it, and it deserves to be stated at its strongest because the subsequent history of the Act is partly its history. The administrative objection ran as follows. Government depends on the frankness of advice recorded on files. If every noting is disclosable, officials will cease to record candid views, decisions will migrate to oral channels, and the record the citizen eventually inspects will be the poorer for the very right that created the inspection. A second objection concerned capacity and abuse: a small fee and an unconditional right would invite applications in vast numbers, some frivolous, some mounted by interested parties against rivals, and a thin administration would spend its hours answering requests rather than doing the work the requests were about. A third objection, pressed in the strategic establishment, concerned the breadth of disclosure in a state facing insurgency and terrorism. These were not frivolous arguments, and the Act bears their marks: the exemption clauses, the Second Schedule for intelligence organisations, the fee, and the exclusion of purely personal information all answer to them. What the sceptics did not obtain, because the committee declined to give it, was the structural protection the administration most wanted, an appellate process contained within government and a regime without personal penalties. The verdict embedded in the text is therefore a considered one: disclosure as the default, secrecy as the justified exception, and the justification policed by a body the executive does not control.

## The second drafting frontier: fees, forms and the rule books of the states

The passage of the Act settled the right and left its price to be argued over, and the history of the rule making that followed is a coda the legislative historian should not skip, because it tested whether the states, having served as the statute's laboratory, would also serve as its diluters. The central government's rules set the template the campaign had demanded: a modest application fee of ten rupees, charged at two rupees for each page of information supplied in the standard format, no fee at all for applicants below the poverty line, and no charge where the authority itself had breached the thirty day limit, so that delay made the information free. The states, framing their own rules under the Act for their own authorities, diverged in both directions. Some reproduced the central template. Others prescribed higher application fees, charged sums for forms and formats that raised the cost of asking, or drafted procedural requirements, particulars to be furnished, proofs to be attached, purposes to be hinted at, that sat uneasily with the Act's command that no reasons be sought. The monitoring organisations, the Commonwealth Human Rights Initiative prominent among them, catalogued these divergences state by state in the Act's first years, treating the rule books as a second legislative front on which the statute's generosity could be clawed back in the small print. The pattern generalises the central lesson of this history. A transparency statute is enacted once and administered daily, and its text is best understood not as a settlement but as a frontier: the commencement clause of 2005, the file notings ruling of 2006 and the fee rules of the states are engagements on the same line, the boundary between a citizenry entitled to the record and an administration habituated to keeping it. The campaign's insistence, from the muster rolls onwards, that machinery is meaning, was vindicated nowhere more thoroughly than in these unglamorous contests over ten rupees and a form.

## One year in: the file notings fight

The first attempt to reopen the settlement came from inside the executive within a year of commencement, and its subject was a phrase the campaign had planted in the statute precisely in anticipation of it. The definition of information in the Act includes, in its illustrative list, opinions and advices. The Department of Personnel and Training, the nodal department for the Act, nevertheless maintained on its official guidance that file notings, the comments recorded by successive officers on a government file as it travels toward a decision, were not covered by the Act, a position the Central Information Commission rejected in its decisions, holding that a noting is part of the record and within the definition. On 20 July 2006 the Union Cabinet approved a proposal to amend the Act to exclude file notings from its coverage, with the government indicating that notings relating to social and development projects would remain accessible. The proposal would have inverted the definition the committee had settled: the citizen would receive the decision and be refused the reasoning, which is to say she would receive the conclusion of the file while its argument stayed sealed.

The response reconstituted, in miniature, the coalition that had made the Act. The National Campaign organised a national protest; Anna Hazare began a fast in Maharashtra in support of the demand that the amendment be dropped; opposition parties, several of which had voted for the Act a year earlier, attacked the proposal as a repeal in disguise; and the press, which had been a co author of the statute from the Press Council draft onward, treated the amendment as an institutional affront. In August 2006 the government announced, through the Minister of State for Parliamentary Affairs, that on the Prime Minister's decision the amendment Bill would not be introduced in the session then sitting, citing the apprehensions expressed and the need to discuss the question with all stakeholders. It was never introduced at all. The department in time accepted the position its own statute had always stated, that file notings are information except where an exemption applies. The episode is conventionally filed as a controversy of the Act's implementation, but it belongs equally to its legislative history, because it established the political price of narrowing this particular statute. A law with a mass constituency, a press corps invested in its survival and a recent, well documented birth could not be quietly amended in its first year. The movement had not only drafted a text; it had built the conditions under which the text would be defended.

The pre history of the Cabinet's decision shows the same pattern in miniature. Even before the amendment was proposed, the department's published guidance to citizens had carried the restrictive reading, presenting file notings as outside the Act save for those touching social and development matters, so that the interpretation a citizen met on the government's own website contradicted the text she was invoking. It fell to the young Central Information Commission, under the first Chief Information Commissioner, Wajahat Habibullah, to correct the position through its decisions, holding in its early rulings that a noting is part of the record of a decision and falls within the Act's definition of information unless a specific exemption is made out. The Cabinet's July 2006 decision can be read as the executive's attempt to reverse by amendment what its own commission had decided by interpretation, which is why the campaign treated it as a constitutional impropriety as well as a policy error, and why the abandonment of the proposal settled more than a definition. The point was never reopened by any later government proposal that reached Parliament, and the definition the committee wrote in 2005, reasoning and all, is the definition the Act retains.

## The extent question: Jammu and Kashmir's parallel track

The one territorial silence in the Act of 2005 opens a window onto the constitutional geography within which the whole history unfolded. The Act by its own terms extended to the whole of India except the State of Jammu and Kashmir, an exception that was not a judgment on the state's need for transparency but a consequence of its constitutional position: under the arrangements flowing from Article 370 of the Constitution, a central statute on a subject of this character did not automatically run in the state, whose legislature retained the field for its own authorities. Jammu and Kashmir accordingly legislated for itself, first with an Act of 2004 that contemporaries judged weak beside the movement's standards, and then, after sustained campaigning within the state drawing openly on the national experience, with a stronger Act of 2009 modelled closely on the central statute, complete with its own State Information Commission. The episode is instructive in two directions. It demonstrates, first, that the central Act's design had become a template with gravitational force even where it had no legal force, the surest sign that the drafting contest of 2004 and 2005 had settled a national standard rather than a central government procedure. It demonstrates, secondly, the federal logic that runs through this entire history in both directions: the states taught the Centre, the Centre's text then taught the one state it could not reach, and the laboratory and the legislature exchanged roles across a constitutional boundary. The exception itself ended with the constitutional reorganisation of 2019, when the central Act was extended to the successor Union Territories, closing a fourteen year anomaly that the passage generation had accepted without serious contest, one of the few points on which the campaign of 2005 chose not to spend its credit.

## Amendment as continuing passage: 2006, 2019 and the politics of the second thoughts

A passage history does not wholly end at commencement, because the meaning of a statute's birth is tested by the terms on which it is later amended, and the Right to Information Act's amendment record is continuous with its enactment record in a way that illuminates both. The pattern was set within fourteen months. The file notings proposal of 2006 attempted a subtraction by definition, was defeated by the campaign's mobilisation before it reached Parliament, and established the precedent that the Act's core machinery enjoyed a political protection out of proportion to its age, a statute defended by a constituency that had written it and knew where its load bearing walls stood. The amending Act of 2019, which altered the tenure and service terms of the information commissioners and is treated in full in the companion treatment of that amendment, tested the proposition from the other side and is the more instructive episode for the historian: the amendment passed, against the opposition of the same campaign networks, which demonstrated that the protection of 2006 had been a function of a particular Parliament and a particular government arithmetic rather than a permanent settlement. Read together, the two episodes complete the argument of this article. The legislative history of the Act is not a story with an ending in 2005 but a continuing negotiation over a text, in which the side that understands the text's origins holds an advantage it must renew in each Parliament. That is precisely the position the campaign's founders said they expected, in the years when they were insisting that commencement be written into the statute and the penalty onto the officer: they had counted the governments that promised openness and failed to legislate, and they built accordingly, for opponents they had not yet met.

One strand of the debate deserves separate notice because it recurs in every transparency controversy in the common law world, and 2005 was its Indian hearing. This is the candour argument: the claim that officials who expect their advice to be read will cease to record honest advice, so that disclosure purchases accountability at the price of decision quality and drives real deliberation off the file entirely. The argument had been made, and largely discounted, in the British and Australian freedom of information debates of the preceding generation, where the predicted collapse of written advice failed to materialise at the scale foretold; officials adapted, minutes grew more careful, and government continued. The Indian committee heard the same prediction from the administrative ministries, and the campaign's answer drew directly on the file notings experience to come: the record that candour fears to lose is frequently, in the Indian file system, the record of who decided and on what material, precisely the record corruption requires to be illegible. The committee's settlement, preserving the substance notes of decisions while protecting genuinely exempt content through the clauses, and relying on severability to separate the two, treated candour as an interest to be accommodated clause by clause rather than as a veto over the statute. When the executive revived the argument in blanket form in 2006, seeking to exclude the notings wholesale, it was in effect asking Parliament to reverse the committee's adjudication of a debate it had already heard and decided, which is a further reason the proposal attracted the charge of bad faith that helped to defeat it.

## The counter reading: what the celebratory account leaves out

The standard telling of this history, a people's movement invents a method, shames a state, drafts a law and conquers a Parliament, is broadly accurate, and the records assembled in this article support more of it than sceptics usually allow. But it flattens three things that a legislative history is obliged to restore. The first is contingency. The campaign won in 2005 not only because its arguments were strong, for they had been equally strong in 2002 when they lost, but because the political architecture of 2004 gave it a transmission belt into government that has no constitutional basis and no guarantee of recurrence: a ruling coalition dependent on outside support, governing under a negotiated common programme, advised by a council on which the movement's concerns had direct representation. Critics of the National Advisory Council, including the principal opposition party, attacked it as an unaccountable centre of power parallel to the Cabinet, and while that attack was partisan in motive it identified a real feature of the episode: the most democratic statute of its decade travelled to enactment through one of the least formally accountable institutions in Indian public life. The historian has to hold both propositions, because both are true, and because the second explains the first's timing better than any account of pure persuasion.

The second flattening concerns the states. The laboratory thesis, that the states wrote the central Act, is this article's organising claim, and it is defensible at the level of design: the penalty, the time limit, the external appeal and proactive disclosure all have state antecedents. But the state laws were, in operation, modest instruments, used by few, resisted by their own administrations, and in the Tamil Nadu case drafted to be resisted. The laboratory produced prototypes, not a working regime, and the citizen of 2004 who relied on a state statute was in most states barely better placed than the citizen of 1994. The honest form of the claim is therefore about drafting, not about lived rights: a decade of state experimentation stocked the shelf from which Parliament's committee selected, tested each device against real administrative behaviour, and exposed the weak designs for what they were. That is a large contribution. It is not the same as saying the right existed in the states before the Centre created it in fact.

### What did the campaign not get in the final Act?

It did not get coverage of private bodies, which the Act reaches only where substantially financed or controlled by government, or through records a public authority can obtain. It did not get a time limit binding the Information Commissions' disposal of appeals. And it did not get the territory entire: Jammu and Kashmir stood outside the Act at its commencement.

The third flattening concerns the credit. The origin story in its popular form assigns the statute to one organisation and, within it, sometimes to one person. The record shows a longer cast: the judges who built the doctrine across three decades; the editors who treated a provincial dharna as national news; the consumer movement that ran the official drafting process of 1997; the Delhi activists who urbanised the device; Hazare's Maharashtra movement; the officials inside the system who leaked records to the early jan sunwais at personal risk and who later, as committee witnesses and ministers, carried the text; and the parliamentarians of the standing committee who did the unglamorous work of clause by clause repair. Singular credit is not only unjust to that cast; it misdescribes the mechanism, which was coalition all the way down. A reader who understands how this Act was made understands something transferable about Indian lawmaking: that a statute of consequence is assembled from doctrine, agitation, prototype and procedure in proportions no single author controls.

A final strand of the counter reading cuts in the opposite direction and should be given its weight. The celebratory account can understate the government's own agency in its victory narrative. A governing coalition determined to frustrate the statute had quieter instruments available, as the fate of the 2002 Act demonstrates: it could have introduced the narrow bill and defended it, starved the committee of time, or let the session end. It did none of these things. The National Advisory Council was its own creation, the Group of Ministers its own mechanism, and the hundred amendments its own signatures. Nor was the moment unique to this statute: the same parliamentary session produced the rural employment guarantee, another rights based law with movement parentage travelling through the same advisory channel, which suggests that 2004 and 2005 constituted an exceptional legislative season for a particular governing arrangement rather than a permanent conquest of the state by its citizens. The sober conclusion is that the campaign's achievement was real but conditional: it had prepared, over fifteen years, a complete alternative statute and the constituency to defend it, so that when an unusual political configuration opened the door, what walked through it was not a slogan but a text. Preparation of that order is the transferable lesson, and it is a harder one than either the heroic or the cynical version of the story suggests.

## What the movement bequeathed besides a statute

The passage of the Act was the campaign's object, but the campaign's methods outlived the bill and entered Indian public administration through other doors, and a passage history that ends at the statute book understates what the fifteen years produced. The jan sunwai's central device, the public verification of official records against lived experience in the presence of the record keepers, reappeared in statutory form in the rural employment guarantee legislation of the same year, whose social audit provisions require the village assembly to scrutinise the works and wage records of the programme in open meeting: the muster roll hearing of Kot Kirana, generalised into a standing institution of the Indian state. The habit of publicity travelled with it. In the movement's home districts, campaign accounts describe the pressure brought to bear on panchayats to paint works expenditure and beneficiary lists on public walls, converting the wall of the panchayat office into a notice board that no clerk could misplace, a practice whose logic the Act's proactive disclosure clauses formalised in print. The personnel travelled too. A generation of activists, lawyers, journalists and, in time, information commissioners learned the grammar of the record in the RTI campaigns of the states, and carried it into food security, health, education and electoral reform movements, each of which found its own muster roll, the list, the register, the beneficiary roll, where the fraud of its sector was written down. This diffusion matters to the legislative historian for a precise reason. The statute of 2005 was possible because a technique of accountability had been proven at village scale before it was codified at national scale, and the technique proved more portable than any single organisation. When later movements demanded audits, disclosures and hearings as entitlements, they were, often without knowing it, re-enacting the legislative history this article has traced: the discovery that in a paper state, the record is the regime, and that whoever may read the record aloud may govern the consequences.

## The scholars' statute: two readings of 2005

The historiography of this statute is unusually rich, because the movement documented itself as it went and the academy arrived early, and the two principal readings of 2005 that the literature sustains deserve to be stated as readings rather than as facts. The movement centric account, carried in the writings and speeches of the campaign's own participants and widely reproduced, presents the Act as the state's reluctant transcription of a popular invention: the method devised in Rajasthan, the drafts prepared outside government, the committee stage captured by superior preparation, the executive's subsequent retreats defeated by organised vigilance. Academic students of the Rajasthan campaign, among them Rob Jenkins and Anne Marie Goetz, whose field studies of the Sangathan's accountability politics remain standard references, supply this account with its analytical frame, treating the movement's achievement as a new kind of claim making in which poor citizens used the state's own records as their evidence and its own professed norms as their lever. The institutional account, by contrast, developed in the comparative public administration literature, reads 2005 as a case of reform by an unusual governing configuration: Alasdair Roberts, in his assessment of the Act's first four years published in Public Administration Review under the interrogative title A Great and Revolutionary Law?, treated the statute as among the most ambitious transparency laws enacted anywhere, and directed attention to the conditions, the advisory council channel, the coalition's programme commitments, the committee system, that converted a campaign text into law where identical campaigns elsewhere had stalled. The two readings are not symmetrical in their politics but they agree on the evidentiary core, which is why this article has been able to rely on both: a complete draft existed outside the state before the state wanted one, a committee enlarged rather than diluted it, and a predecessor statute's silent death taught the drafters their most important clause. Where the readings diverge, over credit and over causation, the honest legislative history follows the documents and lets the divergence stand, since a statute with two plausible parents is in no danger of being orphaned by the archives.

## The silences of the passage record: what 2005 did not decide

A legislative history is also a record of questions not asked, and the silences of 2005 map the controversies of the decades that followed with uncomfortable precision. The debates and the committee's report show little sustained attention, first, to the private sector's penumbra: bodies that are neither government nor substantially financed by it, yet deliver water, power, education and credit under public licence, stood outside the Act's direct reach, and the boundary thus drawn, between the record the citizen may demand and the record of the services she actually uses, was accepted with far less scrutiny than the exemptions that consumed the committee's hours. Secondly, the passage record contains almost nothing on the informational position of the institutions of accountability themselves: the higher judiciary, the election machinery and the legislatures appear in the Act simply as public authorities among others, and the question of how disclosure duties sit with judicial independence and legislative privilege, which would generate some of the Act's hardest litigation, was left wholly to the courts of a later generation. Thirdly, nobody in 2005 costed the statute. No financial memorandum of consequence estimated the administrative burden of a right that every citizen could exercise for ten rupees, and the commissions were sent into the world without a considered settlement of their staffing, a silence whose consequences the Second Administrative Reforms Commission was already recording within a year of commencement. The silences should not be read as negligence. They are the normal condition of legislation passed in a moment of consensus, when the agreement to enact displaces the patience to specify, and they are recorded here because they belong to the passage history as surely as the clauses do: the Act of 2005 decided the great question, who may see the record, and left the second generation of questions, whose record, at what cost, and under whose supervision, to be fought out in the commissions and courts whose contests the companion articles in this cluster chronicle.

## What the passage record predicted, and the verdict

Read in 2005, the passage record predicted the Act's future with uncomfortable accuracy. A statute whose commencement had been made self executing duly commenced, on the day its text appointed, without the notification drama that had killed its predecessor. A statute whose enforcement ran through commissions appointed and funded by the executives they policed duly developed its characteristic pathology at exactly that joint, in appointment delays and accumulating appeals, rather than at the point of the citizen's request. A definition drafted to include the reasoning on files duly generated the executive's first counter attack within a year, at exactly the word the campaign had fought to include. And a statute with this parentage, born of audit and argument, duly became the instrument its makers intended, the standard means by which citizens, journalists and opposition parties compel the state to show its record; the later contests over the tenure and independence of the commissioners who guard it, taken up in the series account of the [RTI Amendment Act 2019](/2011/11/01/rti-amendment-act-2019-explained/), are the continuation of the 2005 argument by other means. This article is a history of how the Act was made and not advice on any individual application; a reader with a live request should take advice on their own facts, since limitation periods in appeal are short and strictly applied. Subject to that caution, the verdict on the legislative history itself can be stated plainly. The Right to Information Act is the clearest modern Indian demonstration that a statute's quality is decided before the first clause is drafted, in the quality of the knowledge brought to the drafting: knowledge of what a falsified muster roll does to a wage, of what a state prototype teaches, of what a dead letter costs, and of what a committee can do to a bill whose own government has undersold it. Parliament passed the Act. Rajasthan, the states, the courts and a coalition of unlikely allies wrote it.

The verdict of the state's own review machinery arrived quickly, and its terms should be recorded because they were not a foregone conclusion. The Second Administrative Reforms Commission, appointed by the same government and chaired by Veerappa Moily, devoted its first report, delivered in 2006, to the new statute, and titled it Right to Information: Master Key to Good Governance, a bureaucratic benediction as unqualified as the movement could have wished and all the more significant for coming from a body staffed by the administrative tradition the Act had defeated. The commission's considered judgment was that the statute's design was sound and that its failures, where they appeared, would be failures of implementation: of commissions starved of staff, of proactive disclosure treated as a formality, of penalties imposed too rarely to discipline the reflex of refusal. That judgment also supplies the honest rider to every sentence of praise in this article. The passage record shows a movement and a legislature jointly producing, almost by accident of a hung Parliament and an advisory council, a statute measurably stronger than the settled preference of the administration that had to operate it. Statutes so produced live on sufferance, and the defence of this one has been a continuing legislative activity in itself, of which the abandonment of the file notings amendment in 2006 was the first engagement and the amending Act of 2019, treated in the companion article, the most consequential since.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which movement led to the Right to Information Act?

The demand was led by the Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan, a membership organisation of labourers and farmers founded in 1990 in Rajsamand district, Rajasthan. The Sangathan began by fighting for minimum wages on public works and discovered that falsified muster rolls were the mechanism of theft, which turned its struggle into a demand for the right to inspect official records. Its jan sunwai method of public audit, and the forty day Beawar dharna of 1996, carried the demand nationally, where the National Campaign for People's Right to Information, formed the same year, converted it into draft legislation and parliamentary lobbying. The Act of 2005 is the product of that sequence: a wage movement supplied the method and the pressure, and a national coalition supplied the drafting.

### Q: Did any Indian state have an RTI law before the central Act?

Yes, nine of them. Tamil Nadu and Goa legislated in 1997, Rajasthan and Karnataka in 2000, Delhi in 2001, Assam and Maharashtra in 2002, Madhya Pradesh in 2003 and Jammu and Kashmir in 2004. The quality varied enormously. Tamil Nadu's Act carried a long schedule of restrictions and no penalty, while Karnataka's tested a penalty on defaulting officers and Maharashtra's Act of 2002, extracted by Anna Hazare's campaign, was a genuinely strong statute. Most were little used, but collectively they proved that a disclosure regime could be administered, supplied tested design features to the central drafters, and created the political embarrassment that a citizen had a statutory right against her state government that she lacked against the Government of India.

### Q: What happened to the Freedom of Information Act 2002?

Parliament passed it in December 2002 and it received presidential assent in January 2003, but it was never brought into force. Its commencement depended on a notification by the Central Government, and the notification was never issued: the machinery was not appointed, the rules were still being drafted in 2004, and not a single request was ever made under it. Campaigners did not press for its activation, because the text was weak, carrying no penalty, no independent appeal body and wide exemptions. The government elected in 2004 preferred a fresh statute, and Section 31 of the Right to Information Act, 2005 repealed the 2002 Act outright, closing the career of a law that governed for zero days.

### Q: Why was the 2004 RTI Bill changed before it became law?

Because the Bill as introduced in December 2004 betrayed the promise it was meant to fulfil. It applied only to public authorities of the Union Government, leaving the states, where citizens meet the state in ration shops, police stations and land records, outside it, and its penalty and appeal provisions were weaker than the package the National Advisory Council had endorsed. The National Campaign and allied organisations protested publicly, and the government referred the Bill to a Group of Ministers and to the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Personnel, Public Grievances, Law and Justice. Between them, those forums restored the wider scheme: the committee recommended extension to the whole country, the government moved over a hundred amendments, and the Bill that passed in May 2005 covered India, not only the Centre.

### Q: Who drafted the Right to Information Bill?

No single person, and the honest answer is a sequence. The civil society text descends from the draft prepared in 1996 by the Press Council of India under Justice P B Sawant working with the National Campaign for People's Right to Information. A rival official text came from the Working Group chaired by H D Shourie, which reported in 1997 and fed into the Freedom of Information Bill of 2000. In 2004 the National Campaign drafted a set of amendments to the 2002 Act, most of which the National Advisory Council endorsed and forwarded to the Prime Minister. The government's Bill of December 2004 was then substantially rewritten through the Group of Ministers and the Standing Committee, so the enacted statute is the product of movement drafting, official drafting and parliamentary revision combined.

### Q: Was there a fight over including file notings in the RTI Act?

Yes, and it happened twice. The Act's definition of information expressly lists opinions and advices, which settled the matter textually in 2005, but the Department of Personnel and Training maintained in its guidance that file notings, the comments officers record on a file, were not covered, a position the Central Information Commission rejected. In July 2006 the Union Cabinet approved an amendment to exclude file notings, keeping only those on social and development projects accessible. The campaign mobilised nationally, Anna Hazare fasted in support, opposition parties attacked the proposal, and in August 2006 the government announced on the Prime Minister's decision that the Bill would not be introduced. It never was, and the department accepted that notings are disclosable unless an exemption applies.

### Q: What role did the National Advisory Council play in the RTI Act?

The Council, created in 2004 to oversee the United Progressive Alliance's National Common Minimum Programme and chaired by Sonia Gandhi, was the channel through which the campaign's drafting reached the Cabinet. In August 2004 the National Campaign for People's Right to Information sent the Council a detailed set of suggested amendments to the Freedom of Information Act, 2002. The Council endorsed most of them and forwarded them to the Prime Minister, and they formed the benchmark against which the government's Bill of December 2004 was judged, and found wanting. The Council did not draft the final Act, which was remade in the Group of Ministers and the Standing Committee, but its endorsement converted a civil society text into an official commitment the government could be held to.

### Q: How long did the RTI campaign take from movement to statute?

About fifteen years, on any fair reckoning. The Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan was founded in 1990 and developed the jan sunwai by 1994; the Beawar dharna and the formation of the National Campaign followed in 1996; the first state statutes arrived in 1997; the first central statute was enacted in 2002 and never commenced; and the Right to Information Act was passed in May 2005 and came fully into force on 12 October 2005. If the measurement runs from the first public hearing at Kot Kirana in December 1994 to commencement, the interval is just under eleven years. Either way, the statute was the end of a long campaign, not a sudden reform, which is why the movement's design knowledge was so detailed when the drafting moment finally came.

### Q: What was a jan sunwai and how did it expose corruption in wage records?

A jan sunwai, or public hearing, was the method the Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan developed in Rajasthan from December 1994, first at Kot Kirana in Pali district. Official records of public works, muster rolls, bills and vouchers, obtained by whatever route was open, were read aloud item by item before an assembled village, with an independent panel presiding and officials invited to answer. The audience could test the paper against their own knowledge: an entry paying a man who had migrated, a bill for material that never arrived, a road that existed only in the accounts. Contradictions were established in public, on the spot, and movement accounts record officials and representatives returning money they could not account for. The method made records, rather than allegations, the terrain of proof.

### Q: Who started the Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan and where did it work?

The Sangathan was founded in 1990 at Devdungri, a village in Rajsamand district in Rajasthan, by Aruna Roy, Nikhil Dey and Shankar Singh, together with the labourers and farmers who became its membership. Aruna Roy had served in the Indian Administrative Service and resigned in 1975 before working in rural development in the region. The organisation worked across Rajsamand, Pali, Ajmer and neighbouring districts of Rajasthan on land, water, the public distribution system and, decisively, on minimum wages in drought relief and development works. It was deliberately structured as a non-hierarchical membership organisation rather than a funded non-governmental organisation, a choice its founders regarded as protection against the dependencies that funding brings, and its working area remained rural Rajasthan even as its campaign went national.

### Q: What did the Beawar dharna of 1996 demand?

The dharna, a sit in at Chang Gate in the town of Beawar in Ajmer district beginning on 6 April 1996 and lasting forty days, demanded that the Rajasthan government honour its promise of a year earlier and give citizens a legal right to inspect and obtain copies of records of public works, beginning with the records of development expenditure in their own villages. Its distinctiveness was the demand itself: the protesters, overwhelmingly rural labourers sustained by contributions from some hundreds of villages, were not asking for food, employment or higher wages but for information, under the slogans of our money, our accounts, and we will know, we will live. The protest drew national press attention, brought journalists, lawyers and writers to Beawar, and led directly to the formation of the National Campaign later that year.

### Q: When was the National Campaign for People's Right to Information formed and what did it do?

The National Campaign was formed in Delhi in 1996, in the months following the Beawar dharna, by the journalists, lawyers, academics, retired civil servants and activists who had converged on the Rajasthan movement. It was a coalition rather than a membership organisation, and its work was drafting and advocacy: it helped prepare the Press Council draft of 1996, pressed state governments for statutes, analysed and criticised the Freedom of Information Bill of 2000 and the Act of 2002, and in August 2004 sent the National Advisory Council the amendment package that set the terms for the 2005 statute. Its members later gave evidence before the Standing Committee that reshaped the Bill. The Campaign's function across the decade was to keep a technically literate draft permanently on the table, so that whenever a political opening appeared, the text was ready.

### Q: What part did the Press Council of India play in drafting a right to information law?

The Press Council, under its chairman Justice P B Sawant, a retired judge of the Supreme Court, worked with the National Campaign to prepare a draft Right to Information Bill in 1996 and sent it to the Government of India. The draft mattered for two reasons beyond its text. It carried the authority of a statutory body and a retired Supreme Court judge, which made it harder for the government to treat the demand as agitation rather than law reform. And it embodied the civil society design, an enforceable right with an appeal outside the department and exemptions to be justified, at a time when the official drafting line, represented by the Shourie Working Group of 1997, was markedly more guarded. The architecture of the eventual Act of 2005, independent commissions and personal penalties, descends from the Press Council draft's line rather than the official one.

### Q: What was the Shourie Working Group and what did it produce?

The Working Group on Right to Information and Promotion of Open and Transparent Government was constituted by the Government of India in January 1997 and chaired by H D Shourie, the consumer rights advocate associated with the Consumer Education and Research Centre, which had circulated its own draft information legislation earlier in the decade. The Group reported in May 1997, recommending that India enact a freedom of information law and attaching a draft Freedom of Information Bill to its report. Its text was more cautious than the Press Council and National Campaign draft of the previous year, with a wider field of exemption and a weaker appellate structure, and it became the ancestor of the government's Freedom of Information Bill of 2000 and of the stillborn Act of 2002, rather than of the stronger statute that replaced them.

### Q: Did the Supreme Court recognise a right to know before Parliament legislated?

Yes, repeatedly, and the campaign built on that foundation. In State of Uttar Pradesh v Raj Narain in 1975, Justice Mathew reasoned that the people of a responsible government are entitled to know the particulars of every public transaction. In S P Gupta in 1981, Justice Bhagwati held that open government follows from the right to know implicit in the freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(1)(a), and that disclosure must be the rule and secrecy the justified exception. The Court reaffirmed the right in the Cricket Association of Bengal case in 1995 and in Dinesh Trivedi in 1997, and gave it operative force in the election disclosure cases of 2002 and 2003. What the Court never supplied was machinery, time limits, commissions or penalties, which is why a statute was still necessary.

### Q: What did the National Common Minimum Programme promise about the right to information?

The National Common Minimum Programme, the governing document of the United Progressive Alliance coalition that took office in May 2004, promised in terms that the Right to Information Act would be made more progressive, participatory and meaningful. The sentence mattered because it converted a campaign demand into a coalition commitment against which the government could be measured, and because the Programme's implementation was assigned to the newly created National Advisory Council, giving the promise an institutional address. When the government's Bill of December 2004 emerged confined to the Union Government and weaker than the Council's endorsed package, the campaign's protest was framed precisely as a breach of the Programme's own words, a framing the government found difficult to answer and which set up the Bill's extensive revision in committee.

### Q: What did the Parliamentary Standing Committee recommend on the RTI Bill?

The Standing Committee on Personnel, Public Grievances, Law and Justice, chaired by E M Sudarsana Natchiappan, examined the Right to Information Bill after its introduction in December 2004, took evidence from civil society witnesses including members of the National Campaign, and reported to the Lok Sabha on 21 March 2005. Its recommendations strengthened the Bill across its architecture, and most consequentially supported extending the statute beyond the Union Government to public authorities across the whole country, with the independent information commissions and enforcement provisions that made the right usable against state administrations as well as central ones. The government accepted the direction of the report and returned to Parliament with over a hundred amendments of its own, so the committee stage, rather than the original drafting, is where the enacted statute was substantially made.

### Q: How many amendments did the government move to its own RTI Bill before it passed?

Over one hundred. The Right to Information Bill introduced in December 2004 returned to the Lok Sabha in May 2005 accompanied by more than a hundred government amendments, an extraordinary figure for a bill of its size, reflecting how thoroughly the Group of Ministers and the Standing Committee had reworked the original text. The amendments ran predominantly in one direction, outward in coverage, from the Union Government to the whole country except Jammu and Kashmir, and upward in enforceability, in the appellate structure, the commissions and the penalty on defaulting officers. The amended Bill was taken up on 10 May 2005, passed by the Lok Sabha on 11 May and by the Rajya Sabha the following day, and received presidential assent on 15 June 2005.

### Q: How strong were the state right to information laws compared with the central Act?

Considerably weaker, with honourable exceptions. The central Act of 2005 exceeded every state statute in the combination it offered: a thirty day time limit, a personal penalty of two hundred and fifty rupees a day on the defaulting officer, appeals to independent commissions outside the administration, a proactive disclosure duty, and a public interest override of its own exemptions. Most state laws lacked the penalty and the independent appeal; Tamil Nadu's Act of 1997 was weighed down by a long list of restrictions, and usage of most state statutes was low. Karnataka's Act had tested the penalty idea and Maharashtra's Act of 2002 was a strong text, so the central drafters were selecting the best state features rather than starting afresh, but the Act as enacted set a standard none of the nine state laws had reached on its own.

### Q: Why did the RTI Act not extend to Jammu and Kashmir when it commenced?

Because of the state's constitutional position at the time. The Act's extent clause carried it to the whole of India except the State of Jammu and Kashmir, whose special position under Article 370 meant that a central statute of this kind did not apply there of its own force. Jammu and Kashmir was not left without a law: its legislature had enacted its own right to information statute in 2004, which the state later replaced with a stronger version in 2009 modelled on the central Act. The exclusion therefore reflected constitutional geography rather than a judgment that the state's citizens should have a lesser right, and it ended in 2019, when the reorganisation of the state brought the central Act into the successor union territories and displaced the separate state legislation.
