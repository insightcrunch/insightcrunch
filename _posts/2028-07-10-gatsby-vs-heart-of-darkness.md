Set the two books beside each other and the same shape appears twice. A man of no fixed origin acquires a reputation before he acquires a face. People who have never met him trade stories about where his money came from and what he did to get it. Then a second man, decent enough to be trusted and compromised enough to be interesting, travels toward him, hears the stories, meets the man, watches him fail, and afterward tells us what happened. That shape is the reason a Gatsby vs Heart of Darkness comparison is worth running at full length rather than mentioning in passing. Joseph Conrad built it in 1899. F. Scott Fitzgerald rebuilt it in 1925 on a stretch of Long Island shoreline, and the rebuilding was deliberate.

![Gatsby vs Heart of Darkness comparison of Nick Carraway and Marlow as witness-narrators - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-54.webp)

What follows is not a claim that Fitzgerald wrote an American Congo. The subjects of the two books could hardly be further apart: one is about a European extraction industry operating on another continent under a fiction of moral improvement, the other about a bootlegger buying a house across a bay from a woman he loved five years earlier. The comparison earns its keep at the level of method. Both novelists decided that the most powerful way to present a magnetic and ruined man was to refuse to enter his head, and both found the same solution: hand the book to somebody standing slightly to the side.

## What the Gatsby vs Heart of Darkness Comparison Actually Sets Side by Side

Before any real analysis begins, the terms have to be fixed, because loose comparison is what gives comparative criticism a bad name. This article compares two things and only two things: the narrative architecture of Conrad's novella and the narrative architecture of Fitzgerald's novel, and the way each architecture produces its central figure.

Conrad's book was serialized in Blackwood's Magazine across three installments in 1899 and collected in 1902. It runs to roughly forty thousand words. Charles Marlow, a seaman, sits on a yawl anchored in the Thames estuary and tells four listeners about a journey he made years earlier up a great river in Africa on behalf of a trading company. His assignment was to captain a steamer and, as it turns out, to retrieve a company agent named Kurtz from an inner station. One of the four listeners, never named, relays Marlow's account to us. Kurtz has collected more ivory than any other agent, has been spoken of by every European Marlow meets, and turns out to have made himself an object of worship among the people he was sent to administer. Marlow brings him downriver. Kurtz dies aboard. Marlow later visits the woman Kurtz was engaged to and tells her something other than what Kurtz actually said at the end.

Fitzgerald's novel appeared in April 1925 and runs to roughly fifty thousand words. Nick Carraway, a Midwesterner recently arrived on Long Island to learn the bond business, rents a small house next door to a mansion whose owner throws enormous parties. The owner turns out to be Jay Gatsby, born James Gatz in North Dakota, who has made a fortune in ways the novel never fully itemizes and who bought the house because it sits across the bay from Daisy Buchanan, Nick's cousin, whom he loved in 1917. Nick arranges their reunion. The affair collapses. Daisy, driving Gatsby's car, kills Tom Buchanan's mistress. George Wilson shoots Gatsby and then himself. Nick arranges the funeral, at which almost nobody appears, and afterward writes the book we are reading.

The structural claim of this article is simple and testable. Strip both books to their skeletons and you find the same four bones: a peripheral narrator with a professional excuse to be present, a central figure introduced by reputation long before he is introduced in person, a journey toward that figure that is also a journey into what he has done, and a final act in which the narrator misrepresents the dead man to the living for reasons the book treats as forgivable. Everything else, the setting, the moral subject, the register, differs. Those four bones do not.

### Are Nick and Marlow doing the same narrative job?

Yes, in structural terms. Both are competent professional men with a plausible reason to be near the central figure, both hear about him before meeting him, both come to admire him against their own judgment, and both survive to narrate. Their moral positions differ in degree, not in kind, and both narratives are retrospective acts of loyalty.

Fixing the comparison this tightly matters because the loose version of it, the one that says both books are about darkness in the human heart, is unfalsifiable and therefore useless in an essay. The tight version can be checked line by line, which is what the rest of this article does.

## The Documented Debt: What Fitzgerald Actually Took From Conrad

The Conrad connection is not a modern critical invention imposed on the novel from outside. Fitzgerald left a paper trail.

In the introduction he wrote for the Modern Library reissue of the novel in the summer of 1934, Fitzgerald reflected on the ten months he had spent writing it and noted, among the conditions of its composition, that he had just re-read Conrad's preface to The Nigger of the "Narcissus." That preface, first printed as an afterword to the magazine serialization in 1897, is the closest thing Conrad wrote to an artistic manifesto, and its governing demand is that written words should make the reader see. Conrad argued there that fiction working honestly on the senses does more than fiction working on argument, and that the writer's obligation runs to the visible, the specific, and the rendered rather than the explained.

That single acknowledgment is worth a great deal, because it tells us what kind of debt we are looking for. Fitzgerald did not say he was imitating Conrad's plots or his subjects. He pointed at Conrad's theory of how prose should behave. And the theory Conrad set out in that preface has a direct structural consequence: if the writer's job is to make the reader see rather than to explain, then a central character is better rendered from the outside, through what a watching person can actually observe, than from the inside through authorial assertion. Interiority explains. Observation shows.

Scholarship has pressed the point much further. James E. Miller Jr., writing on Fitzgerald's technique in the late 1950s and expanding the argument in the following decade, made the case that the leap in craft between Fitzgerald's first two novels and this third one is best explained by Conrad, specifically by the adoption of a narrator who is a character rather than a camera. Robert Emmet Long, in a detailed study of the novel's composition published in 1979, traced Conradian sources across Fitzgerald's reading and drafting and treated the resemblance as a matter of documented literary history rather than coincidence. Later comparative work has narrowed the focus to the two books at hand and to what is usually called indirect narration: the technique of building a character out of report, rumor, and delayed encounter rather than direct presentation.

### How have scholars traced the Conrad influence in criticism?

Mid-century critics including James E. Miller Jr. argued that the craft leap in Fitzgerald's third novel came from adopting a Conradian character-narrator. Robert Emmet Long's 1979 study of the novel's composition documented specific Conradian sources, and later comparative scholarship narrowed the claim to indirect narration shared by both books.

Two cautions belong here. Influence is not theft, and it is not equivalence. Fitzgerald read widely and borrowed from many places, and the parts of the novel that feel most his own, the color scheme, the party sentences, the compression of the final page, owe nothing to Conrad. The influence is architectural. It sits in how the book is built rather than in what the book is about, and an essay that treats it as anything more will overreach in ways an examiner will notice.

## Point of Contact One: The Witness Who Is Not the Hero

Start with the most obvious shared decision and the one that generates all the others. Neither book is narrated by its title figure or by an omniscient author. Both are narrated by a man who is present, implicated, and not central.

Nick establishes his credentials in the first paragraphs, and he establishes them in a way that should make a careful reader suspicious rather than reassured. His father's advice about remembering that not everyone has had his advantages produces, he says, a habit of holding back judgment, and he adds that "reserving judgements is a matter of infinite hope". Then, having claimed that habit, he spends the book judging almost everyone in it. He tells us that he is "inclined to reserve all judgements" and within twenty pages describes Tom Buchanan's body and voice with open contempt. He announces that he is "one of the few honest people that I have ever known", a sentence whose form is boastful even as its content is modest, and follows it with the drier observation that "everyone suspects himself of at least one of the cardinal virtues", which quietly undercuts the claim he has just made.

Marlow's self-presentation works on the same principle by opposite means. Where Nick advertises tolerance and delivers judgment, Marlow advertises his hatred of lies and then tells one. Both narrators, in other words, open by naming a moral standard and close by breaking it, and in both books the gap between the standard and the conduct is where the interest lives.

The reason the peripheral narrator is the right choice for this material is worth stating plainly, since it is the single most useful thing a student can take from the pairing. A magnetic man is magnetic to somebody. Magnetism is not a property that can be described from inside the magnetic person, because from the inside there is only intention, effort, and anxiety. It can only be registered by an observer. Put the reader inside Gatsby and the parties become logistics; put the reader beside Nick and the parties become spectacle. Put the reader inside Kurtz and the ivory becomes bookkeeping; put the reader beside Marlow and it becomes horror gathering in the corner of the eye.

Fitzgerald understood this well enough to make Nick say it himself. Nick's remark that "life is much more successfully looked at from a single window" is not incidental scene-setting; it is the novel explaining its own method inside the story, a move Conrad had made when he had the frame narrator describe how Marlow's meaning sat outside his tales rather than inside them like a kernel in a nut.

For the fuller treatment of how Fitzgerald handles the technique inside this novel, the series covers [narrative point of view in The Great Gatsby](/2026/01/12/gatsby-point-of-view/) in a dedicated craft article; the present piece is concerned with what happens when that technique is set beside its source.

### Why does Fitzgerald keep Gatsby offstage for two chapters?

Delay converts a man into a question. By withholding Gatsby through the Buchanan dinner, the valley of ashes, and most of the first party, Fitzgerald forces the reader to assemble him from gossip. When he finally speaks, the reader measures him against an accumulated rumor, which is exactly the effect Conrad achieves with Kurtz.

The delay is not a small thing in either book. Conrad keeps Kurtz off the page for the majority of a short novella, and the effect is that every European Marlow meets adds a layer of description that contradicts the last one. Fitzgerald compresses the same effect into his first three chapters. The reader hears about the parties in chapter one, is taken to the valley of ashes in chapter two, and attends a party in chapter three where the guests speculate that their host killed a man, that he was a German spy during the war, that he is a bootlegger. Only after all of that does Gatsby introduce himself, and he does it so quietly that Nick does not realize who he is talking to.

## Point of Contact Two: Reputation Arrives Before the Man

The second shared bone is the one most readers notice first and most essays handle worst. Both books construct their central figure out of talk.

In Fitzgerald, the talk is precisely catalogued. At the first party two girls in yellow report that their host is a bootlegger, and Nick records the remark without comment. Later a guest confides the murder rumor. Jordan Baker mentions that Gatsby once told her he was an Oxford man and adds, without evidence, that she does not believe it. Tom Buchanan, hearing the same claim, sneers that the man wears a pink suit and could not possibly have been at Oxford. Tom also volunteers his own theory: he asks whether their host is some big bootlegger and then admits, when pressed, that he did not hear it anywhere but simply imagined it, on the general principle that newly rich people usually are. Much later, after Gatsby is dead, Tom claims he picked him for a bootlegger the first time he saw him and was not far wrong.

Notice what Fitzgerald is doing there. The rumors are not all false. Gatsby did work with Meyer Wolfsheim; he did sell alcohol over drugstore counters; the money is criminal. But the rumors are also not evidence, because the people producing them have no information. Tom's accusation is correct and Tom's method is worthless, and the novel lets both facts stand. That is a more sophisticated handling of gossip than the version most student essays produce, which treats the rumors purely as mystification and then treats their eventual confirmation as a twist.

Conrad's handling is the ancestor of it. Marlow hears about Kurtz from the company's chief accountant, from the manager of the central station, from the brickmaker who has never fired a brick, and from a young Russian trader who has fallen entirely under the man's influence. Each account is shaped by the speaker's interest. The accountant admires efficiency and reports ivory totals. The manager fears a rival and damns him with concern. The Russian worships and reports transcendence. By the time Marlow reaches the inner station he has a composite portrait assembled entirely from people with reasons to lie, which is precisely the epistemological condition Fitzgerald reproduces on West Egg.

The technical name for this is delayed and mediated characterization, and the reason both writers use it is that it makes the reader do work. A character delivered whole is received passively. A character assembled from contradictory report is constructed by the reader, and readers defend what they have built. This is why both title figures survive their own exposure. We learn that Gatsby's money is dirty and his Oxford claim is nearly a lie, and we continue to care, because we assembled him ourselves and are not willing to throw away the labor.

Fitzgerald then does something Conrad does not, and it is one of the sharper differences between the two performances. He gives us a chapter of documented biography. The opening of the sixth chapter tells us flatly that the young man from North Dakota changed his name at seventeen when he saw Dan Cody's yacht, that "James Gatz that was really, or at least legally, his name," and that the figure the reader has been assembling was itself an assembly. Nick reports that the truth was that this man "sprang from his Platonic conception of himself," that "he was a son of God," and that he had to be about his Father's business. Conrad never grants Marlow that kind of dossier on Kurtz. He gives him a report Kurtz wrote and a postscript scrawled at the bottom of it that contradicts everything above, and leaves the biography a blank.

That difference is diagnostic. Fitzgerald wants the American self-invention story to be legible, because self-invention is his actual subject. Conrad wants his agent's origins to stay vague, because Kurtz is meant to be a European product in general rather than a particular European.

### Do rumor and hearsay build both title figures before they appear?

Yes, and by the same method. Conrad's Marlow assembles Kurtz from an accountant, a manager, a brickmaker, and a Russian trader, each with a motive. Fitzgerald assembles Gatsby from partygoers, from Jordan, and from Tom. In both books the reader constructs the man and then defends the construction.

## Point of Contact Three: The Compromised Loyalty of the Witness

Here the comparison stops being a matter of technique and becomes a matter of ethics, and here it produces the reading that makes the pairing worth writing about.

Both narrators end their books by protecting the dead man's memory at the cost of the truth, and both books present the choice as understandable without presenting it as clean.

Marlow, who has told his listeners that he detests lying above almost anything, visits the woman Kurtz intended to marry more than a year after the death. She asks what Kurtz said at the last. Marlow knows exactly what Kurtz said, because he was in the cabin. He tells her that the last word Kurtz spoke was her own name. The lie is a kindness, it is also a betrayal of Marlow's own stated principle, and Conrad stages it so that the reader feels both at once. The room is dark. Marlow notices that the heavens do not fall.

Nick's version is distributed rather than concentrated, which makes it easier to miss. He tells us in the second paragraph of the book that Gatsby "turned out all right at the end," a verdict delivered before any evidence, and he explains that what he objects to is not Gatsby but "what foul dust floated in the wake of his dreams." He tells us that Gatsby had "an extraordinary gift for hope" and "a romantic readiness such as I have never found in any other person." He shouts across the lawn, in the last conversation the two men have, that Gatsby is "worth the whole damn bunch put together," and immediately tells the reader that he was glad he said it, since it was the only compliment he ever paid the man, having disapproved of him from beginning to end. He tells Henry Gatz nothing that would puncture the father's pride. And at the funeral, when Owl Eyes delivers the flat verdict on the "poor son-of-a-bitch," Nick records it and moves on.

The most exact parallel to Marlow's lie is not any single sentence but the book itself. Nick's narrative is a defense brief filed for a dead bootlegger by the one person who knew both what he had done and why he had done it. The organizing sentence is the one about finding himself on Gatsby's side, and alone. Everything after it is a man who has appointed himself an advocate arranging the record.

### Does Nick lie for Gatsby the way Marlow lies for Kurtz?

Not with a single sentence, but with a whole book. Marlow tells one concentrated falsehood to Kurtz's fiancee. Nick's protection is distributed: a favorable verdict in the second paragraph, a father left with his illusions, and a narrative organized to redeem a criminal he claims to have disapproved of throughout.

The structural point underneath both is that a witness-narrator is never neutral, and both novelists knew it. The witness position is often taught as though it produced objectivity, as though standing outside the action gave a clear view. Both books argue the reverse. Standing outside the action is what makes partisanship possible, because a participant is judged by his conduct while a witness is judged by nothing at all. Marlow can lie because nobody in the drawing room can check him. Nick can compose a defense because Gatsby is dead and cannot be embarrassed by it. The series treats this problem at length in the article on whether [Nick Carraway is a reliable or unreliable narrator](/2023/06/12/nick-carraway-reliable-narrator/), and the Conrad pairing supplies the strongest external evidence that Fitzgerald built the unreliability on purpose.

## Point of Contact Four: The Frame Around the Frame

The fourth bone is the retrospective frame, and it is the point at which the two books visibly differ in machinery while agreeing in effect.

Conrad's frame is literal and doubled. A named group of men sit on a boat in the Thames at dusk. One of them, unnamed, describes the setting and then hands the narration to Marlow, who talks for the rest of the book. Occasionally the outer narrator interrupts to note that it has grown dark or that Marlow has paused. The reader is therefore always aware of two distances: the distance between the events and Marlow's telling, and the distance between Marlow's telling and the listener who relays it.

Fitzgerald internalizes the frame instead of staging it. There is no boat and no listener. But Nick tells us in the opening pages that he came back from the East the previous autumn, that he wanted the world at a moral attention, and that Gatsby is exempt from his reaction. The whole book is therefore written by a man who already knows how it ends and who has had a year to decide what it meant. The tense of the famous last paragraph, with its shift into a first-person plural that includes the reader, only works because that distance has been established from the start.

The effects converge. In both books the events arrive pre-interpreted by someone who has already drawn conclusions, and in both books the reader is invited to notice the interpretation as well as the events. That is the whole point of the technique, and it is why [frame narrative and retrospection in Gatsby](/2026/02/02/gatsby-frame-narrative/) is a subject the series treats as craft rather than trivia.

One further wrinkle deserves attention because students often raise it. Conrad's outer frame produces a specific irony that Fitzgerald has no exact equivalent for: the Thames, we are told at the opening, has also been one of the dark places of the earth, which folds imperial London into the same moral category as the river Marlow travels. Fitzgerald's closest move is the final page, where Nick imagines the island as Dutch sailors first saw it, "a fresh, green breast of the new world," and notes that "Its vanished trees, the trees that had made way for Gatsby's house," had once flattered the last and greatest of human dreams. Both endings pull back from the individual case to the civilization that produced it. Conrad pulls back at the beginning; Fitzgerald pulls back at the end.

### Where does the framing device differ between the two texts?

Conrad stages the frame physically, with an outer narrator on a boat relaying Marlow's spoken account. Fitzgerald internalizes it: Nick writes rather than speaks, a year after the events, with no listener present. The doubling is explicit in one book and structural in the other.

## Point of Contact Five: The Journey Toward the Man

The fifth bone is easy to miss in the American novel because it has been folded flat, but it is there, and finding it strengthens the pairing considerably.

Conrad's novella is organized as a physical approach. Marlow travels from a European city to a coastal station, from the coastal station overland to the central station, and from there upriver by steamer to the inner station where Kurtz is waiting. The geography is the plot. Each stage brings him closer to a man he has been hearing about, and each stage supplies another distorted portrait, so that distance travelled and information acquired increase together while clarity does not. The famous slowness of the middle section is not an accident of pacing; it is the structure insisting that you cannot arrive at such a person quickly.

Fitzgerald has no river, so he substitutes a social approach with the same shape. Nick's movement toward Gatsby happens in stages, and the stages are as carefully ordered as Marlow's stations. First he sees a figure on a lawn at night, stretching out his arms toward the dark water in a way Nick finds curious, and sees nothing beyond "a single green light, minute and far away." Second he hears the parties from his own porch. Third he is invited, attends, and meets the man without knowing it. Fourth he is taken to lunch in the city, introduced to Meyer Wolfsheim, and given a version of the life story that includes the Oxford claim, the war medals, and the sad thing that happened. Fifth he is recruited through Jordan to arrange the reunion. Sixth, only after all of this, he is given the true biography by the narrator he himself is, which is to say Fitzgerald hands the origin story to the reader over Nick's shoulder in the sixth chapter.

Six approaches, each closer than the last, each supplying a portrait that does not match the previous one. That is Conrad's river laid horizontally across a summer.

The two structures produce a shared and slightly uncomfortable effect. In both books, the closer the witness gets, the less stable the central figure becomes. Marlow expects to find a remarkable man and finds something that cannot be classified. Nick expects a bootlegger and finds a boy from North Dakota with a Platonic conception of himself. Proximity should resolve mystery. In both novels it deepens it, and both writers achieved that by making every stage of the approach an encounter with a new distortion rather than with new evidence.

There is a further echo worth noting. Both journeys have a threshold moment where the witness registers that he has crossed into a zone with different rules. Marlow's is the crossing into the river's interior, where the steamer's world contracts to the deck and the fog. Nick's is the drive over the Queensboro Bridge in the fourth chapter, where the city rises in white heaps and sugar lumps and he reflects that "Anything can happen now that we've slid over this bridge," anything at all, and that "even Gatsby could happen, without any particular wonder." The bridge is Fitzgerald's compression of Conrad's river: a crossing after which the improbable becomes ordinary. Read the two passages side by side and the debt is unmistakable, not because the language resembles Conrad's, which it does not, but because the structural function is identical.

The approach structure also explains a feature of the American novel that puzzles first-time readers, which is why so much of the first half seems to be about parties Nick did not enjoy and people he did not like. The parties are stations. Each one moves him closer while telling him something false. Reading them as social satire alone misses that they are doing the work Conrad's stations do, which is to make the reader travel toward a man through a series of unreliable informants.


## The Decisive Differences

A comparison that finds only similarity has not been run properly. Four differences are large enough to change the reading, and an essay that names them will beat an essay that does not.

The first is the moral scale of the subject. Conrad's novella is about an extraction economy operating on another continent under an official story about civilization, and the horror it produces is administrative as much as personal. Marlow sees a grove of dying laborers, a man carrying a rifle to guard a prisoner, six black shapes in chains, and a boiler rusting in the grass. Kurtz is the extreme case of a system, and the system is the point. Fitzgerald's novel is about a man who wanted a woman and bought a house. The dead are Myrtle Wilson, Gatsby, and George Wilson: three people, all of them killed in the space of a day, in a story whose largest moral crime is carelessness. Anyone who writes that the two books share a subject has not counted the bodies.

The second is what the central figure wants. Kurtz wants everything and articulates none of it coherently; his eloquence is famous precisely because it is empty at the center, and the report he writes about improving the people he governs ends in a postscript demanding their extermination. What Kurtz has become is unstable, expansive, and finally unnameable. Gatsby, by contrast, wants one exact thing, and the novel states it with almost embarrassing precision. He wants Daisy to tell Tom she never loved him. He wants to repeat the past, and when Nick objects that this cannot be done, he answers with genuine surprise: "Can't repeat the past?" and then "Why of course you can!" Kurtz's desire has no floor. Gatsby's has a floor, a date, and an address.

The third is the temperature of the prose around the central figure. Conrad's book withholds, hesitates, and qualifies; its characteristic move is to describe an event and then confess that the description has failed. Fitzgerald's book, at the moments that matter, does the opposite. It commits. The sentence about the kiss on the sidewalk in Louisville is not hedged: "He knew that when he kissed this girl, and forever wed his unutterable visions to her perishable breath," his mind would never romp again like the mind of God. That is a writer betting everything on a single clause. Conrad's method is erosion; Fitzgerald's, at the high points, is detonation.

The fourth is the position of the narrator relative to the reader's world. Marlow is a professional seaman addressing other professional men in a specific place at a specific hour; his account is bounded by that occasion. Nick is writing to nobody in particular, which means he is writing to us, and the final paragraph makes the inclusion explicit by shifting to the first-person plural. Conrad's ending closes a circle. Fitzgerald's opens one.

### Is the parallel between Gatsby and Heart of Darkness overstated?

Frequently. The shared architecture is real and documented, but the subjects are not equivalent: colonial extraction and its administrative violence sit at a different moral scale from a Long Island affair. The honest claim is that Fitzgerald borrowed Conrad's method, not his material, and adapted it to a smaller, sharper story.

That last formulation is worth memorizing, because it is the sentence that keeps a comparative essay out of trouble. Method borrowed, material distinct. Everything defensible in the pairing fits inside it, and most of what goes wrong in student writing on this subject comes from ignoring the second half.

## The Witness and the Enigma: A Comparison Table

The findable artifact for this article is a paired reading of the two books along the axes that actually carry the comparison. Call it the witness-and-enigma table.

| Axis | Heart of Darkness (Conrad, 1899) | The Great Gatsby (Fitzgerald, 1925) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Witness figure | Charles Marlow, seaman, hired to captain a river steamer | Nick Carraway, bond salesman, renting the house next door |
| Witness credential | Professional competence and a stated hatred of lying | Midwestern tolerance and a claimed habit of withholding judgment |
| Frame | Explicit and doubled: an unnamed listener relays Marlow's spoken account aboard a yawl on the Thames | Internalized: Nick writes a year after the events, no listener staged |
| Enigmatic figure | Kurtz, company agent at the inner station | Jay Gatsby, born James Gatz, host of the parties |
| Source of the enigma | Contradictory reports from an accountant, a manager, a brickmaker, and a Russian trader | Contradictory rumors from partygoers, from Jordan Baker, and from Tom Buchanan |
| Length of delay before first appearance | Most of a short novella | Three chapters of nine |
| What the figure wants | Unstable, expansive, finally unnameable | One woman, one sentence from her, and the year 1917 restored |
| Origin story supplied | Withheld; a written report and a contradicting postscript stand in for biography | Supplied in full at the opening of the sixth chapter, name change and all |
| Moral scale | An extraction economy and its administrative violence | Three deaths in a single day and the carelessness that caused them |
| The witness's final act | A direct lie to the dead man's fiancee about his last words | A book-length defense of a dead bootlegger, plus a father left with his illusions |
| Closing gesture | The frame closes; the Thames is named as another dark place | The frame opens outward; the island is imagined as the sailors first saw it |
| Effect on the reader | The reader is left holding an account that admits its own inadequacy | The reader is folded into the first-person plural of the last sentence |

The table is not decorative. Read the rows in order and the argument of this article emerges without commentary: the top half is nearly identical, the middle rows diverge on subject, and the bottom rows show two writers using the same instrument to reach opposite ends of a moral scale.

## What Each Book Illuminates in the Other

A comparison that only sorts is a filing exercise. The reason to run this one is that each book makes something in the other visible that would otherwise stay dark.

Conrad clarifies Nick. Readers who come to the novel without the Conrad frame tend to treat Nick as a transparent window with a slight midwestern accent, and then feel obscurely cheated when his judgments turn out to be inconsistent. Set Marlow beside him and the inconsistency stops looking like a flaw in the writing and starts looking like the design. Marlow's stated hatred of lying exists in the book so that his lie will land. Nick's stated habit of reserving judgment exists in the book so that his judgments will land. In both cases the narrator's self-description is not information about the narrator; it is a device that loads the ending. Once a reader sees the mechanism operating in the older book, it is very hard to unsee it in the newer one.

Fitzgerald clarifies Kurtz, or rather clarifies what Conrad gave up by keeping Kurtz's biography blank. The sixth chapter of the American novel demonstrates what happens when the writer does supply the origin: the mystery does not evaporate, it relocates. Knowing that a boy in North Dakota rowed out to a yacht and changed his name does not make Gatsby ordinary. It makes him legible in a way that is more disturbing than mystery, because the reader can now see the mechanism of self-invention working and can see that it worked, and can therefore ask the question the novel actually wants asked, which is what such a machine is for. Conrad protects his effect by withholding; Fitzgerald risks his effect by disclosing, and the risk pays. That comparison is available only when the books are read together.

There is a third illumination that runs in both directions. Both novels place a woman at the end of the witness's obligation. Marlow's lie is told to the Intended. Nick's arrangements are made in part for Henry Gatz but the emotional center of the closing chapters is Daisy's absence: no flowers, no message, no appearance. Conrad's woman is present and deceived. Fitzgerald's is absent and undeceivable. Both books, having spent themselves on a man, end by measuring him against a woman who is not going to be told the truth, and the difference between deception and abandonment is one of the sharpest small contrasts the pairing offers.

## Two Documents: The Report and the Schedule

The finest small test of the comparison is what each book does with a piece of paper.

Conrad gives Kurtz a document. Before the journey ends, Marlow comes into possession of a report the man had written for an international society devoted to suppressing what Europeans called savage customs. It runs seventeen pages, it is eloquent, and it argues that white men arriving among these people appear to them with the force of supernatural beings and may therefore exert power for good. At the bottom, scrawled later in an unsteady hand, sits a postscript demanding that the same people be exterminated. The document is the whole character in miniature: a beautiful surface, an argument that flatters its audience, and a single line underneath it that cancels the argument entirely. Marlow tears the postscript off before handing the report over, which is his first act of protective editing and a rehearsal for the lie at the end.

Fitzgerald gives Gatsby a document too, and the parallel is close enough to be worth teaching. Henry Gatz arrives for the funeral carrying a boyhood copy of a Hopalong Cassidy book, and on the flyleaf his son had written a daily schedule and a set of general resolves. The father produces it with pride. The schedule is a self-improvement program of the most ordinary kind: hours for exercise, for study, for practising elocution and poise, resolves about saving money and being better to parents. It is the most touching object in the book precisely because it is so unremarkable. And it performs exactly the function Kurtz's report performs, which is to expose the machinery of the man in a form he did not intend anyone to read.

The difference between the two documents is the difference between the two books. Kurtz's paper shows a civilizing mission with a murder written at the bottom. Gatsby's paper shows a boy in North Dakota trying to become a better person on a timetable. One document indicts a system; the other breaks a reader's heart. Both are produced by the narrator at the moment when the reader most needs the central figure explained, and both explain him by showing rather than telling, which is the Conradian principle Fitzgerald said he had been re-reading.

The narrator's handling of the paper is the other half of the parallel. Marlow physically removes the incriminating line before passing the report to a journalist. Nick does not edit the schedule; he reproduces it and lets Henry Gatz keep his interpretation of it, which is that his son was bound to get ahead. Both witnesses control what the world gets to see, and both make the same choice about which version of the dead man survives. The Conrad scene is the more shocking because the editing is literal. The Fitzgerald scene is the more insidious because the editing is invisible.

## The Moral Vocabulary of Each Witness

A subtler point of contact sits in the language each narrator reaches for when he tries to say what the central figure was.

Marlow's vocabulary is negative and apophatic. He describes Kurtz in terms of what he was not, what could not be conveyed, what the listeners will not understand because they had their policemen and their butchers and their neighbors. His most characteristic move is the confession of failure: he says the thing cannot be told and then keeps telling it. The prose circles, qualifies, and admits inadequacy, which is Conrad's way of making the reader feel a magnitude the sentences refuse to state.

Nick's vocabulary runs the other way. When he tries to say what Gatsby was, he reaches for the highest register available and commits to it without hedging. He speaks of an extraordinary gift for hope and a romantic readiness he never found in anyone else. He calls the man a son of God who had to be about his Father's business. He says Gatsby had committed himself to the following of a grail. He describes the reunion in the fifth chapter by reporting that "the incarnation was complete." That is religious diction deployed on a bootlegger, and Fitzgerald knows it. The novel's most sustained irony is that its narrator can find no vocabulary for his subject except the vocabulary of scripture and romance, and applies it to a man who sold grain alcohol over drugstore counters.

The comparison sharpens both. Conrad's witness cannot find the words and says so. Fitzgerald's witness finds words far too large and does not notice. Both writers are dramatizing the same limitation, which is that a witness attempting to convey a magnetic person will always fail, but they dramatize it in opposite directions: one by visible strain, the other by unexamined excess. An essay that catches this difference will be making an observation about prose rather than about plot, which is the harder and better kind of comparative point.

There is a practical consequence for students. Because Nick's inflation is unmarked, it is easy to mistake it for the novel's own view. It is not. The distance between what Nick says about Gatsby and what the book shows about Gatsby is where the irony lives, and it is measurable: track the religious and chivalric diction against the documented facts of the fortune, and the gap is the reading. The series treats the underlying question of whether Gatsby's aspiration deserves that diction in the article on [Jay Gatsby as a self-made man reconsidered](/2023/05/15/jay-gatsby-self-made-man/).

## Recurring Misreadings Worth Avoiding

Three misreadings recur in student work on this pairing often enough to be worth naming.

The first is treating Nick as Marlow with a lighter accent. The two narrators occupy the same structural position but they are not the same kind of person, and the differences matter. Marlow is a professional seaman who has seen a great deal, whose worldliness is earned, and whose account is delivered to men who share his frame of reference. Nick is twenty-nine, has been in New York for a matter of months, is out of his depth socially and financially, and is writing to nobody. Marlow's authority comes from experience; Nick's comes from proximity and from being the only one who stayed. Flattening the two into a single type loses the fact that Fitzgerald deliberately made his witness younger, greener, and more easily impressed.

The second is treating the Conrad influence as a secret to be revealed. It is not a secret. Fitzgerald mentioned the relevant reading himself, and the scholarship has been public since the 1950s. An essay that presents the connection as a discovery will read as underinformed. Present it as established, then do something with it, which is where the marks are.

The third is the assumption that shared method implies shared politics. Conrad's novella has been the subject of a long argument about whether its critique of imperial extraction is compromised by its own representations, an argument that has shaped its teaching for decades. None of that argument transfers to Fitzgerald's novel, which is not about empire and does not deploy the same representational apparatus. Importing the debate wholesale is a category error. The books share an instrument. They do not share a politics, a target, or a set of critical problems.

Getting these three right converts a competent essay into a strong one, because each avoided error is an opportunity to demonstrate discrimination, and discrimination is precisely what a comparative question is testing.

## The Verdict, and the Factor That Decides It

The verdict this article defends: the Gatsby vs Heart of Darkness comparison is legitimate, documented, and productive at the level of narrative method, and illegitimate at the level of subject, theme, and moral scale. The deciding factor is the treatment of the central figure's interiority.

That is a narrow criterion and it is chosen deliberately, because it is the one place where the two books can be shown to make the same choice for the same reason. Both novelists had access to omniscience and refused it. Both had access to first-person narration by the central figure and refused that too. Both settled on a witness with a job, a conscience, and a reason to be nearby, and both then built the entire architecture of the book on what such a witness can and cannot know. Once that decision is made, the rumor structure follows, the delayed entrance follows, the retrospective frame follows, and the compromised final loyalty follows. Four apparent similarities turn out to be one decision with four consequences.

Everything else in the comparison is a difference. Conrad is writing about what an economic system does to the men who administer it. Fitzgerald is writing about what a promise does to a man who believes it. The books do not share a theme, and an essay that claims they do will be arguing against the evidence in both. What they share is a solution to a technical problem, and the technical problem was the same: how to present a man whose whole power is the effect he has on other people.

## How to Write the Comparative Essay

Most comparative essays on this pairing fail in the same three ways, and all three are avoidable.

The first failure is the shopping list. The essay finds seven similarities, states them in seven paragraphs, and concludes that the books are similar. Nothing has been argued, because a list is not a claim. The fix is to identify one decision that generates the similarities and to organize the essay around that decision. For this pairing, the generating decision is the refusal of interiority, and the four bones described above are its consequences. An essay structured as decision, consequence, consequence, consequence, consequence, difference, verdict will outperform an essay structured as similarity one through seven every time.

The second failure is equivalence creep. The essay begins by comparing methods and drifts, usually around the third paragraph, into comparing subjects, and by the conclusion is claiming that both books are about the darkness in the human heart or the corruption of the ideal. That claim cannot survive contact with either text. Conrad's book is specific about an economy and a continent. Fitzgerald's is specific about a bay, a summer, and a woman. The fix is a sentence written into the introduction and enforced throughout: this essay compares how the two books are built, not what they are about. Then keep the promise.

The third failure is unsourced influence. The essay asserts that Fitzgerald was influenced by Conrad and offers nothing, which invites the reader to wonder how the writer knows. The fix is to cite what is actually on the record: Fitzgerald's own 1934 introduction and its reference to Conrad's preface, and the line of scholarship from Miller through Long that developed the technical claim. An essay that grounds the influence in evidence has earned the right to build on it.

A workable thesis for a standard comparative paper might run something like this. Both novels solve the same problem, how to render a man whose power is entirely the effect he produces on others, by handing the narration to a compromised witness, and the shared solution generates their shared architecture of rumor, delay, retrospection, and final loyalty; but the identical method produces opposite books, because Conrad uses it to indict a system while Fitzgerald uses it to mourn a promise. That thesis is specific, arguable, checkable, and it contains its own counter-argument, which is what examiners reward.

For evidence selection, the highest-yield passages on the Fitzgerald side are the opening paragraphs where Nick establishes and undermines his own neutrality, the party gossip in the third chapter, the origin narrative that opens the sixth, the exchange about repeating the past, the lawn compliment in the eighth, and the funeral and final page in the ninth. On the Conrad side, the highest-yield material is the frame opening, the sequence of secondhand descriptions of Kurtz, the report with its postscript, and the final interview. Six passages against four is a reasonable weighting for an essay on a course that centers the American novel.

One more piece of practical advice. Do not summarize Conrad's plot at length. Examiners assume familiarity or will accept a two-sentence orientation, and every paragraph spent on the journey upriver is a paragraph not spent on the argument. State what is needed for the comparison, then compare. The series article on writing [comparative essays using this novel](/2028/09/04/gatsby-self-made-man-literature/) treats the related lineage of self-made figures in fiction and is useful for widening the frame if a longer paper is required.

A note on secondary sources. Comparative papers on this pairing benefit enormously from naming the critics rather than gesturing at consensus. Writing that scholars have noted the Conrad connection is weaker than writing that Miller argued the technical case in the late 1950s and Long documented the compositional evidence in 1979, and the second version costs no more words. The same discipline applies to Conrad's side: the long argument over what the novella does and does not indict has named participants and dated interventions, and a paper that treats it as a background hum will look less informed than one that treats it as an argument with positions.

Students working with a second text under exam conditions should also resist the temptation to allocate equal space. A comparative question set on the American novel wants the American novel foregrounded, with the second text used as the instrument that reveals it. Sixty forty is a defensible split. Fifty fifty usually produces two half-essays.

## Closing Verdict

Two witnesses circling an enigma. That is the shape both books make, and naming it is the most useful thing this comparison produces.

Conrad found the shape first. He needed a way to present a man who could not be presented directly, because the whole point of Kurtz is that he is an effect rather than a person, and he solved it by inventing a narrator who could go and look. Fitzgerald, who told us himself that he had been re-reading Conrad's argument about how prose should behave, took the same solution and pointed it at a different target. The result was a novel whose narrator is a witness rather than a hero, whose title figure arrives by rumor, whose events reach us pre-judged by a man who has had a year to think, and whose last act of narration is an act of loyalty to somebody who does not deserve it.

The comparison earns nothing if it stops at resemblance. It earns a great deal if it is used to ask why the resemblance exists, and the answer is that both writers faced the same technical impossibility and reached for the same instrument. What they did with the instrument could hardly be more different. Conrad turned it on an empire and produced an indictment. Fitzgerald turned it on a bootlegger with a green light in his sightline and produced an elegy.

One last observation for readers who want to carry the comparison further. The witness-narrator did not stop with these two books. The peripheral teller who reconstructs a greater figure has continued to appear across the century that followed, in novels about ambition, in novels about crime, and in a good deal of the film that grew out of both. Every one of those descendants inherits the same bargain: intimacy purchased at the price of neutrality. Conrad wrote the terms, Fitzgerald refined them, and the form has been paying out ever since.

Read together, the two books demonstrate something that neither demonstrates alone: that narrative method is not neutral packaging around content but the thing that decides what content can be. Move the narrator and you move the book. That is the finding, and it is the reason this pairing keeps returning to reading lists long after the fashion for source-hunting has passed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How does The Great Gatsby compare to Heart of Darkness?

The two books share an architecture rather than a subject. Both hand the narration to a peripheral professional man who has a legitimate reason to be near the central figure, both introduce that figure through contradictory rumor long before he appears in person, both are told retrospectively by someone who already knows the ending, and both close with the narrator protecting the dead man's reputation at the cost of the truth. The subjects diverge completely. Conrad is writing about an extraction economy operating under a fiction of moral improvement; Fitzgerald is writing about a man who bought a house across a bay from a woman he loved in 1917. The productive comparison stays at the level of method and refuses to slide into claims about shared themes.

### Q: How are Nick and Marlow alike as witness-narrators?

Both are competent professional men, both have an occupational excuse to be present, and both open their accounts by announcing a moral standard that the narrative will later show them breaking. Marlow declares that he detests lying and ends by telling one. Nick declares a habit of withholding judgment and spends the book judging. In both cases the self-description is not information about the narrator but a device that loads the ending. Both are also admiring against their own better judgment: Marlow finds himself defending a man he knows to be monstrous, and Nick tells us he disapproved of Gatsby from beginning to end while composing a book that redeems him. The witness position, in both books, produces partisanship rather than neutrality.

### Q: How do Gatsby and Kurtz function as enigmatic figures?

Both are constructed from testimony rather than presented directly, and in both cases the testimony comes from people with reasons to distort. Marlow collects accounts from a company accountant, a station manager, a brickmaker, and a Russian trader, each shaped by ambition or fear or worship. Nick collects rumors from partygoers who say their host is a bootlegger or a murderer or a wartime spy, from Jordan Baker who doubts the Oxford claim, and from Tom Buchanan who invents an accusation that happens to be correct. Because the reader assembles both men out of contradictory scraps, the reader becomes invested in the assembly, which is why neither figure collapses when the unflattering facts arrive. Enigma is generated by method, not by concealment.

### Q: What did Fitzgerald owe to Conrad's method?

The debt is architectural rather than thematic. Fitzgerald acknowledged in the introduction he wrote for the 1934 Modern Library reissue that he had just re-read Conrad's preface to The Nigger of the "Narcissus" while working on the book, and that preface argues that fiction should make the reader see rather than explain. The structural consequence of that principle is a character rendered from outside by an observer instead of from inside by authorial assertion. Everything that follows in the novel, the delayed entrance, the rumor structure, the retrospective frame, the compromised narrator, is downstream of that one decision. What Fitzgerald did not take was subject, setting, register, or moral scale, all of which are entirely his own.

### Q: Is the parallel between the novels overstated?

Often, yes. The overstatement usually takes the form of claiming that both books are about the corruption of an ideal or the darkness in the human heart, which is vague enough to be true of most serious fiction and therefore argues nothing. It also flattens a real difference in moral scale: Conrad's novella records chained laborers, a grove of the dying, and an administrative violence operating on a continental scale, while Fitzgerald's novel records three deaths in a single day caused by carelessness. The defensible version of the claim is narrow and holds up well: Fitzgerald borrowed Conrad's narrative method and adapted it to entirely different material. Method borrowed, material distinct.

### Q: How do both narrators reconstruct their subject indirectly?

Each narrator builds his subject in layers, and the layers contradict each other. Conrad's Marlow spends most of the novella collecting descriptions of a man he has not met, so that by the time he arrives at the inner station he carries a composite portrait assembled from unreliable informants. Fitzgerald compresses the same effect into three chapters: the parties are described before the host is named, the host's crimes are speculated about before his face is described, and when he finally introduces himself Nick does not recognize who he is speaking to. The technique is usually called delayed and mediated characterization. Its effect is to transfer the work of construction from writer to reader, which makes the resulting figure harder to dismiss.

### Q: Did Fitzgerald read Conrad before writing his 1925 novel?

Yes, and he said so. In the introduction written for the Modern Library reissue in the summer of 1934, Fitzgerald reflected on the ten months he had spent on the book and noted that he had just re-read Conrad's preface to The Nigger of the "Narcissus" during that period. Conrad was among the writers Fitzgerald read closely and admired, and the reading is well documented in the biographical record. Scholars including James E. Miller Jr. and Robert Emmet Long built substantial technical arguments on that foundation, tracing the leap in craft between Fitzgerald's earlier novels and this one to the adoption of a Conradian character-narrator rather than to general maturation.

### Q: Which scenes place Kurtz and the bootlegger in the same structural slot?

The clearest pairing is the delayed first appearance. Conrad withholds Kurtz through the coastal station, the central station, and most of the river journey, and every stop adds a contradictory account. Fitzgerald withholds Gatsby through the Buchanan dinner, the valley of ashes, and most of the first party, and every scene adds a rumor. The second pairing is the final interview: Marlow visits Kurtz's fiancee and misrepresents his last words, and Nick manages Henry Gatz and the funeral and composes a defense of a man he claims to have disapproved of throughout. The third is the document scene, Kurtz's report with its contradicting postscript beside the schedule in the back of Gatsby's boyhood book.

### Q: Why does delayed entrance matter so much to both authors?

Delay converts a person into a question, and questions hold readers in a way that answers do not. A character who walks on in chapter one is received passively, evaluated once, and filed. A character talked about for a hundred pages before he speaks has already been imagined by the reader in several incompatible versions, so his actual arrival becomes an event with stakes. There is a second effect that matters more for these two books. Because the reader has assembled the figure from fragments, the reader has invested labor in him, and readers defend what they have built. That investment is why neither Kurtz nor Gatsby collapses when the damaging evidence arrives.

### Q: Do rumor and hearsay build both title figures before they appear?

Entirely, and both writers make sure the rumors come from compromised sources. In Conrad the informants are a station manager who fears a rival, a brickmaker angling for advancement, and a young trader who has fallen under a spell. In Fitzgerald the informants are partygoers who have never met their host, a golfer who doubts the Oxford story without evidence, and Tom Buchanan, who admits under questioning that he did not hear the bootlegging claim anywhere but simply imagined it on the general principle that newly rich people usually are. Fitzgerald's sharper move is to let the baseless accusation turn out to be true, so that the reader learns something about method as well as about Gatsby.

### Q: What separates a jungle station from a Long Island lawn as a moral testing ground?

Scale, system, and consent. Conrad's setting is an outpost of an extraction economy where violence is administrative, continuous, and carried out under an official story about improvement, so the individual agent is a symptom of a machine that would run without him. Fitzgerald's setting is a private lawn where the harm is intimate, occasional, and produced by carelessness rather than policy, and where the people damaged are damaged by neighbors rather than by an institution. The difference matters for essays because it determines what each book can indict. Conrad can indict an empire. Fitzgerald can indict a class, a marriage, and a summer, which is a smaller target hit much harder.

### Q: Are Marlow and the Long Island bond man equally compromised by their loyalty?

They are compromised differently, and the difference is instructive. Marlow's compromise is concentrated into one act at one moment: he tells the fiancee that Kurtz spoke her name at the end, knowing that he did not. Nick's compromise is diffuse and structural. He delivers a favorable verdict on Gatsby in the second paragraph, before presenting any evidence; he leaves Henry Gatz with his illusions intact; and he organizes an entire narrative to redeem a criminal he claims to have disapproved of from beginning to end. Marlow lies once and knows it. Nick lies by arrangement and never quite admits it, which arguably makes him the more thoroughly implicated of the two.

### Q: Which of the two witnesses ends up lying for the man he watched?

Both do, but only Conrad stages it as a scene. Marlow's falsehood to the Intended is explicit, deliberate, and told by a man who has already announced that he hates lying more than almost anything, which is why the moment carries the weight it does. Nick never tells a comparable single lie, and readers who look for one will not find it. His equivalent is the book itself, which selects, arranges, and interprets in a dead bootlegger's favor. The sentence that gives the game away is his admission that he found himself on Gatsby's side, and alone. Everything after that is advocacy conducted by a man who never formally announces that he has become an advocate.

### Q: Does imperial plunder map onto bootleg money in any useful way?

Only loosely, and the mapping should be handled with care in an essay. Both fortunes are illegitimate and both are laundered through respectability, and in both books the people who benefit prefer not to ask questions about the source. That much is a real parallel. But the differences are larger than the resemblance. Conrad's ivory is extracted through forced labor from people who have no recourse, on a scale that requires state cooperation. Gatsby's alcohol is sold to customers who want it in a country that briefly criminalized a legal product. Treating the two as morally equivalent will look naive to an examiner. Treating them as two versions of respectability built on unexamined money is defensible.

### Q: How should a student structure a paired study of these two works?

Organize around one generating decision rather than a list of similarities. Open by naming the shared problem both novelists faced, which is how to present a man whose power is entirely the effect he has on others. Identify the shared solution, which is the compromised peripheral witness. Then take the consequences in order: rumor structure, delayed entrance, retrospective frame, final act of loyalty. Devote a substantial section to the decisive differences in subject and moral scale, since an essay that finds only similarity has not compared anything. Close with a verdict that names the deciding factor. A sixty forty split favoring the novel on the syllabus is usually the right weighting.

### Q: What does each title figure want that the other does not?

Kurtz's desire has no floor. What he has become in the interior is expansive, unstable, and finally unnameable, and Conrad signals this by making his eloquence famous and its content empty: the report he writes about improving the people he governs ends in a scrawled postscript demanding the opposite. Gatsby's desire is embarrassingly precise. He wants Daisy to tell Tom she never loved him, and he wants the summer of 1917 restored on its original terms. When Nick objects that the past cannot be repeated, Gatsby is genuinely startled and insists that of course it can. One man wants everything and cannot say what. The other wants one sentence from one person and can say it exactly.

### Q: How have scholars traced the Conrad influence in criticism?

The technical argument developed through the middle of the twentieth century. James E. Miller Jr., writing on Fitzgerald's fictional technique in the late 1950s and expanding the case in the following decade, argued that the leap in craft between Fitzgerald's first two novels and his third is best explained by the adoption of a Conradian character-narrator rather than by general maturation. Robert Emmet Long's 1979 study of the novel's composition documented Conradian sources across Fitzgerald's reading and drafting in detail. Later comparative scholarship narrowed the focus to these two books specifically and to indirect narration as the shared technique, which is where the discussion largely sits today.

### Q: Does the shorter work's frame-within-a-frame have a counterpart on West Egg?

Not a literal one. Conrad stages his frame physically: named listeners on a yawl in the Thames estuary at dusk, one of them relaying Marlow's spoken account and occasionally interrupting to note the failing light. Fitzgerald has no boat, no listener, and no outer voice. What he has instead is temporal distance built into the narration from the opening pages, where Nick tells us he came back from the East the previous autumn wanting the world at a moral attention. The effect converges even though the machinery differs: in both books the events arrive already interpreted by a man who has had time to decide what they meant, and the reader is invited to notice the interpreting.
