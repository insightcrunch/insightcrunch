Almost every argument about the Prevention of Money Laundering Act 2002 begins at the wrong end. The public argument is about who has been raided, arrested or attached, and it is conducted as if the statute were simply a power the Enforcement Directorate holds. It is not. It is a machine with a dependency built into its centre, and everything about how it behaves in real cases follows from that dependency. The offence of money laundering is a standalone offence, with its own definition, its own punishment and its own courts, yet it cannot exist at all unless a completely different crime, listed in a schedule at the back of the Act and usually investigated by a completely different agency, has first generated property. Remove the other crime and the offence collapses, the attachment falls, and the prosecution has nothing to prosecute. Keep that single structural fact in view and the rest of the statute, its extraordinary powers, its reversed burdens, its strange procedural habits and its political charge, becomes intelligible. Lose sight of it and every debate about the Act degenerates into competing descriptions of the same raids.

![The Prevention of Money Laundering Act 2002 and the dependency chain running from the scheduled offence through attachment to trial - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-43.webp)

This article is the pillar treatment of the statute. It sets out what the Act was built to do and why it was built under external pressure rather than domestic demand, how its seventy-five sections are organised, which provisions carry the weight, how attachment rather than conviction became its operative sanction, what the courts have settled about its working parts and what they have left open, and how the strongest case for the design and the strongest case against it each run. The constitutional litigation over the Act is owned by the companion article on the Supreme Court judgment, and the amendment history is summarised here only where the operative text cannot be understood without it. What follows concentrates on the statute as a working system: the dependency chain that drives it, the timelines that govern it, and the points at which a real case is actually won or lost.

## Why this statute exists: an imported design

The Prevention of Money-Laundering Act, 2002 is one of the few major Indian criminal statutes that Parliament enacted primarily because the world asked it to. The idea that laundering the proceeds of crime should itself be a crime, separate from the crime that produced the money, was assembled internationally in the last decades of the twentieth century. The United Nations Convention against Illicit Traffic in Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances, adopted at Vienna in 1988, obliged signatories to criminalise the conversion and concealment of drug money. The Financial Action Task Force, created by the Group of Seven at the Paris summit of 1989, turned that obligation into a standing set of recommendations against which national regimes are periodically assessed, and the Basel Statement of Principles of 1988 had already warned banks that the banking system itself was the instrument through which dirty money is cleaned. The Political Declaration and Global Programme of Action adopted by the United Nations General Assembly in February 1990, and the declaration that followed the Assembly's special session on drugs in June 1998, called on member states in terms to enact national money laundering legislation. The United Nations Convention against Transnational Organized Crime, adopted at Palermo in 2000, generalised the obligation beyond drugs to serious crime as a whole.

India's statute recites this lineage on its face. The preamble to the Act refers to the two United Nations declarations, and the statement of objects and reasons accompanying the Bill named the Vienna Convention, the Basel principles and the Financial Action Task Force recommendations as the commitments being implemented. The statute exists, in other words, substantially because of an external commitment rather than because any Indian constituency demanded it, and that origin explains several of its most unusual features. A domestically generated criminal statute ordinarily grows out of the Penal Code's habits: a defined act, a culpable mind, a trial before an ordinary court, a burden on the prosecution throughout. This statute was drafted to satisfy an international template whose premise is different. The template assumes that laundering is by design concealed, that the trail of property is the only reliable evidence, that the person best placed to explain the provenance of an asset is the person holding it, and that a regime which waits for a completed trial before touching the property will touch nothing, because the property will have moved. The reverse burden, the attachment machinery that operates before any finding of guilt, the summons power exercisable against persons who are not accused of anything, and the standalone offence bolted onto other people's crimes are all consequences of that premise. They are unusual in Indian criminal law because they were not designed within Indian criminal law's traditions; they were imported with the template.

The legislative route was slow even by the standards of imported legislation. A Bill was introduced in the Lok Sabha in 1998 and examined by the Standing Committee on Finance, whose recommendations reshaped parts of it; the Bill lapsed with the House, a fresh Bill followed in 1999, and the Act was finally passed at the end of 2002. It received the assent of the President on 17 January 2003 as Act 15 of 2003, keeping the short title of the year of its passage. It then waited. The Act was brought into force only on 1 July 2005, roughly two and a half years after assent, and that gap is itself instructive about the kind of statute this is. A conventional penal statute needs nothing but a commencement notification. This one needed an apparatus: rules for the maintenance of records by banks and financial institutions, an Adjudicating Authority to hear attachment cases, an Appellate Tribunal to hear appeals from it, a financial intelligence unit to receive and analyse the reports the Act requires, and a directorate staffed to investigate. The Act and its principal rules were commenced together on 1 July 2005, and the Directorate of Enforcement, an organisation that began life in 1956 as an enforcement unit administering foreign exchange regulation, became the investigating agency for the new offence. A statute whose commencement has to wait for its machinery to be built is a statute whose centre of gravity is administrative rather than courtroom-based, and the enforcement record examined later in this article bears that out.

India's engagement with the template did not end with enactment. India joined the Financial Action Task Force as a full member in 2010, having been an observer before that, and the periodic mutual evaluations conducted under the Task Force's methodology have repeatedly shaped the amendment agenda, a point that matters when the amendment arc is considered below: several of the most consequential widenings of the Act, including the clarification that simple possession of proceeds is itself the offence, were made in response to observations recorded in that evaluation process rather than to any domestic scandal or committee report. The mutual evaluation adopted in 2024 placed India in the regular follow-up category, the outcome recorded for jurisdictions whose frameworks are assessed as substantially in place with identified improvements outstanding, and its discussion of the enforcement side repeated the pattern this article documents from the domestic record: strength in identifying and attaching assets, and a thinner record in completed prosecutions. The loop is worth noticing because it explains the statute's legislative metabolism. A domestic criminal statute is amended when a scandal, a committee or a court exposes a defect. This statute is amended when an external assessment cycle identifies a gap against an international template, which is why its amendment history reads as a sequence of clarifications and widenings adopted in Finance Acts with limited parliamentary debate, and why the constituencies that scrutinise it most closely sit as much in the evaluation process as in Parliament.

## The architecture of the Prevention of Money Laundering Act 2002

The Act contains seventy-five sections arranged in ten chapters, followed by the Schedule, and its organisation rewards a reader who sees it as two statutes sharing a cover. The first statute, in Chapters Two, Three, Five, Six and Seven, is an enforcement code: it defines the offence, arms officers with powers of attachment, search, seizure, summons and arrest, creates an Adjudicating Authority and an Appellate Tribunal, and sends trials to Special Courts. The second statute, in Chapter Four, is a reporting code: it conscripts banks, financial institutions, intermediaries and designated businesses into producing the paper trail on which the first statute feeds, obliging them to verify the identity of their clients, maintain records of transactions for five years, and furnish information to the financial intelligence unit. The two halves meet in the definition of proceeds of crime. The reporting half exists to make property visible; the enforcement half exists to make visible property freezable. Most commentary treats Chapter Four as an administrative annex. In the working of the Act it is the foundation, because the Directorate's knowledge of where property sits almost always begins in records that a reporting entity was obliged to create.

Chapter One contains the definitions, and two of them do more work than any others. The definition of proceeds of crime in Section 2(1)(u) is analysed in its own section below. The definition of a scheduled offence in Section 2(1)(y) ties the Act to the Schedule. Chapter Two, containing only Sections 3 and 4, states the offence and its punishment, and is the shortest operative chapter in the Act; everything else in the statute is machinery for those two sections. Chapter Three, Sections 5 to 11, contains the attachment and adjudication code: provisional attachment, adjudication, the taking of possession, and the vesting of confiscated property in the Central Government. Chapter Four, Sections 12 to 15, contains the obligations of reporting entities and the penalty for failing them. Chapter Five, Sections 16 to 24, contains the powers of survey, search and seizure, search of persons, arrest, retention of property and records, and, at Section 24, the burden of proof. Chapter Six, Sections 25 to 42, constitutes the Appellate Tribunal and governs appeals to it. Chapter Seven, Sections 43 to 47, creates the Special Courts and contains, in Section 45, the bail code. Chapter Eight, Sections 48 to 54, constitutes the authorities, including the Director and the Adjudicating Authority, and contains at Section 50 the summons power. Chapter Nine, Sections 55 to 60, deals with reciprocal arrangements with other countries for assistance and for the transfer and attachment of property across borders. Chapter Ten, Sections 61 to 75, is the miscellaneous chapter: it applies the Code of Criminal Procedure where the Act is silent, penalises false information and vexatious search, makes companies and their responsible officers liable for offences committed by the company, gives the Act overriding effect, and empowers the Central Government to make rules and to amend the Schedule by notification, a delegated power whose significance is easy to miss. The Schedule itself can grow without a fresh Act of Parliament each time, and it has grown, repeatedly, which is why the Schedule is treated below as the statute's true centre of political gravity.

One structural consequence follows from this architecture and is worth stating plainly before the detail begins. Because the offence chapter is two sections long and the machinery chapters are seventy-three sections long, disputes under this Act are almost never about whether the conduct described in Section 3 sounds blameworthy. They are about whether the machinery was set in motion lawfully: whether a scheduled offence exists, whether the property is proceeds of that offence, whether the officer recorded the required belief, whether the adjudication timelines were met, whether the bail conditions were applied. The statute is litigated at its joints, and the sections that follow take the joints in turn.

## The Schedule: where the Act's real reach is decided

Every proceeding under the Act begins with a question that is not about money at all: was a scheduled offence committed, and did it generate property? A scheduled offence is an offence listed in the Schedule to the Act. The Schedule is organised in three parts. Part A lists offences under a long series of enactments, including specified offences under the Indian Penal Code, the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act, the Explosive Substances Act, the Arms Act, the Wild Life (Protection) Act, the Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, the [Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988](/2011/12/15/prevention-of-corruption-act-1988/), whose offences supply a substantial share of the public-sector cases the Directorate pursues, and the [Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967](/2011/05/15/uapa-1967-complete-guide/), whose inclusion is what allows terrorist financing to be pursued as money laundering rather than only as a terrorist act. No monetary threshold applies to Part A: a scheduled offence there can found a money laundering case whatever the sum involved. Part B is far narrower than it once was and consists of the offence of false declaration or false documents under Section 132 of the Customs Act, 1962, which counts as scheduled only where the total value involved is one crore rupees or more. Part C captures offences with cross border implications, meaning conduct abroad that would have been a scheduled offence if committed in India whose proceeds are brought into India, and scheduled offences committed in India whose proceeds are transferred or sought to be transferred abroad.

The Schedule's history is the single most consequential story in the Act's development, because the Schedule is the tap that controls how much of Indian criminal life can generate a money laundering investigation. As commenced in 2005, the Schedule was divided between a Part A of grave offences and a Part B whose offences counted only where the value involved reached thirty lakh rupees or more. The amendment of 2009 added Part C and the cross border category. The amendment that took effect on 15 February 2013 then restructured the Schedule fundamentally: the offences that had sat in Part B behind the thirty lakh threshold were moved into Part A, where no threshold applies, the threshold survived only for the narrow Customs false-declaration entry, and a series of further enactments was added to the list. In one legislative move, the monetary filter that had kept lower-value crime outside the Act was removed for the great bulk of the Schedule. Later additions widened the list further, bringing in, among others, offences under the Companies Act relating to fraud, offences under the securities laws, and offences under environmental and intellectual property statutes that sit oddly beside the drug trafficking and terrorism offences with which the Schedule began. The Schedule that a reader finds in the Act is therefore not the Schedule of 2005 with additions at the margin. It is a list that reaches from waging war against the state to trademark and copyright offences, and the breadth is the critics' strongest textual point and the enforcement side's recurring embarrassment in argument, because the extraordinary machinery of the Act, designed against organised crime, is available in principle wherever a listed offence has produced property, however modest the offence or the sum.

The roll of enactments whose offences appear in Part A conveys the breadth better than any summary. Alongside the Penal Code offences, the narcotics offences that were the design's original target, and the explosives, arms and anti-trafficking entries, the list takes in offences under the Transplantation of Human Organs Act, the Biological Diversity Act, the Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights Act, the Copyright Act and the Trade Marks Act, the environment and pollution statutes, the Emigration Act, the Passports Act, the Antiquities and Art Treasures Act, the Child Labour and bonded labour statutes, the securities laws, the Companies Act offences including fraud under Section 447, and the Information Technology Act. A list assembled in this way has no organising principle of gravity. Its organising principle is the generation of property: any offence that can produce money or assets is a candidate for inclusion, and successive governments have included offences on exactly that logic, each inclusion defensible in isolation and transformative in aggregate, because each one multiplies the number of ordinary investigations by other agencies that can serve as the foundation for the Directorate's parallel proceeding with its reversed burdens and its attachment-first procedure.

The manner of the Schedule's growth deserves a paragraph of its own, because it explains why the breadth is never corrected. Each addition arrives as a small, technical amendment, often one clause in a Finance Act or in an amendment bill whose headline subject is something else, adding an enactment's offences to Part A on the stated ground that the offence generates proceeds and international standards expect coverage. No single addition is large enough to organise opposition around, there is no constituency whose interest lies in keeping an offence off the list, and the evaluation process supplies a standing external reason to add and no corresponding reason to subtract. The result is a ratchet: the Schedule moves in one direction only, and the debate about the Act's breadth is therefore never a debate about a text Parliament chose at a moment in time but about an accumulation no single Parliament quite chose. When the constitutional challenge invited the Court to confront the disparity between the Schedule's gravest and slightest entries, the Court's answer was in substance that the choice of predicate offences is a legislative judgment into which the laundering offence's own conditions, the requirement of proceeds and of knowing dealing, import the necessary discipline. That answer places the discipline exactly where this article has located it, not in the list but in the dependency, which is why the threshold litigation under the Act is fought over proceeds and predicates rather than over the Schedule's composition as such.

Two disciplines keep that breadth from being unlimited, and both come from the definition of the offence rather than from any threshold. The first is that a scheduled offence must actually have been committed and must have generated property; suspicion of an unlisted crime, or of a listed crime that generated nothing, starts nothing. The second is the narrowing performed by the Supreme Court in Pavana Dibbur v. Directorate of Enforcement, decided on 29 November 2023. Section 120B of the Penal Code, criminal conspiracy, is a scheduled offence, and the Directorate had treated a conspiracy charge as sufficient foundation even where the offence conspired about was not itself scheduled, which would have allowed the Act to be attached to almost any crime whatever, since conspiracy can be alleged in almost any case. A Bench of Abhay Oka and Pankaj Mithal JJ. rejected that use of the provision: an offence under Section 120B becomes a scheduled offence only if the conspiracy alleged is a conspiracy to commit an offence specifically included in the Schedule, for otherwise every unscheduled offence could be converted into a scheduled one by the simple device of alleging an agreement. The same judgment settled a second foundational point: it is not necessary that the person prosecuted for money laundering be an accused in the scheduled offence case. A person who never participated in the predicate crime but who knowingly handles its proceeds is exactly the person the offence was drafted to reach. The dependency, in other words, is on the existence of the scheduled offence and its proceeds, not on the identity of the predicate offender or on the laundering accused having played any part in the predicate.

Practice has generated a family of threshold questions about the predicate that the pillar should record, because they recur in case after case. The scheduled offence need not have been committed by the person the Directorate proceeds against, as Pavana Dibbur settled. The Directorate may record its case on the foundation of the predicate investigation without waiting for that investigation to conclude or for a charge sheet to be filed, the interim provisions requiring reason to believe and not proof. The death, absconding or discharge of a particular predicate accused does not by itself dissolve the proceeds or the laundering case against others, although a final outcome that negates the scheduled offence itself, acquittal on the merits, discharge, or quashing, does. A composition of the scheduled offence, which the criminal procedure treats in law as an acquittal of the compounded charge, carries the matter into the same territory. And the identity of the predicate agency matters practically: a predicate investigated by a state police force, by the Central Bureau of Investigation on consent or court direction, or by a specialised body such as the Narcotics Control Bureau or the Serious Fraud Investigation Office, reaches the Directorate by different routes and at different speeds, so that two laundering cases identical in law can differ entirely in tempo because their foundations are being built by different institutions on different clocks.

It is equally important to see who investigates what. The Directorate of Enforcement does not investigate the scheduled offence. That investigation belongs to the agency charged with the underlying crime, the police, the Central Bureau of Investigation, the Narcotics Control Bureau, the customs authorities, the securities regulator's prosecution wing, and it proceeds under the ordinary criminal law in the ordinary courts. The Directorate's jurisdiction begins where that investigation produces, or reveals, proceeds. The two investigations run in parallel, on different files, under different statutes, before different forums, and the money laundering case is formally independent of the predicate case even while it is substantively parasitic on it. This is the feature of the design that litigants most often misjudge: the Directorate need not wait for a charge sheet in the predicate case, still less for a conviction, before it attaches property or records a case of its own, but its case has no independent life. The dependency table that follows the offence sections below sets out, stage by stage, what happens to the money laundering case when the predicate case fails, because that table is the practical law of this statute in a single artefact.

## Proceeds of crime: the definition everything turns on

Section 2(1)(u) defines proceeds of crime as any property derived or obtained, directly or indirectly, by any person as a result of criminal activity relating to a scheduled offence, or the value of any such property, or, where the property is taken or held outside the country, property equivalent in value held within the country or abroad. Three features of this definition decide most contested cases. The first is the phrase "directly or indirectly", which detaches the definition from the physical asset the crime produced and follows value through substitutions: the bribe becomes a flat, the flat is sold and the price becomes a fixed deposit, the deposit is pledged for a loan, and each succeeding asset remains proceeds of crime because each was obtained indirectly as a result of the criminal activity. The second is the phrase "the value of any such property", which permits the Directorate to attach property that is not itself tainted at all, where the actual proceeds have been dissipated, layered beyond tracing or moved out of reach: the court accepts an asset of equivalent value as the statutory substitute for the asset that cannot be found. The third is the closing limb on property taken abroad, which matches the equivalent-value technique to the cross border case and, read with the reciprocal assistance provisions in Chapter Nine, gives the Act its extraterritorial technique.

The raw material the definition works on is itself defined widely. Section 2(1)(v) provides that property means any property or assets of every description, whether corporeal or incorporeal, movable or immovable, tangible or intangible, and includes deeds and instruments evidencing title to, or an interest in, such property or assets, wherever located. Bank balances, shares, partnership interests, actionable claims, intellectual property and beneficial interests under informal arrangements are all property for the Act's purposes, which is why attachment orders under Section 5 so characteristically run against a schedule of unlike assets, a flat, a demat account, a fixed deposit, a vehicle, a shareholding, united only by the proposition that each either derives from the scheduled offence or stands for value that does. The breadth of the property definition also explains the form an attachment dispute takes: the contest is rarely about whether the thing attached is property, and almost always about derivation, about whether the chain from the criminal activity to this asset, through however many substitutions, has been shown on material, or has merely been asserted.

The equivalent-value limb is the most litigated, because it authorises the attachment of property that its owner may have acquired honestly and even before the scheduled offence occurred, on the footing that it stands in for value that the offence generated. The Supreme Court in Vijay Madanlal Choudhary sustained the definition, treating the value limb as an integral part of the statutory scheme rather than an excess, while the adjudication provisions give a third party who claims an interest in attached property the opportunity to establish that the property is theirs and was acquired for legitimate consideration. The working position is therefore double-sided: the Directorate may reach an untainted asset of equivalent value, but an owner or a bona fide claimant who can show that the asset is not proceeds and was not acquired out of proceeds has a statutory route to its release, and the Adjudicating Authority is obliged to hear that claim. What the definition does not permit is the attachment of property with no nexus to any scheduled offence at all. Proceeds of crime is a relational concept. Property is not rendered attachable by the character of its owner; it is rendered attachable by its derivation from, or its standing as the value of property derived from, a listed crime.

### Can property acquired before the scheduled offence was committed be attached under the PMLA?

Yes, in a defined situation. Where the property generated by the scheduled offence has been spent, transferred or placed beyond reach, the definition reaches the value of that property, and an untainted asset of equivalent value, including one acquired earlier, may be attached in its place. Property with no derivation from the offence and no equivalent-value link cannot be attached.

The question of property acquired before the offence arises in a second form as well, which concerns time rather than substitution. The Act commenced on 1 July 2005, and scheduled offences committed before that date generated proceeds that were, in some cases, still being held and enjoyed after it. The Supreme Court's reading of Section 3, which treats the process or activity connected with proceeds of crime as continuing for as long as a person enjoys the proceeds by concealing, possessing, acquiring or using them, means that the offence of money laundering can be committed after commencement in respect of proceeds generated by a scheduled offence committed before commencement, because what is punished is the post-commencement dealing with the proceeds and not the predicate crime itself. The predicate must, however, be a scheduled offence, which imports its own temporal discipline: the conduct must answer to an offence that stands in the Schedule. Practitioners who miss the distinction between the date of the predicate and the date of the laundering lose cases at the threshold in both directions, claiming a retrospectivity bar that does not exist and overlooking the Schedule-status question that does.

## The offence and its punishment: Sections 3 and 4

Section 3 provides that whosoever directly or indirectly attempts to indulge, or knowingly assists, or knowingly is a party to, or is actually involved in, any process or activity connected with the proceeds of crime, including its concealment, possession, acquisition or use, and projecting or claiming it as untainted property, is guilty of the offence of money laundering. The verbs matter more than the nouns. The section does not require the accused to have committed the scheduled offence, to have known the precise offence from which the property derived in every case in the way a receiver of stolen property must, or to have succeeded in cleaning anything. Attempting to indulge is enough. Assisting is enough. Being knowingly a party is enough. The conduct elements are involvement in a process or activity connected with proceeds of crime, and the listed processes are concealment, possession, acquisition, use, and the projection or claiming of the property as untainted. In Vijay Madanlal Choudhary the Supreme Court read the connectives disjunctively, so that any one of these processes suffices and the projection of the property as untainted is not an essential additional ingredient that the prosecution must always prove. A person who merely continues to possess proceeds of crime, knowing their character, is within the section.

Parliament revisited the section through the Finance Act, 2019, which inserted an Explanation with effect from 1 August 2019. The Explanation clarifies that a person is guilty if found to have directly or indirectly attempted to indulge, or knowingly assisted, or knowingly been a party, or been actually involved, in one or more of six processes or activities connected with proceeds of crime, namely concealment, possession, acquisition, use, projecting as untainted property, or claiming as untainted property, in any manner whatsoever. It then declares that the process or activity connected with proceeds of crime is a continuing activity and continues until a person is directly or indirectly enjoying the proceeds of crime through any of those processes. The amendment responded to an observation in the Financial Action Task Force evaluation process that concealment, possession, acquisition and use had not been criminalised with sufficient clarity as standalone conduct; whether it changed the law or merely declared what the section had always meant was itself litigated, and the Supreme Court's disjunctive reading of the original text in the 2022 judgment substantially supported the declaratory view. For the practitioner the practical content is this: the offence is a continuing one, the clock does not stop when the money is first placed or layered, and a holder of proceeds who does nothing at all except retain and enjoy them remains, on the statute's terms, in the course of committing the offence.

Section 4 punishes the offence with rigorous imprisonment for a term that shall not be less than three years and may extend to seven years, together with a fine. Where the proceeds of crime relate to an offence under paragraph 2 of Part A of the Schedule, the narcotics offences, the maximum rises to ten years. The original text capped the fine at five lakh rupees; the 2013 amendment removed the cap, leaving the fine at large. Two features of the punishment deserve emphasis because they shape the whole enforcement pattern. The first is that the sentence, while serious, is not of the order that the rhetoric around the Act suggests: seven years is the ordinary ceiling, and the statute's severity lies not in its sentence but in its process. The second is that conviction requires a trial, and a trial requires the prosecution to prove a scheduled offence, proceeds, and knowing involvement, in a Special Court, which is why, as the enforcement record below shows, concluded trials are rare events measured against thousands of investigations. The gap between the modest sentence and the formidable process is the space in which the Act actually operates.

Section 70 completes the offence provisions by extending liability to companies: where a contravention is committed by a company, every person who was in charge of and responsible to the company for the conduct of its business, as well as the company itself, is liable, subject to the defence that the contravention occurred without that person's knowledge or despite due diligence, and a director, manager, secretary or other officer is liable where the contravention occurred with that person's consent, connivance or neglect. The Directorate's practice of arraying companies as accused entities and their directors in their personal capacity follows from this section, and so does one of the commonest summons patterns in the Act's working life, in which officers of a company are called to explain transactions that the company, not they personally, executed.

## The amendment arc: how the operative Act was built

The Act that commenced in 2005 is not the Act in force in its operative detail, and a pillar treatment must account for the distance between the two, because several of the provisions on which cases turn entered the statute long after enactment. The amendments of 2005 were minor. The first substantial revision came with the Amendment Act of 2009, in force from 1 June 2009, which added Part C to the Schedule and with it the cross border category of scheduled offences, widened the lists within the Schedule, and strengthened the provisions on attachment of property held abroad and on reciprocal assistance, reflecting the evaluation of India's regime that the Financial Action Task Force process had by then produced. The decisive revision was the Amendment Act of 2012, Act 2 of 2013, in force from 15 February 2013. It restructured the Schedule as described above, collapsing the old threshold-based Part B into Part A; it replaced the reporting chapter's narrower framing with the reporting entity concept; it removed the ceiling of five lakh rupees on the fine under Section 4; it recast the definition of the offence in Section 3 into the form that, with the later Explanation, the courts then construed; and it widened a series of definitions, including the introduction of a statutory conception of investigation that runs from the recording of the case to the filing of the complaint.

The later amendments arrived through Finance Acts, a route that itself became part of the constitutional litigation, since changes to a criminal statute carried in a money bill are exposed to the objection that they travelled by a procedure designed for taxation and appropriation. The Finance Act, 2015 adjusted the definition of proceeds of crime in its application to property taken abroad. The Finance Act, 2016 dealt with the appellate structure and related matters. The Finance Act, 2018 carried the re-enactment of the bail conditions in Section 45 after Nikesh Tarachand Shah, and inserted Section 8(8), the restoration route for third parties with a legitimate interest in attached property, so that the same statute that restored the prosecution's bail advantage also created the innocent claimant's clearest textual remedy. The Finance Act, 2019 was the most operationally significant of the series: it inserted the Explanation to Section 3 declaring the offence's continuing character and enumerating the six processes; it omitted the provisos to Sections 17 and 18 that had tied search and search of persons to the prior reporting of the scheduled offence; it raised the investigation-period ceiling on a confirmed attachment from ninety days to 365 days; it added an Explanation to Section 2(1)(u) clarifying that property derived from any criminal activity relatable to a scheduled offence answers the definition; and it inserted the closure report mechanism in Section 44 together with the Explanation that the Special Court's trial of the scheduled offence is a distinct trial and not a joint one. The Finance Act, 2023 tightened the identification obligations in the reporting chapter, defining politically exposed persons and lowering beneficial ownership thresholds. Read as a sequence, the arc has a consistent direction: each revision widened the class of cases the Act can reach, strengthened the interim machinery, or clarified a text that litigation had exposed as uncertain, and none of them narrowed the Schedule, shortened the attachment periods or softened the bail standard. Statutes drift in the direction of the pressures applied to them, and the pressures on this one, from the evaluation process abroad and the enforcement constituency at home, have all pushed the same way.

## The dependency chain: what happens when the predicate fails

The table below is the findable artefact of this article and the single most useful summary of the statute that can be written, because the question that dominates real litigation under the Act is not what money laundering is but what becomes of a money laundering case when the case underneath it moves. Each stage of the money laundering proceeding is set against the consequence of the predicate case failing at or before that stage.

| Stage of the money laundering proceeding | What the stage involves | If the predicate case fails at this point |
|---|---|---|
| Predicate offence registered | Another agency registers and investigates a scheduled offence under the ordinary criminal law | Nothing exists to attach to; a case built on an unscheduled offence, or on no offence, is void at the root |
| Criminal activity generates property | The scheduled offence produces money or assets capable of being proceeds of crime | If the activity generated no property, there are no proceeds and the offence in Section 3 cannot be made out |
| Proceeds of crime identified | The Directorate records an Enforcement Case Information Report and traces property to the criminal activity | If the property cannot be related to the scheduled offence, attachment and prosecution both lose their subject matter |
| Money laundering offence alleged | A person is said to have been involved in a process or activity connected with the proceeds | If the predicate is quashed here, the allegation has nothing to attach to and the proceeding cannot survive |
| Provisional attachment | An authorised officer attaches property for up to 180 days on recorded reasons to believe | Attachment rests on the predicate's existence; discharge or quashing of the predicate removes the foundation and the attachment must fall with it |
| Adjudication | The Adjudicating Authority decides whether the property is involved in money laundering and confirms or refuses attachment | A predicate that has ended in acquittal or discharge leaves no proceeds to confirm against; a third party's claim is decided on the same nexus |
| Prosecution complaint and trial | The Directorate files a complaint before the Special Court, which tries the Section 3 offence | Final acquittal or discharge in the predicate, or its quashing, ends the money laundering prosecution, because there are no proceeds of crime |
| Confiscation | On conviction, attached property is confiscated to the Central Government under Section 8(5) | On acquittal in the money laundering trial itself, the attachment is released under Section 8(6); confiscation without conviction is not available |

The principle the table expresses was stated by the Supreme Court in Vijay Madanlal Choudhary in terms that admit of little argument: if a person is finally discharged or acquitted of the scheduled offence, or the criminal case against that person is quashed by a competent court, there can be no offence of money laundering against that person or against anyone claiming title to the property through that person, because there are no proceeds of crime. The converse is equally important and less often understood. The money laundering case does not need the predicate case to succeed first. Attachment, arrest and prosecution can all proceed while the predicate investigation is incomplete and even while the predicate accused contests the charge, because the statute asks at the interim stages only whether there is reason to believe that a scheduled offence generated property and that the property is being dealt with. The dependency is therefore asymmetrical in time: the money laundering case may run ahead of the predicate case for years, but it cannot outlive the predicate case's final failure. Every strategic decision in litigation under this Act is a judgment about where on that timeline the case stands.

## A case through the chain: the statute at work

Abstract statement of the dependency principle understates how completely it governs practice, so consider a composite case of the kind the Directorate's published record contains in many variants, stripped of names because the pattern, not any individual matter, is the lesson. A state police force registers a case against a contractor and two public officials for offences under the Prevention of Corruption Act and the Penal Code, alleging that public works contracts were awarded against bribes routed through a subcontracting firm. That registration is the first link: a scheduled offence under Part A of the Schedule, investigated by an agency that is not the Directorate and will never be. The Directorate, on the material in that case, records an ECIR and opens the second track. It summons the subcontracting firm's bankers under Section 50, obtains the account records the bank was obliged by Chapter Four to maintain, and reconstructs the movement of the alleged bribe money into a residential flat purchased in the name of the official's spouse and into fixed deposits held by a family trust. The flat and the deposits are identified as proceeds of crime: the flat directly, as property derived from the criminal activity, and a second, older property is identified in the alternative as property of equivalent value, in case the trail to the flat fails on some link.

An authorised officer then records reasons to believe, in writing, that the properties are proceeds of crime and are likely to be dealt with so as to frustrate confiscation, and attaches them provisionally under Section 5. The flat's owner, the spouse, who is not an accused in the corruption case and may have had no part in the contracts, receives the attachment order and the obligation to answer it, because attachment follows the property. Within thirty days the Directorate files its complaint before the Adjudicating Authority, which issues notice under Section 8 calling on the spouse and on the Directorate to show the sources of the funds and the nexus to the alleged offence. The spouse's case is that the flat was bought substantially from her own professional income, with only a minor contribution from the impugned accounts, and she produces tax records reaching back years. The Authority confirms the attachment over the flat and the deposits, finding the nexus established on the material, and declines to release the older property, which remains attached on the equivalent-value footing; an appeal lies to the Appellate Tribunal within forty-five days, and the Tribunal's order would be appealable to the High Court within sixty days. Meanwhile the corruption trial has barely begun. Witnesses in it are being examined in a different court in a different city, and its pace will determine, years later, whether the entire money laundering structure stands: if the officials and the contractor are finally acquitted, or the case is quashed, the attachment confirmed so laboriously falls with the predicate, and the deposits and the flat are released, whatever the adjudication found, because there will be no proceeds of crime for them to be proceeds of. If the predicate results in conviction and the Directorate's own complaint before the Special Court, in which the contractor, the officials and the spouse are arrayed with the firm under Section 70, ends in conviction for money laundering, the Special Court orders confiscation under Section 8(5), and the property vests in the Central Government. Every feature of the statute's real character is visible in this single pattern: the second agency building on the first agency's case, the summons doing the evidentiary work, the property rather than the person as the first target, the non-accused holder bearing the first burden, the adjudication decided on papers years before any trial, and the whole edifice resting on a predicate whose outcome nobody in the money laundering proceeding controls.

## Attachment: the sanction that operates first

Section 5 empowers the Director, or an officer not below the rank of Deputy Director authorised by the Director, to attach property provisionally where the officer has reason to believe, recorded in writing on the basis of material in possession, that a person is in possession of proceeds of crime, that the proceeds are likely to be concealed, transferred or dealt with in a manner that would frustrate confiscation, and that immediate non-attachment would frustrate the proceeding. The order is served on the person holding the property, and the officer must forward a copy to the Adjudicating Authority and file a complaint before that Authority within thirty days, stating the facts on the basis of which the attachment was made. A provisional attachment endures for a period not exceeding 180 days from the date of the order. Three features of this scheme deserve attention. The first is that no court is involved at the point of attachment: the order is executive, made by the investigating agency itself, subject to subsequent adjudication. The second is that the belief is not free-standing opinion; it must rest on material in the officer's possession and be recorded in writing, and the Supreme Court has treated the recorded reasons as the justiciable core of the order, so that an attachment whose reasons disclose no material connecting the property to a scheduled offence is vulnerable. The third is that the 180-day period is a hard ceiling for the provisional stage: if adjudication does not confirm the attachment within it, the attachment lapses.

Adjudication under Section 8 is the first genuinely adversarial moment in the process. On receiving the complaint, the Adjudicating Authority issues a notice to the person whose property is attached, and to any person claiming an interest in it, calling upon them to indicate the sources of their income and to show cause why the property should not be declared to be property involved in money laundering and confiscated. The notice must allow the person not less than thirty days to reply. The Authority considers the reply, hears the parties, takes account of all relevant material, and records a finding. The timelines on either side of that hearing are enforced by the courts with some strictness, because they are the only discipline the scheme imposes on the investigating agency: the Punjab and Haryana High Court in Seema Garg v. Deputy Director, Directorate of Enforcement released attached property where the provisional stage and the investigation-period continuation had been allowed to run past their statutory ceilings, holding in substance that an attachment the statute no longer authorises is an attachment that cannot be kept, however strong the underlying suspicion. If it finds that the property is involved in money laundering, it confirms the attachment; if it does not, the attachment is released. A confirmed attachment does not transfer title and does not confiscate anything. It continues the freeze. Where the confirmation is made during the pendency of investigation, the attachment continues for a period not exceeding 365 days, a ceiling that the Finance Act, 2019 raised from the earlier ninety days, with periods during which the investigation was stayed by a court excluded from the computation; thereafter the attachment continues during the pendency of the proceedings before the Special Court and becomes final only if a confiscation order follows conviction. Section 8(4) permits the Directorate to take possession of confirmed attached property, but the Supreme Court read that power with restraint, holding that possession, as distinct from the freeze itself, is to be taken in exceptional circumstances and not as a matter of routine on confirmation, a reading that a Bench of the Court in the Benami transactions case decided weeks after the main judgment observed would require further expounding in an appropriate case. The distinction between a frozen asset and a seized household is the difference the reading preserves.

Confiscation, when it comes at all, comes at the end. Section 8(5) provides that where the Special Court, on conclusion of the trial, finds the offence of money laundering to have been committed, it shall order that the property involved in the money laundering stand confiscated to the Central Government. Section 8(6) provides the mirror image: where the offence is found not to have been committed, or the proceedings end without conviction, the attachment is released and the property restored. A third provision, Section 8(8), inserted in 2018, allows the Special Court, on an application by a person who claims a legitimate interest in attached property and establishes that the property was acquired without knowledge of its tainted character, to direct restoration of the property to that claimant even where confiscation is otherwise ordered. The sequence of these provisions is the answer to the most widespread popular error about the Act, repeated in a large share of the commentary: attachment is not confiscation. Attachment is a freeze imposed by an officer and continued by an authority on a prima facie standard; confiscation is a divesting of title that only a court can order and only after a conviction. What makes the distinction cold comfort in practice is the timeline. Between the provisional order and any trial's conclusion lie adjudication, appeals, investigation, complaint, charge and trial, and the property remains frozen through all of it, so that the interim order, made on recorded belief within days of the investigation's opening, is for most affected persons the operative event of the entire statute. That is the sense in which attachment is the sanction: not because the Act says so, but because it is the consequence that actually arrives.

### How do seizure, freezing and attachment differ under the PMLA?

Seizure is the taking of physical custody of property or records found during a search under Section 17. Freezing, under Section 17(1A), is an order prohibiting transfer or dealing where seizure is impracticable. Attachment under Section 5 is the Act's considered freeze, ordered on recorded reasons for up to 180 days and continued only after adjudication.

The terminology is not pedantry, because each act has a different authorising officer, a different threshold and a different lifespan, and orders under the Act have been set aside for conflating them. A search team seizes what it finds because it is there and may be evidence or proceeds. A freezing order immobilises what cannot conveniently be carried away, most often a bank account, and it travels to the Adjudicating Authority for confirmation on the same track as a seizure, with the retention of seized property beyond 180 days requiring the Authority's permission under Section 20. An attachment order, by contrast, is not an incident of a search at all: it is a deliberate act by a senior officer on recorded reasons, directed at identified property wherever held, and it is the only one of the three that begins the adjudication clock in the form described above. A person served with process under the Act needs to know which of the three has occurred, because the forum, the timeline and the grounds of challenge differ for each.

## After confiscation: vesting, management and the limits of the state's gain

Where confiscation is ordered, Section 9 provides that the property vests in the Central Government free from all encumbrances, and Section 10 obliges the Central Government to appoint an administrator to receive and manage the confiscated property through an administrator's account, with disposal governed by the rules. The vesting provision matters beyond symbolism, because it determines what happens to the interests that cluster around valuable property: a mortgagee bank that lent against a flat later confiscated, a tenant in occupation, a co-owner with an untainted share. The statute's answer is layered rather than simple. Vesting free of encumbrances is the rule the confiscation order produces, but the adjudication stage is where third party interests are meant to be separated out, the Adjudicating Authority being required to hear claimants, Section 8(8) preserves a restoration claim for the legitimate-interest holder even against confiscation, and the courts have repeatedly directed that property in which an untainted co-owner or a bona fide lender holds a genuine interest cannot be swept into the state's hands merely because it was convenient to attach the whole. The honest summary is that the state's title on confiscation is strong against the offender and contestable as against everyone else, and that a significant share of the Act's appellate litigation is fought by banks and purchasers whose quarrel is not with the prosecution of the launderer but with the treatment of their own interest in the launderer's asset. For the researcher this is the point at which the statute's property-law character is most visible: a criminal statute that operates, for much of its practical life, as a code for sorting competing claims to assets, with the criminal trial as the distant event that finally converts a provisional sorting into a permanent one.

## The Adjudicating Authority as an institution

Between the officer who attaches and the court that tries stands a body that most accounts of the Act pass over, although it decides the question most litigants care about first: whether the freeze continues. The Adjudicating Authority is constituted under Section 6, which provides for a Chairperson and two other members, one appointed from the field of law and one from the fields of administration, finance or accountancy, appointed by the Central Government, with benches sitting principally in Delhi and exercising jurisdiction across the country. Section 7 provides its staff. Its procedure is its own: it is not bound by the Code of Civil Procedure but is guided by the principles of natural justice and, subject to the Act, regulates its own procedure, while holding the powers of a civil court for summoning and enforcing attendance, requiring the discovery and production of documents, receiving evidence on affidavits and issuing commissions. Appeals from its orders lie to the Appellate Tribunal and then to the High Court, as the appeals section below sets out.

The Authority's character explains both its strengths and the criticism it attracts. It is a specialist body that does nothing but attachment adjudication, and in the ordinary run it hears the Directorate and the property holder on documents and decides within the 180-day frame the provisional order allows, which gives the scheme a speed the ordinary courts could not match. It is also, structurally, an executive-appointed tribunal deciding the fate of property attached by an executive agency, in proceedings where the governing presumption in Section 24 works in the agency's favour once foundational facts are shown, and the property holder's experience of the hearing is of being required to justify title and sources of funds to the satisfaction of a body that did not appoint the investigation but sits in judgment on its first fruits. Defenders of the design answer that adjudication by a tribunal is not adjudication by the Directorate, that the Authority does release property and does refuse confirmation where the nexus is not shown, that its orders are reasoned and appealable on fact and law to the High Court, and that the alternative, routing every confirmation through the ordinary courts, would reproduce in the attachment track the very delays that make the trial track inconclusive. Critics answer that an institution's formal separateness matters less than its appointment structure and its affirmance pattern. The statute does not resolve that argument; it builds the institution and leaves its legitimacy to be earned order by order, which is one reason the quality of reasoning in confirmation orders has itself become a ground of challenge in the appellate track.

## Search, seizure and arrest: Sections 16 to 21

Chapter Five equips the Directorate with the coercive toolkit. Section 16 confers a power of survey: an authorised officer may enter premises within the limits of the area assigned to the officer, where there is reason to believe that an offence has been committed, require facilities for inspection, examine records, and place marks of identification. Section 17 confers the power of search and seizure: where the Director or an authorised officer, on the basis of information in possession and for reasons recorded in writing, believes that a person has committed an act constituting money laundering or is in possession of proceeds of crime or of records relating to it, the officer may enter and search any building, place, vessel, vehicle or aircraft, break open locks where keys are withheld, seize property and records found, and place marks of identification or make inventories. Section 17(1A) adds the freezing power described above. As originally framed, the provisos to Sections 17 and 18 made the exercise of these powers conditional on the scheduled offence having first been reported to the police or a complaint filed, which tied the Directorate's coercive hand to the predicate agency's first step; the Finance Act, 2019 omitted those provisos, so that a search may be authorised on the Directorate's own information without waiting for the predicate case to be formally reported, a change whose significance is that the two parallel tracks described earlier can be started by the Directorate itself. Section 18 permits the search of a person about to enter or leave, or inside, premises being searched. Section 20 governs retention: property seized may be retained for up to 180 days, and for any longer period only with the Adjudicating Authority's permission, on an application that must be made within the initial period. The freezing limb of Section 17 received its own discipline from the Supreme Court in Opto Circuit India Ltd v. Axis Bank, decided in 2021, which concerned the freezing of bank accounts under Section 17(1A): the Court held that the power to freeze is not a free-standing shortcut but must satisfy the same conditions as the search and seizure power it supplements, resting on recorded reasons and the belief the section requires, and that the legality of the order is to be judged on the reasons the order itself records rather than on explanations assembled afterwards. The pattern across the search provisions is the pattern of the Act generally: the grant of power is wide, and the courts have located the operative limits in the paperwork the statute attaches to it, the information in possession, the reasons recorded in writing, the copy forwarded to the Adjudicating Authority, so that a search or freezing order made without its paper foundations is exposed in a way that the same action, properly recorded, is not.

### Does the Enforcement Directorate need a court warrant to search premises under the PMLA?

No. Search under Section 17 is authorised within the Directorate itself, by the Director or an officer the Director authorises, acting on information in possession with reasons recorded in writing. No magistrate's warrant is required, which is one of the features that distinguishes the Act's search regime from the ordinary criminal process.

Section 19 contains the arrest power and states its conditions with a precision that litigation has repeatedly enforced. Where the Director, a Deputy Director, an Assistant Director or another officer authorised by the Central Government has, on the basis of material in possession, reason to believe, recorded in writing, that a person has been guilty of an offence punishable under the Act, that officer may arrest the person. The arrested person must be informed, as soon as may be, of the grounds of arrest; the arresting officer must forward a copy of the arrest order together with the material in possession to the Adjudicating Authority in a sealed envelope; and the arrested person must be produced before a Special Court or a Magistrate within twenty-four hours, excluding journey time. In Pankaj Bansal v. Union of India, decided on 3 October 2023, a Bench of A. S. Bopanna and P. V. Sanjay Kumar JJ. held that the constitutional protection in Article 22(1) and the scheme of Section 19 require the grounds of arrest to be furnished to the arrested person in writing, that reading the grounds aloud or permitting the person to read them does not satisfy the requirement, and that an arrest made without written grounds, and any remand founded on it, cannot stand. A Bench of Bela Trivedi and Satish Chandra Sharma JJ., later in 2023, declined to apply that requirement retrospectively to arrests made before the judgment, and treated written grounds supplied within twenty-four hours, with the grounds communicated orally at the arrest, as satisfying the provision for arrests after it. In Arvind Kejriwal v. Directorate of Enforcement, decided in July 2024, the Court held that the recorded reasons to believe are themselves open to judicial review, that the power of arrest is not to be exercised merely because it would assist the investigation, and it referred aspects of the arrest standard to a larger Bench. The thread running through these decisions is that Section 19's paper conditions, material in possession, recorded reasons, written grounds, are not formalities surrounding the power; on the courts' reading, they are the power's limits, and an arrest that fails them fails altogether.

### What must the Enforcement Directorate record before it can arrest a person under Section 19?

The authorised officer must have material in possession and must record in writing the reason to believe, founded on that material, that the person has been guilty of an offence punishable under the Act. The grounds of arrest must then be furnished to the arrested person in writing, and the arrest order with the material forwarded to the Adjudicating Authority.

## The summons power: Section 50

Section 50 is, by volume, the most used provision in the Act, because it reaches everyone and not only the accused. It vests the Director and the officers the Director authorises with the powers of a civil court under the Code of Civil Procedure for the purposes of the Act: discovery and inspection, enforcing the attendance of any person and examining that person on oath, compelling the production of records, receiving evidence on affidavits, issuing commissions for the examination of witnesses and documents, and any other prescribed matter. A person summoned is bound to attend, personally or through an authorised agent, to state the truth upon any subject respecting which the person is examined or makes a statement, and to produce the documents called for. The proceedings before the officer are deemed to be judicial proceedings within the meaning of the provisions of the Penal Code that punish false evidence and insults to a person sitting in a judicial proceeding, so that a lie told in answer to a Section 50 summons is punishable as false evidence, and the statement recorded is not a confession to a police officer. The Supreme Court in Vijay Madanlal Choudhary held that officers of the Directorate are not police officers, that a statement recorded under Section 50 is therefore admissible in evidence, and that the protection against self-incrimination in Article 20(3) is not attracted at the stage of summons, because a person summoned is not, merely by being summoned, a person accused of an offence. The review petitions against that judgment, on which notice was issued in August 2022, are confined to two questions, the non-supply of the Enforcement Case Information Report and the reversal of the burden of proof, and the admissibility holding stands outside them.

What a summons means in practice is widely misunderstood, and the misunderstanding runs in both directions. A summons is not an accusation: the Directorate summons bankers, accountants, company officers, family members, counterparties and professional advisers, most of whom will never be accused of anything, because the statute's method is to reconstruct the movement of property from the records and recollections of everyone who touched it. A summons is also not an invitation that can be declined: attendance is compulsory, the statement is on oath, and the answers given can be used in the proceedings, including, if the person is later made an accused, in the prosecution's case on the material the person provided. The person summoned is entitled to legal consultation before attending, and the practical disciplines of the provision, verify what records exist before answering from memory, answer truthfully and within the question asked, keep a record of what was produced, are the substance of competent advice in this area. This account is general information about how the statute works and is not advice on any individual matter; anyone served with a summons or an attachment order under this Act needs advice on their own facts, because its timelines are short and its consequences for error are long.

One frontier of the summons power has proved genuinely difficult. The Directorate's summonses reached, in a number of matters, advocates in respect of legal advice rendered to clients under investigation, and the profession objected that a summons compelling counsel to answer about advice given in confidence dissolves the legal professional privilege that the evidence law protects and without which candid advice is impossible. The Supreme Court took up the question in 2025 in proceedings concerning the summoning of advocates by investigating agencies, and the Directorate issued internal instructions requiring approval at the highest level of the organisation before an advocate is summoned in that capacity. The episode is instructive about Section 50's design: the power is framed without an internal exception for any class of person, so every limit on it must be found either in a privilege recognised by another statute, in the agency's own self-restraint, or in a court's intervention, and the summons of a witness who is also an officer of the court exposed that structural fact more clearly than any other use of the provision.

The Enforcement Case Information Report deserves a word here, because it is the document around which the summons practice revolves. When the Directorate decides to investigate, it records an ECIR, its internal case-opening document, modelled loosely on a first information report but differing from one in the respects that matter: it is not registered by a police officer under the Code of Criminal Procedure, it does not set the criminal law in motion against a named accused in the manner of an FIR, and the Supreme Court has held that it is an internal document of the Directorate which the person under investigation is not entitled to receive as a matter of right, the entitlement being instead to the grounds of arrest if arrested, to the reasons recorded for attachment if property is attached, and to the material relied upon at the stages the Act specifies. Critics regard the non-disclosure as the scheme's most consequential opacity, because the investigation's founding document is invisible to its subject; the Court's answer is that the ECIR is not the source of any coercive consequence, each coercive step carrying its own recorded reasons which are disclosable, and that equation of the ECIR with an FIR is a category error. The disagreement is one of the two questions the review will revisit.

## Bail: Section 45 and the reversed presumption of release

Section 45 declares every offence under the Act cognizable and non-bailable, and then imposes the twin conditions: no person accused of an offence under the Act shall be released on bail unless the Public Prosecutor has been given an opportunity to oppose the application and, where the application is opposed, the court is satisfied that there are reasonable grounds for believing that the accused is not guilty of the offence and that the accused is not likely to commit any offence while on bail. The conditions are in addition to the ordinary limitations in the Code of Criminal Procedure. Their effect is to reverse the ordinary starting point of bail law. Instead of the prosecution showing why custody should continue, the accused must satisfy the court, at the threshold and on the prosecution's material, of reasonable grounds for believing in the accused's innocence, a standard pitched between a prima facie view and a finding, applied on incomplete evidence, and in practice the point at which most contested PMLA custody is decided.

The conditions have a constitutional biography that the pillar must record because the operative text cannot be understood without it. As enacted, the twin conditions applied only to a person accused of an offence punishable with imprisonment of more than three years under Part A of the Schedule, which is to say they borrowed their severity from the predicate offence's punishment rather than from the laundering offence itself. In Nikesh Tarachand Shah v. Union of India, decided on 23 November 2017, a Bench of Rohinton Nariman and Sanjay Kishan Kaul JJ. struck the provision down as manifestly arbitrary and violative of Articles 14 and 21: the classification had no rational relation to the object of the Act, since it made bail in a laundering case depend on the gravity of a different offence committed possibly by a different person, and it visited a reverse burden on accused persons without regard to their own alleged conduct. Parliament's response, through the Finance Act, 2018, was not to abandon the conditions but to remove the defect identified: the conditions were re-enacted in a form that applies to offences under the Act itself, severing the link to the predicate's punishment. The amending technique raised its own question, whether a legislature may cure a struck-down provision by re-enacting its substance in corrected form, and also generated a line of High Court authority, of which the Madhya Pradesh High Court's decision in the Vinod Bhandari case is an example, doubting whether the amendment had revived the conditions at all. In Vijay Madanlal Choudhary the Supreme Court upheld the amended Section 45, holding the conditions a reasonable incident of a special statute addressed to an offence the Court characterised as a grave threat to the financial system and the integrity of the country, and disagreeing with the premise on which Nikesh Tarachand Shah had proceeded. A first proviso softens the conditions for defined categories: a person under the age of sixteen years, a woman, a person who is sick or infirm, or a person accused of money laundering of a sum of less than one crore rupees may be released on bail if the Special Court so directs, and the Court has treated that proviso as a discretion to be exercised with sensitivity to the vulnerability of the categories named, not as an entitlement and not as a nullity.

The conditions also colour the stage before arrest. In P. Chidambaram v. Directorate of Enforcement, decided in 2019, the Supreme Court refused anticipatory bail in a money laundering case, treating the statutory scheme and the gravity of the accusation as decisive, and observing that money laundering is an offence against the economy of the country rather than a private wrong; the case became the standing reference for the proposition that pre-arrest protection under this Act is assessed within the same restrictive frame as bail after arrest, and not by the more generous standards that govern ordinary economic offences. Read with the later Article 21 bail cases, the position that emerges is a spectrum rather than a rule: anticipatory bail is exceptional and scheme-bound, bail after arrest is governed by the twin conditions, and bail after prolonged custody with no trial in prospect is governed by the Constitution. The accused's realistic prospects move along that spectrum with time in custody, which is a further reason the interim stages of this statute, and not its trial, are where its human consequences are decided.

The final element of the bail picture is the counterweight the Court has built outside Section 45 altogether. Where custody has been long and the trial cannot conclude within a reasonable time, the Court has granted bail on the ground that indefinite pre-trial incarceration offends Article 21 notwithstanding the twin conditions. In Manish Sisodia v. Directorate of Enforcement, decided in August 2024, the Court granted bail in a case involving some 493 witnesses and documents running to tens of thousands of pages, holding that the likelihood of the trial concluding in the near future was impossible, that prolonged incarceration before a pronouncement of guilt should not be permitted to become punishment without trial, and that the principle that bail is the rule and refusal the exception is superior to statutory restriction where the two irreconcilably conflict. The working law of PMLA bail is therefore a two-door system: the statutory door of Section 45, which is narrow, and a constitutional door, which opens where the state's own timeline makes the trial illusory. Most real bail litigation is an argument about which door the case should be put through.

## The reverse burden: Section 24

Section 24 provides that in any proceeding relating to proceeds of crime, where a person is charged with the offence of money laundering under Section 3, the Authority or the court shall, unless the contrary is proved, presume that such proceeds of crime are involved in money laundering; and in the case of any other person, the Authority or court may presume the same. The provision reverses, for the fact it specifies, the ordinary rule that the prosecution proves every element. Once it operates, the person charged must prove that the property is not involved in money laundering, and in adjudication proceedings the same presumption confronts a person whose property is attached and who says it is clean.

The provision's scope is narrower than its reputation, and the narrowing is where the courts have done their most important work on it. The Supreme Court in Vijay Madanlal Choudhary sustained Section 24 against the charge that it reversed the presumption of innocence, on the footing that the presumption it creates operates only after the prosecution has established the foundational facts: that a scheduled offence was committed, that property was derived from it, and that the property in question answers to that derivation or its value. A presumption that arises only on proved foundations, and that can be rebutted on a preponderance of probabilities by the person best placed to explain the property's provenance, was treated by the Court as within the class of reverse burdens the Constitution tolerates for economic offences. The distinction that matters in practice is between the burden of persuasion on guilt, which never leaves the prosecution at trial on the offence itself, and the statutory presumption on the character of the property, which shifts an evidential burden to the holder once the prosecution has laid its foundation. It is also the second of the two questions on which review of the 2022 judgment was sought and notice issued, the petitioners contending that the reversal of the presumption of innocence is itself the constitutional vice whatever the foundations laid. The enforcement justification for the provision is the one the international template supplies: the provenance of an asset lies peculiarly within the knowledge of the person who holds it, bank records and title documents are in that person's hands, and a regime that required the state to prove a negative about every rupee's ancestry would be a regime designed to fail. The critic's answer is that the same logic can be, and in the Schedule's breadth increasingly is, applied to persons whose connection with any crime is remote, and that a presumption drafted for drug money operates in disputes over ordinary commercial property.

## Special Courts, trial and appeals: Sections 43 to 47 and 25 to 42

Trial under the Act takes place before a Special Court, a Court of Session designated by the Central Government in consultation with the Chief Justice of the High Court under Section 43. Section 44 provides that an offence punishable under Section 4 shall be triable only by the Special Court constituted for the area in which the offence was committed, and that the Special Court may also try the scheduled offence out of which the laundering case arises, so that the two connected prosecutions can be heard by one court; the Finance Act, 2019 added an Explanation clarifying that the trial of the scheduled offence by the Special Court is a distinct trial and not a joint trial with the laundering offence, preserving the formal separateness of the two prosecutions even where one judge hears both. Cognizance is not taken on a police report. A further proviso to Section 45(1) bars the Special Court from taking cognizance of an offence under Section 4 except upon a written complaint made by the Director or by an officer authorised in that behalf, which makes the Directorate's prosecution complaint, and not any chargesheet in the predicate case, the founding document of the trial. The same 2019 amendment formalised the exit for a case that goes nowhere: where, on conclusion of investigation, no offence of money laundering is made out, the authority is to submit a closure report before the Special Court. Section 46 applies the Code of Criminal Procedure to proceedings before the Special Court, and deems the Special Court a Court of Session and the complainant's counsel a public prosecutor for those purposes.

### Why are money laundering trials held before Special Courts instead of the ordinary criminal courts?

Parliament routed the offence to designated Courts of Session so that the laundering trial and the connected scheduled offence could be heard by one court with familiarity with financial evidence, and so that the trial would not be scattered among magistrates' courts applying the ordinary calendar. The Special Court is a Court of Session, not a new species of tribunal.

Appeals run on two tracks because the Act creates two kinds of adjudication. An order of the Adjudicating Authority confirming or refusing attachment is appealable to the Appellate Tribunal constituted under Chapter Six, under Section 26, by any person aggrieved, including the Director; the appeal must be filed within forty-five days of receiving the order, with power in the Tribunal to condone delay for sufficient cause, and the Tribunal is directed to endeavour to dispose of the appeal within six months. The Tribunal also hears appeals from the penalty orders made against reporting entities. From the Appellate Tribunal, Section 42 provides an appeal to the High Court, within sixty days of the communication of the Tribunal's order, with a further period of up to sixty days available where sufficient cause prevented a timely filing, on any question of law or of fact arising out of the Tribunal's order; the High Court for this purpose is the one within whose jurisdiction the aggrieved party ordinarily resides or carries on business. The Appellate Tribunal itself is a body constituted by the Central Government with a Chairperson and members drawn from the judicial and administrative streams, and Section 41 bars civil courts from entertaining any suit or proceeding in respect of a matter that the Director, the Adjudicating Authority or the Tribunal is empowered to determine, and bars injunctions against action under the Act, which is why challenges to coercive steps travel either through this appellate structure or through the writ jurisdiction of the High Courts under Article 226, and why the High Courts insist, with limited exceptions, that the statutory remedy be exhausted first. In the criminal track, orders of the Special Court, including conviction, acquittal and confiscation, travel through the ordinary appellate and revisional structure of the Code of Criminal Procedure, with the High Court as the appellate court for a Court of Session. A litigant under this Act therefore commonly fights in three forums at once, the adjudication track over the property, the criminal track over guilt, and the constitutional track by writ petition over the legality of the coercive steps, and the interaction of the three, in particular the question which forum's finding on the predicate's existence binds the others, is where the dependency principle stated earlier does its practical work: an acquittal in the predicate case is a fact the other two tracks cannot survive ignoring, whatever their formal independence.

## Investigation: from the ECIR to the prosecution complaint

The word investigation is itself defined, by an insertion made in 2013, to include all proceedings under the Act conducted by the Director or by an authorised authority for the collection of evidence, and the working shape of an investigation follows from the instruments the earlier sections describe. It begins, ordinarily, with inputs the Directorate does not generate: the registration of a scheduled offence by another agency, a dissemination from the Financial Intelligence Unit built on reports filed under Chapter Four, a reference from a regulator, or material from a foreign counterpart under the reciprocal arrangements. The Enforcement Case Information Report is recorded. Thereafter the investigation proceeds largely on paper and in summons rooms: bank records and property records are requisitioned, persons who handled the money are examined on oath under Section 50 in successive rounds, the trail is assembled into a chain from the predicate offence to identified assets, and the interim decisions, to attach, to search, to arrest, are taken at the points where the officer judges the recorded material sufficient for the belief each section requires. Because there is no statutory deadline for completing a money laundering investigation, and because the investigation commonly waits on documents, on the predicate agency's progress and on the outcome of challenges to its own interim steps, investigations run for years as a matter of course, and the absence of a limitation clock for the investigative stage is one of the design's most consequential silences.

The investigation ends, for prosecutorial purposes, in one of two documents. Where the material supports a charge, the Directorate files a prosecution complaint before the Special Court, on which alone the court can take cognizance of the Section 4 offence, setting out the scheduled offence, the proceeds, the processes by which the accused dealt with them, and the property attached and sought to be confiscated in due course; supplementary complaints may follow as the investigation adds accused, property or material, which is one reason trials acquire the scale the bail courts have remarked upon, with witnesses counted in the hundreds and documents in the tens of thousands of pages. Where the material does not support a charge, the authority submits the closure report that the Finance Act, 2019 wrote into Section 44. Between those outcomes sits the remand practice: an arrested person is produced before the Special Court or the Magistrate within twenty-four hours, and the court may remand the person to the Directorate's custody or to judicial custody under the Code of Criminal Procedure as applied by Section 65, with the accused retaining the ordinary right to default bail if the complaint is not filed within the period the Code prescribes for an offence of this punishment class. One limit on the arrest power at this stage deserves record: in Tarsem Lal v. Directorate of Enforcement, decided in May 2024, the Supreme Court held that once the Special Court has taken cognizance on the complaint, the Directorate cannot arrest an accused who was not arrested during the investigation without the court's permission, sought on the ground that custodial interrogation is necessary; the power to arrest, in other words, belongs to the investigative stage and does not survive unchanged into the trial stage. The sequence from ECIR to complaint is therefore best understood as the statute's true procedural spine, with attachment, search, summons and arrest as events along it, and the trial, when it comes, as the testing of a file assembled over years by a process the accused could observe only at the points where the Act required reasons to be shown.

## The other half of the Act: reporting entities and the paper trail

Chapter Four obliges every reporting entity, a category defined in Section 2(1)(wa) to include banking companies, financial institutions, intermediaries such as stock brokers and mutual funds, and persons carrying on designated businesses or professions, to maintain records of transactions and of the identity of clients, to verify client identity, and to furnish information to the Director of the Financial Intelligence Unit, India. Records must be maintained for five years from the date of the transaction or from the end of the business relationship, as the provision specifies. The information furnished flows to FIU-IND, the unit the Government set up in 2004 as the central national agency to receive, process, analyse and disseminate information relating to suspect financial transactions to enforcement agencies and to counterpart units abroad. The rules under the Act particularise the flow: reporting entities furnish reports of cash transactions above the prescribed value of ten lakh rupees, including series of transactions integrally connected within a month, reports of transactions in immovable property above the prescribed value, and suspicious transaction reports, which carry no monetary floor at all because their trigger is the character of the transaction rather than its size, a transaction that appears designed to evade reporting, that has no economic rationale, that involves proceeds the entity has reason to suspect, or that fits the other prescribed indicia. The suspicious transaction report is the single most consequential document in the chapter, since it is the point at which the private compliance apparatus of the banking system converts its own unease into state information, and the volume of such reports is the raw material from which the Directorate's disseminations and, downstream, a share of its cases are built. Section 13 arms the Director with enforcement powers over this chapter: after inquiry, the Director may issue a warning in writing, direct compliance with specific instructions, direct periodic reporting on remedial measures, or impose a monetary penalty of not less than ten thousand rupees and up to one lakh rupees for each failure. Section 14 protects reporting entities and their officers from civil proceedings for furnishing information under the chapter, without which protection no bank would report its own customers.

The designated business limb of the definition does quiet but extensive work. By notification, the Central Government has brought within it dealers in precious metals and precious stones, real estate agents, and, in May 2023, chartered accountants, company secretaries and cost accountants when they carry on specified financial transactions on behalf of clients, such as buying and selling immovable property, managing client money or bank accounts, and creating or managing companies and trusts. Each notification extends the identification and record-keeping net over a profession through which laundering characteristically passes, and each has been contested by the profession concerned on the ground that compliance duties sit uneasily with professional confidentiality, a tension the statute resolves without discussion by simply imposing the duty and protecting the reporting, not the silence.

The category of reporting entity has been widened by notification and amendment in step with the Schedule's own growth. The 2013 amendment replaced the narrower framing of the original chapter, which had spoken of banking companies, financial institutions and intermediaries, with the reporting entity concept and its designated business limb. In March 2023 the Government notified persons carrying on activities involving virtual digital assets, exchanges and transfers of such assets, safekeeping and administration of them, and related financial services, as persons carrying on a designated business or profession, bringing the cryptocurrency sector inside the reporting chapter without any amendment to the Act itself. The Finance Act, 2023 tightened the identification side of the chapter, including by defining politically exposed persons and lowering the beneficial ownership thresholds at which the natural persons behind a client entity must be identified. For the general reader the significance of this chapter is easily understated: the Act's criminal provisions generate the headlines, but the reporting chapter is what makes the financial system legible to the state, and it operates continuously, on every customer of every bank, whether or not any offence is ever suspected. The summons and attachment powers described earlier are almost always exercised on foundations that this chapter's records laid down first.

## Parallel proceedings and overlapping regimes

A money laundering case almost never travels alone. The scheduled offence is investigated by its own agency and tried in its own court on its own clock. Alongside it may run proceedings under other asset-focused statutes, and the overlaps are structural rather than accidental. The foreign exchange regime is the oldest neighbour: the same Directorate administers the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999, but that statute, after the [transition from FERA to FEMA](/2013/02/01/fera-to-fema-transition/), treats contraventions as civil defaults punishable by penalty and compounding, so a course of conduct that moves money abroad in breach of exchange law may attract a civil penalty proceeding under FEMA and, if a scheduled offence underlies it, a criminal one under this Act, the two proceeding independently because they punish different things. Undisclosed foreign assets attract the [Black Money (Undisclosed Foreign Income and Assets) and Imposition of Tax Act, 2015](/2012/09/01/black-money-act-2015-explained/), which taxes the asset and penalises its non-disclosure, and a foreign asset that represents the proceeds of a scheduled offence can in principle be taxed under the one statute and attached under the other. Property acquired out of crime may also be a benami transaction where it is held in another's name, engaging the prohibition statute with its own attachment and confiscation machinery administered by the income tax authorities. Tax proceedings, customs proceedings and securities enforcement can all touch the same money at the same time.

Parliament has not subordinated these regimes to one another by any general priority rule, and the Act's overriding effect clause in Section 71 resolves only direct inconsistency. The practical consequence is cumulative exposure: one course of dealing can produce a tax demand, an exchange penalty, a benami attachment and a money laundering prosecution, each lawful in itself, with the affected person's real complaint being not any single proceeding but the aggregate. Courts have generally declined to treat the multiplicity as double jeopardy, because the proceedings differ in character and object, and the Article 20(2) protection is against being prosecuted and punished twice for the same offence. The multiplicity is, nonetheless, part of the honest description of the statute's place in the system, and it explains why the Directorate's summons so often reaches persons who experience the process not as one case but as a climate.

## The Directorate of Enforcement as an institution

The Act's powers are exercised by a single agency, and its institutional character shapes how the statute behaves. The Directorate of Enforcement sits in the Department of Revenue of the Ministry of Finance. It began in 1956 as an enforcement unit administering the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act, 1947, was renamed a Directorate in 1957, and accumulated statutes over the decades: it enforces the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999 as the civil successor to the exchange control regime, it administers this Act's criminal provisions from 2005, it is the investigating agency under the Fugitive Economic Offenders Act, 2018, and it sponsors cases under the Conservation of Foreign Exchange and Prevention of Smuggling Activities Act. Its head is the Director of Enforcement, and its working structure is a headquarters in Delhi with zonal and sub-zonal offices through which the Section 50 summonses, the Section 17 searches and the Section 5 attachments described in this article are issued. The officers who exercise the Act's powers are drawn substantially from the revenue and police services on deputation, and the Director under the Act, the authority who must authorise attachments and in whom the Section 50 powers vest, is the Director of Enforcement acting through the hierarchy the Act itself prescribes, down to the Assistant Directors who conduct much of the summons-level work. Two institutional facts matter for understanding the statute's record. The first is that the same agency holds both the civil exchange portfolio and the criminal laundering portfolio, so the choice of which statute's machinery to engage against a course of conduct is an internal allocation decision, not an inter-agency negotiation. The second is that the Directorate investigates but does not prosecute in its own name before its own courts: complaints go to Special Courts, attachments to the Adjudicating Authority, and every coercive step is reviewable by a body the Directorate does not control, which is the structural answer the scheme gives to the concentration of powers in its earlier chapters.

The Directorate works through zonal offices in the principal cities and sub-zonal offices beneath them, headed at headquarters in Delhi by the Director and staffed by officers drawn substantially on deputation from the police, customs, income tax and other services, a structure that gives the institution its reach and also its most discussed institutional feature: an agency of this power is led and largely manned by officers whose careers belong to other services, with a small direct cadre of its own. The leadership question reached the constitutional courts over tenure. Amendments made in 2021 to the statutes governing the Director's appointment permitted extensions of tenure year by year up to a total of five years, and the Supreme Court, in a judgment of July 2023, upheld the enabling amendments while holding that the serial extensions actually granted to the incumbent Director were impermissible in light of the Court's earlier directions in the same litigation, directing that he demit office that month. The episode matters to a statute pillar for a structural reason: the Act concentrates the summons, attachment, arrest and complaint powers in a hierarchy that answers to a single Director, so the manner of that office's appointment and tenure is part of the working constitution of the Act, and the courts' supervision of it is one of the few checks on the institution that operates outside any individual case.

## What the Act does not do

A pillar treatment owes the reader the statute's negative space, because most misdescription of the Act consists of attributing to it powers it withholds. The Directorate cannot investigate the scheduled offence: if the underlying crime is not being investigated, or has not been committed, the Directorate has no roving commission to inquire into crime generally, and its case must be built on the predicate agency's foundation or not at all. The Act cannot confiscate anything before conviction: the entire pre-trial apparatus, for all its practical weight, produces freezes and custody, not divesting of title. The Act cannot create an offence out of wealth, disproportion or suspicion: unexplained assets, however suggestive, are not proceeds of crime unless they are related to a scheduled offence, and the income tax law's separate machinery for taxing unexplained money is a different statute answering a different question. The Act cannot dispense with its own paper conditions: reasons not recorded, grounds not furnished in writing, timelines exceeded, each of these is not an irregularity in the exercise of the power but, on the courts' consistent reading, a failure of the power's preconditions. And the Act cannot reach the person who merely receives money in the ordinary course without knowledge of its character: the offence's conduct verbs are qualified by the requirement of knowing involvement, and the bona fide recipient's protection runs through the definition of the offence itself and not only through the restoration provisions. These limits are not loopholes in an otherwise seamless design; together with the dependency principle they are the design, and the litigation record shows them deciding cases. Two silences in the remedial scheme complete the negative space. The Act provides no compensation for the owner whose property is attached, held for years and then released, no interest on the value frozen, and no damages for a business damaged by an attachment that the prosecution ultimately fails to sustain; the owner's statutory remedy is restoration of the property itself and nothing more, and the loss of the intervening years is borne where it fell. And the Act provides no mechanism by which a person can require the Directorate to close an investigation that has produced neither attachment, arrest nor complaint within any period, because the Act sets the investigation no deadline and imposes on the agency no duty to report its state to the person under inquiry. These silences are design choices as much as the choices the Act makes loudly, and they belong in any complete account of what the statute is: a law that is exact about the state's interim powers and silent about the citizen's interim losses.

## The enforcement record, stated with attribution

The Act's first decade was quiet, and the Directorate's own account supplies the numbers. In its annual report for 2024 to 2025, the Directorate recorded that up to March 2014, across the first nine years of the Act's operation, 1,883 Enforcement Case Information Reports had been registered, fewer than two hundred a year on average, that 84 prosecution complaints had been filed, and that assets under attachment stood at Rs 5,171.32 crore. The same report recorded that between April 2014 and March 2024, 5,113 fresh investigations were initiated and 1,332 prosecution complaints filed. The Finance Ministry's figures placed before Parliament run in the same direction and further: in an answer given in the Rajya Sabha on 6 August 2024, the Minister of State for Finance stated that the Directorate had recorded 7,083 ECIRs in total up to 31 July 2024, and that the value of proceeds of crime attached stood at approximately Rs 1,31,375 crore, with a further Rs 3,725.76 crore seized and Rs 4,651.68 crore frozen. On any of these series, registrations rose several-fold and attached value rose by an order of magnitude between the Act's first decade and its second. That is the verifiable content of the proposition that the statute woke up.

The Directorate's annual report for 2024 to 2025 carried the pattern into its most recent tabulated year, recording 775 fresh investigations and 333 prosecution complaints in that year and 34 individuals convicted, figures consistent with the longer series: the investigative intake of a single year in the Act's third decade approaches half the entire intake of its first nine years, while the year's convictions remain a small double-digit number. The same report's framing is itself evidence of where the institution locates its achievement, in investigations opened, complaints filed and value attached, the interim metrics, rather than in trials concluded.

The outcomes at the far end of the process are of a different order. A Finance Ministry answer placed before the Rajya Sabha, tabulating conviction orders year by year, arrived at a total of 56 orders of conviction covering 124 persons. Set beside more than seven thousand recorded cases, the number of concluded trials ending in conviction is very small, and the Directorate's explanation, that trials are hostage to the predicate prosecutions running in other courts and to the interlocutory litigation the Act's own machinery invites, and the critics' explanation, that cases are opened for their interim effects and not built to be tried, are both consistent with the arithmetic. What the arithmetic does not support is either side's favourite single percentage: a conviction rate computed only over trials that have concluded is high, and a conviction rate computed over all investigations opened is negligible, and each side of the political argument quotes the denominator that suits it. The completion figures themselves, which are the honest core of the matter, are these: thousands of investigations, attachments in the tens of thousands of crores, dozens of convictions. The constitutional and enforcement-evidence debate built on those numbers is treated in the judgment article; here they stand as the record against which the statute's design claims must be tested.

## The two cases, stated fairly

The enforcement case, at its strongest, is the one the Union of India advanced through the Solicitor General, Tushar Mehta, in the constitutional litigation, and it begins from the character of the crime. Laundering is a concealment offence: its whole object is to break the visible connection between property and crime, through layering across accounts, entities and jurisdictions, often using professional intermediaries and instruments designed for opacity. By the time an ordinary investigation, proceeding at the pace of the ordinary criminal process and requiring proof beyond reasonable doubt of every element before any property can be touched, has identified the proceeds, the proceeds are gone. A regime equal to the crime must therefore be able to freeze first on recorded suspicion and adjudicate later, must be able to compel the paper trail from the institutions through which the money moved, must be able to question the persons who handled it under a duty of truth, and must place the burden of explaining an asset's provenance on the person who alone can know it. The international framework to which India has committed itself, and under which its regime is periodically evaluated, requires each of these features. The enforcement side adds that the statute is not safeguard-free: attachment requires recorded reasons and dies in 180 days unless an independent authority confirms it, arrest requires recorded belief founded on material, bail has humane exceptions, confiscation requires a conviction, and the courts have enforced each safeguard in real cases, quashing attachments, voiding arrests and granting bail where the conditions were not met.

The critical case, at its strongest, accepts the reality of laundering and disputes the calibration. Its textual core is the Schedule: a machinery justified by drug cartels and terrorist finance has been extended, amendment by amendment, to a list of offences running to ordinary commercial, environmental and intellectual property crime, and each extension multiplies the class of persons against whom recorded-suspicion attachment, reversed burdens and stringent bail can be deployed on the foundation of an ordinary criminal case. Its procedural core is the sequence: because attachment precedes adjudication, custody precedes trial, trials rarely conclude, and the founding case document is not disclosed, the interim process is for most people the whole of the law's impact, and interim process imposed on a prima facie standard is a sanction in everything but name, imposed without a finding of guilt. Justice Rohinton Nariman's judgment striking down the original bail conditions named one part of this objection, the irrationality of calibrating a laundering accused's liberty to a different offence's gravity; a Constitution Bench's later treatment of the analogous Benami machinery, and the observation recorded there that the Supreme Court's own reading of the possession power under this Act required further expounding, named another; and Justice L. Nageswara Rao, speaking after his retirement from the Court, said publicly that he might have taken a different view in the constitutional case and that the judgment was generally felt to have set back personal liberty. The further allegation that the statute is deployed disproportionately against political opponents of the government of the day is made by the opposition parties, is denied by the government, and is contested on evidence that requires the case-by-case treatment given to it in the judgment article; a pillar article records the allegation as a live and disputed feature of the statute's public life, and does not adjudicate it.

A third position, distinct from both the enforcement defence and the repeal-minded critique, is visible in the courts' own conduct since the constitutional judgment, and it may be the most consequential of the three because it is the one being implemented. On this view the statute stands, the Schedule stands, and the interim machinery stands, but each power is to be held to its textual conditions with increasing literalism: grounds of arrest in writing because the section read with Article 22 requires them, the conspiracy entry confined to conspiracies aimed at scheduled offences because the Schedule's text will not bear the wider use, arrest after cognizance only with the court's leave because the investigative stage has ended, bail on Article 21 where the trial cannot be completed because a condition the legislature omitted cannot be supplied against the citizen. Pankaj Bansal, Pavana Dibbur, Tarsem Lal and Manish Sisodia are the instances, and none of them questions the Act's existence or the 2022 holdings they build around. Whether this incremental discipline is a sufficient answer to the calibration critique is contested in exactly the terms the two cases above frame: the enforcement side treats it as proof the safeguards work, the critical side treats it as the judiciary repairing case by case a structure whose defects are general. The pillar's task is to record that the discipline is real, that it has released real people and real property, and that it operates entirely within the design it disciplines.

Between these cases, the record supports one conclusion with some confidence and leaves another genuinely open. It supports the conclusion that the statute's centre of gravity is interim: the design choices all push consequence forward in time, to the moment of attachment and arrest, and push the moment of authoritative determination, the trial, into a distance that most cases never traverse, so that evaluating the Act by its conviction machinery misses what it does, and evaluating it by its interim machinery is unavoidable. It leaves open whether that interim weight is the unavoidable price of a functioning anti-laundering regime or a disproportion that a narrower Schedule and faster trials could cure, because that question turns partly on facts about predicate-case outcomes that no single source establishes and partly on a judgment about how much pre-conviction coercion a financial crime can justify, on which reasonable positions differ.

## The clocks of the Act: every timeline in one account

Because the statute's consequences are interim, its timelines are its substance, and a reader who holds them together holds the procedural law of the Act. An arrested person must be produced before the Special Court or a Magistrate within twenty-four hours of arrest, excluding the time necessary for the journey. A provisional attachment under Section 5 must be followed by a complaint to the Adjudicating Authority within thirty days, and the Authority's show cause notice must give the property holder not less than thirty days to reply. The provisional attachment itself cannot exceed 180 days without confirmation, and property or records seized in a search cannot be retained beyond 180 days without the Authority's permission. A confirmed attachment made during investigation continues for no more than 365 days on that footing, the period of any court-ordered stay of the investigation being excluded, and thereafter endures through the proceedings before the Special Court until confiscation or release concludes them. An appeal against the Authority's order lies to the Appellate Tribunal within forty-five days, the Tribunal is to endeavour to dispose of it within six months, and an appeal from the Tribunal lies to the High Court within sixty days, extendable by a further sixty for sufficient cause. A reporting entity must keep its records for five years. A trial has no clock at all: no provision of the Act sets a period within which the prosecution complaint must be tried or concluded, and the investigation that precedes it has no statutory deadline either. Read together, the pattern is exact. Every step the state takes against property or liberty is tightly clocked, and the step that would vindicate or condemn the whole proceeding, the trial, is the one step the Act leaves unclocked, which is why the constitutional courts have had to supply the missing discipline from Article 21 in the bail cases rather than find it in the statute.

## The miscellaneous chapter's working provisions

Chapter Ten is usually skipped, and four of its provisions do real work. Section 65 applies the Code of Criminal Procedure to arrest, search and seizure, summons, production and trial under the Act insofar as the Code is not inconsistent with the Act, which is the conduit through which remand, default bail and trial procedure enter the scheme. Section 66 permits the Director and the specified authorities to furnish information in their possession to officers of other agencies and governments where it is relevant to laws those agencies administer, and to receive information in return, a provision that formalises the intelligence circulation on which parallel proceedings feed. Section 71 gives the Act overriding effect over inconsistent laws, the provision invoked when the laundering scheme meets a state law or a sectoral statute pointing the other way. Section 72 provides for the continuation of proceedings in the event of death or insolvency of a person against whom proceedings are pending, so that the attachment and adjudication tracks are not defeated by the death of the person proceeded against, the property remaining answerable through the legal representative. Section 63 penalises the giving of false information to the authorities and the making of a vexatious search by an officer, the Act's only internal acknowledgement that its powers could be abused by the persons wielding them as well as resisted by the persons subject to them. A reader assembling the statute's safeguards should count these provisions with the better known ones: override is limited by inconsistency, information-sharing is purposive, proceedings survive death only against the property interest, and the officer who searches vexatiously is, on the text, answerable for it.

## The judicial position in brief, and the verdict

The constitutional challenges to the Act were decided by the Supreme Court on 27 July 2022 in Vijay Madanlal Choudhary v. Union of India, (2022) 10 SCC 24, where a Bench of A. M. Khanwilkar, Dinesh Maheshwari and C. T. Ravikumar JJ., answering more than two hundred petitions, upheld the attachment scheme in Section 5, the adjudication and possession provisions in Section 8 read with the restraint described above, the search and seizure powers in Sections 17 and 18, the arrest power in Section 19, the presumption in Section 24 as operating on foundational facts proved by the prosecution, the twin bail conditions in Section 45 as re-enacted in 2018, and the summons and statement regime in Section 50, with Directorate officers held not to be police officers; the Court also held that the Enforcement Case Information Report need not be supplied to the accused, that the offence in Section 3 is constituted by any one of the listed processes, and that a money laundering case cannot survive the final failure of its predicate. Review petitions were entertained on two questions only, the refusal of an ECIR copy and the reversal of the burden of proof, the notice having been issued by a Bench headed by the then Chief Justice, N. V. Ramana, in August 2022; the balance of the judgment operates undisturbed, and the later decisions on written grounds of arrest, the conspiracy narrowing and the Article 21 bail door, described in their sections above, have been built on its foundations rather than against them. The full treatment of that litigation, its reasoning and the enforcement evidence marshalled in it belongs to the companion article on the [Supreme Court's PMLA judgment](/2013/03/15/pmla-supreme-court-judgment/), and this pillar states the outcomes only so that the operative law can be used. Readers who teach or present on the Act can carry the dependency chain set out above into a classroom or a briefing through the [ReportMedic presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), which opens slide files in the browser for working through the stages in sequence.

The verdict on the statute, for this series, is the claim with which this article began and which the intervening detail has been assembled to support. Attachment is the sanction. A statute whose offence cannot outlive another case, whose trials conclude in dozens while its investigations run to thousands, and whose severest reliable consequence, a property freeze of indefinite practical duration coupled with custody under a reversed bail standard, is available within weeks of a case opening, is in operation an asset-freezing and detention regime to which a conviction machinery is attached but rarely run. That is not a denunciation; the freeze-first design is what the international template prescribes, and the evidence that laundering defeats slower regimes is real. It is, however, the accurate description, and every reform proposal, every defence strategy, every judicial safeguard and every political argument about this Act becomes clearer when it is stated. The reader who understands that the case is usually decided at the attachment hearing and the bail hearing, and that the trial is the part of the statute that exists mainly as a horizon, understands the Prevention of Money-Laundering Act, 2002 as it actually is.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the offence of money laundering under Indian law?

Section 3 of the Prevention of Money-Laundering Act, 2002 makes it an offence to be involved, directly or indirectly, in any process or activity connected with the proceeds of crime. The processes named are concealment, possession, acquisition, use, and projecting or claiming the property as untainted, and an Explanation added in 2019 confirms that any one of them, in any manner whatsoever, suffices. The offence is a continuing one: it lasts for as long as a person keeps enjoying the proceeds. It is a standalone offence, tried separately, but it depends entirely on the existence of a scheduled offence that generated the proceeds.

### Q: What is a scheduled offence and why does it matter?

A scheduled offence is an offence listed in the Schedule to the Act, organised in Parts A, B and C. It matters because it is the foundation of everything else in the statute: unless a scheduled offence has been committed and has generated property, there are no proceeds of crime, and without proceeds of crime there can be no money laundering offence, no attachment and no prosecution. Part A lists offences under many enactments, from the Penal Code and the narcotics law to the corruption law, with no monetary threshold. Part B covers a Customs false-declaration offence above one crore rupees. Part C covers offences with cross border implications.

### Q: What counts as proceeds of crime under the PMLA?

Section 2(1)(u) defines proceeds of crime as any property derived or obtained, directly or indirectly, by any person as a result of criminal activity relating to a scheduled offence. The definition also reaches the value of any such property, which allows property of equivalent value to be attached where the original proceeds have been spent or moved beyond reach, and, where the property is taken or held outside the country, property equivalent in value held within the country or abroad. Property bought with the proceeds, property into which they have been converted, and the price received on selling them all remain proceeds of crime through each substitution.

### Q: How does provisional attachment work under the PMLA?

Under Section 5, the Director or an authorised officer not below the rank of Deputy Director may attach property provisionally where the officer has reason to believe, recorded in writing on the basis of material in possession, that the property is proceeds of crime and is likely to be concealed or transferred so as to frustrate confiscation. The order must be served on the holder, a copy goes to the Adjudicating Authority, and a complaint setting out the facts must be filed before that Authority within thirty days. A provisional attachment lasts for a maximum of 180 days unless the Adjudicating Authority confirms it within that period.

### Q: What powers of arrest does the Enforcement Directorate have?

Section 19 empowers the Director, a Deputy Director, an Assistant Director or another authorised officer to arrest a person where the officer has, on the basis of material in possession, reason to believe, recorded in writing, that the person has been guilty of an offence punishable under the Act. No warrant or court order is needed beforehand. The arrested person must be informed of the grounds of arrest, which the Supreme Court has held must be furnished in writing, the arrest order and the material must be forwarded to the Adjudicating Authority in a sealed envelope, and the person must be produced before a Special Court or a Magistrate within twenty-four hours, excluding journey time.

### Q: Why did the PMLA take until 2005 to come into force?

The Act received Presidential assent on 17 January 2003 as Act 15 of 2003 but was commenced only on 1 July 2005, because it could not operate until its machinery existed. Rules had to be framed for the record-keeping duties of banks and financial institutions, an Adjudicating Authority and an Appellate Tribunal had to be constituted, a financial intelligence unit had to be set up to receive the reports the Act requires, and the Directorate of Enforcement had to be organised to investigate. The Act and its principal rules were therefore brought into force together. The delay reflects the statute's administrative design: its powers run through institutions that had to be built first.

### Q: What is an ECIR and how does it differ from an FIR?

An ECIR, or Enforcement Case Information Report, is the internal document the Directorate records when it opens a money laundering investigation. It differs from a first information report in decisive respects. An FIR is recorded by the police under the Code of Criminal Procedure, sets the criminal process in motion against the offence it names, and is a document the informant and, in practice, the accused can obtain. An ECIR is recorded by the Directorate under the PMLA's own scheme, is founded on the existence of a scheduled offence investigated by another agency, and is an internal document: the Supreme Court has held that the person under investigation is not entitled to a copy of it as a matter of right.

### Q: Does an acquittal in the predicate offence end a money laundering charge?

Yes, if the acquittal is final. The Supreme Court in Vijay Madanlal Choudhary held that where a person is finally discharged or acquitted of the scheduled offence, or the criminal case is quashed by a competent court, there can be no offence of money laundering against that person or against anyone claiming title to the property through that person, because there are no proceeds of crime. The position is different while the predicate case is merely pending or has ended in a way that is under appeal: the money laundering case may proceed in the meantime, since the interim stages of the Act require only reason to believe that a scheduled offence generated the property.

### Q: What is the punishment for money laundering under Section 4 of the PMLA?

Section 4 punishes money laundering with rigorous imprisonment for a term of not less than three years and up to seven years, together with a fine, which has carried no statutory ceiling since the amendment of 2013 removed the original cap of five lakh rupees. Where the proceeds of crime relate to an offence under paragraph 2 of Part A of the Schedule, the narcotics offences, the maximum term rises to ten years. Punishment follows only on conviction by the Special Court at the end of a trial. The sentence is separate from attachment and confiscation of property, which operate on their own tracks and timelines.

### Q: What does a summons under Section 50 of the PMLA require a person to do?

A summons under Section 50 requires the person summoned to attend before the officer, personally or through an authorised agent, to answer questions on oath truthfully, and to produce the documents and records called for. The officer exercises the powers of a civil court, including enforcing attendance and examining on oath, and the proceedings are treated as judicial proceedings, so a false statement is punishable as false evidence. A summons is not an accusation: witnesses, bankers, accountants and family members are summoned as well as suspects. Non-attendance and false answers both carry consequences, and statements recorded under the section are admissible in evidence in the proceedings.

### Q: What is the role of the Adjudicating Authority in a PMLA attachment case?

The Adjudicating Authority is the first independent check on an attachment. On receiving the Directorate's complaint, it issues notice to the person whose property is attached and to anyone claiming an interest in the property, calling on them to disclose the sources of their income and assets and to show cause why the property should not be declared property involved in money laundering. It considers the replies, hears the parties, examines the material, and records a finding. If it finds the property involved in money laundering, it confirms the attachment; if not, the attachment is released. Its order is appealable to the Appellate Tribunal under Section 26.

### Q: Is attachment under the PMLA the same thing as confiscation?

No, and the difference is fundamental. Attachment is a freeze: it prevents the property from being transferred or dealt with, but it changes no ownership, and it is imposed first by an officer on recorded reasons and then continued by the Adjudicating Authority on a prima facie assessment. Confiscation is the permanent divesting of the property to the Central Government, and under Section 8(5) only the Special Court can order it, and only after a trial that ends in conviction for money laundering. If the trial ends in acquittal, Section 8(6) requires the attachment to be released. Commentary that describes every attachment as a confiscation misstates the Act, although the freeze itself can last for years.

### Q: Can the ED attach property belonging to a person who is not accused of any offence?

Yes. Attachment under the Act is directed at property, not only at accused persons. Property that is proceeds of crime, or property of equivalent value, may be attached in the hands of whoever holds it, including relatives, companies and third parties who are not accused in either the scheduled offence or the money laundering case. The protection for such a holder is procedural and real: the holder receives notice in the adjudication, may appear before the Adjudicating Authority to establish that the property is not proceeds of crime or was acquired for legitimate consideration without knowledge of its character, and may seek restoration, including under Section 8(8) where a legitimate interest acquired in good faith is shown.

### Q: What must banks and other reporting entities do under the PMLA?

Chapter Four requires every reporting entity, which includes banking companies, financial institutions, intermediaries and persons carrying on designated businesses or professions, to verify the identity of its clients, maintain records of transactions and of client identity for five years, and furnish information to the Financial Intelligence Unit, India in the prescribed manner. The Director may inquire into compliance and, for each failure, issue a warning, direct specific corrective steps, require periodic compliance reports, or impose a monetary penalty of not less than ten thousand rupees and up to one lakh rupees. Reporting entities and their officers are protected from civil proceedings for furnishing information under the chapter.

### Q: How does the PMLA reach offences and property outside India?

Part C of the Schedule covers offences with cross border implications: conduct abroad that would be a scheduled offence if committed in India, where the proceeds are brought into India, and scheduled offences committed in India whose proceeds are transferred abroad. The definition of proceeds of crime supports this reach, covering, where property is taken or held outside the country, property equivalent in value held within the country or abroad, so the Directorate can attach the domestic equivalent of money that has left. Chapter Nine adds the machinery of reciprocal arrangements with other countries, under which assistance can be sought and given for investigation, attachment and confiscation across borders. Persons carrying on virtual digital asset businesses were brought within the reporting chapter by notification in 2023.

### Q: Does the PMLA apply when the scheduled offence was committed before the Act came into force?

It can. The Act punishes the dealing with proceeds of crime, and Section 3, as clarified in 2019, treats that dealing as a continuing activity that lasts while a person conceals, possesses, acquires, uses or enjoys the proceeds. Where a scheduled offence predates the Act's commencement on 1 July 2005 but the proceeds continued to be held or dealt with after that date, the post-commencement dealing can constitute money laundering, because the act punished is the laundering and not the earlier predicate crime. The predicate must still be an offence that stands in the Schedule. Challenges based purely on the age of the predicate offence, without addressing the continuing dealing, have not succeeded on that ground alone.

### Q: What is the burden of proof under Section 24 of the PMLA?

Section 24 creates a presumption on the character of property. Where a person is charged with money laundering, the court or the Adjudicating Authority shall presume, unless the contrary is proved, that the proceeds of crime in question are involved in money laundering; in the case of any other person proceeded against, the Authority or court may draw the same presumption. The presumption does not operate in the air. The prosecution must first establish the foundational facts, that a scheduled offence was committed and that the property is derived from it or represents its value. Once those foundations are proved, the burden of showing that the property is untainted falls on the person holding it.

### Q: Can a company be prosecuted for money laundering under the PMLA?

Yes. Section 70 provides that where an offence under the Act is committed by a company, the company itself and every person who was in charge of, and responsible to, the company for the conduct of its business at the time are deemed guilty, unless that person proves the offence was committed without their knowledge or that they exercised due diligence to prevent it. A director, manager, secretary or other officer is also liable where the offence occurred with that person's consent or connivance, or is attributable to that person's neglect. In practice the Directorate arrays companies as accused and proceeds against their responsible officers alongside, and company officers are routinely summoned under Section 50 to explain the company's transactions.

### Q: What happens to attached property at the end of a PMLA trial?

The trial's outcome decides the property's fate. If the Special Court finds the offence of money laundering committed, Section 8(5) requires the court to order the property involved in the money laundering to be confiscated to the Central Government, and the attachment becomes final. If the offence is found not committed, Section 8(6) requires the attachment to be released and the property restored to its holder. A narrow exception survives even a confiscation order: under Section 8(8), a person who establishes a legitimate interest in the property, acquired in good faith and without knowledge of its tainted character, may apply to the Special Court for restoration of the property or the interest.

### Q: Must a person prosecuted for money laundering also be an accused in the scheduled offence case?

No. The Supreme Court held in Pavana Dibbur v. Directorate of Enforcement that it is not necessary for a person charged under Section 3 to have been shown as an accused in the scheduled offence. The offence targets the handling of proceeds, and the classic launderer is often a person who had no part in the underlying crime: a professional who layers the money, a relative who holds the property, a company that receives it. What the prosecution must establish is that a scheduled offence was in fact committed by someone and generated the property, and that the accused knowingly dealt with that property. Where no scheduled offence exists at all, the prosecution fails at its root.
