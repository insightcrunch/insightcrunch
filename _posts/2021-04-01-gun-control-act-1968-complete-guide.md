## What the Gun Control Act Does and the Design That Decides the Rest

When a firearm changes hands in the United States, which federal rules attach to the transaction, and why do the same rules fall silent when the setting changes from a dealer's counter to a transfer between two private individuals in the same state? That operative question, rather than the identity of the firearm itself, organizes the statute examined in this article. A sale by a licensed dealer carries a federal license behind it, a set of records created at the moment of transfer, conditions on the age and residence of the buyer, and a bar on delivery to a person within a prohibited category. A transfer between two nonlicensees who reside in the same state carries almost none of that federal apparatus. The difference does not turn on the weapon, on its price, or on whether money changed hands at all. The difference turns on the channel through which the firearm moves and on the licensed status of the person who transfers it. Every later provision in the statute, from the definition of engaging in the business to the import standard and the amendments enacted eighteen years after passage, returns to that initial allocation of duties between a regulated commercial channel and everything that lies outside it.

![Licensed firearms dealer completing a federal transaction record under the Gun Control Act - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The formal identity of the statute is exact. The Gun Control Act of 1968, Public Law 90-618, published at 82 Stat. 1213, was signed on October 22, 1968, by President Lyndon B. Johnson and enacted by the 90th Congress as H.R. 17735, a bill introduced by Representative Emanuel Celler on June 10, 1968. The act is codified principally at chapter 44 of title 18 of the United States Code, sections 921 to 931. Title I of the act, titled State Firearms Control Assistance, wrote chapter 44. Title II amended the National Firearms Act, the registration and transfer tax regime codified at chapter 53 of title 26. Title III amended the Omnibus Crime Control and Safe Streets Act of 1968, including possession provisions that had stood in an appendix to title 18. Section 101 of the act stated the purpose of the title in two parts held together in a single clause. The purpose was to provide support to federal, state, and local law enforcement officials in their fight against crime and violence. The purpose was expressly not to place any undue or unnecessary federal restrictions or burdens on law abiding citizens in acquiring, possessing, or using firearms for lawful purposes. That second statement belongs to the enacted text rather than to later commentary, and the structure of chapter 44 gives it operational meaning, because the burdens the statute imposed fell in the first instance on persons engaged in a business rather than on persons acquiring firearms for the lawful purposes the clause named.

The claim at the center of this article can be named and then tested against each part of the chapter. Call it the licensed channel claim: the statute regulates dealers, not firearms, meaning that its requirements attach to transactions in the licensed channel rather than to firearms as objects. The claim is stated as a claim about statutory design, and the text either bears it out provision by provision or the claim fails. A firearm, as section 921(a)(3) defines the term, is any weapon that will or is designed to or may readily be converted to expel a projectile by the action of an explosive, together with the frame or receiver of any such weapon, any firearm muffler or silencer, and any destructive device. That definition decides which objects fall inside the chapter at all, and the antique firearm exclusion in section 921(a)(16) removes an older class of arms from the chapter entirely. Beyond that threshold question of definition, however, the chapter imposes almost no condition that follows a firearm wherever the firearm goes. No provision licenses the owner of a firearm. No provision registers a firearm or its owner in a federal system. No provision sets a federal storage standard for a firearm held in a home. The duties that fill the chapter instead attach at defined moments to defined persons, and the person who carries the heaviest concentration of those duties is the licensee.

Section 923 states the core of that arrangement. A federal license is required for a person engaged in the business of importing, manufacturing, or dealing in firearms, and separate license classes cover the importer, the manufacturer, the dealer, and the collector of curios and relics. Licensed importers, manufacturers, and dealers must maintain records of importation, production, shipment, receipt, sale, or other disposition, in the acquisition and disposition record that trade practice calls the bound book. A licensee may not sell or deliver a firearm until the required records are complete. For an over the counter transfer to an unlicensed person, the licensee completes the Firearms Transaction Record, ATF Form 4473, on which the buyer certifies eligibility and identity. Licensees report multiple handgun sales, report theft or loss from inventory, answer tracing requests within the period the statute prescribes, and submit to compliance inspection of records and inventory under limits the statute sets. Each of those duties presupposes licensed status. A person outside the licensed channel owes none of them, because the duties were written as conditions of holding the license and conducting the licensed business.

The transfer rules in section 922 extend the same design outward from the licensee to the movement of firearms in commerce. A licensee sells in person at licensed premises, observes age floors that differ for handguns and for long guns, honors the law of the state where the transfer occurs, and reaches a buyer in another state only through the routes the statute preserves, which for a handgun means delivery through a licensee in the buyer's home state. A nonlicensee, by contrast, meets federal transfer rules at two points only. Movement across a state line is the first point, because sections 922(a)(3) and 922(a)(5) close private interstate acquisition and private interstate transfer outside narrow exceptions for bequest, intestate succession, and temporary sporting loans. Transfer to a prohibited person is the second point, because section 922(d) bars any person, licensed or not, from selling or disposing of a firearm to a person the transferor knows or has reasonable cause to believe falls within a prohibited category. Between those two points, the federal statute leaves a transfer between nonlicensees in the same state without a license requirement, without a record requirement, and without a check requirement.

Two parts of the chapter qualify the licensed channel claim without displacing it, and an accurate account states both qualifications directly. The prohibited person provisions in section 922(g) attach to persons rather than to transactions, making possession, shipment, transport, and receipt unlawful for a person within any of nine categories, from a felony conviction to a qualifying restraining order and a misdemeanor crime of domestic violence. That possession bar operates wherever the person is found, inside or outside a licensed transaction, although enforcement of the bar at the point of acquisition depends in practice on the checkpoint that a licensee transfer supplies. The import provisions operate at the national border rather than at a dealer's counter, barring importation in section 922(l) except as section 925(d) authorizes it and conditioning one authorization channel on a firearm being generally recognized as particularly suitable for or readily adaptable to sporting purposes. Even those qualifications confirm the underlying allocation in a different form. The possession bar defines who may not receive a firearm at all, while the licensed channel supplies the machinery that examines status before a retail transfer. The import bar defines what may enter commerce, while licensed importers conduct the entry. Status and entry are regulated directly. Ordinary domestic transfers are regulated through the dealer.

The later layers of the chapter preserve that allocation each time Congress returned to the statute. The Firearm Owners' Protection Act of 1986 narrowed the definition of engaged in the business, adopted willfulness as the operative mental state for revocation and for the principal offenses, limited routine compliance inspections, removed the contiguous state condition from the out of state long gun exception, added a safe passage rule for interstate transport, barred any regulation establishing a registration system, and, through the Hughes Amendment, closed new manufacture of machine guns for private transfer after May 19, 1986. The Brady Act layer added a background check duty in section 922(t), operating through the national instant criminal background check system from November 30, 1998, and that duty attaches to licensee transfers. Additions in 1994 and 1996 enlarged the list of prohibited categories. None of those enactments licensed owners, registered firearms generally, or placed a federal record or check duty on a transfer between two nonlicensees in the same state. The history of amendment therefore reads as a series of adjustments to the licensed channel and to the categories of prohibited persons, conducted inside the design enacted in October 1968.

The sections that follow take the design apart in the order the statute presents it. The lineage and enactment history come first, because the October act repealed one predecessor, amended a second, and superseded a firearms title enacted four months earlier in the same year. Licensing and records follow, because the bound book and the transaction record are the working instruments of the licensed channel. Transfer rules and the private transfer space come next, followed by the nine prohibited categories with the statutes that added each later category, the import provisions and the sporting purposes standard, the revision of 1986 taken amendment by amendment, and the arguments over the architecture stated as the holders of each position state them. A comparison table set after the transfer section places the licensee and the nonlicensee side by side, requirement by requirement, so that the allocation described in this opening can be checked against the individual duties rather than accepted as a summary. The closing section then states what the statute leaves undone, in the same provision by provision manner, because the boundaries of this statute are as much a part of its content as the duties inside them.

## The Statute's Lineage and the Road to Enactment

The Gun Control Act of 1968 did not create federal firearms law from nothing. By the autumn of 1968, Congress had legislated on firearms for more than three decades, and the statutes already on the books had divided the field into distinct channels. One channel, built in 1938, licensed the commercial trade and policed a small set of interstate transactions involving two classes of person. Another channel, built in 1934, treated a defined group of weapons through registration and a transfer tax rather than through the ordinary rules of commerce. A third body of law arrived only in June 1968, when an omnibus crime statute wrote the first version of chapter 44 of title 18 and added a separate set of possession bars in another title. The October statute gathered these strands together, repealed one predecessor outright, amended a second, and replaced the first version of the third.

The Federal Firearms Act of 1938 supplied the commercial foundation. It was the first federal statute to license manufacturers and dealers in firearms, and that step carried a particular significance. Before a licensing system exists, federal law can punish specified transactions after they occur, but it has no standing register of the persons lawfully conducting the trade and no license whose terms define the conditions of lawful dealing. The 1938 act crossed that threshold. The persons who made firearms and the persons who sold them became identifiable to the federal government through a license, and the conduct of the trade could be organized around that status. The October act repealed the 1938 statute, but it did not abandon the licensing principle the earlier act had introduced thirty years before. It reenacted many of the 1938 provisions in expanded form, and licensing stood at the center of the reenactment, with the licensee, a person holding a federal license, as the basic unit around which the 1968 framework was constructed.

The prohibitions of the 1938 act were narrower than its licensing idea. The statute barred the interstate shipment of firearms to convicted felons and to fugitives, and it barred receipt of firearms by persons in those two classes. Each element of that formulation marked a boundary. The regulated act was shipment in interstate commerce, together with receipt, rather than possession in the abstract. The regulated persons numbered two classes, persons convicted of felonies and persons fleeing from justice, rather than a longer catalogue of statuses. The statute thus combined a commerce element with a status element, and a violation required the two to meet. That combination became a durable feature of federal firearms drafting. Later enactments broadened the list of statuses and broadened the conduct covered beyond shipment and receipt, but the underlying technique of defining an offense by joining a person's status to an act in commerce descended from the 1938 model.

The limits of the 1938 act followed from the same boundaries. Because its bars addressed interstate shipment and receipt, and because its status list stopped at convicted felons and fugitives, large areas of firearms activity lay outside its prohibitions. Its treatment of the mails illustrates the point. Interstate mail order sales of firearms to individuals remained lawful under the 1938 act, and that survival mattered decades later, when the manner in which a rifle had been acquired became a central fact in a presidential assassination. Handgun mail order had been restricted earlier through postal law and through the June 1968 act, so the mail order question that persisted into the 1960s concerned rifles and shotguns in particular. The 1938 act also left other classes of person, whose conduct the 1968 Congress would address by name, outside the federal prohibition, and left channels at its edges, including mail order acquisition by individuals across state lines, comparatively untouched.

Alongside the 1938 act stood a second and separate regime, the National Firearms Act of 1934. The two statutes are sometimes treated as successive drafts of a single policy, but their methods differed in kind. The 1934 act addressed a defined set of weapons through registration and a transfer tax, a fiscal and record based method administered under chapter 53 of title 26, the Internal Revenue Code. The weapons within that regime included machine guns, silencers, and short-barreled rifles and shotguns, and the regime rested on identifying the weapon, recording its transfer, and collecting the tax attached to that transfer. In the organization of the federal firearms laws, the National Firearms Act forms Title II, while the Gun Control Act of 1968 forms Title I. The distinction is not a mere numbering convention. Title I regulates commerce in firearms generally, through licensing and transfer rules, while Title II imposes its registration and tax structure on the narrower class of weapons within its definitions, under a different statutory text in a different title of the United States Code.

Because the regimes were separate, the 1968 Congress had to legislate on both fronts in October, and the resulting statute did so through its own Title II. Title I of the Gun Control Act of 1968, titled State Firearms Control Assistance, wrote chapter 44 of title 18. Title II of the act turned to the 1934 statute and amended it. The amendments added destructive devices to the definitions of the National Firearms Act, and they expanded the definition of machinegun. The amendments also addressed a constitutional defect in the registration scheme. In Haynes v. United States, 390 U.S. 85 (1968), the Supreme Court had identified a self-incrimination defect in the earlier scheme, a defect that threatened the registration mechanism at the heart of the 1934 act. Congress did not respond to Haynes by abandoning registration. Congress repaired the mechanism instead, amending the text so that registration could operate without the flaw the Court had found. The Supreme Court upheld the amended scheme in United States v. Freed, 401 U.S. 601 (1971), closing the sequence that Haynes had opened.

The difference in treatment reflected the difference between the predecessors. The 1938 act was a narrower version of what the 1968 act undertook to do, so it could be repealed and reenacted in expanded form. The 1934 act did work the 1968 act did not duplicate, so it was retained and amended rather than replaced.

The legislative road to October 1968 ran through an earlier failure in the Senate. President John F. Kennedy was assassinated on November 22, 1963, and the rifle used in that assassination had been bought by mail order. That acquisition method connected the event to a specific feature of the inherited law, because interstate mail order sales to individuals had remained lawful under the 1938 framework. Senator Thomas J. Dodd of Connecticut introduced interstate firearms bills in 1963 and again in 1965, cited as S. 1975 and S. 1592 respectively. The subject of the bills was the interstate dimension of firearms commerce, the same dimension the 1938 act had regulated in part and left open in part. Where the 1938 act had barred interstate shipment to two classes of person while leaving mail order acquisition by individuals lawful, legislation addressed to interstate firearms traffic confronted the channel itself rather than only the status of the recipient.

Neither of Dodd's bills passed, and the failure left the 1938 framework in place as the governing federal structure for the trade through the middle of the decade. When Congress acted in 1968, it acted on a subject with a documented legislative history of nonenactment, and the final act went well beyond the interstate question alone, reaching licensing, prohibited statuses, and the National Firearms Act amendments described above. The breadth of the final act should not obscure the narrower origin of its legislative momentum in the mail order rifle of November 1963.

The calendar of 1968 then supplied events that belonged to the record of that year in their own right. Martin Luther King Jr. was assassinated on April 4, 1968. Senator Robert F. Kennedy was shot on June 5, 1968, and died on June 6, 1968. These dates frame the legislative sequence that followed. The June firearms legislation was signed on June 19, 1968, thirteen days after the death of Senator Kennedy. The bill that became the Gun Control Act of 1968 had been introduced on June 10, 1968, between the death of Senator Kennedy and the signing of the June act. Within a span running from early April to late October, two public figures were killed and two firearms statutes were enacted.

The first of the two 1968 statutes was [the Omnibus Crime Control Act of 1968](/2017/12/15/omnibus-crime-control-act-1968/), formally the Omnibus Crime Control and Safe Streets Act of 1968, Public Law 90-351, signed on June 19, 1968. Its Title IV, which carried the same title, State Firearms Control Assistance, that the October act would later give to its own Title I, first enacted chapter 44 of title 18, four months before the Gun Control Act of 1968. Title IV was aimed chiefly at handguns and at interstate trade. It barred interstate handgun trade to individuals, and it set a minimum age of twenty-one for handgun purchases from licensees. Each measure operated on a channel rather than on firearms commerce in general. The interstate channel between a seller and an individual buyer was closed for handguns, and the licensed channel was conditioned by an age floor for that class of weapon. Chapter 44 thus entered the United States Code through a crime bill in June, in a form directed principally at one category of firearm moving through one kind of transaction, before the October statute rewrote the chapter in broader terms.

The June act addressed prohibited persons through a different title and a different technique. Its Title VII separately barred receipt, possession, or transportation in commerce by five classes of person: persons convicted of felonies, veterans discharged under dishonorable conditions, persons adjudicated mentally incompetent, aliens unlawfully in the country, and persons who had renounced citizenship. The provisions sat in sections 1201 to 1203 of the appendix to title 18, apart from chapter 44 itself. Two features of the Title VII scheme distinguish it from the older 1938 bars. First, the conduct covered extended beyond shipment and receipt to possession and transportation in commerce, so the prohibition attached to continued custody of a firearm and not only to acquisition. Second, the classes numbered five rather than two, and they reached statuses unconnected to a criminal conviction. The June statute therefore contained two firearms frameworks in two places, a commerce framework in Title IV and a possession framework in Title VII, and the October statute dealt with them differently.

President Lyndon B. Johnson stated, on signing the June act and in messages to Congress during that summer, that the firearms provisions enacted in June fell short of the measures he had requested. The measures he identified were owner licensing and registration. Johnson's documented position was that Congress had enacted a firearms title addressed to handgun trade and to prohibited persons while declining two measures that would have applied to firearm owners generally: a system for licensing owners and a system for registering firearms. The gap between request and enactment was therefore precise. It did not concern the age floor for handgun purchases, the interstate handgun provisions, or the Title VII classes, all of which the June act contained. It concerned two specified mechanisms that the June act did not contain, and the President named the shortfall at the time of signing and returned to the subject in messages to Congress during the summer of 1968.

The October act did not close the gap Johnson had named. Congress did not enact owner licensing in the Gun Control Act of 1968, and Congress did not enact registration of firearms or of firearm owners in that act either. The distinction between licensing dealers and licensing owners is essential to stating the point accurately. The 1968 act rested on a licensing system, but the licensees were the importers, manufacturers, and dealers engaged in the trade, in a line of descent from the 1938 act. A license to engage in the business is a different legal instrument from a license to own, and the statute enacted the first while omitting the second. Registration presents the same contrast. The National Firearms Act regime, which the October act amended, registered a defined class of weapons, but that system predated 1968 and covered the weapons within its definitions. A general registration of firearms or owners, of the kind Johnson stated he had requested, formed no part of the October enactment.

### What did the Gun Control Act replace?

The Gun Control Act of 1968 repealed the Federal Firearms Act of 1938 and reenacted many of its provisions in expanded form. It also superseded and expanded the firearms title first enacted that June, replacing the initial version of chapter 44 of title 18 with a broader statutory framework.

The replacement operated differently against each predecessor. Against the 1938 act, the operation was repeal coupled with reenactment. The older statute ceased to stand as an independent text, while many of its provisions continued inside the new chapter 44 in expanded form, so a reader in search of the 1938 rules after October 1968 had to look within the successor statute rather than beside it. Against the June act, the operation was supersession within a single code chapter, as the June version of Title IV, only four months old, gave way to the October text. Against the National Firearms Act, there was no replacement at all. Title II amended the 1934 statute and left it standing in chapter 53 of title 26. The act's Title III then amended the June omnibus statute itself, including the Title VII possession provisions in the appendix.

The vehicle for the October statute was H.R. 17735, introduced by Representative Emanuel Celler on June 10, 1968, and associated with the House Judiciary Committee. The bill was introduced four days after the death of Senator Robert F. Kennedy and nine days before the President signed the June omnibus act, so the successor bill existed on the House calendar before the statute it would partly supersede had been signed. The Gun Control Act of 1968 was enacted by the 90th Congress and codified principally at chapter 44 of title 18, sections 921 to 931, with its stated purpose in section 101 directed to supporting federal, state, and local law enforcement in the fight against crime and violence. Section 101 also stated what the purpose was not: it was not the purpose of the title to place undue or unnecessary federal restrictions or burdens on law-abiding citizens in acquiring, possessing, or using firearms for lawful purposes.

Passage proceeded through both chambers during the summer and early autumn. The House passed H.R. 17735 on July 24, 1968, by a vote of 305 to 118. The Senate passed the bill on September 18, 1968, by a vote of 70 to 17, acting in lieu of S. 3633, the Senate measure on the same subject. The two chambers then resolved the differences between their positions through a conference, and the conference report was agreed to on October 10, 1968. President Johnson signed the bill on October 22, 1968, and it became Public Law 90-618, published at 82 Stat. 1213. The interval between introduction and signature ran a little longer than four months. Two statutes bearing the same title for their firearms provisions, State Firearms Control Assistance, had accordingly been enacted by the same Congress within a single year, each writing chapter 44 of title 18, with the later enactment displacing the earlier one.

The closing act of the sequence was an act of consolidation. The October statute superseded and expanded the firearms title that the June act had created, replacing a handgun centered Title IV with a comprehensive chapter governing the licensed trade and the transfers that ran through it. It repealed the Federal Firearms Act of 1938 and carried the substance of many 1938 provisions forward in expanded form. It amended the National Firearms Act of 1934 rather than disturbing its separate registration and tax regime, and it left unenacted the owner licensing and registration measures that President Johnson stated he had requested. The road to enactment had run from the licensing experiment of 1938, through the failure of Senator Dodd's interstate bills of 1963 and 1965, through the assassinations of April and June 1968 and the partial framework enacted on June 19 of that year. On October 22, 1968, that road ended in a statute that gathered the commerce rules, the prohibited person bars, and the amended registration regime into a single public law.

## The Licensing System and the Records It Keeps

The licensing system in chapter 44 of title 18 of the United States Code attaches to a defined object, a defined set of occupations, and a defined body of paper. The Gun Control Act of 1968 did not license firearm owners, and the statute imposed no federal license requirement on a person who possessed a firearm for personal use. The license requirement instead fell on commerce. A person who imported firearms, manufactured firearms, or dealt in firearms as a business carried on that activity under a federal license issued under section 923, kept records prescribed under that section, and submitted to the inspection, reporting, and tracing duties that followed from licensed status. The result was a channel structure. Inside the licensed channel, each transfer left a record held by a licensee. Outside that channel, transfers between nonlicensees residing in the same state carried no federal record requirement under the statute, apart from the separate bar on transfers to persons known to fall within a prohibited category. The records system therefore has to be understood on its own terms, as a set of duties placed on licensees and preserved at licensed premises, rather than as a central file maintained by the federal government.

Section 921(a)(3) supplies the definition that determines the reach of the entire system. Under that provision, the term firearm means any weapon that will expel a projectile by the action of an explosive, any weapon designed to expel a projectile by the action of an explosive, and any weapon that may readily be converted to expel a projectile by the action of an explosive. The definition also includes the frame or receiver of any such weapon, any firearm muffler or firearm silencer, and any destructive device. Section 921(a)(4) in turn defines destructive device to cover categories that include a bomb, a grenade, a rocket, a mine, and large bore weapons, subject to exceptions stated in that provision. The breadth of the opening language matters for licensing because the duty to hold a license, the duty to keep records, and the duties that govern transfer all turn on whether the item in question falls within the statutory definition. A frame or receiver counts as a firearm for purposes of the chapter even before assembly into a complete weapon, and a muffler or silencer counts as a firearm under the definition even though the transfer and possession regime for silencers and destructive devices belongs to the National Firearms Act registration system under chapter 53 of title 26.

The definition also contains an exclusion that removes a class of older and muzzle loading arms from the chapter altogether. Section 921(a)(16) excludes antique firearms from the definition that governs chapter 44. The excluded class covers firearms manufactured in or before 1898, certain replicas that were not designed for conventional ammunition, and muzzle loading firearms that use black powder or black powder substitutes and are incapable of using fixed ammunition. Because an antique firearm sits outside the chapter definition, the licensing provisions, the transfer provisions, and the prohibited person provisions of chapter 44 do not apply to an antique firearm as such. The exclusion draws a line based on date of manufacture, design, and loading method rather than on the identity of the person holding the item. A dealer handling only items within the antique exclusion therefore stands in a different position under the chapter from a dealer handling firearms manufactured after 1898 that use fixed ammunition, and the records duties described in section 923 follow that same line.

With the object defined, section 923 identifies the persons who must hold a license. The statute requires a federal license for anyone engaged in the business of importing, manufacturing, or dealing in firearms. The license classes named in the fact of the statutory scheme include the licensed importer, the licensed manufacturer, the licensed dealer, and the licensed collector. Each class corresponds to a distinct activity. The importer brings firearms into the United States through the import provisions of the chapter. The manufacturer produces firearms. The dealer sells and distributes firearms at wholesale or retail through licensed premises. The collector license serves a narrower purpose. A collector of curios and relics holds a license that reaches only curios and relics, and that license does not authorize a general dealing business in the full range of firearms covered by the chapter. The distinction preserves a place in the licensing structure for collecting activity directed at curios and relics while keeping ordinary commercial dealing under the dealer license and its accompanying record duties.

The threshold question for the dealer, importer, and manufacturer classes is whether a person is engaged in the business at all. Section 921(a)(21) supplies the operative definition, in the formulation adopted through the Firearm Owners' Protection Act of 1986. For a dealer, the definition describes a person who devotes time, attention, and labor to dealing in firearms as a regular course of trade or business with the principal objective of livelihood and profit through the repetitive purchase and resale of firearms. Each element of that formulation carries weight. Devotion of time, attention, and labor separates a business from an isolated act. A regular course of trade or business separates repeated commercial activity from a single transaction. The principal objective of livelihood and profit separates commercial dealing from other reasons a person might acquire or dispose of firearms. Repetitive purchase and resale describes the pattern that the definition treats as dealing, namely acquisition followed by disposition carried on as an ongoing practice rather than as an occasional event.

The same definition states what engaged in the business does not include. The definition excludes a person who makes occasional sales, exchanges, or purchases of firearms for the enhancement of a personal collection or for a hobby. That exclusion places personal collecting activity outside the dealer licensing requirement when the activity remains occasional and directed toward a personal collection or a hobby. A person who sells a firearm from a personal collection on an occasional basis, or who buys and exchanges firearms to improve that collection, does not become a dealer under the definition by reason of those acts alone. The line drawn by section 921(a)(21) therefore runs between repetitive purchase and resale pursued for livelihood and profit on one side, and occasional transactions connected to a personal collection or hobby on the other side. The location of that line determines whether the full apparatus of licensing, recordkeeping, inspection, and reporting applies to a person at all, because those duties attach only after licensed status, or the obligation to obtain a license, has been established.

### What records must a licensed dealer keep?

A licensed dealer must maintain records of every firearm received and every firearm disposed of, including importation, production, shipment, receipt, sale, and other disposition, in an acquisition and disposition record, and must complete a Firearms Transaction Record for each transfer to an unlicensed person.

The acquisition and disposition record required by section 923(g)(1)(A) forms the central ledger of a licensed business. Licensed importers, licensed manufacturers, and licensed dealers must maintain records of importation, production, shipment, receipt, sale, or other disposition of firearms. In trade practice the acquisition and disposition record is kept as the bound book, a continuing record in which each firearm entering the licensee inventory is entered as an acquisition and each firearm leaving that inventory is entered as a disposition. Read in sequence, the entries allow a firearm to be followed into a licensed business and back out of that business, with the identity of the source of acquisition and the identity of the recipient on disposition forming the links that later tracing uses. The record is a licensee record, kept by the licensee as part of the licensed operation, and the statute ties the act of transfer to the completeness of that record. A licensee may not sell or deliver a firearm until the required records are complete, so recordkeeping is not an after the fact accounting exercise that follows a sale at a later convenience. The record stands as a condition that accompanies the transfer itself.

The second principal record is the transaction record completed at the point of retail transfer to an unlicensed buyer. The Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives Form 4473, titled the Firearms Transaction Record, is the form prescribed under the section 923 recordkeeping authority and completed for each over the counter transfer by a licensee to an unlicensed person. On that form, the buyer certifies eligibility and identity, answering the questions that allow the licensee to determine whether the transfer may proceed under the prohibited person provisions and the other conditions in section 922. The licensee in turn records identification presented by the buyer and, after the background check layer added by later legislation took effect, the background check response received in connection with the transfer. The form therefore performs two functions at once. The certification places a signed statement of eligibility and identity from the buyer into the licensee file, and the licensee entries place the verification steps taken at the counter into the same file. Together with the acquisition and disposition entry for the same firearm, the transaction record completes the licensee side of a retail sale, linking a specific firearm, identified in the inventory record, to a specific unlicensed transferee, identified in the transaction record.

Accuracy in those records carries its own prohibition. Section 922(m) bars knowingly making false statements in licensee records, and false statement liability also sits in section 924(a)(3), which covers false statements in records kept by a licensee. The prohibition reaches both sides of the counter in practical effect, because the record contains statements made by the buyer in the certification portion of the transaction record and entries made by the licensee in completing the transaction and inventory records. A knowingly false statement in that material is not treated as a mere clerical defect. The statute places the false statement offense alongside the recordkeeping duty itself, so that the integrity of the bound book and the transaction record is protected by a penalty provision directed at the act of falsification. The distinction between an inadvertent error and a knowingly false statement matters under that structure, since the prohibition in section 922(m) is framed around a knowing act rather than around any imperfection that might appear in a large volume of entries.

Federal oversight of the licensed channel operates principally through inspection of the records and inventory that licensees maintain. Section 923(g)(1)(B) permits officers of the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives, without reasonable cause or warrant, to enter the premises of a licensee during business hours to examine records and inventory for compliance with the chapter. That authority is limited in frequency for routine matters. After the amendment made in 1986, a routine compliance inspection under that provision may occur not more than once during any twelve month period. The limit restricts the number of warrantless routine entries that may be made to check compliance, while leaving other bases of entry intact. Entries outside the routine compliance inspection rest on a warrant, on consent, or on a reasonable cause basis tied to a criminal investigation, and records may also be examined in connection with the tracing of a firearm. The structure therefore separates ordinary compliance review, which is confined to a single inspection in a twelve month period, from investigative access, which follows the standards that govern criminal investigations and trace requests rather than the routine inspection allowance.

Two reporting duties supplement inspection by requiring licensees to send specified information to the federal government at the time an event occurs. Under section 923(g)(3)(A), a licensee must report the sale or other disposition of two or more handguns to an unlicensed person at one time or within five consecutive business days. The report, described in practice as a multiple sales report, is triggered by the pattern of the transactions rather than by any single handgun transfer standing alone. A single handgun sale to an unlicensed person generates the transaction record and the acquisition and disposition entry held by the licensee, while two or more handgun dispositions to the same unlicensed person within the stated period generate an additional report under section 923(g)(3)(A). The second reporting duty concerns loss. Under section 923(g)(6), theft or loss of a firearm from the inventory or collection of a licensee must be reported within forty eight hours after discovery of the theft or loss. The forty eight hour period runs from discovery rather than from the unknown moment at which a firearm left the inventory, and the duty covers both theft and loss, so that a missing firearm discovered through inventory review falls within the same reporting obligation as a firearm taken in a burglary or robbery of the licensed premises.

The records held by licensees serve their most concentrated law enforcement function through tracing. A firearm trace begins with the serial number marked by the manufacturer and proceeds through the chain of licensee records to the first retail purchaser. The Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives conducts tracing through the National Tracing Center in Martinsburg, West Virginia. Working from the serial number, the tracing center contacts the manufacturer to identify the first licensee to which the firearm was shipped, then follows the acquisition and disposition records of successive licensees through distribution to the retail dealer, and then uses the transaction record held by that dealer to identify the first retail purchaser. Each step depends on a record created at the time of a licensed transfer and retained by the licensee who made that transfer. The system is sequential by design. No single licensee holds the complete chain, and the chain can be reconstructed only by moving from one licensee record to the next in the order in which the firearm passed through the licensed channel.

Section 923(g)(7), enacted in 1994, imposes the response duty that makes that sequential process work within a defined period. Under that provision, a licensee must respond to a request from the Attorney General, administered through the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives, for information contained in licensee records needed to determine the disposition of a firearm in the course of a bona fide criminal investigation. The response must be given immediately, and in no event later than twenty four hours after receipt of the request. The twenty four hour limit applies to the records request described in the provision, tied to a bona fide criminal investigation and to the disposition of a firearm, rather than to requests at large. A licensee that receives such a request therefore cannot treat the response as routine correspondence to be answered in the ordinary course of business over an open ended period. The duty is to search the records, locate the acquisition and disposition information and any corresponding transaction record, and transmit the information within the period the statute prescribes.

The tracing chain depends on records remaining available after a licensed business closes. Section 923(g)(4) addresses that contingency by requiring a licensee that goes out of business to deliver its records to the Attorney General. Those out of business records are held at the National Tracing Center for use in traces, so that the closing of a dealership, a distributorship, or another licensed operation does not break the chain for firearms that passed through that business before closure. Without that delivery requirement, the sequential structure of tracing would fail at every point at which a licensee in the middle of a chain had ceased operations and discarded or dispersed its bound book and transaction records. With the requirement in place, the records of a discontinued business remain searchable through the same tracing center that coordinates traces involving operating licensees, although the records themselves originated as licensee records created and held at licensed premises during the life of the business.

Read together, the inspection, reporting, and tracing provisions draw on a single body of material rather than on separate record systems maintained for separate purposes. The bound book entry created at acquisition, the disposition entry created at sale, and the transaction record completed at the retail counter supply the information examined during a compliance inspection, the information produced after a theft or loss is discovered in inventory, and the information transmitted in response to a trace request. The duties differ in trigger, timing, and recipient, but the underlying records remain the records that section 923 required the licensee to create and preserve in the first instance.

The same statute that created the record duties also fixed a boundary on the federal use of those records. Section 926(a) grants rulemaking authority for carrying out the provisions of chapter 44, subject to a proviso added in 1986. Under that proviso, no rule or regulation prescribed under the chapter may require that records kept by licensees be transferred to a facility owned, managed, or controlled by the federal government, and no rule or regulation may establish any system of registration of firearms, firearm owners, or firearm transactions. The language speaks directly to the two arrangements that the rest of section 923 might otherwise be mistaken to create. Licensee records remain licensee records, held at licensed premises and produced in response to inspection, reporting, and tracing duties stated in the statute. Firearms, firearm owners, and firearm transactions are not placed into a federal registration system by regulation under the chapter. The delivery of out of business records under section 923(g)(4) operates as a specific statutory provision for discontinued businesses and sits within that larger structure, rather than as a general centralization of the records of operating licensees.

The consequence stated in the statute is plain in its effect. There is no federal registry of firearms or firearm owners under the Gun Control Act of 1968. A person who acquires a firearm from a licensee appears in the records of that licensee, in the acquisition and disposition record and in the transaction record for that sale, and information from those records reaches the federal government through the defined channels of compliance inspection, a multiple handgun sale report, a theft or loss report, a trace request in a bona fide criminal investigation, or delivery of records after a business closes. Outside those channels, the statute does not assemble the contents of licensee bound books and transaction records into a single federal file of owners or transactions. That allocation, between records held by licensees on one side and a prohibition on a federal registration system and on federally required central transfer of licensee records on the other side, defines the records system that section 923 built and section 926(a) confined.

## Transfer Rules and the Space the Statute Leaves to Private Sales

The transfer provisions of the Gun Control Act of 1968 operate through a single organizing distinction. On one side of the line stands the licensee, a person holding a federal license to import, manufacture, or deal in firearms, whose transactions the statute surrounds with conditions on place, on the residence of the buyer, on age, and on compliance with state law. On the other side stands the nonlicensee, who holds no such license and engages in no business of dealing. Section 922 of title 18 of the U.S.C. addresses both figures, but not in the same way or to the same depth. Its most detailed commands run to the licensed channel, through which firearms move in interstate commerce in the ordinary course of trade. Its commands to nonlicensees are narrower, and they cluster at two points: movement across a state line, and transfer to a person whom the transferor knows or has reasonable cause to believe falls within a prohibited category. That space is not an exception carved out of a general private transfer rule, because no general private transfer rule exists in the statute. Congress built a licensing system for the business of dealing, attached conditions to that business and to interstate movement, and imposed one direct prohibition on transferors generally. The space left to private sales is the area that those choices, taken together, did not reach.

The channel rule appears in section 922(a)(1). That provision makes it unlawful for any person except a licensee to engage in the business of importing, manufacturing, or dealing in firearms, and for a person other than a licensee to ship, transport, or receive a firearm in interstate or foreign commerce in the course of such a business. The prohibition attaches to an activity, the business of dealing, rather than to ownership or to an isolated act of sale. A person who devotes time, attention, and labor to dealing in firearms as a regular course of trade or business, with the principal objective of livelihood and profit through repetitive purchase and resale, belongs inside the licensing system and must hold a license. A person who makes an occasional sale or purchase for the enhancement of a personal collection or for a hobby stands in a different position under the definition of engaged in the business, because the definition, in the form given to it by the revision of 1986, excludes that occasional activity. Once a seller crosses into the business of dealing without a license, the conduct violates the channel rule itself, apart from any condition a licensee would have had to observe in a comparable transaction. Once a seller remains outside that business, the channel rule does not convert the seller into a licensee or impose licensee duties in another form. The occasional private sale and the dealer's sale are not two grades of the same regulated act. They are different kinds of act in the eyes of the statute, and the statute treats them through different provisions.

Section 922(c) gives the channel rule its physical shape. A licensee may sell or deliver a firearm to a person who is not a licensee only in a transaction conducted in person at the licensee's premises. Buyer and licensee meet face to face, the transaction occurs at the place the license authorizes, and the licensee completes the records that section 923 requires before delivery. The provision contains no route by which a licensee may complete an ordinary retail sale to an unlicensed buyer at a distance and send the firearm directly to that buyer. Read together with the interstate provisions of section 922(a), section 922(c) ended the interstate mail order sale of firearms to individuals, a practice that had remained lawful under the Federal Firearms Act of 1938, the predecessor statute that the Gun Control Act of 1968 repealed and replaced in expanded form. Handgun mail order had been restricted earlier, through postal law and through the Omnibus Crime Control and Safe Streets Act of 1968, signed on June 19, 1968, whose firearms title first enacted chapter 44 of title 18 and barred interstate handgun trade to individuals four months before the October act. The October statute completed the arrangement for firearms generally. An individual who wanted a firearm from outside the home state could not order it by mail from a distant seller for direct delivery. The firearm had to move, if it moved lawfully at all, through licensees and through an in person transaction.

The in person requirement also explains the recordkeeping that surrounds a licensee sale. Section 923 obliges licensed importers, manufacturers, and dealers to maintain records of importation, production, shipment, receipt, sale, and other disposition, in the acquisition and disposition record kept in the trade as the bound book, and the Firearms Transaction Record, ATF Form 4473, is the form prescribed under that authority for an over the counter transfer by a licensee to an unlicensed person, on which the buyer certifies eligibility and identity and the licensee records identification. Those papers belong to the licensed channel by design, so that a record exists at the point of retail disposition. No comparable federal form or record attaches under this statute to a transfer between two nonlicensees, and Congress placed no documentation duty on a private transferor.

Section 922(a)(3) states the first of the interstate rules for nonlicensees, from the side of the person who acquires the firearm. A nonlicensee may not transport into the state of residence, or receive in that state, a firearm obtained outside that state. The provision closes the obvious evasion of the channel rule. Without it, a resident could travel to another state, buy a firearm in a private transaction there, and carry the firearm home, accomplishing in two steps what the licensed channel forbids in one. The exceptions are narrow and specific. A firearm acquired by bequest or intestate succession falls outside the prohibition, provided the acquisition is lawful in the state of residence. A firearm obtained from a licensee in another state in compliance with section 922(b)(3) also falls outside the prohibition, because that route is the licensed channel operating as the statute intends. Stated at the precision the text supports, interstate acquisition by a nonlicensee runs through a licensee. A private purchase in another state, followed by transport home, is not a lawful alternative route, and the statute leaves no general exception for a purchase the buyer regards as private, occasional, or small in scale. The scale of the acquisition does not alter the rule. The state line does.

Section 922(a)(5) states the matching rule from the side of the transferor. A nonlicensee may not transfer, sell, trade, give, transport, or otherwise dispose of a firearm to another nonlicensee who the transferor knows or has reasonable cause to believe does not reside in the state in which the transferor resides. The knowledge element is part of the offense as written. It is that knowledge or reasonable cause to believe, joined to the cross state character of the transfer, that brings a private transfer within a federal prohibition. The exceptions again are narrow and specific. A temporary loan or rental for lawful sporting purposes falls outside the prohibition, so a firearm may pass temporarily into the hands of a resident of another state for a lawful sporting use and return. A transfer by bequest or intestate succession also falls outside the prohibition, provided the disposition is lawful in the state in which the recipient resides. Outside those exceptions, the rule admits no distinction based on the relationship between the parties, the price paid or the absence of a price, or the number of firearms involved. A gift to a friend who lives across a state line is a transfer within the provision just as a sale is, because its verbs reach giving and transporting as well as selling and trading.

### Can a private individual sell a firearm to a resident of another state?

A nonlicensee may not transfer a firearm to a nonlicensee who resides in another state, except through the channels the statute preserves. Section 922(a)(5) allows a temporary loan or rental for lawful sporting purposes and a transfer by bequest or intestate succession where state law permits it.

The answer follows from the way sections 922(a)(3) and 922(a)(5) approach the same transaction from opposite directions. Section 922(a)(5) binds the seller or giver, who may not knowingly make the transfer to a resident of another state outside the two exceptions. Section 922(a)(3) binds the buyer or recipient, who may not bring the firearm into the home state or receive it there after obtaining it outside that state, outside the bequest channel and the licensee channel. A private interstate sale between two nonlicensees therefore fails at both ends. The lawful route, where one exists, runs through a licensee in the manner section 922(b)(3) prescribes, with the firearm passing from licensee to licensee where the statute requires that passage and reaching the buyer in an in person transaction in the buyer's home state in the case of a handgun. The temporary sporting loan stands apart because the statute treats a loan or rental for lawful sporting purposes as temporary and purpose bound possession, not as a change of ownership, and the bequest stands apart because succession at death is a transfer by operation of law rather than a market transaction between living parties. Neither exception supports a completed private sale, and neither can be stretched by labeling a sale as a loan or an anticipated bequest.

Section 922(b)(3) governs the dealer counterpart of the interstate problem, the sale by a licensee to a buyer who does not reside in the state where the licensee's premises are located. The general rule is a prohibition, and its exceptions define the whole lawful geography of interstate retail sales. Transfers to other licensees fall outside the prohibition, and licensed importers, manufacturers, and dealers transact with each other across state lines as a matter of course. Loans and rentals for lawful sporting purposes also fall outside the prohibition, as do transfers for the use of the United States, a state, or their departments, agencies, and political subdivisions. For a sale to an unlicensed buyer who lives in another state, the operative exception is the long gun exception. A licensee may sell or deliver a rifle or a shotgun to such a buyer in a face to face transaction, where the buyer meets the licensee in person, if the sale complies fully with the laws of both states, the state where the sale occurs and the state where the buyer resides. No corresponding exception exists for a firearm other than a rifle or a shotgun. A handgun therefore reaches an out of state buyer only through a licensee in the buyer's home state. The dealer in the first state may transfer the handgun to a dealer in the second state, and the second dealer then completes the retail sale in person at licensed premises in the buyer's own state. In its original form in the 1968 act, the long gun exception was limited to residents of states contiguous to the state of the licensee's premises. The revision of 1986 removed the contiguity condition, and the exception took the nationwide form described in this section from that change, the full account of which belongs to the later section of the article on the 1986 revision.

Two further conditions in section 922(b) qualify every licensee sale, including a sale under the long gun exception. Section 922(b)(1) sets age floors that a licensee may not sell below. A licensee may not sell or deliver a firearm other than a shotgun or a rifle, in practice a handgun, to a person under the age of twenty one years. A licensee may not sell or deliver a shotgun or a rifle to a person under the age of eighteen years, and the floor for ammunition for a shotgun or a rifle is likewise eighteen years. Section 922(b)(2) adds a state law condition of a different kind. A licensee may not sell or deliver a firearm in a sale where the transfer would violate the law of the state in which the transfer occurs. Federal permission in section 922(b) is therefore never, by itself, a complete statement that a given licensee sale is lawful. The sale must also survive the law of the state of the premises, and, in an out of state long gun sale, the law of the buyer's state as well. The design makes the licensee a point at which federal and state conditions meet, and places the practical burden of knowing two bodies of law, in an interstate sale, on the licensed seller rather than on the buyer. A private transferor carries no equivalent federal duty under section 922(b), because that provision by its terms governs licensees, though state law binds private parties directly under its own authority.

The consequence of this architecture, for transfers that cross no state line and involve no licensee, can be stated precisely. A transfer between two nonlicensees who reside in the same state carries no federal license requirement under this statute, because neither party engages in the business of dealing by reason of an occasional private transfer. It carries no federal record requirement, because the recordkeeping duties of section 923 run to licensees and to the transactions licensees conduct. It carries no federal check requirement under the 1968 act as enacted, because the act created no system for checking a buyer at all, and the check duty added in a later decade, described in the closing passage of this section, attaches by its terms to licensee transfers and to no others. The intrastate private transfer is therefore outside nearly the whole of the federal transfer apparatus of the statute. That statement requires one qualification, and the qualification is the one transfer rule in section 922 that reaches every transferor. Section 922(d) makes it unlawful for any person, licensee or nonlicensee alike, to sell or otherwise dispose of a firearm to a person who falls within one of the prohibited categories defined by the statute, where the transferor knows or has reasonable cause to believe that the recipient falls within such a category. The categories include a person convicted of a crime punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year, a fugitive from justice, and an unlawful user of a controlled substance, together with a person under indictment and the other categories set out in the article's treatment of section 922(g). A private seller who knows, or has reasonable cause to believe, that the buyer belongs to such a category commits a federal offense by completing the sale, even though the same sale to a buyer not known to be prohibited would attract no federal license, record, or check requirement at all. The distinction lies entirely in the transferor's knowledge or reasonable cause to believe about the recipient's status. Section 922(d) gives the private transferor no federal instrument for resolving doubt about that status, because the instruments of verification in the statute belong to the licensed channel. A private transferor who proceeds in the face of knowledge or reasonable cause to believe violates section 922(d). A private transferor who proceeds without such knowledge does not violate that provision, and the 1968 act imposes no further federal condition on the transaction.

Describing that result as the statute's deliberate structural choice means describing the choices that produced it. Congress legislated in 1968 against the background of a request for more. President Lyndon B. Johnson, on signing the June 1968 act and in messages to Congress during that summer, stated that the firearms provisions fell short of the owner licensing and registration measures that Johnson had requested, and Congress did not enact owner licensing or registration in October either. The enacted purpose clause in section 101 of the October act points in the same direction as the enacted structure. The clause declares that the purpose of the act is to support federal, state, and local law enforcement in the fight against crime and violence, and that the purpose expressly is not to place undue or unnecessary federal restrictions or burdens on law abiding citizens in acquiring, possessing, or using firearms for lawful purposes. A statute whose stated purpose disclaimed unnecessary burdens on acquisition, and whose Congress declined a documented request to license owners and register firearms, produced a transfer system addressed to commerce and to dealers. The private transfer space is the residue of those decisions, not a provision that any section announces. No section of chapter 44 says that private intrastate transfers are exempt, because no section purports to regulate them in the first place, apart from section 922(d). The absence is the boundary of the regulation. A reader who approaches the statute looking for a private sale provision will find instead provisions about licensees, about interstate movement, and about prohibited recipients, and the private intrastate sale sits where those provisions end. That is the organizing fact of the transfer system.

A later layer altered the conditions of licensee transfers without altering the boundary described in this section. [the Brady Act of 1993](/2021/04/15/brady-act-1993-guide/) added a background check duty to chapter 44, codified at section 922(t), under which a licensee, before transferring a firearm, must contact the national instant criminal background check system and receive a response. That system, known by the abbreviation NICS, has operated since November 30, 1998, and a fuller account of [how the NICS background check law works](/2021/06/15/nics-background-check-law/) belongs to the article devoted to that system. The duty in section 922(t) attaches to licensee transfers. It did not extend a federal check requirement to transfers between nonlicensees, and it therefore deepened the regulation of the licensed channel while leaving the private transfer space in the position the 1968 structure had given it. The period before the national system began operating was governed by interim provisions of the Brady Act that directed state and local officers to conduct background checks, and in Printz v. United States, decided in 1997, the Supreme Court invalidated that interim direction to state officers without disturbing the licensee framework of the Gun Control Act of 1968. The 1968 act built the channel and drew its boundary. The Brady layer added a check inside the channel. Neither enactment converted the private intrastate transfer between nonlicensees into a federally licensed, recorded, or checked transaction, and section 922(d) remained, across both layers, the single federal transfer prohibition addressed to every person who transfers a firearm, whatever the transferor's licensed status.

## The Nine Categories of Prohibited Persons

The central possession rule of the Gun Control Act of 1968 sits in section 922(g) of title 18. That subsection makes it unlawful for a person who falls within any of nine stated categories to ship or transport in interstate or foreign commerce, or possess in or affecting commerce, any firearm or ammunition, or to receive any firearm or ammunition that has been shipped or transported in interstate or foreign commerce. The phrasing matters because the prohibition attaches to the person rather than to a particular transaction. A single qualifying status brings the whole range of conduct within the subsection, covering movement, possession, and receipt alike, and it covers ammunition as well as firearms. The commerce language also matters, because it supplies the jurisdictional element that later decisions construed, and because it ties a status based rule to the federal power over commerce that structures the rest of chapter 44. The section applies in the disjunctive, so proof of one category is enough and proof of more than one is not required.

Section 922(d) supplies the companion rule on the other side of a transaction. That subsection makes it unlawful for any person to sell or otherwise dispose of any firearm or ammunition to a person in the same categories, knowing or having reasonable cause to believe that the recipient holds that status. Section 922(d) also reaches a person under indictment for a felony, a status that section 922(g) itself does not list. The two subsections therefore work as a pair. Section 922(g) governs what a person in a listed category may do, and section 922(d) governs what another person may knowingly transfer to such a person. Because section 922(d) applies to any person, and not only to a licensee, it is the transfer rule of the statute that reaches a private seller directly, even though most of the transfer machinery of chapter 44 runs through the licensed channel.

The nine categories did not arrive at one time, and their order in the statute preserves that history. The first seven categories formed the set enacted in 1968, carried forward from the June 1968 act and from the narrower bars of the Federal Firearms Act of 1938, which the 1968 act repealed and reenacted in expanded form. An eighth category followed in 1994 and a ninth in 1996, and both additions concerned domestic relations rather than the older grounds of conviction, flight, substance use, mental adjudication, alienage, military discharge, and renunciation. Reading the categories in groups, by the Congress that enacted them and by the kind of status each one describes, shows how the subsection grew from a set built largely around adjudicated or formally recorded conditions into one that also reaches a court order in force and a class of misdemeanor convictions defined by the relationship between the parties.

The first category covers a person who has been convicted in any court of a crime punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year. The test turns on the punishment authorized for the offense rather than on the sentence actually imposed, so the statutory line is drawn by the maximum term the law attached to the crime. The second category covers a fugitive from justice, a status that rests on flight rather than on a conviction. Together the first two categories carry forward the oldest federal approach, visible in the 1938 act, which barred interstate shipment of firearms to convicted felons and fugitives and receipt by them. In the 1968 act those grounds were restated inside a broader possession prohibition and joined by five further categories, each of which likewise depends on a status that can be identified from a record, a determination, or the person’s own formal act.

The third category covers a person who is an unlawful user of or addicted to any controlled substance. As enacted in 1968, the category named marijuana and specified classes of substances, described in the original text through depressant or stimulant substances and narcotic drugs. The Firearm Owners’ Protection Act of 1986 generalized that wording, replacing the specified classes with the phrase any controlled substance, understood by reference to the definition in the Controlled Substances Act. The change widened the verbal reach of the category without changing its basic structure, since the prohibition continued to turn on unlawful use of, or addiction to, a substance within the federal definition rather than on a conviction for a drug offense stated as such in section 922(g).

The fourth category covers a person who has been adjudicated as a mental defective or who has been committed to a mental institution. Both branches of the category rest on a formal determination by a competent authority rather than on a diagnosis standing alone. One branch concerns an adjudication, and the other concerns commitment, and the statute places them side by side as alternative routes into the same prohibition. The fifth category then turns from adjudication to immigration status, and it has two branches of its own. One branch covers a person who is illegally or unlawfully in the United States. The other covers a person who, except as provided in section 922(y)(2), has been admitted to the United States under a nonimmigrant visa. Section 922(y)(2) states exceptions to that second branch, and among the exceptions described for the nonimmigrant category is admission connected with a valid hunting license or permit, so the visa branch does not operate as an unqualified bar on every nonimmigrant admitted person in every circumstance.

The sixth category covers a person who has been discharged from the Armed Forces under dishonorable conditions, and the seventh covers a person who, having been a citizen of the United States, has renounced citizenship. Both categories rest on a completed formal act recorded by the government, a discharge of a stated character in one instance and a renunciation in the other, rather than on conduct that a court would have to reconstruct from contested facts in each prosecution. Those two grounds also illustrate the continuity between the October 1968 act and the legislation that preceded it in the same year, since the Title VII possession provisions of the Omnibus Crime Control and Safe Streets Act of 1968 had separately barred receipt, possession, or transportation in commerce by, among others, veterans discharged under dishonorable conditions, persons adjudicated mentally incompetent, aliens unlawfully in the country, and persons who had renounced citizenship. The Firearm Owners’ Protection Act of 1986 later repealed those appendix provisions and consolidated their substance into sections 922(g) and 924, leaving the categories to operate within chapter 44 itself.

### When did the domestic violence categories enter the statute?

The restraining order category entered in 1994 through the Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act, Public Law 103-322. The misdemeanor crime of domestic violence category entered on September 30, 1996, through the Lautenberg Amendment enacted within the Omnibus Consolidated Appropriations Act, 1997, Public Law 104-208.

Category (8), added by the Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994, Public Law 103-322, covers a person who is subject to a qualifying court order. The order must restrain the person from harassing, stalking, or threatening an intimate partner of the person or a child of an intimate partner. Unlike the first seven categories, which generally rest on a past event such as a conviction, a discharge, or a renunciation, category (8) rests on an order in force, and the statute therefore specifies both the procedure by which the order must have been issued and the content the order must contain before the prohibition attaches.

The procedural elements come first in the statutory definition. The order must have been issued after a hearing of which the person had actual notice and at which the person had an opportunity to participate. The substantive elements then concern what the hearing produced. The order must include a finding that the person represents a credible threat to the physical safety of the intimate partner or child, or the order must, by its terms, explicitly prohibit the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the partner or child that would reasonably be expected to cause bodily injury. Either substantive route suffices, and either route ties the prohibition to a judicial determination about threat or force rather than to the existence of a restraining order in the abstract. The protected persons are likewise defined by relationship, since the order must concern an intimate partner or the child of an intimate partner, and not a stranger or a member of the public at large.

Category (9), added two years later, covers a person who has been convicted in any court of a misdemeanor crime of domestic violence. The definition of that predicate offense appears in section 921(a)(33). Under that definition, the offense must be a misdemeanor under federal, state, tribal, or local law, and it must have, as an element, the use or attempted use of physical force, or the threatened use of a deadly weapon. The offense must also have been committed against a person standing in a stated relationship to the defendant, namely a current or former spouse, a person with whom the defendant shares a child in common, or a cohabitant. The relationship element and the force element operate together, so a misdemeanor without the force element falls outside the definition, and a force based misdemeanor against a person outside the stated relationships likewise falls outside it.

The 1996 amendment, commonly called the Lautenberg Amendment, was enacted on September 30, 1996, as part of the Omnibus Consolidated Appropriations Act, 1997, Public Law 104-208. The category as enacted stated no exception for a person whose duties involved carrying a firearm in an official capacity, a point that gave category (9) a reach different in practical effect from a prohibition addressed only to private possession, since a qualifying conviction barred possession in connection with employment that required a firearm as well as possession outside that employment. Category (9) also differed from category (1) in the grade of the predicate offense. Category (1) requires a crime punishable by more than one year of imprisonment, while category (9) reaches a misdemeanor, an offense by definition below that threshold, provided the relationship and force elements of section 921(a)(33) are present.

A related prohibition stands outside the nine categories altogether. Section 922(n) covers a person who is under indictment for a felony, and it makes it unlawful for such a person to ship or transport in interstate or foreign commerce any firearm or ammunition, or to receive any firearm or ammunition that has been shipped or transported in such commerce. Indictment is a charge rather than a conviction, and the subsection therefore operates during the interval between accusation and resolution, on a narrower set of acts than section 922(g) imposes after a qualifying conviction. Section 922(d), as noted in connection with the transfer rule, also bars a transfer to a person under indictment on the same knowledge standard that governs transfers to persons in the nine categories, so the indictment status has consequences on both the receipt side and the transfer side of a transaction.

A qualifying conviction does not always count. Under sections 921(a)(20) and 921(a)(33)(B), which supply the definitional rules for the felony category and the domestic violence misdemeanor category respectively, a conviction does not count if it has been expunged or set aside, or if the person has been pardoned or has had civil rights restored, unless the pardon, expungement, or restoration expressly provides that the person may not ship, transport, possess, or receive firearms. The exception for an express restriction matters because it makes the terms of the state action controlling. A restoration that restores civil rights while expressly withholding firearm rights leaves the federal prohibition in place, while a restoration without such an express provision removes the conviction from the federal definition.

Federal law also states an administrative route to relief in section 925(c), under which a person prohibited under section 922(g) may apply for relief from the federal disability, with the application made to the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives. Annual appropriations riders beginning with fiscal year 1993 barred the bureau from using appropriated funds to investigate or act upon applications filed by individual persons under that provision, so the federal route has been inoperative in practice from that period, while restorations granted through state processes of expungement, pardon, and civil rights restoration continued to operate under sections 921(a)(20) and 921(a)(33)(B). The result is an asymmetry written into the operation of the statute rather than into its substantive text. The categories themselves are federal, and the definitional exclusions for restored convictions are federal, but the functioning path to removing a conviction from those definitions has run through state action rather than through the federal application procedure that section 925(c) describes.

The Supreme Court construed the commerce element of the possession offenses in two early decisions. In United States v. Bass, decided in 1971, the Court read a commerce nexus into the receipt offense, requiring a connection between the receipt and interstate commerce rather than treating receipt alone as sufficient. In Scarborough v. United States, decided in 1977, the Court held that the required nexus is satisfied where the firearm had earlier traveled in interstate commerce, so the government did not have to show that the defendant’s own possession or receipt itself moved the firearm across a state line. Read together, the two decisions fixed the practical scope of the commerce element for the prohibitions that the 1986 act later consolidated into section 922(g), with Bass establishing that a nexus was required and Scarborough establishing how limited the proof of that nexus could be.

A later group of decisions construed the domestic violence misdemeanor category. In United States v. Hayes, decided in 2009, the Court held that the domestic relationship required by section 921(a)(33) need not be an element of the predicate misdemeanor offense, so a misdemeanor assault statute of general application could supply the predicate where the government proved the relationship as a fact. In United States v. Castleman, decided in 2014, the Court held that the force element encompasses the degree of force that supports a common law battery conviction, including offensive touching, rather than requiring violent force of a greater degree. In Voisine v. United States, decided in 2016, the Court held that a misdemeanor assault conviction based on reckless conduct can qualify as a predicate, since a reckless use of force falls within the definition as the Court construed it. Each decision resolved a question about how closely the predicate offense, as defined by the jurisdiction that enacted it, had to match the federal definition, and each answer broadened the set of state misdemeanors capable of supplying a category (9) predicate beyond the narrowest reading offered in the case.

A further decision in this group addressed knowledge. In Rehaif v. United States, decided in 2019, the Court held that in a prosecution under section 922(g), the government must prove that the defendant knew of the status that placed the defendant within the relevant category. The case arose under category (5), the immigration status category, and the holding was stated for the section 922(g) offenses generally. Challenges to category (9) on a different ground produced a consistent result at a lower level. Courts of appeals rejected arguments that applying the 1996 amendment to convictions entered before its enactment violated the constitutional prohibition on ex post facto laws, and that rejection is properly stated as a consensus of the courts of appeals rather than as a holding of the Supreme Court, which did not supply the governing decision on that question in the cases described in this section.

Taken together, the categories rest on three different kinds of legal fact. Some rest on a judgment entered by a court, namely a felony conviction under category (1), a domestic violence misdemeanor conviction under category (9), and the adjudication branch of category (4). Some rest on a condition or course of conduct described in the text, namely flight under category (2), unlawful use of or addiction to a controlled substance under category (3), and unlawful presence under the first branch of category (5). Others rest on an act or order of a government authority, namely commitment under the second branch of category (4), admission under a nonimmigrant visa under the second branch of category (5), discharge under dishonorable conditions under category (6), renunciation under category (7), and a qualifying restraining order under category (8). The division is not stated in the statute as a taxonomy, and a single prosecution does not require proof about any category other than the one charged. The division instead helps explain why the proof in a section 922(g) case varies so much from category to category, why Rehaif required proof that the defendant knew of the relevant status, and why sections 921(a)(20) and 921(a)(33)(B) speak in terms of convictions even though several categories involve no conviction at all. It also explains the place of section 922(n) beside the nine categories, since indictment is a procedural stage in a criminal case rather than a judgment, a condition, or a completed act of renunciation, discharge, commitment, or admission, and Congress placed it in a separate subsection with a separate, narrower prohibition. That structure also governs the transfer side, because section 922(d) repeats the same statuses for a seller who knows or has reasonable cause to believe the status exists.

The penalty provisions close the structure. Section 924(a)(2) provides the general penalty for a knowing violation of section 922(g) or of section 922(n), set at a fine and imprisonment for not more than ten years in the codified text described in this article. The same knowledge theme that Rehaif addressed for the elements of a section 922(g) offense therefore also appears in the penalty provision’s description of the offense as a knowing violation. Section 924(a)(3) separately covers false statements in licensee records, the subject that section 922(m) addresses in barring knowingly false statements in such records. The nine categories thus operate within a complete statutory sequence, in which section 921 supplies the definitions, section 922(g) and section 922(d) state the possession and transfer prohibitions, section 922(n) adds the indictment rule, sections 921(a)(20) and 921(a)(33)(B) state when a conviction does not count, section 925(c) states a relief procedure whose operation appropriations riders suspended in practice, and section 924 supplies the penalties.

## Imports and the Sporting Purposes Standard

The import provisions of the Gun Control Act of 1968 begin with a prohibition and then build a set of authorizations inside that prohibition. Section 922(l) provides that importation of a firearm into the United States is unlawful except as section 925(d) provides. The structure matters because the statute does not treat importation as an ordinary commercial activity that proceeds unless a specific restriction applies. The order runs in the opposite direction. Importation is barred as a general rule, and a lawful import depends on fitting within an authorization that section 925(d) creates. A firearm that falls outside those authorizations does not enter through a gap in the scheme. The bar in section 922(l) remains in place, and the absence of an authorization is itself the legal reason that importation may not proceed.

That structure places section 925(d) at the center of the import system. Section 925(d) directs the deciding official to authorize importation in the circumstances that its paragraphs describe, and the paragraphs operate as distinct channels rather than as a single general permission. Each channel addresses a defined category of importation and attaches conditions to that category, so the question for any proposed import is first a question of classification. The firearm, the purpose of the importation, and the circumstances stated in the relevant paragraph must align. An import that satisfies one paragraph does not need to satisfy another, and an import that satisfies none of them receives no authorization from the section.

Orientation to the four channels requires attention to their different functions even though the later administrative record concentrated overwhelmingly on one of them. The paragraphs of section 925(d) together provide the routes by which firearms may be authorized for entry notwithstanding the general bar, and each route is bounded by the conditions Congress wrote into it. Some channels address narrow and specific situations defined in the statute, while paragraph (3) addresses firearms in terms of a general standard tied to sporting purposes. Paragraph (3) therefore carried a different kind of weight from a channel written for a single transaction type or a single class of recipient. Its standard could be applied repeatedly, to new models and to new configurations, without any change to the statutory text, because the standard described a quality that a firearm either possessed or did not possess in the judgment of the administering official.

The identity of the deciding official changed over time, and the change forms part of the administrative history of the standard. During the import determinations of 1989 and of the period from 1997 to 1998, import authority rested with the Secretary of the Treasury, acting through the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco and Firearms. The codified text of the statute in its later form assigns the authorization decision to the Attorney General. That reassignment took place in 2003, when import authority passed from the Treasury Department to the Department of Justice. The transfer altered the department responsible for administering section 925(d), but the transfer did not rewrite the standard in paragraph (3).

### What makes a firearm importable under the statute?

Under section 925(d)(3), a firearm is importable when the Attorney General determines that the firearm is not subject to the National Firearms Act, is not otherwise barred from importation, and is generally recognized as particularly suitable for or readily adaptable to sporting purposes under the statutory standard as written.

The paragraph contains three elements, and each element performs separate work. The first element excludes firearms that are subject to the National Firearms Act, the registration and transfer tax regime codified at chapter 53 of title 26, United States Code. The second element requires that the firearm not be otherwise barred, which connects paragraph (3) to the rest of the statute and prevents the sporting purposes authorization from overriding an independent prohibition located elsewhere in the law. The third element contains the standard itself, and the standard is the element that required judgment. The exclusions could be answered by reference to defined categories and existing prohibitions. The sporting purposes element could be answered only by evaluating the firearm against a phrase that Congress left in general terms.

The statutory language of the standard repays close reading because every later determination rested on it. Section 925(d)(3) asks whether the firearm is "generally recognized as particularly suitable for or readily adaptable to sporting purposes." The phrase "generally recognized" locates the judgment outside the preferences of a single importer or a single purchaser. The question is not whether one person intends to use a firearm for a sporting purpose, and the question is not whether a firearm could conceivably be used in a sporting setting. The question is whether the firearm, as a type, holds a general recognition of the kind the statute describes. That framing directs attention to the characteristics of the firearm and to the place of firearms of that type in sporting practice, rather than to the stated intention behind a particular shipment.

The phrase "particularly suitable" sets the first branch of the standard, and the word "particularly" does work within it. Suitability in the abstract would describe almost any firearm that could be fired in a sporting context, because suitability without qualification asks only about capability. Particular suitability asks for a closer fit between the firearm and sporting purposes, a fit that distinguishes the firearm from firearms in general. The second branch, introduced by the word "or," asks whether the firearm is "readily adaptable" to sporting purposes. The disjunctive structure means that the two branches state alternative routes to the same conclusion. A firearm might meet the standard through particular suitability, or a firearm might meet the standard through ready adaptability, and failure on one branch does not by itself resolve the other.

Read as a whole, the standard asks a judgment about a type of firearm rather than a judgment about an individual transaction. The administering official evaluated the design, the configuration, and the characteristics of the firearm or of the class of firearms under review, and then measured those characteristics against the statutory phrase. That evaluation could proceed firearm by firearm, where a single model was presented for an import decision, and the evaluation could also proceed class by class, where a group of firearms sharing design characteristics received a common determination. The administrative history of the provision used both forms. Individual models were approved or barred in particular decisions, and groups of models derived from common designs were addressed together when the administering department concluded that the shared characteristics controlled the analysis.

A separate provision, section 925(e), restricts the importation of surplus military firearms, and the relationship between section 925(e) and section 925(d)(3) illustrates the care with which the import scheme sorts firearms into categories. Surplus military firearms are not governed solely by the sporting purposes analysis. Section 925(e) imposes its own restriction on that category and permits importation only within narrow channels, including importation as curios or relics for licensed collectors. The existence of a dedicated provision for surplus military firearms shows that Congress treated the military origin and surplus status of a firearm as a distinct legal fact with distinct consequences. A firearm in that category could not simply be routed through the general sporting purposes channel as though the separate restriction did not exist. The two provisions operated together, with section 925(e) marking out a category for narrower treatment and section 925(d)(3) supplying the general standard for firearms presented under that paragraph.

The first large application of the standard to a class of foreign semiautomatic rifles came in 1989, during the administration of President George H. W. Bush. In March 1989, that administration suspended the importation of a class of foreign semiautomatic rifles pending a review of the eligibility of those rifles under the sporting purposes standard. The suspension operated as an interim measure. Rifles within the class could not proceed on the basis of the approvals that had preceded the suspension while the administering officials examined whether the statutory standard was met. The review therefore reopened a question that earlier approvals appeared to have settled for the models involved, and the reopening itself demonstrated a feature of the standard that the statutory text makes possible. An authorization under paragraph (3) rests on an administrative judgment about a type of firearm, and a later administration of the same text could revisit that judgment through a new review without any amendment to the statute.

In July 1989, the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco and Firearms made the determination permanent for 43 models of semiautomatic rifles that the bureau had previously approved for importation. The number and the reference to prior approval both carry weight in the record. The determination did not address a set of firearms that had never been considered. The determination addressed models that had passed through the authorization process before, and the July decision reversed the outcome for those models on the basis of a renewed application of the same statutory phrase. After July 1989, the general bar in section 922(l) applied to those 43 models because section 925(d)(3) authorization was withheld, and the models could not be imported through the channel that earlier approvals had opened. The episode established, as a practical matter, that the sporting purposes standard could support class wide determinations covering dozens of named models in a single administrative action.

The second large application came during the administration of President William J. Clinton. In November 1997, that administration directed a further review of import eligibility and suspended outstanding import permits for modified versions of the barred designs. The object of the 1997 review differed in one respect from the object of the 1989 review. The 1989 determinations had addressed a class of foreign semiautomatic rifles in the configurations then presented. The 1997 review addressed modified versions of designs that had already been barred, which raised the question whether modification altered the analysis under the standard. A manufacturer or importer could change features of a rifle after a determination, and the modified rifle would then be presented as a firearm that met the standard even though the design from which the modified rifle derived had not met the standard. The suspension of outstanding permits while the review proceeded prevented the modified versions from entering under permits issued before the question was answered.

In April 1998, the Treasury Department announced the conclusion of that review. The department determined that modified semiautomatic rifles able to accept large capacity military magazines did not meet the sporting purposes standard. The determination covered more than fifty models derived from the AK, the FN-FAL, the HK, the SIG, and the Uzi designs, and the Treasury Department announcement placed the figure at approximately 59 models. The reasoning recorded in the determination tied the result to a specific capability, the ability to accept large capacity military magazines, in combination with the design lineage of the rifles under review. The modified rifles had been altered from the barred designs, and the department concluded that the alterations did not bring the rifles within the statutory description where that magazine capability remained. The April 1998 determination thus extended the logic of the earlier decisions to a new generation of configurations, again through administrative application of the phrase written in 1968 and again without new legislation on the standard itself.

Taken together, the determinations of 1989 and of the period from 1997 to 1998 show the range of actions that the standard supported. A suspension pending review allowed the administering department to pause importation while eligibility was examined. A determination after review allowed the department to bar a defined set of models on a permanent basis. A second review in a later administration allowed the department to examine modifications and to decide whether changed features changed the legal result. Each step drew its authority from section 925(d)(3), and each step operated on the same statutory words. The sequence also shows that the decisive facts, as the administering officials treated them, were facts about the firearms under review, including design origin, configuration, and magazine capability, rather than facts about individual importers or individual purchasers.

Officials administering the standard argued that the characteristics of the rifles under review were the characteristics of military designs rather than the characteristics of sporting arms. Officials presented that fit between design characteristics and design origin as the basis for withholding authorization under paragraph (3).

Importers and manufacturers organizations argued that the words "readily adaptable" in the standard were being read out of the provision by the administering officials. Those organizations presented the adaptability language as an independent route to authorization that the statutory "or" preserved.

The structural significance of the sporting purposes standard lies in the relationship between the brevity of the text and the scale of the decisions that rested on it. A single statutory phrase, written in 1968, supplied the entire legal basis for the import determinations of later decades. Congress did not enact a new import statute in 1989, and Congress did not enact a new import statute in 1997 or 1998. The Treasury Department in each period acted under the authority that the 1968 act had created, and the legitimacy of each determination depended on whether the determination could be sustained as an application of the words "generally recognized as particularly suitable for or readily adaptable to sporting purposes." That concentration of consequence followed from the placement of the phrase inside the import scheme. Because section 922(l) bars importation except as section 925(d) provides, the sporting purposes determination stood between a foreign manufacturer and the United States market as a threshold question. The phrase therefore functioned as both a permission and a boundary, opening the import channel for firearms that met its terms and leaving the general bar in place for firearms that did not, without requiring Congress to revisit the underlying enactment in each new dispute over a design.

A firearm manufactured abroad that received authorization could proceed through the licensed import channel and then through the ordinary distribution channels that the statute regulates. A firearm that was denied authorization could not proceed at all, regardless of demand for the firearm or of the willingness of a licensed importer to handle the transaction. Domestic manufacture presented different legal questions under different provisions, and the import standard did not govern firearms manufactured within the United States. The standard governed entry, and control over entry gave the phrase an effect that extended to every later transaction in an imported firearm, since a firearm that never entered could never reach the licensed dealers and purchasers who stood at the end of the domestic chain. Understanding the import provisions therefore requires holding two propositions together. The general rule of the statute is exclusion, stated in section 922(l) subject only to the authorizations that follow. The principal authorization for sporting firearms, in section 925(d)(3), is written as a general standard whose application depends on administrative judgment about types of firearms.

## The Firearm Owners' Protection Act Revision

The Firearm Owners' Protection Act, Public Law 99-308, 100 Stat. 449, was signed on May 19, 1986 by President Ronald Reagan. The bill was H.R. 4332 in the House. The Senate had passed its own version the previous year, and the House passed the amended bill by a vote of 292 to 130 on April 10, 1986. The act is also known by the names of its principal sponsors, Senator James McClure and Representative Harold Volkmer. The statute did not replace chapter 44 of title 18 with a new chapter. It revised the Gun Control Act of 1968 section by section, changing definitions, mental state requirements, inspection authority, transfer conditions, and possession rules while leaving the basic structure of the earlier act in place. A licensed channel for importers, manufacturers, and dealers remained. Prohibited person categories remained. Records that licensees were required to keep remained part of the system. The revision recalibrated the terms on which that structure applied, narrowed some applications, clarified others, and added one possession prohibition that had no counterpart in the 1968 act in the same form.

Section 1(b) of the act states congressional findings. The findings, as the text presents them, invoke the Second Amendment, the Fourth Amendment, the Fifth Amendment, the Ninth Amendment, and the Tenth Amendment. The text states that additional legislation was required to correct existing firearms statutes and enforcement policies. The findings reaffirmed the purpose clause of the 1968 act. That clause, in section 101 of the earlier statute, stated that the purpose was to support federal, state, and local law enforcement in the fight against crime and violence, and that the purpose was not to place undue or unnecessary federal restrictions or burdens on law-abiding citizens in acquiring, possessing, or using firearms for lawful purposes. The findings are the act's own stated basis, attributed to the text, and the function of the findings has to be kept distinct from the function of the operative amendments. The findings do not license a dealer, define an offense, or authorize an inspection. The findings frame the amendments that do those things, identify the constitutional provisions Congress named, and give the correction of existing statutes and enforcement policies as the reason Congress stated for additional legislation. The operative sections that follow carry the legal duties, and each amendment has to be read at the level of precision the text gives it.

The first recalibration concerned the definition that decides who must hold a dealer license. Section 921(a)(21) defines the phrase engaged in the business, and the 1986 formulation supplied the test for a dealer. A dealer is a person who devotes time, attention, and labor to dealing in firearms as a regular course of trade or business with the principal objective of livelihood and profit through the repetitive purchase and resale of firearms. Each element does distinct work. Devotion of time, attention, and labor points to activity rather than to a single transaction. A regular course of trade or business points to repetition and continuity. The principal objective of livelihood and profit identifies the purpose that separates dealing from other reasons for buying or selling a firearm. Repetitive purchase and resale describes the pattern of conduct through which that objective is pursued. The definition then states an exclusion. The phrase does not include a person who makes occasional sales, exchanges, or purchases of firearms for the enhancement of a personal collection or for a hobby, or a person who sells all or part of a personal collection of firearms. The exclusion is tied to occasional activity, to a personal collection, and to a hobby, and it is read against the affirmative test that precedes it. The consequence sits at the gateway to licensing. A person whose conduct meets the dealer definition falls under the requirement in section 923 that a person engaged in the business of dealing in firearms hold a federal license. A person whose conduct falls within the exclusion is outside that licensing trigger on that ground. The line matters beyond licensing alone, because other provisions of chapter 44 attach consequences to the distinction between a licensee and a person who is not a licensee, including rules for transfers, records, and interstate movement. By writing the principal objective test and the personal collection exclusion into the definition, the act moved the boundary question into statutory text.

The second recalibration concerned mental state. The act made willfully the operative standard for license revocation and for the principal offense provisions. Section 923(e) permits revocation of a license for a willful violation, and the principal offenses were conformed to the same standard. The mechanism is a change in the threshold the government has to meet. Under the standard the act adopted, willfulness is part of the case to be proved, whether the proceeding seeks the loss of a license or liability for an offense. The act did not remove the underlying duties. Licensees remained obliged to maintain the records section 923 requires, to complete the records that have to be complete before a firearm is sold or delivered, and to observe the transfer rules in section 922. The change addressed the culpability that has to accompany a breach of those duties before revocation or criminal liability follows under the provisions the amendment touched. That distinction, between the existence of a duty and the mental state required to punish a breach of the duty, is the precise effect of the willfulness standard, and the act applied the distinction across both the administrative and the criminal sides of the chapter.

The third recalibration concerned inspection of licensed premises. Section 923(g)(1)(B) permits officers to enter a licensee's premises during business hours, without reasonable cause or a warrant, to examine records and inventory for compliance. The act limited routine compliance inspections under that authority to not more than once during any 12-month period. The limit is a frequency limit, and its operation is mechanical. A routine compliance inspection conducted under the provision counts against the one inspection permitted in the period, and a second routine compliance inspection in the same period falls outside the authority the provision grants. The limit governs one defined channel of access. Other entries rest on a different basis. An entry supported by a warrant rests on the warrant. An entry made with consent rests on consent. An examination of records in connection with tracing a firearm, or an entry supported by reasonable cause tied to a criminal investigation, rests on that investigative basis rather than on routine compliance authority. For the licensee, the limit supplies a rule that can be applied by counting and by dates. For the inspecting officer, the limit requires the routine inspection power to be used within the frequency the text allows.

The fourth recalibration changed the geography of a dealer sale of a long gun to a buyer from another state. Section 922(b)(3) states the general rule that a licensee may not sell or deliver a firearm to a person who does not reside in the state where the licensee's premises are located, and then states exceptions. One exception covers a rifle or shotgun sold in a face to face transaction where the buyer meets the licensee in person and the sale complies fully with the laws of both the state of sale and the buyer's state of residence. As enacted in 1968, that long gun exception had been limited to residents of contiguous states. The act removed the contiguous state condition. After the amendment, the exception turned on the character of the transaction and on compliance with the law of the two states involved, rather than on whether the two states shared a border. A face to face sale of a rifle or shotgun to a resident of a noncontiguous state could qualify if the stated conditions were met. The amendment did not alter the separate treatment of handguns. Handguns move between states through the licensed channel, and an out of state buyer reaches a handgun through a licensee in the buyer's home state. The amendment also retained the state law compliance condition in section 922(b)(2), and the long gun exception adds compliance with the law of the buyer's state as well.

The fifth recalibration concerned ammunition. The 1968 act had placed licensing and recordkeeping requirements on the sale of ammunition. The 1986 act removed most of that licensing and recordkeeping for ammunition sales. The mechanism was subtraction. Rather than replacing one set of ammunition records with another, the act lifted the bulk of the federal requirements that had attached to ammunition as a licensed commodity, so that ammunition could move in interstate commerce to unlicensed buyers subject to the age floors the statute sets. The removal did not erase every ammunition rule. Restrictions on armor piercing ammunition, added during the 1980s, were retained and restated. Describing the amendment as a removal of most licensing and recordkeeping captures that proportion, and it avoids treating the act as if ammunition had been left wholly unaddressed by the chapter.

The sixth recalibration added a provision for interstate transport by a person traveling between places where possession is lawful. Section 926A provides that, notwithstanding any state or local law, a person not otherwise prohibited may transport a firearm for any lawful purpose from a place where the person may lawfully possess and carry the firearm to any other place where the person may lawfully possess and carry it, if the stated conditions are met. Lawful possession at the place of origin and lawful possession at the destination are both required, so the provision addresses passage between two places of lawful possession rather than possession at a place where possession is unlawful. During transportation, the firearm has to be unloaded. Neither the firearm nor any ammunition being transported with it may be readily accessible or directly accessible from the passenger compartment of the vehicle. In a vehicle without a separate compartment, the firearm or ammunition has to be contained in a locked container other than the glove compartment or console. Each condition narrows the circumstances in which the provision applies. The unloaded condition addresses the state of the firearm during movement. The accessibility condition addresses placement inside the vehicle. The locked container rule supplies the method for a vehicle that lacks a separate compartment, and the text expressly excludes the glove compartment and the console as qualifying containers. A transport that fails a stated condition falls outside the terms the provision sets. A transport that meets the conditions falls within the provision as the text defines it. The provision operates as a rule for movement across jurisdictions whose laws might differ along the route, and it does so by specifying the ends of the journey and the manner of carriage rather than by displacing state law for possession at either end.

The seventh recalibration placed a limit in section 926(a) on rulemaking authority under the chapter. The act added a proviso stating that no rule or regulation may require records of licensees to be transferred to a federal facility, and that no rule or regulation may establish any system of registration of firearms, firearms owners, or firearms transactions. The proviso names three objects of a registration system, firearms, firearm owners, and firearms transactions, and it covers each of them. The bar operates on the content of a regulation. A rule that required central transfer of licensee records, or a rule that established a registration system of the kind the proviso names, would exceed the authority section 926 grants. The limit fits the structure of the record system elsewhere in the chapter. Licensee records are created and held by the licensee under section 923, produced for inspection and for tracing under the conditions the chapter states, and delivered to the Attorney General when a licensee goes out of business under section 923(g)(4). That out of business delivery is a requirement in the chapter itself, distinct from a regulation compelling transfer to a federal facility. The proviso addressed the regulatory route to centralization or registration, barring creation by rule of a system that the statute does not itself create.

The eighth recalibration repealed provisions that had stood outside chapter 44 and consolidated their substance inside the chapter. The Omnibus Crime Control and Safe Streets Act of 1968 had enacted, in Title VII, possession and receipt provisions codified at sections 1201 to 1203 of the appendix to title 18. Those appendix provisions barred receipt, possession, or transportation in commerce by defined categories of persons, including convicted felons, veterans discharged under dishonorable conditions, persons adjudicated mentally incompetent, aliens unlawfully in the country, and persons who had renounced citizenship. Section 104(b) of the 1986 act repealed the appendix provisions, and the substance of the prohibitions was consolidated into section 922(g), which states the prohibited categories, and into section 924, which states penalties. Consolidation changed location and organization without treating repeal as abandonment of the prohibitions. After the amendment, the federal bar on possession by a person in a listed category is found in section 922(g), and the penalty is found in section 924. The same revision generalized one category in the consolidated list. The category concerning unlawful use of or addiction to a controlled substance had been framed in 1968 in terms of marijuana and depressant or stimulant substances and narcotic drugs. The act generalized that category to any controlled substance as defined in the Controlled Substances Act.

### What did the Hughes Amendment do?

The Hughes Amendment added section 922(o) to title 18. It made transfer or possession of a machine gun unlawful except for transfers to or by government and for machine guns lawfully possessed before May 19, 1986, ending new manufacture for private transfer after that date.

Section 922(o) was added by the Hughes Amendment, House Amendment 777 to H.R. 4332, offered by Representative William J. Hughes of New Jersey. The provision states that it is unlawful to transfer or possess a machine gun except for two classes of transfer and possession. The first exception covers a transfer to or by, or possession by or under the authority of, the United States, a state, or a department, agency, or political subdivision of the United States or of a state. The second exception covers a machine gun lawfully possessed before May 19, 1986. The structure is a general prohibition with exceptions defined by the identity of the government transferee or possessor and by the date of lawful possession. No exception in the text creates a route by which a newly manufactured machine gun could pass to a private possessor. The consequence follows from the interaction of the prohibition, the exceptions, and the date. A machine gun manufactured after May 19, 1986 cannot be lawfully possessed by a private person, because manufacture after that date cannot produce a machine gun that was lawfully possessed before that date, and a private transfer does not fall within the government exception. New manufacture for the private market therefore ended on that date as a matter of the provision's operation. The transferable stock for private possession is the fixed set of machine guns registered under the National Firearms Act and lawfully possessed before the cutoff. A fixed set can be transferred under the rules that govern those transfers, and individual items can leave circulation, but the set cannot be enlarged by new manufacture for private transfer. That closed character distinguishes section 922(o) from provisions elsewhere in the chapter that regulate a continuing flow of manufacture and sale through licensees. For ordinary firearms, manufacture and dealing continue under license. For machine guns destined for private possession, the provision drew the line at the stock that satisfied the date condition.

The adoption of the Hughes Amendment generated a dispute that has to be reported as a dispute, with each position identified and neither position adopted as the account given here. The amendment was adopted in the House on April 10, 1986 by voice vote, with Representative Charles Rangel presiding. The official record records adoption by voice vote. Members who opposed the amendment contended at the time and afterward that the voice vote did not carry the amendment or that a recorded vote was wrongly refused. Those contentions are the position of the members who made them, stated in opposition to the amendment and to the manner of adoption. The record of adoption is a separate statement about what the House proceedings show. The provision stood in the bill the House passed by 292 to 130 on April 10, 1986, and the provision stood in the act as signed on May 19, 1986. A description that adopted the vocabulary of the opponents for the proceeding would treat a contested contention as an established description of the vote. A description that dismissed the contention would treat disagreement among members who participated in the proceeding as if the contention had not been made. The statement that holds the elements apart is the accurate one. A voice vote was the method recorded. A presiding officer was identified. Opposing members made specific contentions about the outcome of that vote and about the refusal of a recorded vote. The provision was included in the measure that passed and in the law that was signed, and section 922(o) then operated as enacted law, with the prohibition, the government exception, and the date based exception stated above.

Taken together, the amendments are visible as distinct operations only when each amendment is kept at its own level. The definition of engaged in the business determines who enters the licensing system as a dealer. The willfulness standard determines the culpability required for revocation and for the principal offenses. The inspection limit determines how often the routine compliance power may be used in a 12-month period. The removal of the contiguous state condition determines the reach of the face to face long gun exception, subject to compliance with the law of both states. The ammunition amendment removes most licensing and recordkeeping for ammunition while retaining the armor piercing restrictions and the age floors. Section 926A determines the conditions for transport between two places of lawful possession notwithstanding state or local law along the route. The section 926(a) proviso determines that rulemaking may not require central transfer of licensee records or establish a system of registration. The repeal of the Title VII appendix provisions and the consolidation in sections 922(g) and 924 determine where the prohibited person possession rules and the penalties are found. Section 922(o) determines that transfer and possession of a machine gun are unlawful apart from the government exception and the exception for a machine gun lawfully possessed before May 19, 1986. The Firearm Owners' Protection Act revised the Gun Control Act of 1968 by acting on each of those levers in a single statute, and the revision is understood by following the text from the stated findings in section 1(b) through each operative change to the legal consequence that change produces.

## The Arguments Over the Architecture

The statute enacted in October 1968 placed its principal federal requirements on a licensed channel of commerce. Licensed importers, manufacturers, and dealers operate under federal licensing, record, and transfer rules, while a transfer between two nonlicensees who reside in the same state carries no federal license, record, or check requirement, apart from the bar on a transfer to a person known to fall within a prohibited category. That division between the licensed channel and the private transfer space forms the architecture argued over since 1968.

State law is a separate layer, and [federal and state firearms law compared](/2021/08/01/federal-vs-state-firearms-law/) treats what the states add. The federal argument concerns the reach of the federal design: whether federal requirements should extend further into transfers conducted outside the licensed channel, and whether the channel Congress constructed in 1968 marks the proper limit of federal regulation. Those questions received different answers in the debates of 1968, in the revision enacted in 1986, and in later congressional proposals.

This section states the two principal positions as their holders state them. The first seeks broader federal requirements and treats the licensed channel as an incomplete means of applying the statute's prohibited person bars. The second defends the licensed channel design and treats the boundary around transfers between nonlicensees as a deliberate part of the statute. Every substantive claim in each presentation is attributed to a named holder, a statutory text, or a dated legislative proposal. The article takes neither position.

### The case for broader federal requirements, as its holders state it

President Lyndon B. Johnson stated the opening form of this position during the enactments of 1968. On signing the Omnibus Crime Control and Safe Streets Act in June of that year, and in messages to Congress during that summer, Johnson stated that the firearms provisions of the June act fell short of the measures he had requested. The measures he identified were licensing of firearm owners and registration of firearms. Johnson presented owner licensing and registration as requirements that the June enactment lacked and that federal law needed. Congress did not enact owner licensing or registration in the Gun Control Act of 1968 in October, and Johnson's statements therefore record a stated position that the enacted framework stopped short of the system its principal executive proponent had sought. In the presentation made by Johnson, the shortfall was not a disagreement over whether prohibited persons should be barred from acquiring firearms. The June and October enactments both contained such bars. The disagreement, as Johnson framed it, concerned the federal machinery available before a transfer occurred. Owner licensing, in his stated proposal, would have placed a federal qualification step in the path of acquisition itself, and registration would have attached a federal record to the firearm and its owner.

The June 1968 act supplied a congressional statement that holders of broader federal requirements have placed beside Johnson's position. In the findings written into the June act, Congress stated that existing federal controls did not adequately enable the states to control firearms traffic within their borders. That finding, as stated in the act, treated federal law as a support structure for state regulation and found the structure in place before June 1968 insufficient for that purpose. Holders of this position read the finding as an acknowledgment by Congress itself that a federal framework addressed to interstate commerce could leave states without adequate federal assistance where firearms moved across state lines and then circulated inside a state. The argument attributed to these holders proceeds from the finding's own terms: if the stated federal purpose included enabling state control, and Congress found the earlier controls inadequate to that task, then the adequacy of federal assistance remained an open measure against which the October statute could be judged. They present it as the June act's own stated diagnosis, paired with Johnson's stated conclusion that the remedy enacted in 1968 remained narrower than the remedy he had requested.

Handgun Control, Inc., and the Brady Campaign to Prevent Gun Violence, the successor organization to Handgun Control, Inc., made the private transfer space the center of this position in the form in which those organizations stated it. Their argument began with the structure of the check and record system. Under the statute, records, the transaction form, and the background check duty added through the national instant criminal background check system attach to transfers by licensees. A transfer between two nonlicensees residing in the same state passes outside that system, provided that the transferor does not know or have reasonable cause to believe that the recipient falls within a prohibited category. Handgun Control, Inc. and the Brady Campaign argued that this structure leaves the prohibited person bars without a checkpoint at the point of a private sale. In their presentation, a bar stated in section 922(d) and section 922(g) depends for its practical application on a moment at which a buyer's status is examined, a record is made, and a transfer can be stopped before completion. The licensee channel supplies that moment, in their account, while a private sale supplies no equivalent federal moment. The organizations described the resulting space with the word "loophole," a term that belongs to their attributed characterization and not to the statute's description of its own structure. Their argument treated the private transfer space not as a separate subject from the prohibited person provisions, but as a route by which a person within a prohibited category could seek a firearm without passing through the checkpoint that a licensee transfer provides. On that reasoning, the organizations presented extension of a check and record requirement to private sales as completion of the framework that the barred categories presuppose.

A related argument made by holders of this position concerned firearm tracing. Tracing under the statute runs through the records kept by licensees, from the manufacturer's serial number through the chain of licensee records to the first retail purchaser. Holders of this position argued that a trace ends at that first retail sale because the federal record chain ends there, and that a later transfer between nonlicensees adds no federal record through which an investigator can follow the firearm to a subsequent holder. In their presentation, the limit matters because tracing serves an investigative function: a firearm recovered in connection with a criminal investigation is traced so that its path into the hands of the person who possessed it at recovery can be reconstructed. Where a private transfer intervened after the first retail sale, holders argued, the federal records identify the retail purchaser but do not identify the person who acquired the firearm in the private transfer. They presented that limit as a further consequence of confining federal records to the licensed channel. The argument did not claim that tracing fails in every case or that licensee records lack investigative value. It claimed, in the form stated by its holders, that the record system reaches the boundary of the licensed channel and stops there, and that investigations requiring the path beyond the first retail sale must depend on sources outside the federal record chain created by the statute.

Senators Joseph Manchin and Patrick Toomey carried a form of this position into a dated legislative proposal in 2013. Their proposal would have extended background checks to commercial private sales, placing a check requirement on transactions conducted outside a licensee's ordinary retail transfer while leaving the basic licensed channel in place. Manchin and Toomey presented the proposal as an application of the check system to a defined class of sales that the existing federal requirement did not reach. The proposal was considered in 2013 and was not enacted. In the argument of its sponsors, the proposal answered the private sale argument made by Handgun Control, Inc. and the Brady Campaign in narrower form than Johnson's 1968 request: it did not propose federal licensing of firearm owners, and it did not propose federal registration of firearms or owners. It proposed a checkpoint at the point of a commercial private sale, so that a transfer within the described class would include an examination of the buyer's status comparable in function to the examination conducted for a licensee transfer. Stated on its own terms, the proposal treated the absence of a check in a commercial private sale as the point to be addressed, and it treated the background check system built for licensee transfers as the mechanism to be extended to that point.

### The case for the licensed channel design, as its holders state it

The text of the 1968 act stated the opening form of this position in its purpose clause. In section 101, Congress stated that the purpose of the act was to support federal, state, and local law enforcement in the fight against crime and violence. In the same clause, Congress stated that it was not the purpose of the act to place undue or unnecessary federal restrictions or burdens on law-abiding citizens in acquiring, possessing, or using firearms for lawful purposes. Holders of the licensed channel position present the two statements as a single stated balance rather than as separate commitments. Support for law enforcement, in their reading of the clause, operates through the regulated channel that the statute constructs: licensing of persons engaged in the business of importing, manufacturing, or dealing in firearms, records kept by those licensees, and rules governing transfers by and through licensees. The second statement, in their presentation, fixes the boundary of that construction by identifying the burden placed on citizens acquiring firearms for lawful purposes as a subject that the statute itself disclaims as an undue purpose. A statute that regulates the commercial channel through which firearms enter and move in commerce applies federal requirements at the point where the federal system can administer them through licensees, and the purpose clause supplies the stated reason for declining to convert every acquisition by a citizen into a federal transaction requiring federal permission or a federal record.

Congress restated the terms of that balance in the findings written into the Firearm Owners' Protection Act of 1986. In section 1(b) of the 1986 act, Congress invoked the Second, Fourth, Fifth, Ninth, and Tenth Amendments and stated findings concerning the rights protected by those amendments and the enforcement of the firearms laws. The 1986 findings reaffirmed the purpose clause of the 1968 act, and holders of this position present the reaffirmation as a deliberate return to the stated terms of 1968 after eighteen years of administration under the earlier statute. The Second Amendment language speaks, in their presentation, to the individual acquisition and possession that the purpose clause described in terms of lawful purposes. The Fourth and Fifth Amendment language speaks to the administration of the licensing system, including inspection of licensee premises and the penalties and mental state required for enforcement. The Ninth and Tenth Amendment language speaks to powers and rights retained outside federal enumeration and to the place of the states in the federal structure. Holders present the 1986 findings as the act's own statement that corrections to enforcement policy were required in order to bring administration of chapter 44 back within the balance that the 1968 purpose clause had announced.

The sponsors of the 1986 act, Senator James McClure and Representative Harold Volkmer, and the National Rifle Association in the debate over that act, stated the practical form of this argument. In their stated position, the 1968 framework as administered had burdened lawful commerce and travelers beyond the balance announced in the purpose clause. Their argument addressed the administration of licensing, the definition of engaging in the business, the inspection of licensees, the mental state required for offenses and revocation, the movement of long guns between states, the passage of travelers through jurisdictions with differing state and local rules, and the recordkeeping attached to ammunition. McClure and Volkmer presented the recalibrations of 1986 as restorations of the statute's stated balance rather than as departures from the 1968 design. The definition of engaged in the business, with its principal objective of livelihood and profit test and its exclusion of occasional sales for the enhancement of a personal collection or for a hobby, drew the licensing duty around commercial dealing as they described it. The limit of one routine compliance inspection in a twelve month period, the willfulness standard, the removal of the contiguous state condition for face to face rifle and shotgun sales lawful under the laws of both states, the safe passage provision of section 926A for transportation between places where possession was lawful, and the proviso in section 926(a) against a federal registration system each formed part of the package that the sponsors and the National Rifle Association described as a correction.

Holders of this position also make a federalism argument concerning transfers between residents of the same state. In that argument, a transfer between two nonlicensees who live in the same state presents a subject that belongs to the regulatory province of the state rather than to the federal channel built around interstate commerce and licensed dealers. The states have in fact adopted differing answers: several states have enacted their own requirements for private transfers, and other states have not enacted such requirements. A state that judges a check or record requirement for private transfers to be appropriate for transfers inside its borders can enact one under its own law, addressed to its own residents and enforceable by its own officers. A state that judges the federal licensed channel and the bar on transfers to known prohibited persons to be sufficient can leave private transfers to those rules. In the holders' argument, uniformity imposed by Congress on intrastate transfers between residents would displace those differing state judgments with a single federal rule on a subject that the states can reach directly, and the Tenth Amendment finding written into the 1986 act supplies the constitutional register in which they place that objection. It locates the boundary, as its holders state it, at the point where a transfer leaves interstate commerce and the licensed dealers through whom the federal system acts.

A further argument made by holders of this position concerns the character of the private transfer space itself. In their presentation, that space is not an oversight that Congress failed to notice in drafting the 1968 act. It is the boundary that Congress drew when it placed licensing, record, and transfer requirements on transfers by and through licensees, while imposing on nonlicensees a defined set of duties that included the bar on transferring a firearm to a person known to fall within a prohibited category. Owner licensing and registration were before Congress in 1968 because Johnson had requested them, and Congress declined to enact either requirement. The scope of the licensing definition and the position of private transfers were before Congress again in 1986, when the act revised the definition of engaged in the business, addressed occasional sales and personal collections, and left transfers between nonlicensees residing in the same state outside the federal license, record, and check requirements. In the holders' argument, a boundary considered in 1968, described in the statute's structure, and reconsidered in 1986 without being moved carries a different legislative meaning from a gap that entered the statute unnoticed. They present proposals to extend federal checks into that space as proposals to move a boundary that two enactments left where it stood.

## What the Statute Leaves Undone and Where the Cluster Goes Next

A statute is defined as much by the subjects its text does not reach as by the duties its text imposes, and the Gun Control Act of 1968 rewards that form of reading because its omissions were considered at enactment rather than overlooked. The statute does not license firearm owners. The license in section 923 attaches to a person engaged in the business of importing, manufacturing, or dealing in firearms, and to the narrower collecting activity covered by the curios and relics license. A person who acquires and possesses a firearm for personal use holds no federal license under this chapter by reason of that possession and files no federal application to become an owner. President Lyndon B. Johnson stated in 1968 that owner licensing was among the measures he had requested and that Congress had not enacted, and the October text confirms the statement structurally, because every licensing provision in the chapter is addressed to commerce. The distance between a license to deal and a license to own is the distance between the statute Congress enacted and one of the statutes proposed to Congress in the same year.

The statute also does not register firearms or firearm owners. No provision requires an owner to record a firearm with a federal office, and no provision assembles a federal register from the records that licensees keep. Licensee records remain records of the licensee, held at licensed premises, produced for compliance inspection, transmitted in defined reports, and delivered to the Attorney General when a licensee goes out of business. Section 926(a), in the proviso added in 1986, bars any rule under the chapter from requiring licensee records to be transferred to a federal facility or from establishing any system of registration of firearms, firearm owners, or firearm transactions. Licensee records therefore support tracing from a manufacturer's serial number through successive licensees to the first retail purchaser, and the chain ends there because the record chain is a licensee record chain.

Transfers between nonlicensees supply the third omission. The statute imposes no federal license requirement, no federal record requirement, and no federal check requirement on a transfer between two nonlicensees who reside in the same state, beyond the section 922(d) bar on a transfer to a person known to fall within a prohibited category. State law may impose requirements of its own on such a transfer, and several states have done so, but those requirements arise under state authority rather than under chapter 44. The omission is therefore federal and specific, and the shortness of the answer a private seller receives under federal law is itself information about the design Congress chose. The statute likewise sets no federal storage standard. No provision of chapter 44 prescribes how a firearm must be stored in a home or in any other private setting. Safe passage under section 926A governs a firearm in the course of transport between two places where the traveler may lawfully possess and carry it, requiring the firearm to be unloaded and inaccessible from the passenger compartment or held in a locked container where the vehicle has no separate compartment. That transport rule illustrates the boundary rather than crossing it, because Congress legislated on the firearm in movement through jurisdictions while stating no general federal rule for storage at rest.

One further boundary places firearms outside a different federal regime altogether, and the mechanism is a definition rather than a firearms provision. Under [the Consumer Product Safety Act of 1972](/2018/10/01/consumer-product-safety-act-1972/), the definition of a consumer product excludes any article that, if sold by the manufacturer, would be subject to the excise tax imposed by 26 U.S.C. section 4181, the firearms manufacturers excise tax, so firearms sit outside that commission's product jurisdiction. The exclusion operates without naming firearms as such: Congress defined the reach of a product safety statute through a tax reference, and a question about federal product safety oversight of firearms therefore receives its answer from a consumer product statute and a section of the Internal Revenue Code, rather than from chapter 44, which contains no product safety standard of its own.

Taken as a whole, the statute is intensive at its center and limited at its edges by the same decisions. The licensed channel carries licensing, records, transaction forms, reports, inspections, tracing responses, age floors, state law conditions, and, after the Brady Act layer, a background check before transfer. The prohibited person provisions reach possession itself and bind any transferor who knows the recipient's status. The import provisions control entry into the country under a general standard administered firearm type by firearm type. Outside those structures stand owner licensing, registration, federal requirements on intrastate transfers between nonlicensees beyond the section 922(d) bar, and a federal storage standard, each absent from the text and each traceable to the enactment record of 1968 or to the revision of 1986, which reconsidered the structure and left those boundaries in place.

The Brady Act profile takes up the statute that added the background check duty, the interim arrangement that preceded the national system, and the constitutional decision that struck the direction to state and local officers while leaving the licensee framework intact. The provisions treatment of the background check system examines the operation of section 922(t) and the national instant criminal background check system in detail. The federal and state comparison sets chapter 44 beside the state requirements governing the subjects the federal statute leaves to state law. The litigation history of the Second Amendment follows the constitutional cases framing the possession questions the prohibited categories raise. The myths examination tests recurring claims about this statute, including the claims that it created a federal registry, licensed owners, or reached every private sale, against the text set out in this guide. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the Gun Control Act of 1968 create?

The act created the modern form of chapter 44 of title 18, the federal framework governing commerce in firearms through licensed importers, manufacturers, and dealers. It required a federal license for engaging in the business of importing, manufacturing, or dealing in firearms, required licensees to keep acquisition and disposition records and to complete a transaction record for retail sales, set conditions on licensee transfers involving the buyer's age, residence, and the law of the state of transfer, stated categories of persons barred from possessing or receiving firearms, barred importation except under statutory authorization, and superseded the firearms title enacted four months earlier while repealing and reenacting much of the Federal Firearms Act of 1938.

### Q: Which president signed the Gun Control Act?

President Lyndon B. Johnson signed the Gun Control Act of 1968 on October 22, 1968, and the act became Public Law 90-618. Johnson had also signed the Omnibus Crime Control and Safe Streets Act of 1968 on June 19 of that year, the statute whose firearms title first enacted chapter 44 of title 18 before the October act rewrote it. On signing the June act, and in messages to Congress during that summer, Johnson stated that its firearms provisions fell short of the owner licensing and registration measures he had requested. Congress did not enact those measures in October either, and Johnson signed the October act in the form Congress passed it.

### Q: Why does the Gun Control Act regulate dealers and not private sales?

The structure follows the choices Congress wrote into the statute. The act placed its licensing, record, and transfer duties on persons engaged in the business of importing, manufacturing, or dealing in firearms, and on the interstate movement of firearms, rather than on ownership or on every transfer. Its stated purpose in section 101 was to support law enforcement in the fight against crime and violence while expressly not placing undue or unnecessary federal restrictions or burdens on law abiding citizens acquiring, possessing, or using firearms for lawful purposes. Congress also declined the owner licensing and registration measures President Johnson stated he had requested. A transfer between nonlicensees in the same state therefore carries no federal license, record, or check requirement under this statute, although section 922(d) bars a transfer to a known prohibited person.

### Q: Who is a prohibited person under the Gun Control Act?

A prohibited person is a person within any of the nine categories in section 922(g), which bars such a person from possessing, shipping, transporting, or receiving a firearm or ammunition in or affecting commerce. The categories cover a person convicted of a crime punishable by more than one year of imprisonment, a fugitive from justice, an unlawful user of or a person addicted to a controlled substance, a person adjudicated as a mental defective or committed to a mental institution, a person unlawfully in the United States or admitted under a nonimmigrant visa subject to stated exceptions, a person discharged from the Armed Forces under dishonorable conditions, a person who renounced United States citizenship, a person subject to a qualifying restraining order, and a person convicted of a misdemeanor crime of domestic violence.

### Q: What did the 1986 amendments change in the Gun Control Act?

The Firearm Owners' Protection Act of 1986, Public Law 99-308, revised chapter 44 without replacing it. The act defined engaged in the business through a principal objective of livelihood and profit test and excluded occasional sales for a personal collection or a hobby, made willfulness the operative mental state for license revocation and the principal offenses, limited routine compliance inspections to once in any twelve month period, removed the contiguous state condition from the out of state long gun exception, removed most licensing and recordkeeping for ammunition sales, added the safe passage provision in section 926A, barred regulations requiring central transfer of licensee records or establishing a registration system, consolidated the former appendix possession provisions into sections 922(g) and 924, and added section 922(o) on machine guns.

### Q: What is the sporting purposes import test in the Gun Control Act?

The sporting purposes test is the standard in section 925(d)(3) for authorizing the importation of a firearm. Section 922(l) bars importation except as section 925(d) provides, and paragraph (3) directs authorization where the firearm is not subject to the National Firearms Act, is not otherwise barred, and is generally recognized as particularly suitable for or readily adaptable to sporting purposes. The standard judges the firearm as a type, by its design and characteristics, rather than by the intention of an individual importer or buyer. Administering officials applied the standard in class determinations in 1989, barring 43 models of foreign semiautomatic rifles, and in the review concluded in April 1998, which covered more than fifty models of modified semiautomatic rifles.

### Q: Did assassinations lead to the Gun Control Act?

The dated record shows a sequence rather than a single event. President John F. Kennedy was assassinated on November 22, 1963, and the rifle used had been bought by mail order. Senator Thomas J. Dodd introduced interstate firearms bills in 1963 and 1965, and neither bill passed. Martin Luther King Jr. was assassinated on April 4, 1968. Senator Robert F. Kennedy was shot on June 5, 1968, and died on June 6, 1968. The bill that became the Gun Control Act of 1968 was introduced on June 10, 1968. The Omnibus Crime Control and Safe Streets Act was signed on June 19, 1968, and the Gun Control Act of 1968 was signed on October 22, 1968, following the assassinations of that year and a legislative effort that predated them.

### Q: What is the public law number of the Gun Control Act?

The public law number is Public Law 90-618, enacted by the 90th Congress and published at 82 Stat. 1213. The bill was H.R. 17735, introduced by Representative Emanuel Celler on June 10, 1968. The House passed the bill on July 24, 1968, by 305 to 118, and the Senate passed it on September 18, 1968, by 70 to 17, acting in lieu of the Senate measure S. 3633. The conference report was agreed to on October 10, 1968, and President Lyndon B. Johnson signed the bill on October 22, 1968. A citation to the act in legal research ordinarily gives the public law number, the Statutes at Large page, and the codification at chapter 44 of title 18.

### Q: Is the Gun Control Act of 1968 in force?

The act remains in force as chapter 44 of title 18, sections 921 to 931, in the form given to it by later amendments. The Firearm Owners' Protection Act of 1986 revised definitions, mental state standards, inspection limits, transfer conditions, and possession rules, and added the machine gun provision in section 922(o) and the safe passage provision in section 926A. Later enactments added the restraining order category in 1994, the domestic violence misdemeanor category in 1996, and, through the Brady Act, the background check duty for licensee transfers in section 922(t), operating through the national system from November 30, 1998. The chapter a reader consults is therefore the 1968 structure as amended, not the original text standing alone.

### Q: Where is the Gun Control Act of 1968 codified?

The act is codified principally at chapter 44 of title 18 of the United States Code, sections 921 to 931. Section 921 supplies the definitions, including firearm, destructive device, antique firearm, and engaged in the business. Section 922 states the unlawful acts, including the transfer rules, the prohibited person provisions in subsection (g), and the import bar in subsection (l). Section 923 states the licensing and recordkeeping system. Section 925 states exceptions and the import authorization provisions, including the sporting purposes standard in subsection (d)(3). Section 926 states rulemaking authority and the safe passage provision appears at section 926A. Section 924 states penalties. The act as enacted appears at Public Law 90-618, 82 Stat. 1213.

### Q: What is the difference between the Gun Control Act and the National Firearms Act?

The two statutes regulate different subjects by different methods, and together they form Title I and Title II of the federal firearms laws. The Gun Control Act of 1968 is Title I, codified at chapter 44 of title 18, and regulates commerce in firearms generally through licensing of importers, manufacturers, and dealers, records, transfer rules, prohibited persons, and import authorization. The National Firearms Act of 1934 is Title II, codified at chapter 53 of title 26, and imposes a registration and transfer tax regime on a defined class of weapons, including machine guns, silencers, and short-barreled rifles and shotguns. The 1968 act amended the 1934 act in its own Title II, adding destructive devices and curing a self-incrimination defect, rather than replacing it.

### Q: Did the Gun Control Act end mail-order firearm sales?

For interstate sales to individuals, the act completed the arrangement that ended the practice. Under the Federal Firearms Act of 1938, interstate mail order sales of firearms to individuals had remained lawful, and the rifle in the assassination of President Kennedy in 1963 had been bought by mail order. The October act required a licensee to sell to an unlicensed person in person at licensed premises under section 922(c), barred a nonlicensee from receiving in the home state a firearm obtained outside that state under section 922(a)(3), and barred a nonlicensee from transferring a firearm to a resident of another state under section 922(a)(5). Handgun mail order had been restricted earlier through postal law and the June 1968 act. A firearm ordered from another state after October 1968 moved through licensees.

### Q: What is an antique firearm under the Gun Control Act?

An antique firearm is a class of arms excluded from the definition that governs chapter 44 by section 921(a)(16). The exclusion covers firearms manufactured in or before 1898, certain replicas that were not designed for conventional ammunition, and muzzle loading firearms that use black powder or black powder substitutes and are incapable of using fixed ammunition. Because an antique firearm sits outside the chapter definition of a firearm, the licensing provisions, the transfer provisions, and the prohibited person provisions of chapter 44 do not apply to an antique firearm as such. The line rests on date of manufacture, design, and loading method rather than on the identity of the owner. The National Firearms Act is a separate regime, and the exclusion in chapter 44 does not by itself answer a question arising under that act.

### Q: How old must a person be to buy a firearm from a licensed dealer?

Section 922(b)(1) sets two age floors for sales by a licensee. A licensee may not sell or deliver a firearm other than a shotgun or a rifle, in practice a handgun, to a person under twenty one years of age. A licensee may not sell or deliver a shotgun or a rifle to a person under eighteen years of age, and the floor for ammunition for a shotgun or a rifle is likewise eighteen years. The floors bind licensees as a condition of a licensed sale, and the June 1968 act had set the handgun floor of twenty one for purchases from licensees four months before the October act restated the framework. State law may set different or additional conditions, and a licensee sale must also comply with the law of the state where the transfer occurs under section 922(b)(2).

### Q: Can a licensed dealer sell a firearm to a resident of another state?

A licensee generally may not sell or deliver a firearm to a person who does not reside in the state where the licensee's premises are located, under section 922(b)(3), and the exceptions define the lawful routes. A licensee may sell a rifle or shotgun to an out of state buyer in a face to face transaction where the buyer meets the licensee in person and the sale complies fully with the laws of both states. No corresponding exception covers a handgun, so a handgun reaches an out of state buyer through a licensee in the buyer's home state, after a licensee to licensee transfer between the two states. Loans and rentals for lawful sporting purposes and transfers to other licensees and to government also fall outside the prohibition. Before 1986, the long gun exception was limited to residents of contiguous states.

### Q: What is ATF Form 4473?

ATF Form 4473 is the Firearms Transaction Record, the form prescribed under the recordkeeping authority in section 923 and completed for each over the counter transfer by a licensee to an unlicensed person. On the form, the buyer certifies eligibility and identity, answering the questions that allow the licensee to determine whether the transfer may proceed under the prohibited person provisions and the other conditions in section 922. The licensee records the identification the buyer presented and, after the background check layer took effect, the background check response received for the transfer. The form is retained by the licensee as part of the licensee records, together with the acquisition and disposition record for the same firearm, and a knowingly false statement in licensee records is barred by section 922(m) and addressed in section 924(a)(3).

### Q: Does the Gun Control Act create a federal registry of firearms?

No provision of the act creates a federal registry of firearms or of firearm owners. The records the statute requires are licensee records, created and held by licensed importers, manufacturers, and dealers at licensed premises, and produced through compliance inspection, defined reports, tracing requests in a bona fide criminal investigation, or delivery to the Attorney General when a licensee goes out of business. Section 926(a), in a proviso added in 1986, states that no rule under the chapter may require licensee records to be transferred to a federal facility or establish any system of registration of firearms, firearm owners, or firearm transactions. Out of business records held at the National Tracing Center support tracing of individual firearms in criminal investigations and do not convert the licensee record system into a registry of owners.

### Q: What is a curios and relics license?

A curios and relics license is one of the license classes in the system section 923 establishes, held by a licensed collector. The license serves collecting activity directed at curios and relics and reaches only firearms within that class. It does not authorize a general dealing business in the full range of firearms covered by chapter 44, and a person who devotes time, attention, and labor to dealing in firearms as a regular course of trade or business with the principal objective of livelihood and profit requires a dealer license instead. The collector license therefore has a defined place inside the licensing structure, narrower than the dealer, importer, and manufacturer classes, and surplus military firearms may be imported as curios or relics for licensed collectors within the narrow channels section 925(e) permits.

### Q: How does firearms tracing work under the Gun Control Act?

Tracing works through the records that section 923 requires licensees to keep, reconstructed in sequence. A trace begins with the serial number marked by the manufacturer and proceeds through the National Tracing Center of the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives in Martinsburg, West Virginia. The center identifies the first licensee to which the manufacturer shipped the firearm, follows the acquisition and disposition records of successive licensees through distribution to the retail dealer, and uses the transaction record held by that dealer to identify the first retail purchaser. Under section 923(g)(7), enacted in 1994, a licensee must respond to a request for record information needed to determine the disposition of a firearm in a bona fide criminal investigation immediately and in no event later than twenty four hours after receipt. Records of licensees that went out of business are held at the center under section 923(g)(4).

### Q: What should a student know about the Gun Control Act of 1968 for an exam?

A student should be able to state the formal identity of the act, Public Law 90-618, 82 Stat. 1213, signed on October 22, 1968, and codified at chapter 44 of title 18, sections 921 to 931, and to explain the design that the provisions share. The statute regulates commerce through a licensed channel, so its licensing, record, and transfer duties attach principally to licensed importers, manufacturers, and dealers, while a transfer between nonlicensees in the same state carries no federal license, record, or check requirement beyond the section 922(d) bar on transfer to a known prohibited person. The student should know the nine prohibited categories and which enactments added the categories of 1994 and 1996, the sporting purposes import standard in section 925(d)(3), the principal changes made in 1986, and the machine gun cutoff of May 19, 1986.
