What should a peacetime volunteer earn for education after service, and should that volunteer pay part of the price before any classroom door opens? Congress faced that question after the draft had ended, after the benefit that had served the Vietnam era cohort had closed to new entrants, and after the savings program that replaced that benefit had failed to draw the recruits the all volunteer force required. The Montgomery GI Bill answered the question with a bargain that had no exact precedent in American veterans law: a generous flat monthly benefit, earned through qualifying service, that a service member first had to buy. The operative problem was manpower as much as education. Recruiting in the late nineteen seventies and the early nineteen eighties produced too few high school diploma graduates, too few recruits in the upper half of the aptitude distribution, and attrition high enough to force the services to recruit replacements for personnel already trained. An education benefit weak in value, deferred in use, and hard to explain at a recruiting station could not correct that condition, and the program on offer between nineteen seventy seven and nineteen eighty four had proved weak on each count.

The formal identity of the program removes any doubt about the legislative vehicle and the code home. The statute was the Veterans' Educational Assistance Act of 1984, enacted as Title VII of the Department of Defense Authorization Act, 1985, Public Law 98-525, signed on October 19, 1984, and codified at Chapter 30 of Title 38 of the United States Code. Congress enacted the program first as a test, covering persons who entered service during a defined window that opened on July 1, 1985. The test became permanent on June 1, 1987, when the New GI Bill Continuation Act, Public Law 100-48, removed the closing date. That continuation statute also gave the 1984 title the citation Montgomery GI Bill Act of 1984, a name honoring House Veterans' Affairs Committee Chairman G. V. "Sonny" Montgomery of Mississippi, the legislator who had carried the case for a peacetime benefit through committee, through a defense authorization conference, and through the continuation fight. Placement inside a defense act and codification inside veterans law expressed the dual character of the design: the Department of Defense paid for the basic benefit as a cost of recruiting, and the veterans agency administered the benefit as readjustment assistance.

The namable claim at the center of the statute is the contributory bargain. The Montgomery GI Bill was the benefit the service member buys, the only major American veterans benefit paid for out of the member's own wages through a 1,200 dollar pay reduction. At entry, a member who did not elect out in writing accepted a reduction of basic pay of 100 dollars per month for the first twelve months of service. That payment purchased eligibility and nothing more tangible than eligibility. The amounts reverted to the Treasury, the reduction could not be stopped or suspended, and in the ordinary case the contribution could not be refunded, even when a veteran never entered a classroom and the ten year period for use expired. In exchange, a qualifying veteran held up to thirty six months of a flat monthly allowance, paid directly to the veteran rather than to the institution, at a rate that opened at 300 dollars per month for full time training and rose through repeated legislation across the working life of the program. Service kickers, college funds, and an added buy up of as much as 600 dollars could raise the monthly amount, while the basic contribution stayed fixed at the figure set in 1984.

The route through the statute follows the logic of that bargain. The account opens with the origins of the design in the closing of the Vietnam era entitlement, the matched savings experiment that enrolled a minority of eligible personnel, the recruiting quality crisis, and the controlled test evidence that showed a generous benefit moving high quality enlistments even with a contribution attached. The account then turns to enactment inside a defense authorization act, to the committees and the funding split that placement produced, and to the continuation legislation that made a three year test permanent. The contributory design receives separate treatment, covering the election at entry, the mechanics and nonrefundability of the reduction, the death payment and the later proportional refund, and the fairness objection pressed against a charge collected from the most junior personnel. Payment and eligibility follow, with the rate structure, the four categories of eligibility, the companion Selected Reserve program, and the ten year delimiting period. Amendments, administration, and the working life of the program lead to the election against the successor program enacted in 2008 and to the Supreme Court decision that redrew the boundary between conversion of a single entitlement and combined use of separate entitlements.

## Origins

The Montgomery GI Bill of 1984 grew out of a gap that Congress created deliberately in the middle of the 1970s and then spent the better part of a decade regretting. For three decades after the Second World War, federal education assistance rested on a simple premise: service earned a benefit, and the government paid for that benefit in full. The Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944, the Korean War era program of 1952, and the Veterans' Readjustment Benefits Act of 1966 all followed that pattern, with no requirement that a service member buy a share of the entitlement out of military pay. That premise changed when conscription expired in 1973 and the all volunteer force took its place. Congress confronted two questions at once: what education assistance peacetime volunteers should receive, and how much of the cost the Treasury should carry for a force that the nation no longer compelled anyone to join. The answer given in 1976 was a smaller, contributory program. The answer given in 1984 bears the name of Representative G. V. "Sonny" Montgomery of Mississippi.

### The Closing of the Vietnam Era Account

The Vietnam era GI Bill, codified in Chapter 34 of Title 38, covered veterans who served on active duty for more than 180 days during the period that began on January 31, 1955. Eligibility for new entrants ended at the close of December 31, 1976. A person who entered active duty on January 1, 1977, or after that day acquired no Chapter 34 entitlement at all, regardless of the length or character of the service that followed. The cutoff was prospective: personnel already in service kept the entitlement that service had earned, and Chapter 34 payments continued for years afterward, with authority terminating on December 31, 1989. For recruiting purposes, the operative fact was the entry date. From the first day of 1977 forward, an enlistment contract could no longer promise the benefit recruiters had described to the preceding cohort, and the replacement was different in kind rather than merely different in amount.

The statute that drew the line was the Veterans' Education and Employment Assistance Act of 1976, Public Law 94-502, approved on October 15, 1976. Title IV of that act, cited in the statute itself as the Post-Vietnam Era Veterans' Educational Assistance Act of 1977, created Chapter 32 and set January 1, 1977, as the effective date of the new program. The purposes written into Chapter 32 were threefold: to provide educational assistance to men and women who entered the armed forces after December 31, 1976, to assist young men and women in obtaining an education that civilian life might not otherwise have made affordable, and to promote the all volunteer military program by attracting qualified men and women to serve. Recruiting was a stated statutory purpose from the first day, and failure to perform that function became the central argument for replacement seven years later.

The design choices of 1976 reflected the fiscal mood in which the draft had ended. The Vietnam era benefit had become expensive, and a contributory structure promised discipline: the Treasury would pay less per veteran, and the veteran who had contributed personal funds would, in theory, treat the entitlement with greater seriousness. A Congressional Budget Office study projected in 1977 that the new program would reach only about 69,000 beneficiaries by 1983, against a Vietnam era caseload of 1.8 million beneficiaries in 1977. Events bore the projection out.

### A Savings Account in Place of an Entitlement

The Veterans' Educational Assistance Program, known by the acronym VEAP and codified as Chapter 32, operated as a matched savings account rather than as a direct entitlement. Participation was voluntary. A service member who elected to participate authorized a monthly deduction from military pay, and each dollar contributed drew a matching contribution of two dollars from the government. Under the program as administered, monthly contributions ran between 25 dollars and 100 dollars. The statute as enacted in 1976 had set a narrower monthly band, between 50 dollars and 75 dollars, and later adjustments widened the range. The lifetime ceiling on personal contributions was fixed at 2,700 dollars. A participant who contributed the full amount accumulated a government match of 5,400 dollars and a total account of 8,100 dollars, held in the Post-Vietnam Era Veterans Education Account in the Treasury. The Department of Defense also held authority to add further contributions, often described as kickers, where a service judged an added incentive necessary for a particular occupation.

Entitlement followed the pattern of contributions. A participant earned between one and 36 months of educational assistance, with the number of months tied to the months of contributions rather than to length of service alone. The monthly payment depended on the total accumulated, the months of entitlement, and the rate of training pursued. A participant who contributed the maximum and spread the account across 36 months received the equivalent of 225 dollars per month before any defense kicker entered the calculation. Benefits had to be used within ten years of release from active duty. A participant who disenrolled could recover personal contributions, but the government match was never the participant's property: disenrollment surrendered the match, and expiration of the ten year period produced a refund of unused personal contributions rather than any payment of matched funds.

Each element of that design carried a recruiting cost. The benefit was deferred, so a seventeen or eighteen year old weighing enlistment against civilian work or civilian college was asked to value money that required a completed term of service before use. The deduction came out of junior enlisted pay, at the point of lowest pay in a military career. The benefit was also opaque in a way that a flat monthly rate was not: a recruiter describing Chapter 34 could quote a single monthly figure, while a recruiter describing VEAP had to explain a contribution schedule, a matching formula, a ceiling, and a refund rule. Close to 700,000 veterans eventually used VEAP benefits according to summaries of the program's full run, but complexity blunted the program's value as an advertisement for enlistment, the function Congress had written into the statute.

The arithmetic of erosion compounded the design problem. VEAP amounts were fixed in nominal terms: the 2,700 dollar ceiling, the 5,400 dollar maximum match, and the 8,100 dollar maximum account did not rise with prices. Consumer prices rose at double digit annual rates in 1979, 1980, and 1981, and college tuition and fees rose alongside general prices. Congressional Research Service comparisons of GI Bill purchasing power, expressed in constant 2005 dollars, recorded a decline during the 1980s from the levels reached in the middle of the 1970s. A maximum account assembled in 1977 dollars and spent on tuition several years later bought less education than the same nominal account would have bought at enrollment.

### Why did so few service members enroll in VEAP?

Participation stayed low because enrollment required junior personnel to surrender scarce pay for a distant benefit whose maximum value was modest. Reports through 1979 recorded participation of 30 percent in the Army, 27 percent in the Navy, 19 percent in the Marine Corps, and 7 percent in the Air Force.

Those service by service figures, drawn from implementation reports to Congress and summarized in a Naval Postgraduate School study of the VEAP decision, describe the program's condition two years after the effective date, and later experience did not transform the picture. Disenrollment rose alongside disappointing enrollment. Married personnel participated at the lowest rates, which the structure of the program made predictable: a household budget carried by junior enlisted pay had the least room for a voluntary deduction of 50 dollars or more each month. Participation ran higher among high school diploma graduates and personnel with some college education, while disenrollment ran highest among participants who had chosen the lowest contribution level, the group for whom the eventual account was smallest and the monthly sacrifice hardest to justify.

The comparison that mattered in congressional debate was the comparison with the successor. When the Chronicle of Higher Education reported on the Montgomery program in 1990, veterans benefits officials described enrollment of close to 80 percent of all enlistees in the new program, a rate the report characterized as nearly double the rate of the program that preceded it. The contrast did not rest on generosity alone, since the 1984 act preserved a contribution. The contrast rested on the size of the benefit relative to the contribution, on the simplicity of a defined monthly payment, and on a structure under which the government share arrived as a program entitlement rather than as the matched balance of a personal savings account. VEAP asked a recruit to save toward college with government help. Chapter 30 promised a stated benefit in exchange for a stated, limited, and front loaded reduction in pay.

### The Recruiting Quality Problem

The weakness of VEAP would have mattered less if the all volunteer force had been meeting its manpower goals with the recruits the services wanted. During the late 1970s and the early 1980s, the services reported the opposite condition. The Department of Defense measured recruit quality principally by two indicators: possession of a regular high school diploma, associated with a markedly higher probability of completing a first term, and scores on the Armed Forces Qualification Test, which sorted recruits into aptitude categories, with Categories I through IIIA representing scores at or above the median of the youth population and Category IV representing the lowest accepted range. Defense planners defined a high quality recruit as a diploma graduate scoring at or above the median, and by that combined measure the force fell well short of the standard planners sought.

The Congressional Budget Office recorded that the share of high quality recruits fell as low as 27 percent in the late 1970s and the early 1980s. A Brookings Institution study drawing on the work of Bernard Rostker put the figure for 1977 at 27.1 percent. Center for Naval Analyses data told the same story from the test score side: in fiscal year 1977, only 34 percent of non prior service accessions scored in Categories I through IIIA, the top half of the aptitude distribution. Army accounts described a sharper deterioration: by 1980 only 54 percent of Army recruits were high school graduates. First term attrition, about 25 percent during the period from 1971 to 1974, rose above 40 percent by 1976, so the services had to recruit additional personnel simply to replace losses among personnel already accessed.

A measurement error deepened the difficulty. New versions of the Armed Services Vocational Aptitude Battery introduced in 1976 had been miscalibrated, so applicants received scores higher than actual performance warranted. An independent study in 1980 established the error, the test was recalibrated, and Congress responded with a statutory ceiling providing that no more than 20 percent of accessions in a service could come from Category IV and that Category IV accessions had to be high school diploma graduates; defense guidance later set the ceiling at 4 percent. Recalibration meant that the late 1970s force had been weaker than official figures at the time had shown, a conclusion that strengthened the case, in hearings between 1980 and 1984, that recruiting required a stronger inducement than pay adjustments and advertising alone had supplied.

The condition of the force acquired a label in 1980, when General Edward C. Meyer, the Army Chief of Staff, testified before a House Armed Services subcommittee that the Army was a hollow force, with tactical forces in the United States some 17,000 soldiers under strength and companies and platoons reduced to skeletons. The phrase connected the recruiting statistics to readiness: a service that could not attract enough diploma graduates faced higher training costs, higher attrition, and units that existed on organizational charts at strengths that rosters did not support. Education benefits entered that debate as a proposed remedy for a readiness problem, advanced by the services as an investment that would lower attrition and training costs by changing the composition of the recruit pool.

### Montgomery and the Case for a New Benefit

The legislator who converted that service argument into a durable bill was Gillespie V. Montgomery of Mississippi, known throughout Congress as "Sonny" Montgomery and on veterans' matters as "Mr. Veteran." Montgomery represented a Mississippi district in the House of Representatives from 1967 to 1997. A veteran of the Second World War, in which service with the 12th Armored Division earned the Bronze Star, and a Mississippi Army National Guard officer who mobilized during the Korean War and retired as a major general in 1980, Montgomery joined the House Committee on Veterans' Affairs early in the congressional career and chaired the committee from 1981. From the chairmanship, Montgomery pressed a proposition that aligned veterans' policy with manpower policy: a peacetime benefit generous enough to matter would draw diploma graduates toward enlistment, would give the services a recruiting instrument that pay raises spread across the entire force could not match for cost effectiveness, and would repay the investment through lower attrition.

Montgomery's position carried weight for reasons beyond seniority. The House Veterans' Affairs Committee held jurisdiction over veterans' education benefits, while the Armed Services Committees held jurisdiction over military pay and over education incentives funded as recruiting tools, and a durable replacement for VEAP needed accommodation across that divide as well as agreement on cost with the executive branch. The Reagan administration approached new entitlement commitments with stated concern for federal spending, and the Department of Defense weighed education benefits against pay and enlistment bonuses with more immediate effects. Montgomery's committee reported legislation in the opening years of the decade, including the Veterans' Educational Assistance Act of 1981, designated H. R. 1400 in the 97th Congress, with eligibility limited to enlistees holding a high school diploma or an equivalency certificate. The General Accounting Office estimated that the bill could cost between two billion dollars and five billion dollars annually, and roughly ten competing GI Bill proposals were pending in the Senate Committee on Veterans' Affairs during the same period. Competing bills, cost estimates spanning billions of dollars, and an administration uncommitted to any design explain why the replacement took until 1984 to enact even though the recruiting evidence had been assembled by 1981.

### Evidence From Test Programs and Youth Surveys

Between the diagnosis and the remedy, Congress obtained something rare in manpower policy: controlled experimental evidence. The Educational Assistance Test Program, conducted by the Department of Defense during fiscal year 1981 under a congressional mandate, tested expanded educational assistance in defined recruiting markets, comparing outcomes where enhanced benefits were offered against outcomes where standard VEAP remained the offer. Richard L. Fernandez of the RAND Corporation reported the results in a 1982 study. The Fernandez study found that a sufficiently generous program could bring high quality recruits into service, and the strongest effect came from the Army program combining VEAP with added defense contributions, described as the Ultra VEAP kicker program, which raised Army enlistments of high quality males by about nine percent without reducing enlistments in the other services. Removing the contribution requirement produced only small gains, virtually none in the Army: a contributory design, if the benefit was large enough, did not by itself suppress enlistment response.

A March 1982 Congressional Budget Office study reached a compatible conclusion, estimating that basic VEAP without kickers offered very little recruiting improvement and that supplemental defense contributions produced the gains planners sought. The Army acted on the same evidence through the Army College Fund, created in 1982 after college bonus experiments in 1979, which layered service funds on top of VEAP for recruits entering designated occupations. The test evidence narrowed the legislative dispute: cost and the proper funding account remained contested, but the proposition that education money moved high quality enlistment decisions had been demonstrated in a controlled field experiment rather than asserted from anecdote.

Survey evidence pointed in the same direction. The Department of Defense tracked youth intentions through the Youth Attitude Tracking Study, a recurring survey on propensity to serve and on the reasons that might move a decision toward enlistment. Across the survey reporting in the period surrounding the VEAP replacement debate, money for education after service ranked among the leading reasons that youth with a stated propensity to enlist gave for considering service, alongside job training and pay. Secretary of Veterans Affairs Togo D. West, in a statement quoted in the Congressional Record in 2000, stated that new recruits cited money for college as the major reason given for enlisting. The cohort the services most wanted, diploma graduates with college ambitions and civilian college options, was the cohort for which a credible college benefit answered the principal objection to enlistment: the years of education forgone during a term of service.

The services and the committees did not agree on every design question, and the disagreements shaped the final statute. A benefit structured as a recruiting incentive argued for defense funding, since the Department of Defense captured the recruiting gain. A benefit without any contribution would maximize the recruiting signal but maximize federal cost and would abandon the principle, defended in the 1976 debates, that a peacetime volunteer should hold a personal stake in the entitlement. A benefit tied to a diploma requirement, as the 1981 committee bill proposed, would deny the incentive to non graduates the services also needed. Each disagreement left a mark on the compromise enacted in 1984: the contribution survived, the payment took the form of a defined monthly stipend rather than a savings balance, and a substantial share of the cost was assigned to the Department of Defense.

### Why the Replacement Took a Contributory Form

The shape of the 1984 act followed from that history. The contributory element survived because the test program had shown that a contribution did not destroy the enlistment effect of a generous benefit, because the contribution reduced measured federal cost at a moment when cost estimates in the billions had stalled competing bills, and because Montgomery and the committee majority treated a personal stake as a legitimate condition for a peacetime benefit. The flat monthly stipend replaced the savings account because VEAP had demonstrated the recruiting weakness of a benefit whose value a recruit could not state in one figure, and because the test programs had shown that stated monthly payments were the quantity to which high quality recruits responded. Defense funding entered the structure because the statute's stated purposes, carried forward from Chapter 32 into Chapter 30, treated the benefit as an instrument for attracting qualified personnel, and an instrument purchased for recruiting belonged partly on the books of the department that did the recruiting.

Origins, in the case of the 1984 act, therefore determined architecture. A lapsed entitlement, a matched savings program that enrolled a minority of eligible personnel and eroded with inflation, a recruit pool in which high quality accessions fell to roughly one quarter of entrants at the low point, a recalibrated aptitude test, a committee chairman with jurisdiction and a settled conviction, and a controlled experiment that priced the remedy together produced a program that kept the contribution of VEAP, discarded the savings account mechanics of VEAP, raised the value of the benefit to a level a recruiter could advertise, and charged the cost in part to the defense budget. The provisions that followed executed choices that the experience of 1977 to 1984 had already made.

## Enactment inside a defense authorization

The Montgomery GI Bill did not begin as a freestanding education statute debated and enacted on a separate track. The program began as one title inside a much larger defense law, and that placement shaped the committees that handled the measure, the department that paid for the basic benefit, the agency that administered payments, and the way later readers had to locate the law in the United States Code. The governing enactment was the Department of Defense Authorization Act, 1985, Public Law 98-525, approved on October 19, 1984, when President Ronald Reagan signed H.R. 5167 into law. Within that authorization act, a distinct title, cited at enactment as the Veterans' Educational Assistance Act of 1984, created a new program of educational assistance for members of the Armed Forces who entered service during a defined test period, and the title placed the operative provisions in Title 38 of the United States Code as a new chapter devoted to educational assistance for the all-volunteer force. The arrangement joined two subjects that Congress often treated in separate legislation. Defense authorization bills set military personnel policy and authorized appropriations for weapons, operations, research, and related activities for a fiscal year. Veterans education legislation set readjustment benefits for service members after or during service and assigned administration to the veterans agency. The 1984 act joined those subjects in a single public law, and the joining was deliberate rather than accidental, because the education benefit was designed in substantial part as a recruiting and retention instrument for a volunteer military.

The defense authorization vehicle explained both the timing and the institutional logic of the enactment. By the early 1980s, the Armed Forces had operated without conscription for more than a decade, following the end of inductions in 1973, and personnel policy depended on attracting volunteers of sufficient quality in a labor market in which the services competed with colleges and civilian employers. Education benefits had formed part of that competition under earlier programs, including the post-Vietnam era program codified in Chapter 32 of Title 38, but that contributory program had drawn criticism for benefit levels and participation patterns that limited value as an inducement to enlist. A replacement program required action by committees with jurisdiction over veterans benefits and by committees with jurisdiction over military personnel. Placing the new program inside the annual defense authorization gave the measure a moving legislative vehicle, a conference process accustomed to resolving large personnel questions, and a direct connection to the defense budget that would carry the cost of the basic benefit. The education title therefore traveled with provisions on pay, force levels, procurement, and other defense matters, and enactment depended on agreement on the defense bill as a whole rather than on passage of an isolated veterans bill through both chambers on a separate calendar.

### A defense bill as the legislative vehicle

Authorization acts occupy a specific place in federal lawmaking, and the placement of the education program inside an authorization act followed from that function. An authorization act establishes or continues a program or agency activity and authorizes appropriations for a stated purpose, while a later appropriations act provides the budget authority that permits an agency to incur obligations and make payments. The Department of Defense Authorization Act, 1985, performed the authorization function for fiscal year 1985 defense activities, and Title VII performed the same function for the new educational assistance program by creating entitlement rules, service requirements, contribution rules, and administrative duties in permanent statutory language. The language did more than authorize spending for one fiscal year. Section 702 of Title VII added a new chapter to Title 38, numbered Chapter 30 in the codification that followed later renumbering, and that chapter contained detailed provisions on eligibility, entitlement, rates, and administration that operated beyond the fiscal year covered by the surrounding defense titles. The distinction between the vehicle and the codified program accounted for a recurring source of confusion in citations. The public law number identified the defense authorization act as enacted, the title number identified the part of that act that created the education program, and the code citation identified the place where the operative benefit rules were classified for continuing use.

### What title of the defense authorization created the new education program?

Title VII of the Department of Defense Authorization Act, 1985, enacted the program. Section 701 supplied the short title, and Section 702 added the new chapter to Title 38 of the United States Code. The placement placed a veterans education benefit inside a defense authorization statute.

The title number mattered for research as well as for citation form. Public Law 98-525 contained many titles addressing distinct defense subjects, and a citation to the public law alone did not identify the education provisions with precision. A citation to Title VII, by contrast, directed a researcher to the portion of the Statutes at Large beginning at page 2553 of volume 98, where Section 701 stated the short title and Section 702 set out the new chapter. Congressional Research Service chronologies of veterans educational assistance programs used exactly that form, listing the active duty program as established by the Department of Defense Authorization Act, 1985, and classified in Title 38, Chapter 30. House and Senate references to earlier and later GI Bill programs likewise identified the 1984 program as Title VII of Public Law 98-525, placing the program in a sequence that included the Post-9/11 program enacted as a title of a supplemental appropriations act in 2008.

### The test program design and the entry window

Congress enacted the program in 1984 on a test basis, and the test design qualified every statement about permanence during the first years of operation. The new chapter applied to individuals who first became members of the Armed Forces or first entered on active duty during the period beginning on July 1, 1985, and ending on June 30, 1988. That three year entry window appeared in the eligibility provisions for basic educational assistance, including the provisions later codified at Sections 3011 and 3012 of Title 38, and parallel language governed the Selected Reserve provisions in Title 10. The window defined a cohort rather than a permanent class. A service member who entered during the window and satisfied the service, contribution, and discharge requirements acquired entitlement under Chapter 30, while a person who entered after June 30, 1988, faced a statutory bar unless Congress extended or removed the terminal date. The design permitted Congress and the Department of Defense to observe recruiting effects, participation rates, retention behavior, and costs for a defined group before deciding whether the program deserved continuation without an endpoint.

The test structure carried practical consequences for administration from the first enrollments in 1985. Recruiters could present the benefit to prospective enlistees as an enacted program with stated rates and conditions, and the services could collect the required pay reductions from participants, set at 100 dollars per month for the first 12 months of pay entitlement for the basic program, with the amounts reduced from pay reverting to the Treasury under the statutory scheme. At the same time, planners in the Department of Defense and officials in the Veterans Administration had to build payment and record systems for a program that Congress had authorized to stop accepting new entrants after three years. Schools and state approving agencies faced the same limited horizon in preparing to approve programs of education for a new chapter of Title 38. The limited horizon also affected congressional oversight. Committees that supported the program understood that a decision on continuation would arrive within a short period, and evaluation of the test cohort became part of the legislative record for the continuation debate in 1987, well before the entry window closed in 1988.

### Committees and the Montgomery name

The committee history of the 1984 act explained why a veterans committee chairman became the central legislative figure in a defense authorization title. Representative G. V. "Sonny" Montgomery of Mississippi chaired the House Committee on Veterans' Affairs from 1981 to 1995, according to Department of Veterans Affairs biographical material, and Montgomery also held a senior position on the House Committee on Armed Services, including service as a ranking member. That combination of assignments placed Montgomery at the junction of the two jurisdictions that the education title joined. The Veterans' Affairs Committee held jurisdiction over veterans benefits, including education readjustment programs in Title 38, while the Armed Services Committees of the House and the Senate held jurisdiction over the defense authorization bill, military personnel policy, and the recruiting rationale for the benefit. A chairman who sat in both arenas could negotiate benefit design with veterans advocates and personnel policy with defense officials inside the same legislative process, and historical accounts of the period described sustained collaboration between Montgomery and Army personnel leadership in building support for a new GI Bill as an instrument for sustaining the all-volunteer force.

The Armed Services Committees contributed more than a vehicle. Defense authorization proceedings in the House and the Senate supplied hearings and reports on manpower quality, recruiting shortfalls in particular skills, and retention of experienced personnel, and those proceedings framed education benefits as personnel expenditures with measurable returns in enlistment contracts. The Senate Committee on Armed Services, chaired during the period by Senator John Tower of Texas, managed the defense authorization in the Senate, and President Reagan, in signing Public Law 98-525, credited Tower and the Armed Services leadership in both chambers for work on the defense bill as a whole. The Veterans' Affairs Committees, in turn, supplied the benefit architecture, including entitlement periods, delimiting dates, approved education standards drawn from Chapter 36 of Title 38, and protections developed in earlier GI Bill programs. Conference negotiations on the defense bill reconciled House and Senate positions across the entire authorization, and Title VII emerged from that reconciliation as agreed text rather than as a separate conference on a veterans bill. The resulting statute bore the marks of both jurisdictions, with service based eligibility rules that reflected personnel policy and payment and approval rules that reflected veterans program administration.

The naming of the program for Montgomery followed the continuation legislation of 1987 rather than the original enactment of 1984. During the first years of operation, official and public references commonly used descriptive names, including the New GI Bill and the All-Volunteer Force Educational Assistance Program, the latter name appearing in the chapter heading for Chapter 30. Section 2 of Public Law 100-48 amended Section 701 of the 1984 title in 1987 and provided that the title could be cited as the Montgomery GI Bill Act of 1984. The amendment attached the chairman name to the original title while retaining the year of original enactment, an unusual citation form that preserved legislative history inside the honorific title. Public usage shortened the result to the Montgomery GI Bill, and agency material, congressional tributes, and education guidance adopted that shortened form for both the active duty program in Chapter 30 and, with a Selected Reserve qualifier, the reserve program. The naming recognized Montgomery sponsorship and committee leadership across the long campaign for a peacetime GI Bill, a campaign that Department of Veterans Affairs and congressional tributes associated with Montgomery reputation as a leading House advocate for veterans and for the National Guard and Reserve.

### Who paid and who administered

The funding and administration split gave the clearest evidence of the dual character of the program, and the split followed the purpose that Congress assigned to the benefit. The Department of Defense paid for the basic educational assistance as a cost of recruiting and retaining personnel, and appropriations for that cost rested on an actuarial basis in the defense budget at the inception of the program, a funding treatment later described in executive branch testimony on proposals to move reserve education funding. The logic treated the expected cost of future education payments as part of the cost of obtaining the service that created the entitlement, in the same manner that accrual concepts treated retirement costs as personnel costs incurred during service. Accrual funding required the Department of Defense to recognize and fund estimated benefit costs while participants served, rather than leaving the entire cost to fall in the years when veterans entered education programs after separation. Later proposals to shift funding for some education programs to the readjustment benefits account of the Department of Veterans Affairs drew objections grounded in that logic, on the stated ground that separating the cost from the defense personnel budget would obscure the full cost of manpower and weaken the connection between the recruiting tool and the budget of the department that used the tool.

Payment mechanics reflected the split between funding source and administering agency. The veterans agency, known in 1984 as the Veterans Administration and elevated to the cabinet level Department of Veterans Affairs by legislation enacted in 1988 and effective in 1989, administered Chapter 30 benefits, determined eligibility on the basis of service and contribution records furnished by the services, approved payment rates under the chapter, and paid monthly educational assistance allowances to eligible veterans and service members. Amounts for Chapter 30 payments moved through the readjustment benefits appropriation structure of the veterans agency, the account through which readjustment benefits for veterans were paid, with the defense funded cost recognized on the defense side of the budget under the accrual arrangement. Service departments maintained the underlying records of entry dates, pay reductions, and service completion that established entitlement, and the veterans agency relied on those records in adjudicating claims. A dispute over a service fact therefore began with military records, while a dispute over enrollment, pursuit, or payment rate fell under veterans agency regulations and the approval system administered with state approving agencies under Chapter 36.

### Permanence through continuation legislation

The test program acquired permanent status before the entry window closed, through a short statute that removed the terminal date and revised the declared purposes of Chapter 30. Public Law 100-48, approved on June 1, 1987, when President Reagan signed H.R. 1085, bore the short title New GI Bill Continuation Act. The Reagan signing statement of the same date described the measure as removing the expiration date for the educational assistance programs under Chapter 30 of Title 38 and Chapter 106 of Title 10. The operative amendments performed that removal by substitution. Sections of Chapter 30 that had limited initial entry to the period beginning on July 1, 1985, and ending on June 30, 1988, were amended to cover service after June 30, 1985, without a closing date, and a parallel amendment made the same substitution in the Selected Reserve provisions of Title 10. Section 5 of Public Law 100-48 also revised the declared purposes in Section 3001 of Title 38, broadening the statement of purposes for the permanent program. The amendments contained no new entry window and no replacement sunset, and the absence of a terminal date in the amended eligibility text formed the statutory basis for describing the program as permanent from June 1, 1987, onward.

The continuation act also settled the naming question and clarified the relationship between the test period and the permanent program. A service member who had entered during the test window held entitlement under the same chapter that governed entrants after the window would have closed, and no separate permanent program replaced the test program in the code. Continuity of codification meant that regulations, entitlement counts, and contribution records created between 1985 and 1987 carried forward without reenrollment under a new statute. The House had approved continuation legislation in the spring of 1987 by a vote reported at the time as 401 to 2, and Montgomery, as sponsor of the extension and chairman of the House Veterans' Affairs Committee, argued on the record against returning a successful program to uncertainty before the test period had run a full course. Pentagon support for continuation, reported during the 1987 debate, rested on recruiting grounds and on concern over a shrinking pool of military age youth in the following decade, a demographic argument that Representative Gerald Solomon of New York also advanced in support of the measure. Continuation therefore rested on early evidence and on projected manpower conditions, rather than on a completed evaluation of the full three year test cohort, since the final year of test entries had not begun when the continuation act became law.

### Reading a statute enacted inside another statute

The enactment history of Chapter 30 offers a compact example of a general phenomenon in federal legislation, in which a complete program statute appears as a title, division, or subtitle of a larger act devoted principally to a different subject. Defense authorization acts, omnibus appropriations measures, budget reconciliation acts, and emergency supplemental acts have all carried program legislation of lasting effect, and the Servicemen's Readjustment Act tradition itself included later programs enacted through larger vehicles, including the Post-9/11 program enacted in 2008 as Title V of a supplemental appropriations act. For a researcher, the phenomenon required attention to the hierarchy of the enacting document. The public law number identified the whole act, the title number identified the program legislation within the act, the section numbers within the title supplied short titles and operative amendments, and the classification tables showed where each operative section was placed in the United States Code. A citation that omitted the title level risked sending a researcher to hundreds of pages of unrelated defense provisions, while a citation that omitted the public law level severed the program from the enactment date and the Statutes at Large pages that proved the original text.

Classification followed function and administration more closely than it followed the subject of the vehicle, and Chapter 30 demonstrated the rule. The education program traveled in a defense act because the defense budget paid for the basic benefit as a recruiting cost and because Armed Services jurisdiction supplied the vehicle, yet the operative law was classified in Title 38 because the veterans agency administered the benefit as a readjustment program and because generations of education benefit law already occupied Title 38. Funding source explained enactment placement, administering agency explained codification, and committee jurisdiction explained how both placements could arise from a single legislative bargain. The same analysis applies to other programs in which a department funds an inducement that a second department performs as a benefit, and classification disputes in such programs usually resolve by asking which agency administers the operative entitlement and which code title collects that agency benefit law. Understanding why a statute sits in a code title apart from the subject of the act that enacted the statute is a research skill that repays practice across many fields of federal law. A careful reader who traces a program from public law to title to code chapter gains a reliable method for reading statutes enacted inside authorization acts and for separating the history of a vehicle from the law that the vehicle delivered.

For a method that applies beyond this program, a guide to [how to read a federal statute](/2022/05/01/how-to-read-a-federal-statute/) sets out the steps for tracing a title inside a larger act back to the code sections that carry the operative rules.

## The contributory design

The defining transaction of the 1984 act took place before a new service member had performed any meaningful service at all. Under the program enacted as Chapter 30 of Title 38, the education benefit was not conferred by service alone. A member bought a place in the program at entry, paid for that place out of basic pay during the first year of service, and held the resulting entitlement subject to the service and discharge conditions of the statute. The contribution was small beside the value of the benefit on offer, but the contribution was also compulsory in structure, difficult to escape once the entry decision had passed, and, in the ordinary case, gone for good if the benefit went unused. That combination made the buy-in the most argued over feature of the program, and the argument concerned whether an earned entitlement ought to carry a price of admission and who bore the loss when a person paid the price and then never entered a classroom.

The contributory design separated Chapter 30 from the Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944, which had imposed no charge on the veteran, and aligned Chapter 30 instead with the Post-Vietnam Era Veterans' Educational Assistance Program, known as VEAP, enacted in 1976. VEAP had rested on matched savings, with a participant contributing between 25 dollars and 100 dollars per month and the government matching at two dollars for each dollar saved. The Congressional Research Service primer on GI bills enacted before 2008 recorded that only 20 percent to 25 percent of new recruits contributed to VEAP and that 40 percent of participants disenrolled to recover contributions. The 1984 act answered that record with a different bargain. The price was fixed, the government share was far larger in proportion, the benefit was paid as a flat monthly allowance rather than drawn from a personal account, and the escape route that VEAP had provided through disenrollment and refund was closed. Section 3011(b) of Title 38 stated the fiscal character of the arrangement plainly, in providing that any amount by which basic pay was reduced reverted to the Treasury and was not to be considered as having been received by the individual. The contribution purchased eligibility and nothing more tangible than eligibility.

### The election at entry into service

Section 3011(c)(1) of Title 38 placed the decision at a single, early point in a service career. An individual in the core category covering persons who first entered active duty after June 30, 1985, could make an election not to receive educational assistance under Chapter 30, and the statute directed that any such election be made at the time the individual initially entered on active duty. An individual who made the election was not entitled to educational assistance under the chapter. The default ran toward participation, because enrollment did not depend on an affirmative application filed years later with the Department of Veterans Affairs. The entrant who did nothing, or who signed the enrollment documentation presented during entry processing without electing out, entered the program, and the pay reduction followed.

The Department of Defense documented the election on DD Form 2366, the statement of understanding for the Montgomery GI Bill Act of 1984. The form recorded that the member was automatically eligible and would be enrolled unless the option to disenroll was exercised, that basic pay would be reduced by 100 dollars per month until 1,200 dollars had been deducted, and that the reduction could not be refunded, suspended, or stopped. Administrative descriptions of the program described disenrollment as a one time, irrevocable decision. A member who declined at entry could not purchase a way back into Chapter 30 after gaining experience, and a member who remained enrolled could not stop the deductions in a month of financial strain. Congress created limited doorways for defined groups outside the core category, in sections 3018, 3018A, 3018B, and 3018C of Title 38, covering persons involuntarily separated during force reductions, persons separating under the Voluntary Separation Incentive and the Special Separation Benefit programs, persons converting from VEAP during a specified election period in 1996 and 1997, and certain persons on active duty under Title 32 of Title 10.

### The amount and mechanics of the reduction

Section 3011(b)(1) of Title 38 supplied the arithmetic for the principal category. Except as otherwise provided, the basic pay of an individual who did not make the election not to receive educational assistance was to be reduced by 100 dollars for each of the first 12 months during which the individual was entitled to basic pay. Twelve reductions of 100 dollars produced the total contribution of 1,200 dollars. Section 3012(c) imposed the parallel reduction for participants whose entitlement rested on a shorter initial period of obligated service, including the combination of two years of active duty followed by service in the Selected Reserve. The amount was uniform across grades, services, and occupational specialties, for the same basic entitlement to 36 months of educational assistance. Uniformity gave the program administrative simplicity and a clear recruiting message, and uniformity also produced the distributional objection examined later in the present section, because a flat reduction took a larger share of income from the member with the smallest pay.

The reduction applied only during months in which the individual was entitled to basic pay, and the amounts reduced were excluded from taxable income in administrative practice, consistent with the statutory statement that the amounts were not to be considered as having been received by the individual. The reduction, once begun, could not be suspended at the request of the member, and completion of the full 1,200 dollars operated as a condition of the entitlement purchased. The buy-in was structured as a threshold, and a threshold either was crossed or was not crossed.

The basic contribution also had a voluntary supplement. Sections 3011(e) and 3012(f) of Title 38 permitted a participant, while on active duty, to make additional contributions toward an increased monthly rate. Department guidance described the option, widely known as the buy up, as permitting contributions of up to 600 dollars, producing an increase of up to 150 dollars per month across 36 months, a maximum additional value of 5,400 dollars. The added amount was realized only through months of benefit actually paid, the contribution had to be made before separation, and the additional contributions were not refundable, a point the Department stated expressly in guidance excluding the buy up from the later Post-9/11 GI Bill refund. Additional contributions entered a payment back to a family only through the death benefit, where section 3017(b) included such amounts in the total from which benefits already paid were subtracted.

### Can the contribution be refunded if the benefit is never used?

In general, no. Amounts reduced from basic pay under Chapter 30 reverted to the Treasury and were not refundable when a veteran never used the benefit. Two narrow exceptions applied: a service connected death benefit under section 3017, and a proportional refund after exhaustion of Post-9/11 GI Bill entitlement.

The nonrefundability rule followed directly from the statutory treatment of the reduction. Because the amounts reverted to the Treasury and were deemed never to have been received by the individual, Chapter 30 contained no account balance to return. The contrast with VEAP was exact, because section 3223 of Title 38 provided that VEAP contributions could be refunded after a participant disenrolled. Chapter 30 contained no disenrollment refund provision, so a participant who allowed the ten year period for use of benefits to expire without enrolling in an approved program received a negative answer grounded in the statute.

The first exception was the death benefit enacted in section 3017 of Title 38 and implemented in section 21.7280 of Title 38 of the Code of Federal Regulations. Section 3017(a) directed the Secretary of Veterans Affairs to make a payment in the event of the service connected death of an individual who was entitled to basic educational assistance under Chapter 30, or who was on active duty and would have been eligible but for the minimum service or educational requirements of the eligibility provisions. The death had to occur while the individual was on active duty or within one year after discharge or release. The regulation required that the death be service connected, applying the standards of section 3.312 of Title 38 of the Code of Federal Regulations, and confined the postdischarge branch to deaths after October 28, 1992. Section 3017(b) fixed the amount as the total of amounts reduced from basic pay, amounts collected under the conversion provisions, and amounts contributed under the additional contribution provisions, minus educational assistance paid under Chapter 30, so the payment returned the unused portion of what the member had paid in. Section 3017(a)(2) fixed the order of payment to the first surviving person in a sequence consisting of the beneficiary designated under the individual's Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance policy, the surviving spouse, the surviving children in equal shares, and the surviving parents in equal shares.

The second exception arrived through the Post-9/11 Veterans Educational Assistance Act of 2008, which created Chapter 33 of Title 38 effective for benefits payable on and after August 1, 2009. Chapter 33 imposed no contribution. A person eligible for both programs could make an irrevocable election to receive benefits under Chapter 33 in lieu of benefits under Chapter 30, under section 21.9520 of Title 38 of the Code of Federal Regulations. Department of Veterans Affairs guidance on Montgomery GI Bill refunds, in place well before the vantage of March 15, 2019, provided that a person who had paid the buy-in and elected the Post-9/11 GI Bill could receive a refund of a proportional amount of the basic contribution if, and after, all entitlement under the Post-9/11 GI Bill was used. A person who did not use all entitlement under Chapter 33 received no refund. The proportion was calculated from the Chapter 30 entitlement remaining at the time of the switch, with the number of months, including a fraction of a partial month, divided by 36 and multiplied by 1,200. A person who had used no Chapter 30 benefits stood to receive the full 1,200 dollars, and a person with 20.5 months remaining stood to receive 683 dollars and 33 cents, under the worked example published by the Department. The refund was paid automatically with the last monthly housing allowance payment under Chapter 33, and the refund covered only the basic contribution, because the buy up was not refunded and college fund amounts had never been paid by the member.

No general hardship route stood beside the two exceptions in the law in force at the March 2019 vantage. Chapter 30 contained no provision authorizing refund on grounds of financial hardship during service, no provision authorizing disenrollment with repayment after the entry election had passed, and no equitable power in administrative officials to enlarge either refund route for an individual case.

### Government money beyond the basic benefit

The member contribution was the smallest of the streams of money that could stand behind a Chapter 30 benefit. The basic allowance itself was a federal benefit paid under sections 3014 and 3015 of Title 38 and funded through appropriations for readjustment benefits. The contribution did not fund the member's own allowance in an actuarial sense, because the 1,200 dollars reverted to the Treasury as a general receipt, while allowances were paid from program appropriations without reference to the amount any individual recipient had contributed beyond the threshold question of eligibility. A contribution of 1,200 dollars gated access to 36 monthly payments that, even at the original rates of the 1980s, summed to a figure many times larger, and the contribution remained fixed at 1,200 dollars from enactment through the March 2019 vantage.

Supplemental educational assistance supplied by the Department of Defense and the Department of Homeland Security formed the remaining stream, administered under Subchapter III of Chapter 30 and paid through the Department of Defense Education Benefits Fund established by section 2006 of Title 10. Section 3021 of Title 38 authorized the Secretary concerned to increase the monthly amount of educational assistance for an individual who held a skill or specialty designated as one in which a critical shortage of personnel existed or for which recruitment was difficult, or, in the case of critical units, retention was difficult, with the increase capped by reference to section 3015(d) of Title 38. Section 3022 authorized supplemental assistance for additional service, and section 3035 allocated payment for the increases to the Defense fund or to Department of Homeland Security appropriations. The supplemental amounts were known across the services as kickers, and the packaged combinations of the basic benefit plus a service funded supplement were marketed under service specific names, of which the Army College Fund was the most extensively advertised, alongside the Navy College Fund and the Marine Corps College Fund. A kicker attached to an enlistment contract for a designated specialty or a longer term of service.

### Why proponents required a payment

Representative G. V. Sonny Montgomery of Mississippi, chairman of the House Committee on Veterans' Affairs and the legislator whose name the program took, defended the contributory structure as part of a considered design rather than as a revenue measure. The design, as Montgomery presented the program, asked the service member to invest in a personal educational future at the moment of enlistment, on the theory that a benefit carrying a personal stake would be valued and used, and that a modest shared cost made a generous benefit fiscally sustainable after the noncontributory Vietnam era benefit had expired and VEAP had failed to attract participation. The contribution screened commitment, because an entrant willing to accept reduced pay across the first year signaled an intention to complete service and to pursue education. The contribution also shared cost, because the federal government undertook to pay the overwhelming share of the value delivered, and the member share offset a small part of program cost while establishing that the benefit was earned through sacrifice in service and through payment in cash.

The Department of Defense evaluation of the program, relayed by Representative Bob Filner of California in a House statement of May 1996, gave proponents figures on which the recruiting claims rested. Enrollment among new recruits rose from 50 percent at the inception of the program in 1985 to 95 percent in fiscal year 1995, more than two million active duty recruits had elected to participate by the date of the statement, and the share of participants using benefits after service rose from 40 percent in 1991 to 46 percent at the end of 1993. The chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, quoted by Representative Filner in the same statement, recorded that recruiters from all services identified the Montgomery GI Bill as the best recruitment tool available to the force. A further argument rested on comparison with VEAP, whose elective structure allowed recruits to contribute nothing and to disenroll and reclaim contributions, with the result that balances at separation were often too small to finance meaningful education, in the account preserved in the Congressional Research Service primer. On the proponent reading, nonrefundability was inseparable from generosity, because the cross subsidy from nonusers to users allowed a 1,200 dollar payment to gate a benefit worth many multiples of that sum.

### The fairness objection and the record of use

The objection to the buy-in began from the pay tables of the junior enlisted force. In a statement supporting H.R. 1212 in the 108th Congress, a bill that would have repealed the 1,200 dollar reduction in pay, Representative Lane Evans of Illinois described the contribution as an unnecessary GI education tax and a hardship on the most junior service members, many of whom, the statement recorded, qualified for food stamps, and observed that no other federal education program charged a participation fee of that kind. Testimony in a 2007 hearing, presented on behalf of Iraq and Afghanistan Veterans of America, stated that nearly 95 percent of active duty service members bought into the program, that only 8 percent used all of the educational benefits, that more than 30 percent never used any benefits, and that such veterans returned more than 230 million dollars to the Treasury through nonrefundable contributions. High enrollment showed that the entry decision operated in practice as a near automatic deduction, and low complete use showed that the purchased entitlement and the realized benefit diverged for the great majority.

The Veterans of Foreign Wars pressed the timing dimension of the objection. Dennis Cullinan, director of national legislative service for the organization, stated in material published by the organization in 2004 that a decision about participation ought not to have to be made at the moment when the service member earned the least, and the organization listed elimination of the buy-in among the central provisions sought in a successor GI Bill. A congressional hearing exchange placed the underlying pay figures on the record. The monthly basic pay of an enlisted member in the first grade in 1985 stood at 573 dollars and 60 cents, so a reduction of 100 dollars consumed a share of monthly basic pay in the neighborhood of one sixth to one fifth. The same exchange noted that the benefit at enactment produced a total value of 10,800 dollars across 36 months, a ratio of nine dollars of benefit for each dollar contributed. The fairness objection answered that the ratio described the experience of the member who used the full benefit, while the member who used none of the benefit experienced a ratio of zero.

Reporting by the American Forces Press Service, carrying statements by Department of Veterans Affairs officials, placed the nonuse problem in plain terms. Dennis Douglas, deputy director for education services at the Department, addressed the fact that half of the service members eligible for Montgomery GI Bill benefits did not use the benefit, describing the forfeiture of a benefit purchased in advance as a waste of a strong investment position and noting a return of 16 dollars in benefits for each dollar invested at the rates then in effect. The official response treated nonuse as an information and encouragement problem. The fairness objection treated the same figures through a different frame, in which a nonrefundable charge collected at entry, combined with a benefit that expired ten years after discharge, transferred money from a large class of young veterans who never reached a classroom to the Treasury. Both frames rested on the same documented pattern, with enrollment at approximately 95 percent of new recruits by the middle of the 1990s and use after service at 46 percent of participants by the end of 1993 on Department of Defense figures relayed in 1996.

The grievance also had an information component. The decision point arrived during entry processing, amid the dense sequence of briefings, examinations, and administrative elections that attend accession, at an age when many entrants held no settled plan for postservice education. Faith DesLauriers, legislative chair of the National Association of Veterans' Program Administrators and director of veterans affairs at Embry Riddle Aeronautical University, stated in 2008, during congressional consideration of the Post-9/11 GI Bill, that the entry point was the wrong time to ask an entrant to make a lasting decision about a later stage of life. The Post-9/11 GI Bill altered the terms of the debate after August 1, 2009, without resolving the position of members whose contributions had been collected under the older design and who never exhausted Chapter 33 entitlement. Legislative proposals across the life of Chapter 30 returned repeatedly to repeal of the pay reduction for future entrants, increases in the monthly rate, and extensions of the period for use, and none altered the treatment of contributions already collected. A member bought eligibility with 1,200 dollars of forgone pay, and the member who never used the benefit bore the full cost of the purchase without a refund, subject only to the death benefit and to the proportional refund earned at the far end of complete use of a successor program enacted a generation later.

The design can be reduced to a single set of terms covering the price, the purchase, the refund rules, the payment route, the use window, and the elections that bound a participant.

## What the program pays and who qualifies


The payment design of the Montgomery GI Bill departed sharply from the tuition-linked structure of the World War II era program. Under Chapter 30 of title 38, United States Code, the federal government paid a flat monthly educational assistance allowance directly to the eligible veteran or service member, in an amount fixed by statute and by the rate schedules published under section 3015. The institution attended received no payment from the Department of Veterans Affairs under the basic program. Tuition, fees, books, housing, and every other cost of attendance remained obligations of the student, who received the stipend and bore the difference whenever charges exceeded the allowance. The 1984 act therefore created a defined contribution toward education rather than a promise to meet the price of education, and every later debate over adequacy followed from that single structural choice.

### A flat allowance paid to the veteran rather than to the school

Because the allowance followed the individual and ignored institutional price, the benefit held constant across an enormous range of choices. A veteran enrolled full time at a public community college with modest tuition received the same monthly payment as a veteran enrolled full time at a private university charging several times as much, provided both veterans shared the same category of eligibility, the same length-of-service rate class, and the same rate of pursuit. The payment did not rise when tuition rose at a particular school between one academic year and the next. The payment did not fall when a veteran selected a low-cost program, transferred to a less expensive institution, or secured employment that covered part of the cost of attendance. Price risk rested entirely with the veteran. A veteran who chose an expensive program absorbed the full gap between the statutory rate and the institutional charge, month after month, for the duration of enrollment. A veteran who chose an inexpensive program retained the full allowance and could apply the surplus to rent, food, transportation, books, or debt reduction, because the statute imposed no accounting that tied expenditure of the allowance to particular educational charges.

That allocation of risk and surplus shaped behavior in ways legislators understood from the beginning. The flat design rewarded price sensitivity, since every dollar saved on tuition remained a dollar available for living costs. Administrative simplicity formed part of the attraction as well. Payment required certification of enrollment and satisfactory pursuit of an approved program, not an audit of institutional charges, and the Department of Veterans Affairs administered one national rate structure instead of thousands of institution-specific tuition determinations. The contrast with the Post-9/11 GI Bill enacted in 2008 threw the design into relief, because Chapter 33 paid tuition and fees to the institution up to statutory caps and paid a separate housing allowance calculated from local housing costs. Chapter 30, by contrast, folded tuition support and living support into a single undifferentiated monthly sum and left the division between the two entirely to the recipient.

Entitlement under the basic program amounted to 36 months of educational assistance, or the equivalent in part-time assistance, under section 3013. A month of full-time pursuit consumed a month of entitlement. Part-time pursuit consumed entitlement at a reduced rate corresponding to the rate of pursuit, so that half-time training stretched the same entitlement across a longer calendar span while drawing half the monthly payment. The 36 month figure corresponded, in the standard case, to four academic years of nine months each. Separate limits in section 3695 capped combined assistance under multiple federal veterans education programs at 48 months. Payment flowed only for periods of actual enrollment in an approved program, and entitlement unused at expiration of the eligibility period lapsed without conversion to cash or to any other benefit.

### Rates by training type and enrollment intensity

The rate schedules under section 3015 divided beneficiaries first by length of service and then by rate of pursuit. Veterans whose qualifying service rested on an enlistment of three years or more drew the higher full rate. Veterans whose entitlement rested on an enlistment of less than three years drew a lower rate, set at four fifths of the corresponding higher rate in the schedules in force during the second decade of the program. Within each service class, institutional training paid in proportion to enrollment intensity. Full-time pursuit paid the full monthly rate. Three-quarter-time pursuit paid three quarters of the full rate. Half-time pursuit paid one half of the full rate. Pursuit at less than half time but more than quarter time paid at the half-time rate, subject to a ceiling at the amount of tuition and fees charged, and pursuit at quarter time or less paid one quarter of the full rate, likewise subject to the tuition and fees ceiling. The schedules in force for training after September 30, 2017 illustrate the proportions with precision. The full-time rate stood at 1,928 dollars per month for the three year or longer class and at 1,566 dollars per month for the shorter enlistment class. Three-quarter-time pursuit paid 1,446 dollars and 1,174 dollars and fifty cents respectively, and half-time pursuit paid 964 dollars and 783 dollars respectively. One year later, for training after September 30, 2018, the full-time rate rose to 1,994 dollars per month for the longer service class, while the half-time rate rose to 997 dollars.

Apprenticeship and on-the-job training followed a declining schedule built on the expectation that trainee wages rose as skill increased. The monthly allowance for such training stood at 75 percent of the full-time rate during the first six months of pursuit, fell to 55 percent of the full-time rate during the second six months, and fell again to 35 percent of the full-time rate for the remaining pursuit of the program. Federal Register rate tables from the middle of the first decade of the twenty-first century display the schedule in dollar terms. For training after September 30, 2004, when the full-time institutional rate stood at 1,004 dollars, apprenticeship or on-the-job training paid 753 dollars per month in the first six months, 552 dollars and twenty cents in the second six months, and 351 dollars and forty cents thereafter. Correspondence training paid 55 percent of the established cost of the lessons completed, and flight training paid 60 percent of approved charges, with entitlement charged under statutory formulas rather than by calendar month alone. Cooperative training paid at the full-time rate applicable to the beneficiary class. Supplemental educational assistance under section 3021 allowed a military department to add as much as 300 dollars per month for members with critical skills or extended service, and the buy-up option allowed a contribution of as much as 600 dollars during active duty in exchange for as much as 5,400 dollars in additional total benefits.

### How much did the monthly benefit pay at different points in the program?

The full time Chapter 30 rate for a veteran with three or more years of service was 1,994 dollars per month for training after September 30, 2018, paid directly to the veteran for each month of enrollment, with lower proportional rates for part time study and a separate lower scale for shorter enlistments.

At the commencement of payments on July 1, 1985, the maximum monthly benefit stood at 300 dollars. That figure set the baseline against which every subsequent increase was measured. Department of Veterans Affairs statements from 2000 observed that 300 dollars covered about 70 percent of average college tuition at the time payments began, a comparison that framed later debates over erosion as tuition charges climbed faster than the statutory rate. Congress raised the maximum to 400 dollars through the Veterans Benefits Act of 1992, Public Law 102-568. The Transportation Equity Act for the 21st Century, Public Law 105-178, raised the rate to 528 dollars in 1998, and the Veterans Benefits and Health Care Improvement Act of 2000, Public Law 106-419, raised the rate to 650 dollars. The Veterans Education and Benefits Expansion Act of 2001, Public Law 107-103, then legislated a stepped series of increases rather than a single adjustment, setting the full-time rate at 800 dollars for months beginning on or after January 1, 2002, at 900 dollars for months during fiscal year 2003, and at 985 dollars for months during fiscal year 2004. Rate tables for training after September 30, 2004 record 1,004 dollars, reflecting the adjustment mechanism that followed the legislated steps.

During the 2007 to 2008 academic year, the full-time rate stood at 1,101 dollars per month. The National Center for Education Statistics, in a 2016 profile of service members and veterans in undergraduate and graduate education, recorded that figure as the amount available to a full-time student for living and education expenses combined during that academic year, citing Department of Veterans Affairs rate material from 2007. The figure implied a maximum of 9,909 dollars across a nine month academic year, regardless of tuition and fee charges. The Supplemental Appropriations Act, 2008, Public Law 110-252, the same statute that enacted the Post-9/11 GI Bill, raised the Chapter 30 monthly benefit to 1,321 dollars for fiscal year 2009. Annual adjustments tied to the Consumer Price Index carried the rate upward through the following decade, reaching 1,928 dollars for training after September 30, 2017 and 1,994 dollars for training after September 30, 2018 in the longer service class. Across the full span from 1985 to 2018, the nominal full-time rate therefore multiplied more than six times over, while the underlying architecture, a single national figure indifferent to institutional price, remained unchanged.

### Eligibility under Chapter 30 and the four categories

Eligibility rested on a combination of contribution, service, education, and character of discharge that applied across the program. The standard participant entered active duty for the first time after June 30, 1985, did not decline participation in writing at entry, and accepted a reduction of basic pay of 100 dollars per month during the first twelve months of service, producing a total contribution of 1,200 dollars. That contribution was nonrefundable in the ordinary course, and entitlement depended on completion of the qualifying service that the contribution anticipated. Two further requirements applied to veterans in every category. Separation had to rest on an honorable discharge, a standard that excluded separations characterized as under honorable conditions for purposes of basic entitlement, and the individual had to hold a high school diploma or an equivalency certificate, with twelve hours of college credit accepted in some cases under the governing rules. Service members could begin using benefits during active service after satisfying the applicable service requirements, and entitlement characteristically matured into its principal use after separation, when the delimiting period governed the time available for study.

The Department of Veterans Affairs organized eligibility into four categories that reflected the successive populations brought under Chapter 30. Category I covered the core population described in section 3011, individuals who first entered active duty after June 30, 1985 and completed the service pattern attached to the enlistment contract. Under the standard Category I route, continuous service of three years satisfied the requirement when the obligated period ran three years or longer. Service of two years satisfied the requirement when the individual had enlisted for less than three years. A third route, known as the two plus four program, combined two years of continuous active duty with four years of service in the Selected Reserve, entered within one year of leaving active duty, and section 3031 adjusted the start of the eligibility period for individuals whose entitlement depended on completion of that four year reserve requirement. Category II covered the conversion population drawn from the Vietnam era program, individuals who had entered active duty before January 1, 1977, who served at least one day between October 19, 1984 and June 30, 1985, who remained on active duty through June 30, 1988, or through June 30, 1987 under the corresponding reserve route, and who retained entitlement under Chapter 34 on December 31, 1989. Category III covered specified involuntary and incentive separations from the drawdown era, including individuals on active duty on September 30, 1990 who separated involuntarily after February 2, 1991, individuals involuntarily separated on or after November 30, 1993, and individuals who separated voluntarily under the Voluntary Separation Incentive or the Special Separation Benefit programs, each subject to a pay reduction of 1,200 dollars before separation. Category IV covered a later conversion election, principally individuals on active duty on October 9, 1996 who held funds in a Veterans Educational Assistance Program account on that date and elected Chapter 30 coverage by October 9, 1997, with a corresponding route for certain full-time National Guard members who had entered duty under title 32 between July 1, 1985 and November 28, 1989.

Reduced entitlement tempered the standard 36 months for certain early separations. Section 3013 provided one month of assistance for each month of continuous active duty for specified individuals separated for a service-connected disability, for a preexisting medical condition, for hardship, or involuntarily for the convenience of the government as a result of a reduction in force, when full entitlement under the standard routes had not attached. In every category, the architecture linked generosity to completed obligated service, and disputes over entitlement characteristically turned on documentary questions of entry date, obligated period, pay reduction records, and characterization of discharge rather than on educational criteria of any kind.

### The companion Selected Reserve program in Title 10

The Department of Defense Authorization Act, 1985, Public Law 98-525, carried a second educational assistance program alongside Chapter 30. Title VII of that statute amended the reserve educational assistance provisions then codified as Chapter 106 of title 10, with amendments effective July 1, 1985, and the program took shape as the Montgomery GI Bill Selected Reserve. A renumbering in 1994 redesignated the title 10 provisions as Chapter 1606, sections 16131 to 16137, and administration divided responsibility between the military departments, which determined basic eligibility, and the Department of Veterans Affairs, which paid the allowance. The program addressed a different personnel problem from Chapter 30. Reserve recruiting and retention depended on part-time service sustained over years, and an allowance payable during reserve service gave drilling reservists and National Guard members a benefit calibrated to continued participation rather than to separation.

Eligibility under Chapter 1606 required a six year obligation in the Selected Reserve. An enlisted member satisfied the requirement by enlisting, reenlisting, or extending an enlistment for not less than six years after June 30, 1985, with concurrent assignment to the Selected Reserve. An officer satisfied the requirement by agreeing to serve six years in the Selected Reserve in addition to any other obligated reserve service. The individual also had to complete initial active duty for training, hold a high school diploma or equivalency certificate earned before completion of that training, and remain in good standing in an active Selected Reserve unit. No contribution applied. Unlike the Chapter 30 participant, whose 1,200 dollar pay reduction formed part of the eligibility structure, the Selected Reserve participant made no buy-in payment, and entitlement rested on the service obligation and satisfactory participation alone.

Payment under Chapter 1606 replicated the flat model at a lower level. Section 16131 of title 10 stated base rates of 251 dollars per month for full-time pursuit, 188 dollars per month for three-quarter-time pursuit, and 125 dollars per month for half-time pursuit, each subject to percentage increases tied to the Consumer Price Index for each fiscal year. Adjusted schedules produced a full-time rate of 375 dollars per month for the period after September 30, 2017, and a full-time rate of 384 dollars per month after September 30, 2018, with a half-time rate of 191 dollars in the 2018 schedule. Maximum entitlement stood at 36 months, or the equivalent in part-time assistance, mirroring the Chapter 30 ceiling. Apprenticeship and on-the-job training under Chapter 1606 applied the same declining proportions used elsewhere in the Montgomery GI Bill structure, at 75 percent of the full-time rate in the first six months, 55 percent in the second six months, and 35 percent for the remaining pursuit. The temporal logic of the reserve program differed fundamentally from the active duty program. Entitlement under section 16133 expired on the date of separation from the Selected Reserve, as the statute stood after amendment by Public Law 110-252 in 2008 removed an outer limit that had stood at fourteen years from the date entitlement began. Exceptions preserved eligibility in defined cases, including separation for a disability not resulting from willful misconduct, specified unit inactivations and involuntary losses of Selected Reserve status during statutory windows that included the period from October 1, 2007 through September 30, 2014, and mobilization to active duty, for which the period of active duty service plus four months did not count against the expiration calculation.

### The ten year period for using Chapter 30 entitlement

Section 3031 imposed the delimiting rule that gave Chapter 30 its characteristic urgency. The period for using basic educational assistance expired at the end of the ten year period beginning on the date of the last discharge or release from active duty. A veteran discharged in 1995 therefore faced expiration in 2005 regardless of how few months of entitlement had been consumed, and entitlement remaining at expiration carried no value. A return to active duty followed by a later separation restarted the calculation from the later date. Special starting rules applied to defined groups. For individuals whose entitlement depended on the two plus four route under section 3012, the ten year period began on the later of the last discharge or release from active duty and the date the four year Selected Reserve requirement was met. For the Category II conversion population under section 3011, the period began on the later of the last discharge or release and January 1, 1990.

Congress and the statute together recognized circumstances in which strict application of the ten year clock would defeat entitlement through no failure of diligence. When a change, correction, or modification of a discharge by competent military authority first established eligibility that the original character of discharge had blocked, the ten year period did not run during the interval in which the individual was prevented from pursuing a program by reason of that discharge character. When a physical or mental disability, not resulting from willful misconduct, prevented initiation or completion of the chosen program, section 3031 provided an extension measured by the length of time the disability prevented pursuit, with the clock resuming on the first day after recovery when resumption was reasonably feasible. Captivity as a prisoner of war after the last discharge or release likewise suspended the running of the period. A term completion rule protected enrollment in progress, extending the period to the end of a quarter or semester when entitlement expired during that term, and to the end of a course when entitlement expired after completion of a major portion of a course at an institution operating on a different calendar. Medical evidence had to establish clearly that a program was medically infeasible during a claimed disability interval, and ordinary hardships of employment, family obligation, or course scheduling fell outside the statutory grounds.

The delimiting rule completed the logic that the payment structure began. A flat allowance of fixed duration, available inside a fixed decade after separation, rewarded prompt enrollment and steady pursuit, and the interaction of the three figures, the monthly rate, the 36 months of entitlement, and the ten year window, defined the benefit more completely than any single figure could. Legislators adjusting the program across the decades after 1984 worked almost entirely on the first figure, raising the monthly rate repeatedly while leaving duration and the delimiting period in original form, and the program operating in March 2019 differed from the program of July 1985 chiefly in the size of the check rather than in the architecture that determined who qualified, how payment flowed, and when eligibility ended.

## Amendments and the program's working life

The 1984 act began as a three year test, and permanence arrived quickly. The New GI Bill Continuation Act, Public Law 100-48, enacted on June 1, 1987, removed the June 30, 1988 termination date for Chapter 30 and confirmed the Montgomery GI Bill name in statute. An earlier section treated that fight in detail. The consequence was practical. Recruiters could offer the benefit without a sunset warning, and the Department of Veterans Affairs could administer Chapter 30 as a continuing entitlement rather than as a pilot approaching expiration. Congress placed no further general expiration date on the program before March 15, 2019, and legislative work after 1987 took the form of raising rates, widening eligible uses, adjusting administration, and, after 2008, managing coexistence with a more generous GI Bill.

### Rate increases that rebuilt the monthly payment

The original maximum of 300 dollars a month for a veteran with three years of service had limited purchasing power, and Congress returned to the rate repeatedly. The Veterans Benefits Act of 1992, Public Law 102-568, raised the maximum to 400 dollars, according to the Congressional Research Service primer on GI Bills enacted before 2008. The Transportation Equity Act for the 21st Century, Public Law 105-178, raised the rate in 1998. A Department of Veterans Affairs release put the full time rate for participants with three years of service at 528 dollars a month from October 1, 1998, up from 439.85 dollars, and the rate for two years of service at 429 dollars. Index adjustments preserved nominal value between statutes, but only a new public law moved the benefit by a large amount.

The Veterans Benefits and Health Care Improvement Act of 2000, Public Law 106-419, enacted on November 1, 2000, supplied the next large step. Reporting on the signing recorded an increase to 650 dollars a month for three years of service, a rise of 122 dollars, and to 528 dollars for two years of service, a rise of 99 dollars. President William J. Clinton described the increase at signing as the largest benefit increase in the history of the GI Bill to that date. The Congressional Research Service sequence placed the act in a clear line: 300 dollars at enactment, 400 dollars in 1992, 528 dollars in 1998, and 650 dollars in 2000. A fixed allowance lost ground fastest at institutions with charges well above a community college average, because tuition, fees, books, rent, and food did not follow the index path of the benefit.

Congress acted again within thirteen months. The Veterans Education and Benefits Expansion Act of 2001, Public Law 107-103, provided three scheduled increases, according to the Congressional Research Service primer. The maximum reached 800 dollars for months beginning on or after January 1, 2002, 900 dollars for months during fiscal year 2003, and 985 dollars for months during fiscal year 2004. A Department of Veterans Affairs release in January 2002 described the first step as a rise from a 672 dollar rate, reflecting intervening index adjustments to the 650 dollar rate, to 800 dollars. The release quoted Secretary of Veterans Affairs Anthony J. Principi linking the increases to concern about underuse and about adjustments failing to keep pace with college costs, and the department reported that about 60 percent of eligible veterans had used some benefit by January 2002.

The Supplemental Appropriations Act, 2008, Public Law 110-252, the same law that created the Post-9/11 GI Bill in Title V, raised the Chapter 30 maximum to 1,321 dollars for fiscal year 2009, according to the Congressional Research Service primer. Congress raised the Montgomery rate in the very statute that created a tuition based successor, and the older program did not end. After fiscal year 2009, annual adjustments under the consumer price index mechanism in section 3015 of title 38 carried the rate upward in smaller steps. By the schedule in force on March 15, 2019, the allowance had multiplied several times over the 300 dollar figure of 1985, with the exact amount varying by service, category, training time, and supplements on an individual record.

### How did the buy-up increase monthly payments?

The buy-up let an eligible service member on active duty contribute up to 600 dollars beyond the basic pay reduction. For full time training, every 20 dollars contributed added 5 dollars to the monthly Chapter 30 payment, so the maximum contribution added 150 dollars a month for 36 months, or 5,400 dollars in total.

Public Law 106-419 created that option for participants eligible under section 3011 and for participants eligible under section 3012 of title 38. The statutory text set a ceiling of 600 dollars, required contributions in multiples of 4 dollars, and directed deposit of amounts received into the Treasury as miscellaneous receipts. The Department of Veterans Affairs rate table translated the formula into practice. A 100 dollar contribution added 25 dollars a month at the full time rate, and a 300 dollar contribution added 75 dollars. The table reduced additions in proportion to training time. At three quarter time, a 600 dollar contribution added 112.50 dollars a month, and at quarter time the addition was 37.50 dollars. A Department of Defense form stated the formula in a second form: for each 4 dollars contributed, the participant received an additional 1 dollar per month for full time training.

Mechanics determined who could benefit. Contributions had to be made while the member remained on active duty, and the Department of Defense form warned that increased benefit option contributions were nonrefundable. A member who contributed and then elected the Post-9/11 GI Bill could not apply the buy-up addition to Chapter 33 payments, and the additional contribution itself was not returned. That treatment differed from the basic 1,200 dollar pay reduction. Under rules administered by the Department of Veterans Affairs, a participant who elected Post-9/11 benefits, exhausted that entitlement, and met the stated conditions could receive a refund of the basic pay reduction, in full where no Chapter 30 months had been used and in proportion to unused months at election. After August 1, 2009, the buy-up decision therefore turned on which chapter a member would elect, because the extra contribution produced value only inside Chapter 30.

### Flexibility added to a fixed monthly allowance

Public Law 106-419 widened the uses of an entitlement as well as the rate. The act added reimbursement for licensing and certification tests, capped at the lesser of 2,000 dollars or the fee charged for the test, with entitlement charged by dividing the amount paid by the full time monthly rate otherwise payable. The Veterans Benefits Act of 2002, Public Law 107-330, broadened the language to include a test to certify or license in a similar or related occupation. The change mattered because many military occupations led to credentials awarded through a single examination, and test reimbursement charged only a fraction of a month of entitlement for a typical fee.

The 2000 act also opened a conversion window for participants in the Veterans Educational Assistance Program, the contributory program that preceded Chapter 30. Section 104 authorized certain participants who had served continuously since October 9, 1996, through at least April 1, 2000, to enroll in Chapter 30 during a one year period, with pay reductions covering program costs.

Accelerated payment addressed a different defect, the mismatch between a modest monthly allowance and a short program charging high tuition at enrollment. The Veterans Education and Benefits Expansion Act of 2001 authorized accelerated payment for education leading to employment in the high technology industry, and Department of Veterans Affairs material placed coverage of up to 60 percent of the cost of qualifying courses in effect from October 1, 2002. The governing regulation stated the payment as the lesser of 60 percent of charged tuition and fees for the term, or the aggregate assistance to which the individual remained entitled. Guidance limited the option to high cost programs in fields that included life science, engineering, mathematics, and computer specialties, with certification of intent to seek employment in a listed industry, and tuition and fees had to exceed double the benefits otherwise payable for the term. Public Law 107-330 refined the phrase in 2002 to a high technology occupation in a high technology industry. The option created no extra entitlement.

Other flexibility provisions worked inside active service. Tuition assistance top-up allowed a service member using military tuition assistance under title 10 to draw on Chapter 30 entitlement for the portion of tuition and fees that tuition assistance did not cover, under the Chapter 30 regulations. That use charged entitlement and traded a post service benefit for a smaller immediate gain. Work study supplied separate support across several GI Bills for a participant enrolled at least three quarter time, through service at a Department of Veterans Affairs facility, a state approving agency, or another approved activity. Distance education, correspondence training, flight training, apprenticeship, and on the job training each carried a distinct payment rule, and amendments adjusted those rules at the margins instead of replacing the monthly allowance design. The program that emerged by 2008 was broader than the allowance enacted in 1984, but payment followed training time, entitlement lasted 36 months, and most benefits reached the veteran as a monthly amount rather than as payment of an institutional bill.

### Administration through the Department of Veterans Affairs and the services

Administration divided labor between the institution that recruited the member and the institution that paid the veteran. The services and the Department of Defense determined the service record on which eligibility rested, recorded the election at entry, and collected the basic pay reduction of 100 dollars a month for twelve months through military payroll systems. The services also transmitted personnel and pay data to the Department of Veterans Affairs, because a claim filed years after separation depended on records created at accession and at discharge. The Department of Veterans Affairs, through the Veterans Benefits Administration and its Education Service, decided claims, issued certificates of eligibility, calculated rates and entitlement charges, and paid the monthly allowance. Under Chapter 30, payment went to the veteran rather than to the school as tuition payment, a structural difference from the Post-9/11 GI Bill. A school could certify enrollment and receive no Chapter 30 tuition check, while the student carried responsibility for paying the bursar from the monthly allowance and other resources.

Schools performed the verification work that connected entitlement to payment. Each approved institution designated a school certifying official, who reported enrollment, training time, term dates, and changes in status. The department paid in arrears for certified training, prorated partial months, and charged entitlement according to training time and the special rules for tests, accelerated payment, and top-up. Trainees verified enrollment at the end of each month through the automated system administered by the department before payment continued. State approving agencies, funded through the readjustment benefits account, approved programs and conducted compliance activity. That structure predated the Post-9/11 GI Bill and carried into administration of Chapter 33 after August 1, 2009.

Defense funding entered through supplemental benefits and a dedicated fund. The Department of Defense Education Benefits Fund, established under title 10 in connection with the 1984 legislation, financed Selected Reserve benefits and kicker benefits for active duty members, according to audited financial statements for the fund for fiscal year 1993, which also described transfers to the veterans department and contributions from service and reserve appropriations. In practice, a kicker was a recruiting tool shaped by a service rather than a universal term of Chapter 30. A service could attach a college fund amount to an enlistment contract for a designated occupation, and the Army, Navy, and Marine Corps offered kickers in the early 1990s for difficult to fill specialties. The Department of Veterans Affairs paid the combined monthly amount on the award, but the supplement originated in defense policy and funding. Public Law 107-330 specified that payments from increased usage under the limited transfer authority in Public Law 107-107 were to be made from the fund or from Department of Transportation appropriations, as appropriate.

### Use, participation, and the limits of the evidence

Participation evidence requires a distinction between eligibility, use of at least some benefit, and exhaustion of 36 months. A measure of some use counted a veteran who took a single reimbursed test alongside a veteran who completed a degree. The January 2002 release stating that about 60 percent of eligible veterans had used some benefit therefore supplied a benchmark and a warning against treating nonusers as a single population. Some nonusers had used tuition assistance or employer training, and some had allowed the ten year period after discharge to lapse.

The Congressional Research Service primer reported a pattern that gives scale to the middle years. Beneficiaries in the active duty Montgomery program held moderately stable at around 290,000 from 1994 through 2001, rose from 2002 through 2008, and peaked at 354,284 beneficiaries in 2008. That peak coincided with the rate increases of 2000 and 2001, with wartime service in Afghanistan and Iraq producing new cohorts, and with the final period before Chapter 33 payments began. The Government Accountability Office offered a sharper measure for the transition. In GAO-13-567, published in 2013, enrollments and total payments for the active duty program decreased by 25 percent from fiscal years 2010 through 2011, while Post-9/11 payments and the number of student veterans served increased by more than 50 percent in fiscal year 2011. The office reported the explanation offered by Department of Veterans Affairs officials: veterans with a choice could be expected to choose the Post-9/11 GI Bill because of more generous benefits.

Annual benefits reports from the Department of Veterans Affairs extended that movement into the final years before March 15, 2019. The reports counted 47,307 Chapter 30 beneficiaries in fiscal year 2016, 34,582 in fiscal year 2017, and 26,441 in fiscal year 2018, against 755,476 Post-9/11 beneficiaries in fiscal year 2017 and 708,069 in fiscal year 2018. Those counts measured beneficiaries who received benefits in a fiscal year, not every living veteran holding unused entitlement. Thousands of veterans retained a right to use Chapter 30 within a delimiting period, and some gained an advantage from Chapter 30 because the monthly allowance could exceed the value of Chapter 33 at a low cost institution. Beneficiary data nevertheless pointed in one direction after August 1, 2009. New entrants weighed a monthly allowance against tuition payment, a housing allowance, and a books stipend, and the center of the GI Bill system moved to Chapter 33.

### A second GI Bill alongside Chapter 30

The Post-9/11 Veterans Educational Assistance Act of 2008, enacted as Title V of Public Law 110-252 on June 30, 2008, and effective for training on or after August 1, 2009, created Chapter 33 as a supplement rather than as a repeal of Chapter 30. A detailed account of Chapter 33 belongs to a companion article. For Chapter 30, three consequences mattered. First, an eligible veteran generally had to elect between the chapters, and an election of Chapter 33 in place of Chapter 30 was irrevocable under rules administered by the Department of Veterans Affairs. Second, the comparison favored Chapter 33 at institutions with high tuition, because Chapter 33 paid tuition and fees to the institution, a housing allowance, and a books stipend, while Chapter 30 paid one monthly allowance to the veteran. Third, Chapter 30 retained a place. A veteran could exhaust Chapter 30 and then receive up to twelve additional months under Chapter 33 in some circumstances under the aggregate limits in title 38, and a veteran at a low cost institution could retain more discretionary cash from a Chapter 30 allowance than from a Chapter 33 package tied to charges. Chapter 33 transformed Chapter 30 from the default GI Bill into an alternative requiring an affirmative comparison.

### The Colmery Act and Chapter 30

The Harry W. Colmery Veterans Educational Assistance Act of 2017, Public Law 115-48, enacted on August 16, 2017, and known as the Forever GI Bill, changed Chapter 30 mainly through provisions governing several GI Bills together, according to Congressional Research Service Report R45205, updated on May 16, 2018. The act gave the Forever name to a Post-9/11 change. Section 112 eliminated the fifteen year delimiting date for a Post-9/11 participant whose last discharge was on or after January 1, 2013, for a spouse using transferred benefits from such a participant, and for a Fry Scholarship recipient who first became eligible on or after January 1, 2013. The Congressional Research Service description identified no corresponding elimination for Chapter 30. A Chapter 30 participant remained subject to the ten year period after the last discharge, subject to statutory extensions, after August 16, 2017. Any account that transferred the Forever label to Chapter 30 misstated the act.

Other Colmery provisions reached Chapter 30 participants as members of the wider GI Bill population. Section 109 restored entitlement for a course or program that a participant could not complete for credit because a school closed or a course was disapproved, for closures and disapprovals on or after January 1, 2015, under the conditions in the act. Section 108 expanded test fee reimbursement to include national tests that evaluated prior learning and provided an opportunity for course credit at an institution of higher learning, effective August 1, 2018. Section 201 made permanent three work study activities first authorized for a limited period by Public Law 107-103: outreach services by employees of a state approving agency, care activities at Department of Veterans Affairs facilities and state homes, and activities related to administration of a national cemetery or a state veterans cemetery. Section 302 expanded the accredited independent study programs that could be approved, adding certificates from area career and technical education schools and postsecondary vocational institutions meeting the stated conditions.

Administration provisions also applied across programs. Section 301 raised mandatory funding for state approving agencies to 21 million dollars in fiscal year 2018 and 23 million dollars in fiscal year 2019 and thereafter, with a further authorization of 3 million dollars for fiscal year 2019 and each succeeding year subject to appropriations. Section 305 required the Department of Veterans Affairs to establish training requirements for school certifying officials at institutions with at least twenty GI Bill and vocational rehabilitation participants, effective August 1, 2018. Section 304 set a reporting fee of 15 dollars for each such participant from August 1, 2018, through July 31, 2020, under the technical correction in Public Law 115-62. Section 310 codified risk based surveys of approved programs. For a Chapter 30 trainee, those sections changed the oversight environment rather than the monthly rate or the 36 month duration. On March 15, 2019, Chapter 30 remained an entitlement with a ten year clock, a monthly allowance, and a beneficiary count in decline.

## The election, the counter-reading, and Rudisill

The arrival of a successor program in 2008 and 2009 did not repeal the 1984 act. Chapter 30 and Chapter 33 of Title 38 coexisted, and the coexistence generated a durable administrative problem in the life of the Montgomery program. A veteran could hold entitlement under the program, eligibility under the later program, or, through separate service, entitlement under both. The election determined which chapter paid, how many months remained after the choice, and whether any route back to the earlier entitlement survived the choice.

The frame for the account that follows is the state of the law on March 15, 2019. One later event falls outside that frame and is presented strictly as a dated event after it. On April 16, 2024, the Supreme Court decided Rudisill v. McDonough, and the decision altered the understanding of the single period rule for veterans with separate entitlements.

### The election and the price of finality

The general bar on duplication appeared in 38 U.S.C. 3322(a). An individual entitled to assistance under the Post-9/11 program who was also eligible under Chapter 30 or another listed program could not receive assistance under two or more such programs concurrently, and instead elected, in the form and manner the Secretary prescribed, under which chapter assistance would be received. Section 3322(d) directed that coordination between the later program and the earlier programs held as of August 1, 2009, the effective date of the Post-9/11 program, be governed by section 5003(c) of the 2008 act, later codified at 38 U.S.C. 3327.

The conversion election under section 3327 applied to an individual who met the requirements for the Post-9/11 program and who fell within one of the listed categories as of August 1, 2009, including an individual entitled to basic educational assistance under Chapter 30. The individual applied for education benefits, ordinarily on VA Form 22-1990 or the electronic equivalent, identified the later program as the program sought, identified the earlier program relinquished in lieu of which the later benefit was claimed, stated an effective date, and acknowledged the character of the election. The governing regulation, 38 C.F.R. 21.9520(c), described an irrevocable election made by properly completing the application form or by submitting a written statement containing identification information, the election statement, the desired effective date, and an acknowledgement that the election was irrevocable. The department then issued a certificate of eligibility stating the entitlement awarded.

Finality was the defining feature of that election. The irrevocability provision of section 3327 stated that an election under subsection (a) or subsection (c)(1) was irrevocable. Once a valid election had been made and processed, the relinquished Chapter 30 entitlement did not revive because the later benefit proved smaller than expected or because a course of study changed. Board of Veterans Appeals decisions after 2009 enforced that finality, and also marked its boundary, holding in some cases that a valid irrevocable election had not been made where the record lacked the required acknowledgement. Section 3327(h), added in 2016, authorized the Secretary, for an individual who on or after January 1, 2017, submitted an election the Secretary determined was clearly against the interests of the individual, or who failed to make an election, to make an alternative election on behalf of the individual, subject to notice within seven days and a thirty day period in which the individual could modify or revoke the election the Secretary had made. That window conferred no general right to unwind an election the individual had made.

The financial consequence of conversion was stated in section 3327(d). Under subsection (d)(1), an individual making an election received assistance under the later program instead of basic educational assistance under Chapter 30. Under subsection (d)(2), for an individual described in subsection (a)(1)(A), the months of entitlement under the later program equaled the months of unused entitlement under Chapter 30 as of the date of the election, plus any months revoked through a transfer related election under subsection (c)(1). A conversion election made after partial use therefore carried the unused balance across and no more. A veteran who first exhausted the full thirty six months under Chapter 30 and then claimed the later program could receive up to twelve additional months, because the aggregate cap described later in the section allowed forty eight months in total.

The choice was consequential for reasons beyond the month count. Chapter 30 paid a monthly allowance to the veteran, set by statute and adjusted by enrollment intensity, type of training, length of service, college fund supplements often called kickers, and any amount purchased through the buy up described later in the section. The veteran bore responsibility for paying tuition and fees from that allowance. The Post-9/11 program paid tuition and fees to the institution, within the limits applicable to public and private institutions, and paid a monthly housing allowance measured by the Basic Allowance for Housing for an E-5 with dependents at the location of attendance. Eligibility percentages tied to length of qualifying service after September 10, 2001, scaled the later benefit for many claimants, and transfer of unused entitlement to a spouse or dependent, available under the later program subject to Department of Defense criteria in 38 U.S.C. 3319, had no counterpart in Chapter 30. An election therefore exchanged a cash allowance for a package whose value depended on tuition charges, place of attendance, enrollment intensity, and eligibility percentage, and the exchange could not be reversed.

### Can the Montgomery program pay more than the later program?

In defined circumstances, the answer was yes. Where tuition charges were low or waived, where housing costs in the locality were modest, and where the later program paid tuition to the institution rather than cash to the student, the flat Chapter 30 stipend, retained in full, could exceed the combined practical value of tuition coverage plus housing support.

The counter-reading rested on the difference between paying a charge and paying a person. Tuition coverage has value only to the extent of the charge it discharges. At a public institution with low charges, or where a state program or an institutional waiver covered tuition, the largest component of the later package could shrink toward the amount of a modest bill while the Chapter 30 allowance arrived at the full statutory rate for the enrollment pursued. The cash character of the allowance also mattered. A stipend paid to the veteran could be allocated across tuition, housing, transportation, and subsistence according to the obligations of the household, whereas tuition paid to an institution and a housing allowance computed by formula addressed assigned categories and no others.

Housing supplied the second setting in which the counter-reading held. The housing allowance under the later program tracked the Basic Allowance for Housing for the locality of attendance, and in a locality with modest housing costs that measure produced a modest payment. Combined with low or waived tuition, the bundle could fall below the flat allowance. Study conducted entirely online sharpened the contrast. Under the rule applicable to online only training, the monthly housing allowance was based on one half of the national average housing allowance. A program of study with low charges, delivered entirely online, therefore paired a reduced housing component with a small tuition component, while the Chapter 30 allowance for the same enrollment followed the rate structure of the 1984 act without a corresponding online reduction.

Stated at its strongest, the counter-reading did not claim general superiority for the earlier program. The later program plainly delivered greater value across the broad run of cases involving substantial tuition charges and attendance in localities with substantial housing costs. The counter-reading claimed a narrower proposition. A benefit measured by actual charges and local housing rates transfers the most assistance to claimants facing the highest charges and the highest local rates, and transfers correspondingly less where charges and local rates fall at the low end. A flat allowance distributes without regard to either variable, and where both variables fell at the low end, the flat distribution could exceed the measured distribution. No individualized determination about any particular enrollment follows from that geometry alone, since entitlement percentages, remaining months, delimiting dates, and the conversion limit in section 3327(d)(2) each altered the result of an actual election.

### The aggregate cap and the single period rule

Above both programs stood 38 U.S.C. 3695, a provision that predated the 1984 act and that each program incorporated by reference. Section 3695(a) provided that the aggregate period for which any person could receive assistance under two or more of the listed provisions of law could not exceed forty eight months, or the part time equivalent. The list included chapters 30, 32, 33, 34, 35, and 36 of Title 38. Each of the two programs at issue conferred an entitlement of up to thirty six months. Thirty six months under one program plus thirty six months under another therefore produced seventy two months of nominal entitlement and forty eight months of payable assistance at most.

The cap coexisted with a separate administrative rule that governed a single period of service. Under the system administered through 2019, a veteran whose eligibility under both programs rested on a single period of qualifying service held one entitlement to be credited to one program or the other. Section 3322 barred concurrent receipt, and sections 3322(d) and 3327 supplied the conversion route. The administered position treated an application for the later benefit by a veteran with unused Chapter 30 entitlement as an election that relinquished the earlier entitlement and limited the later entitlement to the unused balance under section 3327(d)(2). Exhaustion before conversion preserved access to the additional months allowed by the forty eight month cap, while conversion before exhaustion confined combined use to thirty six months in total. One period of service generated one stream of thirty six months, payable under one program or, after a qualifying election, under the other in the amount of the unused remainder.

The premise drew support from the anti duplication purpose that ran through section 3322. The Department of Veterans Affairs maintained that Congress had barred double recovery for the same service, that the election mechanism was the prescribed route from the earlier program to the later one, and that a contrary reading would multiply benefits beyond the conversion terms Congress had written. Veterans challenging the administered position in cases involving separate periods of service answered that the anti duplication provisions barred concurrent payment and double credit for the same service, rather than forfeiture of a second entitlement earned by additional service, and that section 3695 supplied the only aggregate limit on combined use. The administered rule governed ordinary claims processing in the 2019 frame, and the separate entitlements question awaited authoritative resolution.

### The cohorts that kept the program in use

Enrollments under Chapter 30 persisted after August 1, 2009, for reasons grounded in eligibility history rather than in preference alone. The largest cohort consisted of veterans whose service had established entitlement under the 1984 act and who lacked qualifying service after September 10, 2001, sufficient to support entitlement under the later program. For that group, the program was the benefit the service had earned, funded in part by the pay reductions of one hundred dollars a month for twelve months that enrollment had required, and the absence of a later entitlement left no election to make. A second group held qualifying service for the later program and remained within the ten year delimiting period for Chapter 30 without having made a conversion election, and a substantial population carried unused months forward for years after separation.

The reserve components formed a further population with a distinct statutory footing. The Montgomery name extended beyond Chapter 30 to the Montgomery GI Bill Selected Reserve, codified in chapter 1606 of Title 10, and the coordination provisions in sections 3322 and 3327 listed chapter 1606 and chapter 1607 of Title 10 alongside Chapter 30 among the programs from which an individual could relinquish eligibility in electing the later benefit.

A third population had paid to enlarge the program. Under the buy up, a service member on active duty could contribute up to six hundred dollars to increase the monthly Chapter 30 allowance. The increase ran at one dollar of additional monthly benefit for every four dollars contributed, so that the maximum additional contribution produced up to one hundred fifty dollars a month in additional allowance and up to five thousand four hundred dollars across thirty six months of full time use. College fund kickers raised the value of the flat stipend, and both devices fed directly into the counter-reading described in this section.

### Rudisill and the separate entitlements holding

James Rudisill first enlisted in the United States Army in 2000 and served nearly eight years across three separate periods of service. The first period entitled Rudisill to thirty six months of benefits under the program. The second and third periods separately entitled Rudisill to thirty six months of benefits under the Post-9/11 program. Between periods of service, Rudisill earned an undergraduate degree financed in part with twenty five months and fourteen days of Chapter 30 benefits. Rudisill applied for the later benefit to finance graduate study at Yale Divinity School. The Department of Veterans Affairs issued a certificate of eligibility for ten months and sixteen days, the length of the unused Chapter 30 balance, treating the application as a section 3327 election subject to the limit in section 3327(d)(2). Rudisill maintained a different accounting. Two separate entitlements of thirty six months each, governed by the forty eight month cap in section 3695(a), left twenty two months and sixteen days of payable assistance after the Chapter 30 use.

The Board of Veterans Appeals affirmed the department. The United States Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims reversed in BO v. Wilkie, 31 Vet. App. 321 (2019). A Federal Circuit panel agreed, 4 F.4th 1297 (2021). The Federal Circuit then reheard the case en banc and, by a vote of ten to two in a decision reported at 55 F.4th 879 (2022), reversed the panel and sustained the department, on the ground that the application had constituted an election under section 3327(a)(1). The Supreme Court granted certiorari and, on April 16, 2024, reversed the judgment of the Federal Circuit in Rudisill v. McDonough, 601 U.S. 294 (2024). The decision was seven to two. Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice John Roberts and Justices Sonia Sotomayor, Elena Kagan, Neil Gorsuch, Brett Kavanaugh, and Amy Coney Barrett. Justice Kavanaugh concurred, joined by Justice Barrett, and Justice Clarence Thomas dissented, joined by Justice Samuel Alito.

The government maintained that section 3322(d) imposed mandatory coordination, that receipt of any benefit under the later program required an election under section 3327(a), and that such an election brought the limit in section 3327(d)(2) into force, confining combined benefits founded on the claim to thirty six months. Rudisill maintained that section 3322(d) did not apply to a veteran holding two separate entitlements, because such a veteran had no single entitlement to coordinate or convert, and that section 3327(a) created an optional mechanism whose consequences under subsection (d) attached only to an individual who actually made that election. On the Rudisill reading, declining the optional swap forfeited nothing, and the stated limits were the thirty six month limit within each program and the forty eight month cap in section 3695(a).

The Court held for Rudisill. The Court held that servicemembers who, through separate periods of service, accrued educational benefits under both the Montgomery program and the Post-9/11 program could use either one, in any order, up to the forty eight month aggregate benefits cap in section 3695(a). Section 3322(d) governed coordination of entitlement, and a veteran who held two entitlements and sought only to use one of them had nothing to coordinate. Section 3327(a) used permissive language for the swap election, and the consequences in section 3327(d) applied by their terms to an individual making an election under subsection (a). The bar in section 3322(a) prohibited concurrent receipt and required a choice of the chapter under which assistance would be received at a given time, and the Court treated that choice as distinct from the conversion election. Counsel for Rudisill estimated that the ruling could affect roughly 1.7 million veterans, an estimate the Department of Veterans Affairs disputed in reporting by the Associated Press. Federal officials cited in Military Times reporting placed the number affected far lower, at fewer than 30,000.

The decision changed the single period rule by confining the rule to the setting that had generated the conversion mechanism. For a veteran whose two eligibilities rested on a single period of service, the election under section 3327 remained the route for exchanging the earlier entitlement for the later one, with irrevocability and the unused balance limit attached to that exchange. For a veteran who had accumulated separate periods of service qualifying under both programs, the administered premise that an application for the later benefit automatically operated as a conversion election ceased to govern after April 16, 2024. Such a veteran held two entitlements, could draw on either in any order, and faced the forty eight month cap as the aggregate limit on combined use.

### What the settlement settled and what it left open

The settlement enacted in the 1984 act settled the peacetime question that had defeated earlier proposals for a permanent readjustment benefit. Service in an all volunteer force, without a declared war and without the mass mobilization that had carried the earlier GI Bills, would generate an earned education entitlement financed in part by the service member and administered as a standing program rather than as an emergency measure. The act settled the contributory form of that entitlement, the thirty six month measure, the linkage between length of service and the rate of assistance, and the place of the benefit in recruitment and retention policy.

The settlement left open the question that succession inevitably created. The 1984 act had been written for a world with one principal active duty education program, and the text supplied no complete answer for a force in which the same veteran could earn entitlement under two programs through different service. Congress addressed the overlap in 2008 through coordination and election provisions drafted for conversion, and the application of those provisions to separate entitlements divided the Board of Veterans Appeals, the Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims, the Federal Circuit sitting en banc, and the Supreme Court. Rudisill resolved that division on April 16, 2024, by reading the conversion provisions as provisions for a swap and the aggregate cap as the limit on combined use of separate entitlements. What the settlement had left open was the relation of that benefit to any successor earned by later service.

A reader tracing the program forward from the election problem reaches the successor first, in the pillar account of the [Post-9/11 GI Bill](/2019/03/01/post-911-gi-bill-2008-guide/), and then the direct comparison that governed so many individual files after August 2009, in the analysis of the [choice between the Montgomery and Post-9/11 programs](/2019/07/01/montgomery-vs-post-911-gi-bill/). The wider sequence that places the 1984 act between the Vietnam era entitlement and the tuition based successor appears in the [history of veterans legislation in the United States](/2019/07/15/us-veterans-legislation-history/), and the measured record of what education benefits delivered to the veterans who used the benefits appears in the study of [GI Bill education outcomes](/2019/06/01/gi-bill-education-outcomes/). ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the Montgomery GI Bill pay?

The program pays a flat monthly educational assistance allowance for up to thirty six months of approved training, paid for periods of actual enrollment. The original full time rate stood at 300 dollars per month for a veteran with three or more years of service when payments began on July 1, 1985, and legislation and index adjustments raised the rate repeatedly across the following decades. Part time pursuit pays a proportional share of the full time rate, and shorter enlistments draw a lower scale. Apprenticeship training, flight training, and correspondence training carry separate payment rules stated in the rate schedules. The allowance folds tuition support and living support into a single sum, and no separate housing allowance or books stipend forms part of the Chapter 30 payment.

### Q: Why does the Montgomery GI Bill cost 1200 dollars to join?

The contribution was a deliberate design choice carried forward in altered form from the savings program that preceded Chapter 30. Congress and the committee leadership treated a personal stake as a legitimate condition for a peacetime benefit, as a screen for commitment, and as a partial offset to federal cost at a moment when competing proposals carried estimates in the billions of dollars. Controlled test evidence had shown that a contribution did not destroy the enlistment effect of a sufficiently generous benefit, so the bargain kept the payment and raised the value: 100 dollars per month for the first twelve months against thirty six months of a stated monthly allowance. Amounts reduced from pay reverted to the Treasury under Section 3011 of Title 38 and were not treated as pay received by the member.

### Q: Can you get the Montgomery GI Bill buy-in refunded?

In the ordinary case, no. Chapter 30 contains no disenrollment refund, and amounts reduced from basic pay reverted to the Treasury rather than accumulating in a personal account. Two narrow routes return money. Section 3017 of Title 38 directs a payment after the service connected death of an entitled individual, measured by amounts paid in minus assistance already paid and distributed in a statutory order. The second route arrived with the Post-9/11 program: a person who elected that program in place of Chapter 30 and exhausted all entitlement under the later program could receive a proportional refund of the basic contribution, calculated from the Chapter 30 months remaining at the election and paid with the final housing allowance payment. Partial use of the later entitlement produced no refund.

### Q: Who does the Montgomery GI Bill pay, the school or the veteran?

Payment goes to the veteran. Under Chapter 30, the Department of Veterans Affairs pays the monthly allowance directly to the eligible veteran or service member, and the institution receives no payment from the department under the basic program. Tuition, fees, books, housing, and every other cost of attendance remain obligations of the student, who pays the institution from the allowance and other resources and bears the full difference whenever charges exceed the monthly amount. A veteran who selects a low cost program retains the full allowance and may apply the surplus to living costs, because the statute imposes no accounting tying expenditure to particular charges. That structure contrasts with the Post-9/11 program, under which tuition and fees are paid to the institution and a separate housing allowance is paid to the student.

### Q: How long do you have to use the Montgomery GI Bill?

Section 3031 of Title 38 sets a delimiting period of ten years beginning on the date of the last discharge or release from active duty. Entitlement remaining at the end of that period lapses without conversion to cash or to any other benefit. A return to active duty followed by a later separation restarts the calculation from the later date, and special starting rules apply to defined groups, including participants whose entitlement depended on completion of a four year Selected Reserve requirement. The statute provides extensions in defined circumstances, including a disability that prevented pursuit of a program, captivity as a prisoner of war, and a discharge correction that first established eligibility. A term completion rule protects enrollment in progress when the period expires during a quarter, a semester, or a course.

### Q: Was the Montgomery GI Bill a recruiting tool?

Recruiting formed a stated purpose of the design from the beginning. The declared purposes carried into Chapter 30 treated educational assistance as an instrument for attracting qualified personnel to the all volunteer force, the Department of Defense paid for the basic benefit as a cost of recruiting and retention, and the benefit was enacted inside a defense authorization act rather than in freestanding veterans legislation. Controlled test program evidence in the early nineteen eighties showed that a sufficiently generous education benefit raised enlistments of high quality recruits, and service leaders and recruiters identified the program as a leading recruitment instrument during the following decade. Enrollment among new recruits rose sharply after payments began, reaching levels far beyond the participation recorded under the savings program that Chapter 30 replaced.

### Q: Why is the Montgomery GI Bill in a defense authorization act?

The program was enacted as Title VII of the Department of Defense Authorization Act, 1985, Public Law 98-525, signed on October 19, 1984. Placement inside the annual defense authorization gave the measure a moving legislative vehicle, joined the jurisdictions of the veterans committees and the armed services committees in a single conference process, and connected the benefit directly to the defense budget that paid for the basic allowance as a recruiting cost. The operative provisions were nevertheless codified in Title 38, because the veterans agency administered the benefit as readjustment assistance and because earlier education benefit law already occupied that title. Funding source explained enactment placement, administering agency explained codification, and committee jurisdiction explained how both placements arose from a single legislative bargain.

### Q: Is the Montgomery GI Bill still available?

Chapter 30 remains a codified entitlement under Title 38 for persons whose service and contribution record satisfies the governing categories, and beneficiaries continued to receive payments under the program through the period covered by departmental reports before March 2019. Availability depends on the record rather than on a general enrollment offer: entry after June 30, 1985, completion of the 1,200 dollar pay reduction or an applicable conversion route, qualifying service, a high school diploma or equivalency, an honorable discharge, and use within the ten year delimiting period. Beneficiary counts fell sharply after the Post-9/11 program began paying benefits in August 2009, because veterans holding a choice between the chapters generally received greater value from tuition payment, a housing allowance, and a books stipend than from a single flat monthly allowance.

### Q: What is the 600 dollar buy-up and how does the extra contribution change the monthly payment?

The buy up is a voluntary supplement to the basic contribution, authorized by Sections 3011 and 3012 of Title 38 and created by legislation in 2000. A participant on active duty could contribute as much as 600 dollars beyond the 1,200 dollar pay reduction, in multiples of four dollars, before separation. Each four dollars contributed added one dollar per month to the full time rate, stated equivalently as five dollars per month for every twenty dollars contributed, so the maximum contribution added 150 dollars per month across thirty six months, a maximum added value of 5,400 dollars. Reduced training time reduced the addition in proportion. The added contribution was not refundable, produced value only through months of benefit actually paid under Chapter 30, and could not be applied to payments under the Post-9/11 program.

### Q: How does the Montgomery GI Bill pay for flight training and apprenticeship training?

Flight training and apprenticeship training follow payment rules distinct from institutional training. Flight training pays 60 percent of approved charges, with entitlement charged under statutory formulas rather than by calendar month alone, and the training must lead toward a vocational objective in aviation under the approval rules governing the program. Apprenticeship and on the job training follow a declining schedule built on the expectation that trainee wages rise as skill increases: 75 percent of the full time rate during the first six months of pursuit, 55 percent during the second six months, and 35 percent for the remaining pursuit. Correspondence training, by contrast, pays 55 percent of the established cost of lessons completed. Each rule preserves the basic architecture of Chapter 30, under which payment reaches the veteran for certified pursuit of an approved program.

### Q: What happens to Montgomery GI Bill eligibility after an early discharge?

Eligibility turns on completed obligated service, and an early separation generally reduces or defeats entitlement unless a statutory route applies. Section 3013 of Title 38 provides one month of assistance for each month of continuous active duty for specified individuals separated for a service connected disability, for a preexisting medical condition, for hardship, or involuntarily for the convenience of the government as a result of a reduction in force, where full entitlement under the standard routes had not attached. Character of discharge matters independently of length of service, because basic entitlement requires an honorable discharge. Disputes in such cases characteristically turn on documentary questions: entry date, obligated period, pay reduction records, reason for separation, and characterization of service, rather than on educational criteria of any kind.

### Q: Can Montgomery GI Bill benefits be transferred to a spouse or a child?

Chapter 30 contains no general transfer authority. Entitlement belongs to the veteran or service member whose service and contribution created the entitlement, and a spouse or child holds no right to receive Chapter 30 payments in the place of that individual. A limited transfer authority tested for certain career personnel under later defense legislation operated as a defined exception tied to retention, with payments charged under the stated conditions, rather than as a general feature of the 1984 design. Transfer of unused entitlement to a spouse or dependent is a feature of the Post-9/11 program under Section 3319 of Title 38, subject to Department of Defense criteria, and the absence of a counterpart in Chapter 30 formed one of the material differences weighed in an election between the two chapters.

### Q: What is the Montgomery GI Bill Selected Reserve program?

The Selected Reserve program is the companion benefit reshaped by the same 1984 statute: Title VII amended the reserve provisions then codified as Chapter 106 of Title 10, and a renumbering in 1994 redesignated them as Chapter 1606, Sections 16131 to 16137. Eligibility requires a six year obligation in the Selected Reserve, completion of initial active duty for training, a high school diploma or equivalency certificate, and continued service in good standing in an active Selected Reserve unit. No buy in applies: unlike a Chapter 30 participant, a Selected Reserve participant makes no pay reduction, and entitlement rests on the service obligation and satisfactory participation alone. Payment replicates the flat monthly model at a lower level, with a maximum of thirty six months, and entitlement generally expires on separation from the Selected Reserve, subject to exceptions for disability, unit inactivation, and mobilization.

### Q: Does the Montgomery GI Bill reimburse licensing and certification test fees?

Legislation in 2000 added reimbursement for licensing and certification tests to Chapter 30, and legislation in 2002 broadened the covered language to a test to certify or license in a similar or related occupation. Reimbursement is capped at the lesser of 2,000 dollars or the fee charged for the test. Entitlement is charged by dividing the amount paid by the full time monthly rate otherwise payable, so a typical fee consumes a fraction of a month of entitlement rather than a full month. The Harry W. Colmery Veterans Educational Assistance Act of 2017 expanded test reimbursement further, to include national tests that evaluate prior learning and provide an opportunity for course credit at an institution of higher learning, effective August 1, 2018. The provision mattered for military occupations that lead to civilian credentials awarded through a single examination.

### Q: How are Montgomery GI Bill monthly rates raised over time?

Rates rise through two mechanisms. Congress legislated large increases by statute at intervals: 300 dollars per month at the commencement of payments in 1985, 400 dollars under legislation in 1992, 528 dollars in 1998, 650 dollars in 2000, a stepped series reaching 985 dollars for fiscal year 2004 under legislation in 2001, and 1,321 dollars for fiscal year 2009 under the statute that also created the Post-9/11 program. Between and after those enactments, an adjustment mechanism in Section 3015 of Title 38 tied the rate to the Consumer Price Index, carrying the full time rate to 1,928 dollars for training after September 30, 2017, and to 1,994 dollars for training after September 30, 2018, in the longer service class. Legislators adjusted the monthly figure repeatedly while leaving duration and the delimiting period in original form.

### Q: What is the difference between Chapter 30 and the older Chapter 32 VEAP program?

The Veterans Educational Assistance Program, codified as Chapter 32, operated as a matched savings account. Participation was voluntary, monthly contributions ran between 25 dollars and 100 dollars, each dollar contributed drew a government match of two dollars, and the lifetime ceiling on personal contributions stood at 2,700 dollars, producing a maximum account of 8,100 dollars before any defense kicker. A participant who disenrolled could recover personal contributions, while the government match was never the property of the participant. Chapter 30 replaced the account with a defined bargain: a fixed reduction of 100 dollars per month for twelve months, no account balance, no disenrollment refund, and a flat monthly allowance paid as an entitlement for up to thirty six months. Participation under the savings design stayed low, while enrollment under Chapter 30 rose to encompass the large majority of new recruits.

### Q: Can a veteran change an election between the two GI Bill programs after making it?

An election the individual makes is final. The irrevocability provision of Section 3327 states that an election under subsection (a) or subsection (c)(1) is irrevocable, and the governing regulation, 38 C.F.R. 21.9520(c), requires the applicant to acknowledge that character on the application itself. Once a valid election was processed, the relinquished Chapter 30 entitlement did not revive because the later benefit proved smaller than expected or because a course of study changed. One narrow exception covers an election the individual did not personally make. Section 3327(h), added in 2016, permits the Secretary to make an alternative election on behalf of an individual who filed an election clearly against that individual's interests, or who failed to elect, subject to notice within seven days and a thirty day period in which the individual may modify or revoke the election the Secretary made.

### Q: What are service kickers and college funds under the Montgomery GI Bill?

Kickers are supplemental educational assistance funded by a military department and added to the basic Chapter 30 allowance. Section 3021 of Title 38 authorizes an increased monthly amount for an individual who holds a skill or specialty designated as one in which a critical shortage of personnel exists or for which recruitment is difficult, or, for critical units, where retention is difficult. Section 3022 authorizes supplemental assistance for additional service, and payment for the increases is allocated to the Department of Defense Education Benefits Fund or to Department of Homeland Security appropriations. Packaged combinations of the basic benefit plus a service funded supplement were marketed under service specific names, of which the Army College Fund was the most extensively advertised, alongside the Navy College Fund and the Marine Corps College Fund. A kicker attached to an enlistment contract for a designated specialty or a longer term of service.

### Q: Which training programs does the Montgomery GI Bill cover?

Entitlement may be used for approved programs of education under the approval system administered with state approving agencies under Chapter 36 of Title 38. Covered pursuit includes degree and certificate programs at institutions of higher learning, apprenticeship and on the job training under the declining schedule, flight training at 60 percent of approved charges, correspondence training at 55 percent of the established cost of lessons completed, and cooperative training at the full time rate applicable to the beneficiary class. Amendments widened use at the margins, adding reimbursement for licensing and certification tests, accelerated payment for qualifying high cost programs in high technology fields, and tuition assistance top up for a service member drawing on Chapter 30 for charges that military tuition assistance did not cover. Payment flows only for periods of actual enrollment in an approved program.

### Q: Why did Montgomery GI Bill use fall after the Post-9/11 program began?

Beneficiary counts peaked before the later program began paying. The Congressional Research Service counted 354,284 beneficiaries in the active duty Montgomery program in 2008, the final year before Post-9/11 payments started on August 1, 2009. The Government Accountability Office reported in GAO-13-567 that enrollments and total payments for the active duty program fell by 25 percent from fiscal years 2010 through 2011, while Post-9/11 payments rose by more than 50 percent in fiscal year 2011, and recorded the explanation offered by department officials: veterans with a choice could be expected to choose the program with more generous benefits. Department annual benefits reports counted 47,307 Chapter 30 beneficiaries in fiscal year 2016, 34,582 in fiscal year 2017, and 26,441 in fiscal year 2018.
