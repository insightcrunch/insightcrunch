The States Reorganisation Act, 1956, Act 37 of 1956, received the President's assent on 31 August 1956 and redrew the internal map of India from the appointed day, 1 November 1956. It is routinely described as the moment the linguistic principle triumphed: fourteen states and six union territories replaced the patchwork of Part A, Part B, Part C and Part D units inherited from the founding. That description is accurate as a list and misleading as an explanation. The claim defended here is sharper. The 1956 reorganisation worked because it was deliberately incomplete; the deferred cases (Punjab 1966, Bombay 1960, Northeast 1963 to 1972) were the price of implementability. Jawaharlal Nehru's government enacted the portion of the linguistic map that could be carried through Parliament and administered from 1 November 1956, and it consciously left outside the statute the three regional questions whose simultaneous settlement would have made the whole scheme ungovernable. Read through its enacted clauses alone, the Act looks like a principle fulfilled. Read through its silences, the Act is a piece of political engineering whose omissions were load-bearing.

## The States Reorganisation Act 1956 as a Statute, Not a Slogan

A statute can be tested against its own text, and the text of Act 37 of 1956 is unusually revealing about what its makers intended. The long title is plain: an Act to provide for the reorganisation of the States of India and for matters connected therewith. The machinery runs to eleven parts. Part One fixes the appointed day and definitions. Part Two, sections 3 to 17 in sequence, constructs each successor unit territory by territory, taluk by taluk in the hard cases. Part Three creates zones and zonal councils. Part Four reallocates representation in the Council of States and the House of the People and provides for delimitation. Part Five settles the high courts. Parts Six, Seven and Eight handle expenditure and the apportionment of assets and liabilities. Part Nine deals with corporations and inter-state agreements. Part Ten carries the services across the boundary changes. Part Eleven gathers the legal and miscellaneous provisions that keep courts, contracts and public employment functioning while the map moves underneath them. India Code, the Union government's own statutory repository maintained by the Legislative Department, records the Act as Act 37 of 1956, enacted 31 August 1956 under the Ministry of Home Affairs, and that dry registration matters: this was a Home Ministry statute, drafted as an instrument of administration, and its architecture is administrative before it is rhetorical.

That architecture is the first clue to the argument of this article. A statute written to celebrate a principle would linger on the principle. Act 37 of 1956 lingers instead on succession: which district goes where, which judges sit in which high court, which treasury bears which debt, which government servant wakes up on 1 November 1956 employed by which state. The linguistic principle supplied the public justification, and the States Reorganisation Commission's report of October 1955 supplied the map in outline, but the statute's drafters in the Ministry of Home Affairs wrote a succession instrument. The distinction explains the deferred cases. Where succession could be specified cleanly, the Act specified it, down to named taluks in section 4 and section 5. Where succession could not be specified without detonating a coalition the Union government needed elsewhere, the Act left the pre-1956 boundary standing and said nothing at all. Punjab's internal language frontier, Bombay city's ownership, and the hill districts of Assam were not oversights in a long statute. They were the spaces the statute was designed around.

The constitutional reading of the same transformation, the passage from administrative federalism to identity-based federalism under Article 3 of the Constitution, is traced in [the constitutional reading of the 1956 reorganisation](/2019/01/19/linguistic-reorganisation-constitutional-politics/), and this article deliberately stays on the other side of the line: the statutory and managerial side, where the question is not what Parliament may do to a state boundary but how a government sequences what it does so that the doing holds. That division of labour follows the tension the founding itself left open. The Constituent Assembly built a Union whose centre could redraw state boundaries with a state legislature's views solicited but not binding, a design governed by [the federal-unitary tension](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/) that runs through every later boundary dispute. The 1956 Act was the first full exercise of that power, and the Nehru government's handling of it set the template: maximum territorial change in a single statute, minimum simultaneous confrontation, and an unspoken promise that the unfinished business would be taken up case by case, on separate political timetables, in later Parliaments.

## A Founding Position Reversed: From the Dhar Commission to Andhra State

The founding generation had considered linguistic states and recoiled. The Linguistic Provinces Commission chaired by S. K. Dhar, appointed in 1948, reported in December 1948 against forming provinces on a linguistic basis, arguing that administrative convenience should govern and that language-homogeneous provinces would sharpen sub-national loyalties at a fragile moment. The Congress party's own JVP Committee, Jawaharlal Nehru, Vallabhbhai Patel and Pattabhi Sitaramayya, reported in April 1949 in the same direction, retreating from the party's pre-independence commitments and holding that the time was not ripe. The Constituent Assembly wrote a Constitution on that assumption: the First Schedule listed states whose boundaries were the accidental deposit of British provinces and integrated princely territories, not language communities. When the States Reorganisation Commission revisited the question five years later, it was reopening a file the founders had closed twice, and everyone in the Nehru cabinet of 1953 understood that reopening it required a political trigger stronger than a commission's reasoning.

The trigger was Andhra. Potti Sreeramulu began his fast for a Telugu-speaking state in October 1952 and died on 15 December 1952, on the fifty-sixth day by the count kept by his supporters; the violence that followed in the Telugu districts of Madras forced Nehru's hand within days. Andhra State came into existence on 1 October 1953, carved from Madras with its capital at Kurnool, and the precedent was instantly general: if Telugu speakers had won a state by mobilisation and martyrdom, no government could credibly deny a hearing to Kannada, Malayalam, Marathi and Gujarati demands organised on the same lines. Nehru announced in the Lok Sabha on 22 December 1953, in the statement later quoted by the Supreme Court in Babulal Parate v State of Bombay (1960), that a commission would examine the reorganisation question objectively and dispassionately, with the welfare of each constituent unit and of the nation as the stated test. The States Reorganisation Commission was appointed by a resolution of the Ministry of Home Affairs dated 29 December 1953, with Fazl Ali, a former judge of the Supreme Court and former Governor of Orissa, as chairman, and H. N. Kunzru and K. M. Panikkar as members.

The reversal between 1949 and 1953 is often told as Nehru's change of heart, and the seed of the story is real: the Nehru of the JVP report had treated linguistic provinces as a danger to consolidation; the Nehru of December 1953 commissioned the machinery that produced them. But the implementation reading gives the reversal a harder edge. Nehru did not convert to the linguistic principle as a doctrine. He accepted, after Andhra, that the demand could no longer be contained by refusal, and he moved the question from the street into a commission room, where its timetable, its evidence and its terms of reference belonged to his government. The two years the Fazl Ali Commission spent touring, hearing memoranda and drafting gave the Union government something the agitation calendar never would: sequence control. Which cases the Commission recommended, which recommendations the cabinet accepted, which the Bill embodied and which the Act of August 1956 silently dropped form a single chain of decisions, and the deferred cases were decided at every link of that chain, not stumbled into at the end. Patel, whose integration of the princely states had fixed the very units being reorganised, had died on 15 December 1950; [Patel's princely-states integration as the precedent](/2018/12/17/patel-nehru-ambedkar-founding-triangulation/) for centrally managed territorial change survived him as method even though the man who might have forced a harder, faster settlement was gone.

## The Fazl Ali Commission and the Report of October 1955

The States Reorganisation Commission submitted its report on 30 September 1955; the report was published and debated through October 1955 and after, and it remains the intellectual foundation on which the Act rests. Fazl Ali, Kunzru and Panikkar accepted language as a legitimate and powerful basis for state boundaries and rejected, in the same breath, the doctrine of one language, one state as an absolute rule. The Commission weighed four considerations together: linguistic and cultural homogeneity, the preservation of the unity and integrity of India, administrative and financial viability, and the requirements of economic planning. That fourfold test is the report's most consequential sentence-level achievement, because it converted a maximalist demand into a set of trade-offs a government could administer. A proposed state could be linguistically coherent and still fail on viability; a boundary could be administratively tidy and still fail on unity. The Commission's map, sixteen states and three union territories in its original scheme, was therefore already a negotiated object before any politician touched it.

The report's specific dispositions set up the Act's deferrals with precision. For Punjab, the Commission declined to recommend a Punjabi Suba, judging that the demand did not command the general support of the Punjabi-speaking population read as a whole and that the linguistic and religious questions in Punjab were entangled in ways a boundary could not cleanly settle. For Bombay, the Commission proposed a composite arrangement that kept Marathi and Gujarati speakers in joined structures in modified form, with Bombay city treated in a manner that satisfied neither the Samyukta Maharashtra movement nor the Mahagujarat movement completely. For the Northeast, the Commission recommended maintaining Assam substantially as it stood, with the hill districts handled inside the Sixth Schedule framework rather than through new states. Each of these dispositions can be read as prudence or as postponement, and the two readings of the whole 1956 settlement grow from exactly this ambiguity. What the record shows is that the Commission, the cabinet and Parliament each had the opportunity to force the three cases, and each institution, at its own stage between October 1955 and August 1956, chose the disposition that left them open.

Granville Austin, in Working a Democratic Constitution (1999), his history of the Constitution's working from 1950 to 1985, treats the reorganisation as a test the democratic framework passed: a fundamental restructuring of the Union's units accomplished through commission, cabinet, parliamentary debate and statute, without a single boundary imposed by decree. The implementation reading developed here adds a colder observation to Austin's institutional one. The process Austin praises was able to remain process, orderly, deliberative and statutory, precisely because the three cases most likely to produce sustained violence or a parliamentary revolt were kept off the operative clauses. Deliberation and deferral were not alternatives in 1955 and 1956. Deferral was the condition that kept deliberation possible.

## The Cabinet's Winter: From the Report of October 1955 to the Bill of April 1956

The six months between the report's submission and the Bill's introduction are the least narrated and most decisive stretch of the whole sequence, because they are where the report's map was converted into the government's map and where the deferrals were confirmed as policy rather than inherited as recommendations. The Congress Working Committee and the cabinet debated the report through the winter of 1955 and 1956 against a background of street mobilisation that rose each time a specific disposition leaked: Bombay saw its worst violence of the decade in January 1956, after Nehru indicated in public discussion that Bombay city might be constituted a centrally administered territory for a transitional period, a formulation the Samyukta Maharashtra movement read as theft of the capital and Gujarati opinion read as a threat deferred rather than removed. The cabinet's handling of Vidarbha in the same weeks shows the method at work on a smaller case. A separate Vidarbha state had advocates in the Commission's deliberations and in the Nagpur Congress tradition reaching back to the Nagpur Pact discussions of the era; the government weighed it, priced the precedent it would set for other sub-regional claims inside the enlarged Maharashtra, and folded Vidarbha into the composite Bombay instead, where it joined Marathwada as a Marathi region whose grievances would be managed inside a state rather than by statehood. Each winter decision of this kind narrowed the Bill: what reached the House of the People on 18 April 1956 was not the Fazl Ali map with amendments but a second, harder map, drawn by politicians who had counted the votes in the House, the ministries in the states and the crowds in Bombay, Patiala and Ahmedabad, and who had struck from the operative scheme every case whose numbers did not add up.

Govind Ballabh Pant's role in the winter deserves separate record because it institutionalised the deferral method. As Home Minister, Pant ran the consultations with the Akali leadership that produced the Regional Formula, managed the Congress chief ministers whose states were being divided under them, and held the Bombay file through the Joint Select Committee. Pant's political style, committee patience, a preference for formulas that gave every delegation something to carry home, was the administrative personality of the pragmatist reading. Nehru supplied the criterion, implementability; Pant supplied the machinery of formulas, committees and transitional arrangements by which the criterion was applied case by case. When the adjudication section of this article distinguishes strategic from forced deferral, the distinction rests substantially on the winter record: a government improvising under pressure does not produce, in a single season, a regional formula for Punjab, a transitional-territory proposal for Bombay city, a safeguards agreement for Telangana and a staged Bill for the rest. It produces those things when postponement has been elevated, deliberately, into its method of government for the question at hand.

## The Nehru and Fazl Ali Correspondence: Implementability as an Instruction

The least-used primary source for the 1956 settlement is the correspondence between Nehru and Fazl Ali exchanged during the Commission's working life from 1953 to 1955, preserved in the Nehru Papers at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library in New Delhi. The exchange matters because it documents, in the principals' own hands, the criterion that governed the whole exercise before a single recommendation was published. Nehru's letters to the chairman return to a single demand: the Commission's scheme had to be one a government could actually carry. A map that was principled in the abstract and impossible in the state legislatures, in the Congress party's provincial committees, or in the streets of Bombay and Patiala was, for the Prime Minister, a failed map regardless of its symmetry. Robert King, in Nehru and the Language Politics of India (1997), the specialist study of Nehru's handling of the language questions, reconstructs this posture from the published record: Nehru treated reorganisation as a problem of governability to be managed across years, not a principle to be consummated in a single settlement. The correspondence in the Nehru Papers is the archival layer beneath King's reading, and it converts the pragmatist interpretation from an inference into a documented instruction.

### What did Nehru ask Fazl Ali to prioritise in the States Reorganisation Commission's work?

Nehru asked Fazl Ali to prioritise political implementability: a scheme the Union government could carry through Parliament and administer without breaking its governing coalition or the public order of the affected regions. In the correspondence preserved in the Nehru Papers, recommendations were to be weighed against unity, viability and regional consent, not deduced from the linguistic principle alone.

The significance of that instruction becomes visible only against the alternative the Commission might have followed. A principled-purist commission, applying one language, one state as a rule, would have drawn a Punjabi-speaking state in 1955, separated Maharashtra and Gujarat with a disposition for Bombay city in the same instrument, and faced the Naga, Mizo and Khasi-Jaintia hill questions as statehood questions immediately. Each of those drawings had a constituency; each also had an armed or agitating counter-constituency, an unsettled religious or metropolitan complication, and a Congress organisation in the affected region split down the middle. By instructing the Commission toward the implementable, Nehru was not asking Fazl Ali to falsify the inquiry. He was defining the question the inquiry answered: not what map does the linguistic principle logically require, but what map can this Union, in this decade, with these parties and these movements, actually bring into force and hold. The deferred cases are the residue of that definition. They are the points where the implementable map and the principled map diverged, and at every such point, from the report of October 1955 to the Act of 31 August 1956, the implementable map won.

A second feature of the correspondence deserves emphasis because later sections of this article rely on it. Nehru's implementability criterion was temporal as much as territorial. The letters treat the 1956 settlement as a stage, with the hardest cases expressly contemplated as later business, to be taken up when the political facts had ripened or when a regional settlement had been negotiated to the point of enactability. That is why the eventual resolutions, Punjab in 1966, Bombay in 1960 and the Northeast across 1963 to 1972, should not be read as the 1956 Act failing and being corrected. They are the second and third tranches of a sequence whose first tranche was designed, at the level of the Prime Minister's own correspondence, to leave room for them. The design intention is documented before the event, which is what separates strategic deferral from the ordinary political habit of postponing embarrassment, a distinction the adjudication section of this article presses against the strongest counter-reading.

## What the Act Enacted: Fourteen States, Six Union Territories, One Appointed Day

From the appointed day, 1 November 1956, the Union consisted of fourteen states and six union territories, and the Constitution (Seventh Amendment) Act, 1956, effective the same day, deleted the Part A, Part B, Part C and Part D classification from the First Schedule so that the constitutional text and the statutory map changed together. Part Two of Act 37 of 1956 built the fourteen states one section at a time. Section 3 formed the enlarged Andhra Pradesh by uniting Andhra State, created in 1953, with the Telugu-speaking districts of Hyderabad State, the territory known as Telangana. Section 4 transferred the southern taluks of Travancore-Cochin, Thovala, Agastheeswaram, Kalkulam, Vilavancode and the Shencottah area in substance, to Madras, honouring the Tamil-speaking claim on the far south. Section 5 then formed Kerala from the remainder of Travancore-Cochin together with the Malabar district of Madras and the Kasaragod taluk of South Kanara. Section 6 separated the Laccadive, Minicoy and Amindivi Islands from the mainland districts and constituted them a union territory. Section 7 built the enlarged Mysore from the old Mysore state, the Kannada districts of Bombay (Belgaum apart from Chandgad taluka, Bijapur, Dharwar and Kanara), the Kannada districts of Hyderabad (Gulbarga, Raichur and Bidar with specified taluks excepted), South Kanara apart from Kasaragod, Kollegal from Madras, and Coorg. Section 8 formed the composite Bombay described in detail in a later section. Section 9 consolidated Madhya Pradesh from Madhya Bharat, Vindhya Pradesh, Bhopal and the old Central Provinces and Berar minus the Marathi districts assigned elsewhere. Section 10 formed Rajasthan from the existing state with Ajmer, the Abu Road taluka of Bombay and the Sunel enclave added and Sironj surrendered. Section 11 united the Patiala and East Punjab States Union with Punjab. The remaining sections carried Bihar, West Bengal, Assam, Orissa, Uttar Pradesh and Jammu and Kashmir through boundary adjustments of a smaller order, West Bengal gaining the Purulia portion of Manbhum district from Bihar among them.

The six union territories, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Delhi, Himachal Pradesh, the Laccadive, Minicoy and Amindivi Islands, Manipur and Tripura, mark the other end of the Act's judgement about readiness. Manipur and Tripura were princely states integrated in 1949 and administered as Part C units; the Act kept them under central administration rather than graduating them to statehood, a decision whose consequences for the Northeast are taken up in the deferred-case section devoted to that region. Himachal Pradesh, assembled from the Punjab hill states in 1948, likewise remained a union territory, its statehood deferred to 1971. Delhi's position as the capital territory was continued without a legislature of the kind the Part C arrangement had supplied. In each instance the Act applied the same test visible in the Nehru and Fazl Ali correspondence: statehood followed demonstrated administrative and political capacity to sustain it within the settlement's overall balance, and where the test was failed or the question was entangled with a larger unsettled one, the territory waited.

### Why was Telangana merged into Andhra Pradesh in 1956 despite the Commission's reservations?

The Commission recorded reservations about an immediate merger and contemplated a separate Hyderabad state with an option to join Andhra after the general elections expected in 1961. The cabinet overrode the hesitation after the Gentlemen's Agreement of 20 February 1956 settled Telangana safeguards on public employment, development expenditure and ministerial representation, and the merger proceeded in section 3.

The Telangana disposition is the clearest proof that the Act's deferral logic was selective rather than timid. Here was a case where the commission of inquiry counselled delay and the government chose enactment, because a negotiated regional compact existed and because the Congress organisations of both units were inside the bargain. In Punjab, by contrast, no equivalent compact with the Akali Dal existed in 1956 that the cabinet judged durable, and in Bombay the two movements demanded opposite dispositions of the same city. The pattern across the three classes of case, compact and enactment in Andhra, compact attempted and rejected as insufficient in Punjab, and no compact at all in Bombay, is the implementation dimension in its purest form. The statute's map is a map of which bargains had been closed by August 1956, not a map of which claims were just. Readers working through the States Reorganisation Commission's report and the Act's schedules side by side, in the printed volumes or in slide form through [the ReportMedic presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), can watch that pattern emerge section by section: wherever a political settlement preceded the clause, the clause is detailed and final; wherever none did, the clause is absent and the old boundary stands.

Granville Austin's centre-state reading in Working a Democratic Constitution (1999) adds the administrative sequel. The Ministry of Home Affairs, which had drafted the Bill, supervised the succession after 1 November 1956: the transfer of territories, the allocation of services, the division of assets and the constitution of new legislatures and high courts ran through the ministry's states desk, and the smoothness of that succession in the enacted cases became the government's standing argument that sequencing, not coverage, was the right measure of the Act. The fourteen-state settlement held without a single enacted boundary collapsing back into agitation at the all-India scale, which is the factual floor beneath the pragmatist reading. What did not hold were the three arrangements the Act had either constructed as temporary, in Bombay, or declined to touch, in Punjab and the Northeast, and it is to those that the argument turns.

## The Drafters' Hand: Part Two Read Closely

Part Two of the Act rewards a slow reading, because its sequence is an argument. The drafters ordered the state-forming sections to begin with the cases of maximum consent and to end with the cases of maximum delicacy, and the order tracks the politics of 1956 with embarrassing fidelity. Andhra Pradesh comes first, in section 3, the case with a negotiated compact and a precedent already running since 1953. Kerala and Madras follow in sections 4 and 5, transfers negotiated between Congress governments. The island territory of section 6 required no negotiation at all. Mysore in section 7 gathered Kannada districts from three parent units, a complex assembly that succeeded because every affected chief minister was inside the Congress and inside the bargain. Only after seven sections of enacted consent does the reader reach Bombay in section 8, the composite; Madhya Pradesh in section 9, a consolidation of Hindi-speaking units whose merger raised questions of capital and precedence, settled by placing the capital at Bhopal, rather than questions of belonging; Rajasthan in section 10; and Punjab in section 11, where the Act's operative content shrinks to a merger and a silence. The sections that follow carry the continuing states through their adjustments in descending order of disturbance, until Assam's near-total continuity closes the part. A reader who knew nothing of the politics could reconstruct its outline from the statute's own gradient of specificity: where the text names taluks, consent existed; where the text falls silent on the question everyone was asking, it did not.

The drafting technique inside the sections carries the same lesson. Territory is transferred by reference to districts and taluks as they stood on specified dates, with the First Schedule's tables doing the constitutive work and the sections incorporating them; the technique made the Act proof against the boundary micro-disputes that a narrative description would have invited, and it made the appointed day administrable, because a district collector could determine his state from the schedule without awaiting interpretation. The contrast with the deferred cases is instructive once more. The Punjabi Suba question could not be drafted in this technique at all, because the underlying fact, which district was Punjabi-speaking, was itself the dispute, the 1951 census language returns having been poisoned by competitive instruction. Bombay city could not be scheduled because its disposition was a single indivisible award. The Naga and Mizo hills could not be scheduled because the Indian state's writ there ran through exception. The drafters' silence in each case was therefore not a failure of technique but its limit, honestly reached, and the Act's decision to proceed without those cases rather than await a technique that did not exist is the statutory form of the implementability criterion.

## The Machinery Beneath the Map: Zones, Legislatures, Courts, Assets and Services

The parts of Act 37 of 1956 that no summary ever quotes are the parts that made the settlement a working system rather than a proclamation, and they repay attention because they show the Nehru government's implementation method at its most characteristic. Part Three constituted five zones, Northern, Central, Eastern, Western and Southern, and established a zonal council for each: the Union Home Minister as common chairman, the chief ministers of the states in the zone, two ministers nominated from each state and the administrators of union territories in the zone, meeting in an advisory capacity on economic and social planning, border disputes and inter-state transport. The zonal councils were the Act's built-in machinery for the friction the new boundaries would generate, an institutional admission that linguistic states would quarrel and that the quarrels needed a forum short of the Supreme Court or the street. Their advisory weakness was deliberate; Nehru's design left authority with the cabinets and gave the councils the function of habitual consultation, federalism practised as routine rather than asserted as doctrine. The design sits comfortably inside [the asymmetric-federal design](/2019/01/03/federalism-indian-constitution-explained/) the Constitution already permitted, in which uniformity of form coexists with wide variation in arrangement and capacity among the units.

Part Four redistributed representation to match the new map. The Fourth Schedule's allocation of seats in the Council of States was rewritten for the fourteen states, seats in the House of the People were reallocated, the composition of each legislative assembly and legislative council was specified state by state, sitting members were assigned to successor legislatures so that no state faced its first day unrepresented, and delimitation machinery was provided to convert the allocations into constituencies. Part Five performed the same operation for the judiciary: high courts were constituted or continued for the new states, Kerala at Ernakulam, Mysore at Bangalore, Madhya Pradesh at Jabalpur, Rajasthan at Jodhpur, with jurisdiction, judges and pending proceedings transferred by rule rather than left to litigation. Part Six authorised expenditure from the consolidated funds of the successor states for the transitional period. Parts Seven and Eight apportioned the assets and liabilities of the divided Part A and Part B states and of the Union itself, treasuries, public debt, contracts and pension obligations included, and Part Nine preserved the functioning of corporations and inter-state agreements across the boundary changes, so that an irrigation agreement or a state electricity board did not lapse because the map had moved. Part Ten protected the human machinery: members of the All India Services and of the state services were allocated among successor states with conditions of service protected, and Part Eleven carried pending legal proceedings, contracts and liabilities across the appointed day.

This machinery is easily skipped and should not be, because it is where the deferred-case strategy acquired its margin of safety. A government that had written succession machinery this complete for the enacted cases could credibly promise the deferred regions a later statute of the same quality, and could equally credibly argue, in the August 1956 debates and after, that forcing Punjab, Bombay city and the Assam hills into the same instrument would have overloaded machinery that was already processing the largest territorial succession in the Union's history. The implementation argument is cumulative: commission, correspondence, enacted map, machinery, and only then the three silences, each of which the following sections examine on its own evidence.

## Deferred Case One: Punjab, Where Language and Religion Could Not Be Unbraided

The Punjabi Suba demand had been pressed by the Shiromani Akali Dal since 1948, within months of independence, and it presented the Nehru government with the one case in India where the linguistic map and the religious map claimed the same territory in incompatible ways. A Punjabi-speaking state drawn by language would be a state with a Sikh majority in its Punjabi-speaking districts and a very large Hindu minority whose spokesmen, organised through the Arya Samaj tradition and the Hindi movement in the state, denied in large numbers that Punjabi in the Gurmukhi script was their mother tongue at all. The census returns for language in Punjab were therefore themselves a battlefield: the 1951 census recorded mother tongues in patterns that both camps denounced as instructed rather than reported, Hindi-speaking Hindus returning Hindi and Punjabi-speaking Hindus pressed to return Punjabi. The Sachar Formula of 1949, named for Chief Minister Bhim Sen Sachar, had tried to settle the language-in-education question by dividing the state into Punjabi and Hindi zones for schooling purposes, and its failure to still the agitation taught the Union government the lesson the Fazl Ali Commission later ratified: in Punjab, a boundary was not a settlement technology, because the dispute was over which community the state would belong to, expressed in the vocabulary of language.

The Commission's refusal to recommend a Punjabi Suba rested on its finding that the demand lacked the general support of the Punjabi-speaking region read as a whole, which the Akali Dal read, with reason, as the Commission counting the opposition of Hindi-speaking Punjab against a demand that was never addressed to them. The Nehru government's handling ran on a separate track from the Commission's. Through 1955 the Akali Dal's morcha for the Suba filled Punjab's jails with volunteers courting arrest after the state government banned the raising of Punjabi Suba slogans in May 1955, a ban lifted as the agitation grew; negotiations between the Union government and Akali leaders, with Master Tara Singh the dominant Akali figure and Giani Kartar Singh central to the drafting, produced the Regional Formula of 1956. The formula divided the enlarged Punjab into Punjabi and Hindi regions for legislative purposes, with regional committees of the single state legislature empowered over a scheduled list of subjects, and development and language safeguards for each region. The Akali Dal's working bodies accepted the formula in 1956 as an instalment, the Congress sold it as a settlement, and the Act proceeded on that basis: section 11 merged the Patiala and East Punjab States Union into Punjab, and the internal question was left to the formula's committees rather than to a new state boundary.

### What did the merger of PEPSU into Punjab settle, and what did it leave open?

The merger settled the administrative anomaly of the Patiala and East Punjab States Union, absorbed into a single Punjab under section 11: one legislature, one services cadre, one high court at Chandigarh. It left open whether the Punjabi-speaking region would become a state of its own, a question the Regional Formula postponed through regional committees inside the undivided state.

The deferral's price and logic both showed over the following decade. The Regional Formula's committees met, but the formula's opponents in the Hindi region and its disappointed supporters in the Punjabi region treated it as a truce rather than a settlement, and the Akali Dal returned to the demand for a Punjabi Suba with the Punjabi Suba Morcha of 1960 to 1961 under Master Tara Singh and then Sant Fateh Singh, whose fasts and threatened self-immolation in 1966 raised the stakes past anything the Regional Formula could absorb. Nehru died on 27 May 1964 with the Punjab question exactly where the 1956 Act had left it, which is the honest debit entry in the deferral ledger: the strategy bought a decade and spent it without producing the negotiated compact that the Andhra case had shown to be the precondition of enactment. The resolution, when it came under Indira Gandhi's government, came through the Punjab Reorganisation Act, 1966, Act 31 of 1966, effective 1 November 1966, ten years to the day after the 1956 appointed day: the Punjabi-speaking areas formed the new Punjab, the Hindi-speaking areas formed Haryana, the hill areas went to Himachal Pradesh, and Chandigarh became a union territory and joint capital, on boundaries recommended by the commission chaired by Justice J. C. Shah appointed in April 1966. The 1966 settlement was a religious-linguistic accommodation in everything but name, a Punjabi Suba with a Sikh majority achieved once the linguistic framing had been allowed to do its decade of work. The 1956 deferral did not prevent that outcome; it determined its timing, its statutory form and the exhaustion of the alternatives that made it acceptable to a Union government that had refused it for eighteen years.

## Deferred Case Two: Bilingual Bombay, a State Designed With Its Own Dissolution in View

Bombay was the deferred case the Act did not leave untouched but constructed as a deferral, and the distinction is the strongest single exhibit for the pragmatist reading. The States Reorganisation Commission had contemplated separating the Marathi and Gujarati regions with a distinct arrangement for Bombay city; the States Reorganisation Bill, introduced in the House of the People on 18 April 1956 as Bill number 30 of 1956 on the President's recommendation under the proviso to Article 3, proposed three units: a state of Maharashtra including Marathwada and Vidarbha, a state of Gujarat including Saurashtra and Kutch, and Bombay city as a union territory. The Supreme Court's narrative in Babulal Parate v State of Bombay (1960) records what happened next with a lawyer's neutrality: the Bill went to a Joint Select Committee whose report came on 16 July 1956, clauses were amended in Parliament, and the Act as passed created instead, in section 8, a single composite state of Bombay comprising the Marathi districts of the old Bombay state with Vidarbha from Madhya Pradesh and Marathwada from Hyderabad, together with the Gujarati districts, Saurashtra and Kutch, minus the Kannada districts transferred to Mysore and the Abu Road taluka transferred to Rajasthan. The bilingual state was born in the amendment stage, as the cabinet's answer to a parliamentary and street arithmetic that the three-unit scheme could not survive.

### What did the original States Reorganisation Bill propose for Bombay city?

The Bill of April 1956 proposed Bombay city as a centrally administered union territory, separated from the Maharashtra and Gujarat created around it. The proposal pleased neither claimant: the Samyukta Maharashtra movement demanded the city as Maharashtra's capital and economic heart, and Gujarati commercial opinion resisted absorption into Maharashtra, so Parliament's amendment substituted the bilingual composite.

The movements that boxed the cabinet in had names, dates and martyrs. The Samyukta Maharashtra Samiti was formed in February 1956 to unite the Marathi claim, drawing in opposition parties, trade unions and much of Bombay's Marathi public life; police firing on demonstrators in Bombay in the agitation of 1955 and 1956 killed scores, and the movement's own commemoration counts 105 martyrs, remembered at Hutatma Chowk in the city. The Mahagujarat Andolan rose in August 1956 after police fired on student demonstrators in Ahmedabad, with Indulal Yagnik emerging as the movement's leader through the Mahagujarat Janata Parishad. Nehru's defence of the bilingual outcome in the August 1956 Lok Sabha debates was explicitly experimental: the composite state deserved a trial, the arrangement was not presented as eternal, and Parliament retained the power to revisit it. Read alongside the Nehru and Fazl Ali correspondence, the bilingual state is deferral wearing statutory form. The cabinet could not enact the three-unit scheme, would not award the city outright to either claimant, and so enacted the one configuration that kept the question inside a legislature where both communities held seats, postponing the award of Bombay city to a later Parliament with a clearer balance of forces.

The postponement lasted four years. The Congress paid for bilingual Bombay at the 1957 general elections in both regions, the Samiti and the Mahagujarat Janata Parishad converted the statehood demand into durable electoral organisations, and the Union government conceded the bifurcation it had declined to enact in 1956. The Bombay Reorganisation Act, 1960, Act 11 of 1960, effective 1 May 1960, divided the composite state into Maharashtra, with Bombay city as its capital, and Gujarat, with Ahmedabad as its capital. The resolution confirmed the 1956 reading in the act of superseding it: the bilingual state had been a holding device, its four-year life measured the time needed for the bifurcation to become enactable without a contested award of the city by a divided cabinet. Where Punjab's deferral was an omission, Bombay's was a construction, and a construction later dismantled on schedule by its own architects' successors is the hardest kind of evidence for the forced-deferral counter-reading to explain. The political energy of the Nehruvian settlement at its height, the same confidence that had carried the Avadi resolution's socialist pattern through the Congress in January 1955, was spent in Bombay not on winning the argument but on renting time, visible in [the Avadi settlement and its context](/2019/02/05/avadi-resolution-socialist-pattern-1955/) as the era's characteristic method: settle the frame nationally, defer the explosive particular.

## Bombay City: The Prize That Could Not Be Awarded in 1956

No deferred case repays economic reading as Bombay city does, because the city's value was the reason its award was impossible. Bombay in 1956 was the Union's premier port, its largest concentration of textile mills, the seat of its film industry, its stock exchange and the historic home of Gujarati and Parsi mercantile capital, governed through a municipal corporation of unusual strength and a Congress organisation dominated by S. K. Patil, the city's political boss and a power in the national party. The population of the city was majority Marathi-speaking by the census, its working class overwhelmingly Marathi, its mills staffed from the Konkan and the Deccan; its capital, its wholesale trade and much of its mill ownership was Gujarati and Parsi; its tax contribution to the composite Bombay state was out of all proportion to its area. Every proposed disposition therefore redistributed real wealth and real power, not only sentiment. Award the city to Maharashtra and Gujarati capital faced a government answerable to a Marathi electorate that blamed mill owners for the firings of January 1956. Constitute it a union territory and Maharashtra was beheaded, its natural capital held by the centre against the movement's central demand. Leave it in a bilingual state and both communities retained a share of its government and a veto on the other's exclusive claim, which is precisely the outcome the cabinet enacted and precisely why it could not last: a veto shared is a question adjourned, and the city's two publics spent the bilingual state's four years organising to win the adjourned question outright.

Morarji Desai, Chief Minister of Bombay until the reorganisation, embodied the composite's defence at the state level and paid for it: his government's police had fired on the crowds of 1955 and 1956, and his advocacy of the bilingual state made him the Samiti's central villain, a position that followed him into national politics. The 1960 bifurcation, when it came, awarded the city to Maharashtra whole, with its mills, its port and its exchange, and Gujarat's acceptance of Ahmedabad and later Gandhinagar as capitals was eased by the knowledge, plain by 1960, that the bilingual experiment had been tried and had failed to produce a single Bombay public. The economic reading completes the political one. The cabinet of 1956 did not lack the legal power to award Bombay city; Article 3 gave it that power in terms. It lacked the capacity to award the city and still govern it, tax it and police it through the ministry that would have to own the award. Implementability, in the Bombay case, meant the difference between a clause in a statute and a functioning capital, and the four-year composite was the price of converting the first into the second.

## Deferred Case Three: The Northeast, Where the Act Preserved Assam and Governed Through Exception

The Northeast was the deferral of largest territorial extent and longest duration. The 1956 Act left Assam's boundaries substantially as they stood, and section-level silence did the rest: no Naga state, no Mizo state, no reorganisation of the hill districts, which continued under the Sixth Schedule's autonomous district councils, the constitutional device the founding had built for the tribal areas of Assam. The North-East Frontier Agency, administered by the Union through the Governor of Assam, stood outside the state framework altogether. Manipur and Tripura, as the enacted-map section recorded, remained union territories. Where the linguistic principle had supplied the Act's grammar for the mainland, the Northeast was governed by a different grammar altogether: security on a sensitive frontier with Burma and East Pakistan, the distinct treaty and customary histories of the hill peoples, and a Union government judgement, shared across the cabinet, that statehood questions there would be answered by exception and negotiation rather than by any general principle. The Fazl Ali Commission endorsed the posture, recommending the maintenance of Assam's integrity, and the Act enacted the endorsement by doing almost nothing, which in a statute of this operational density is itself a loud decision.

The Naga question exposed the limits of the posture fastest. The Naga National Council under A. Z. Phizo had declared Naga independence on 14 August 1947 and rejected incorporation into the Indian Union; armed insurgency from the mid-1950s made the Naga Hills the one part of the deferred map where postponement was enforced by counter-insurgency rather than by parliamentary arithmetic. The Union government's responses came in instalments that trace the deferral's unwinding: the Naga Hills Tuensang Area was constituted as a centrally administered unit in 1957, the Sixteen Point Agreement of July 1960 between the Union government and the Naga Peoples' Convention settled the terms of a Naga state within the Union, and the State of Nagaland Act, 1962, Act 27 of 1962, brought Nagaland into statehood on 1 December 1963, the first deferred case to be resolved and the only one resolved by a negotiated convention with a body claiming to speak for the territory's people. The Mizo hills followed a harder road: the Mizo National Front's uprising of March 1966 and two decades of insurgency preceded Mizoram's statehood in 1987, far outside any timetable the 1956 settlement imagined. Between those poles, the general Northeast settlement arrived with the North-Eastern Areas (Reorganisation) Act, 1971, Act 81 of 1971, effective 21 January 1972: Manipur and Tripura graduated to statehood, Meghalaya, which had existed as an autonomous state within Assam from 2 April 1970 under the Assam Reorganisation (Meghalaya) Act, 1969, became a full state, and Mizoram and Arunachal Pradesh entered as union territories. The Northeast's deferral therefore ran sixteen years to its general resolution and carried a security history the other two cases never had, which is precisely why the 1956 government's refusal to force it belongs in the implementability ledger: a Parliament that had tried to settle the hills by the linguistic statute of 1956 would have legislated for territories whose consent it did not possess and whose administration it conducted through exception already.

## The Deferred-Case Timeline

The three deferrals, their statutory treatment in 1956 and their resolutions can be set side by side, and the comparison is the findable artifact of this article: the InsightCrunch deferred-case timeline for the States Reorganisation Act, 1956, which maps each deferred case to the arrangement the Act actually made for it and to the instrument that eventually resolved it.

Read down the middle column, the timeline shows the deferral strategy's signature: in no case did the 1956 Act create a vacuum. Punjab received a formula, Bombay received a state, the Northeast received the continuation of its exceptional arrangements. Each holding device was weaker than a settlement and stronger than a refusal, and each was just strong enough to keep the question inside constitutional politics until a resolution became enactable. Read down the final column, a second pattern appears: no two resolutions used the same instrument. Punjab was resolved by linguistic-religious accommodation after a boundary commission, Bombay by simple bifurcation once the city's award had become politically decidable, and the Northeast by layered arrangements, conventions, autonomous councils graduating to states and union territories graduating later, that no single principle describes. Deferral bought not merely time but differentiation: the freedom to settle each case on the terms that case could bear. That freedom is the substance of the namable claim, and it is invisible in any account that treats 1956 as a single event rather than as the first tranche of a settlement sequence running to 1972 and, in Mizoram's case, to 1987.

## The August 1956 Debates: Nehru Defends an Incomplete Act in Parliament

The States Reorganisation Bill's passage through the Lok Sabha in the monsoon session of 1956, with the decisive debates in August 1956, is where the deferral strategy had to be defended in public, and the parliamentary record shows the government declining to disguise it. The amendment that converted the three-unit Bombay scheme into the bilingual composite was moved and carried in the House; members from Maharashtra and Gujarat attacked the composite from opposite sides in the same debate, which was the cabinet's point made by its enemies: no allocation of Bombay city could command the House, so the House was asked to enact the allocation that allocated nothing finally. Nehru's intervention in the August 1956 debate, the mandatory primary text for this article's subject, defended the Bill as a working settlement rather than a final one, presenting the reorganisation as a framework within which outstanding questions would be settled by further experience and further legislation. The Joint Select Committee's report of 16 July 1956 had already modelled the method, adjusting clauses against the memoranda and deputations of the affected regions; Parliament's role in August was to ratify a sequence, not a terminus.

The legal afterlife of the debates confirms their importance. In Babulal Parate v State of Bombay, decided in 1960 as the bilingual state was being dismantled, a Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court upheld the creation of the composite Bombay state against the challenge that Parliament had exceeded its power under Article 3, and the judgment's recital of the legislative history, Nehru's statement of 22 December 1953, the Commission's appointment on 29 December 1953, the Bill of 18 April 1956 and the assent of 31 August 1956, reads as the official chronicle of the deferral sequence itself. The Court treated the elasticity of the 1956 arrangements as constitutionally unremarkable: Parliament's power to form new states and alter boundaries carried the power to form a state many of whose makers expected to divide. Implementation, in other words, survived judicial scrutiny because the Constitution had been written, in the founding debates on Article 3, to privilege the Union's capacity to redraw over any unit's claim to permanence, a thread that runs back through [the Nehruvian implementation context](/2019/01/25/nehruvian-era-settlement-three-tensions/) to the settlement the era constructed across all three of the founding tensions.

The legislative calendar itself tells the deferral story in miniature. The Bill consumed four and a half months between introduction on 18 April 1956 and assent on 31 August 1956, a long gestation for a government commanding the House, and the time was spent exactly where the deferrals were: in the Joint Select Committee's hearings, which reported on 16 July 1956, and in the clause-by-clause management of the Bombay amendment on the floor. The Constitution (Seventh Amendment) Bill travelled with it, because the statutory map and the constitutional text had to change on the same appointed day or not at all; the amendment deleted the fourfold classification of states, recast the First Schedule and adjusted the provisions on high courts and legislative councils that the new map required. Running a constitutional amendment and a succession statute in harness, against a fixed date, while three regional questions were consciously left outside both instruments, is not the profile of a government overtaken by events. It is the profile of a government that had decided, by the winter of 1955 and 1956, precisely how much of the Indian map it intended to settle in 1956, and had built its parliamentary session around that decision and no larger one.

## Strategic or Forced? Adjudicating the Two Readings of 1956

Two readings of the 1956 Act compete in the scholarship, and this article has been building the evidence to adjudicate between them. The principled-purist reading treats the Act as the consummation of the linguistic-states principle, the delayed honouring of commitments the Congress had carried since its Nagpur session of 1920 reorganised its own provincial committees by language: on this reading, the deferrals are blemishes, cases where the principle was betrayed or postponed by cowardice, and the resolutions of 1960, 1966 and 1972 are the principle completing itself late. The political-pragmatist reading, defended here, treats the Act as an implementable subset of the principled map, selected by the criterion Nehru stated to Fazl Ali, with the deferrals as designed components: on this reading, the later resolutions are not the principle completing itself but the strategy completing itself, each settled when its political preconditions, a compact, a decidable city award, a negotiated convention, had been manufactured. Robert King's Nehru and the Language Politics of India (1997) supplies the pragmatist reading's scholarly spine, reconstructing Nehru's language politics as continuous management rather than reluctant conversion; Granville Austin's Working a Democratic Constitution (1999) supplies the institutional evidence that the machinery of implementation, not the coverage of the map, was the era's governing achievement; Louise Tillin's Remapping India (2013) extends the argument forward, reading the state creations of 2000 and after as continuations of the 1956 logic of negotiated, sequenced, centrally managed boundary change.

### How can the 1956 deferrals count as strategy if later agitation forced their resolution?

A deferral counts as strategic when the government chose which cases to postpone, built a holding device for each and kept the initiative over the timing and instrument of resolution. Punjab, Bombay and the Northeast meet the test: the Regional Formula, the bilingual state and the Sixth Schedule continuation were the government's designs, made before the movements took form.

The strongest counter to the pragmatist reading deserves its full force before the verdict. The counter runs: the deferrals were not chosen but imposed. The Akali Dal's mobilisation made Punjab unignorable yet unresolvable, the Samyukta Maharashtra and Mahagujarat movements dictated Bombay's eventual bifurcation against the cabinet's stated preference, and Naga insurgency, not Nehru's sequencing, set the Northeast's timetable. On this view, implementability is a retrospective compliment paid to a government that simply failed to settle three questions and was rescued by the passage of time. The distinction this article has maintained, strategic deferral against forced deferral, answers the counter at the level of decision rather than outcome. A forced deferral is one where the government's own preferred instrument was defeated and replaced by its opponents'. A strategic deferral is one where the government, facing cases it could not settle on acceptable terms, selected the postponement, designed its form, and kept the constitutional initiative. The evidence fits the second pattern. In Bombay, the cabinet's preferred instrument was indeed defeated, but the cabinet then authored the bilingual construction and defended it as an experiment with a revisable life, converting defeat into a holding device of its own design. In Punjab, the Regional Formula was the government's counter-offer, accepted by the Akali leadership of 1956, and the eventual 1966 settlement was enacted by a Congress government through a boundary commission of its own appointment. In the Northeast, the initiative never left the Union government's hands at all: convention, statute and layered reorganisation followed its timetable. Forced resolutions, strategic deferrals: the two are compatible, and their combination is exactly what the Nehru and Fazl Ali correspondence predicts a government pursuing implementability would produce.

The verdict follows. The principled-purist reading cannot explain the selectivity of 1956: why Telangana was enacted over the Commission's hesitation while Punjab was postponed over the Akali Dal's insistence, why Bombay city was neither awarded nor separated but entombed in a composite, why the Commission's own fourfold test subordinated language to unity, viability and planning at the moment of the principle's supposed triumph. The pragmatist reading explains each of those facts with a single criterion documented in the primary record before the event. The 1956 Act was the linguistic principle passed through the filter of governability, and what the filter removed was not abandoned; it was scheduled.

## The Argument Outside the House: Parties, Movements and the Press

The settlement was argued in public as fiercely as in Parliament, and the alignment of forces outside the House explains several of the Act's silences better than the debate transcripts do. The Communist Party of India supported linguistic reorganisation as a matter of settled party doctrine and supplied organisational muscle to the movements in Kerala, Andhra and Maharashtra; in Bombay, the communist trade unions stood inside the Samyukta Maharashtra Samiti beside the socialist leader S. M. Joshi, whose stewardship made the Samiti the broadest opposition front of the decade. The Bharatiya Jana Sangh and the Hindu Mahasabha took the opposite position on Punjab, campaigning for a Maha Punjab that would merge the Hindi region, PEPSU and the hill areas into a single large state and dilute the Punjabi-speaking claim entirely, a counter-map whose existence in the field is part of the evidence that the Fazl Ali Commission weighed when it found the Suba demand short of general support in the region. The Akali Dal, uniquely among the major actors, was simultaneously a movement, a religious body's political wing and a parliamentary party, which is why its negotiations with Pant in 1956 could produce a formula no purely linguistic movement could have signed. The press divided on regional lines with a fidelity that itself mapped the deferrals: Bombay's English-language papers defended the composite or the city's separation according to their ownerships, the Marathi press campaigned for Samyukta Maharashtra as a single cause, the Gujarati press for Mahagujarat, and the national dailies, The Hindu and the Times of India in their editorial columns, treated the reorganisation as a test of Nehru's statecraft and marked the deferrals as its questionable passages. A government implementing under this crossfire needed the holding devices the Act supplied, because every deferred case had a press, a party and a martyrology ready to convert a premature settlement into a permanent grievance.

## The Numbers of the Settlement: Population, Viability and the Fiscal Test

The Fazl Ali Commission's fourfold test contained a fiscal criterion that the summaries omit and the implementation reading restores: a proposed state had to be able to pay for itself. The India of the 1951 census counted some 361 million people, and the demands before the Commission ranged from states of tens of millions to hill territories whose revenues could not have sustained a secretariat. The Commission's viability reasoning is visible in its dispositions: it resisted fragmenting the Hindi-speaking centre into small units, it weighed Bombay's revenues in every configuration of the western map, and it treated the Northeast's territories as fiscally dependent by structure, a condition its recommendations answered with continued central support rather than with statehood. The Act carried the fiscal logic into its own Parts Six, Seven and Eight, apportioning public debt and pension liability with the same care as territory, and the Second Finance Commission, appointed in 1956 under K. Santhanam to report for the award period beginning in 1957, had to construct its devolution for a map that had not existed when its predecessor reported. The settlement's fiscal sequel ran smoothly for the enacted states and awkwardly for the deferred ones, exactly as the deferral thesis predicts: bilingual Bombay's budget was a standing quarrel between its two regions over shares of the city's revenue, Punjab's Regional Formula carried development promises its committees could not fund, and the Northeast's exceptional arrangements ran on central subvention whose scale became a grievance in Assam itself. Implementability was never only a count of parliamentary votes. It was a count of treasuries, cadres and tax bases, and the 1956 Act is the rare Indian statute whose text, from its zonal councils to its debt schedules, shows its makers counting all three at once.

## The Residue: Boundary and River Disputes the Succession Could Not Settle in Advance

A succession statute can assign every district and still leave behind disputes that only appear once the new states begin to govern, and the 1956 Act's residue is the final body of evidence for how its method handled the unforeseeable: by machinery, not by reopening the map. The Belgaum question is the type case. Section 7 had assigned Belgaum district, apart from Chandgad taluka, to Mysore on the linguistic findings available to the drafters; Maharashtra, from its creation on 1 May 1960, claimed Belgaum city and the district's Marathi-speaking border areas as wrongly transferred, and the claim outlasted every ministry in both states. The Union government's answer, a decade after the Act, was the method of 1953 repeated at smaller scale: a one-man commission under Justice Mehr Chand Mahajan, appointed in 1966, whose report of 1967 upheld the greater part of the 1956 assignment while recommending adjustments Maharashtra's government rejected. The dispute was never settled by either state's consent, and its persistence marks the boundary of the deferral repertoire: commissions can price a claim, formulas can postpone it, but a border district whose two publics return opposite census answers reproduces the Punjab problem in miniature, and the Union has preferred, for sixty years in Belgaum's case, the costs of an open file to the costs of an imposed award. The 1956 Act's drafters, who had scheduled Belgaum into Mysore by the best evidence of their season, left their successors the commission device as the standing instrument for exactly such residue.

The river questions followed the same channel into machinery. The reorganisation redrew the basins of the Krishna, the Godavari, the Narmada and the Punjab rivers across new state lines in a single day, converting intra-state allocations into inter-state disputes by the act of succession itself. Parliament anticipated the category in the same legislative season, enacting the Inter-State Water Disputes Act, 1956 under Article 262 of the Constitution, weeks apart from Act 37 of 1956, and supplying the tribunal mechanism through which the Krishna and Godavari disputes were eventually referred in 1969. The pairing of the two 1956 statutes is the implementation method in its most complete form: one statute moved the boundaries, the other built the forum for the consequences, and neither pretended the consequences could be enumerated in advance. Chandigarh's continuing status as a union territory and joint capital, awarded to neither Punjab nor Haryana in 1966 and promised to Punjab under the Rajiv-Longowal Accord of July 1985 without the transfer being executed, belongs to the same residue file: an adjourned award whose adjournment has outlasted every government that inherited it. The residue does not refute the deferral thesis. It refines it, by showing that the 1956 method's true product was not a finished map but a repertoire, commission, formula, tribunal, holding territory, instalment statehood, for processing territorial conflict inside constitutional politics indefinitely, at prices the Union has repeatedly judged lower than the price of finality imposed by force.

## Counting a Language: The Census Problem Inside the Linguistic Principle

Every linguistic boundary in the 1956 Act rested, in the end, on a count, and the count was the weakest instrument the settlement possessed. The census of 1951, the first of the republic, asked for mother tongue and received answers that were already political acts: communities returned the language of their aspiration or their organisation's instruction as readily as the language of their homes. Punjab was the extreme case, where the competitive return of Hindi and Punjabi by the same bilingual population converted the census schedule into a plebiscite neither side accepted, but the phenomenon was general. Maithili speakers in north Bihar were enumerated inside Hindi by a classification that grouped mother tongues under language heads of the census's own construction; Urdu returns fell across north India under pressures the census could record but not explain; the tribal languages of the central belt were gathered into categories that made whole peoples statistically invisible inside the Hindi total. The Fazl Ali Commission heard this evidence and drew the correct inference, visible in its refusal of the one language, one state doctrine: the linguistic map could be a guide to state-making, never a mechanism, because the underlying numbers were negotiated facts rather than natural ones. Script sharpened the problem further. Punjabi in the Gurmukhi script could be fenced from Hindi in Devanagari by an enumerator's rule, while the spoken continuum ignored the fence; Konkani, claimed for Marathi or for itself according to script and camp, presented the same structure on the western coast. The Act's drafters worked around the census problem rather than through it, drawing boundaries at district and taluk level from the accumulation of administrative, historical and movement evidence, and reserving the pure census test for the cases, Punjab above all, where they declined to draw at all. When the Shah Commission returned to Punjab in 1966, it applied census and contiguity criteria with a rigour the 1956 settlement had deliberately avoided, and the fact that a decade of politics had first to exhaust the formula alternative is the measure of what the census could and could not decide in 1956.

## The Resolution Sequence: 1960, 1966 and 1972 Read as One Story

The three resolutions are usually filed in separate histories, a Maharashtra textbook chapter, a Punjab textbook chapter, a Northeast textbook chapter, and the separation conceals the pattern the deferral thesis exists to expose. Read as one sequence, the resolutions show a Union government returning to the 1956 file at intervals, with the same instruments in its hands each time and a different political precondition satisfied each time. For Bombay in 1960, the precondition was exhaustion: the bilingual state had been tried for four years, the 1957 elections had measured the composite's unpopularity in both regions, and the bifurcation statute of 1960 could be presented, truthfully, as the ratification of an experiment's result rather than the surrender of a position. The Bombay Reorganisation Act, 1960 used the 1956 machinery as its template, apportioning assets, services and legislative membership between Maharashtra and Gujarat by the methods Part Two to Part Ten of Act 37 of 1956 had normalised. For Punjab in 1966, the precondition was a formula's failure honestly recorded and a boundary commission's authority borrowed for the hardest line: the Shah Commission, appointed in April 1966 under Justice J. C. Shah, reported within weeks, and the Punjab Reorganisation Act, 1966 drew the linguistic boundary the Fazl Ali Commission had declined to draw, on census and contiguity criteria the commission stated in terms, with Chandigarh's union territory status as the residual holding device for the one asset neither successor could be awarded outright. The parallel with Bombay city is exact and was understood at the time: where a single asset cannot be divided or awarded, the 1956 method centralises it and adjourns the award, and Chandigarh remains the adjournment still running.

For the Northeast in 1963 and 1972, the precondition was negotiation reaching the point of enactability at different speeds in different hills: the Naga Peoples' Convention's Sixteen Point Agreement of July 1960 made Nagaland legislable by 1962; the Khasi, Jaintia and Garo hills' demand for a hill state was first answered with the autonomous state of Meghalaya inside Assam on 2 April 1970, an instalment in the Regional Formula's own currency, before full statehood on 21 January 1972; Manipur and Tripura's graduation the same day closed the union territory chapter the 1956 Act had continued for them. Mizoram's statehood in 1987, after the accord of June 1986 between the Union government and Laldenga's Mizo National Front, stands at the sequence's far end as its limiting case: a resolution whose precondition, the end of an insurgency by negotiated accord, took thirty years to manufacture, and whose statutory form, a state created by reorganisation legislation on the 1956 template, was available throughout. The sequence's lesson for the adjudication is cumulative. A merely forced postponement ends in settlements dictated by the strongest movement of the moment. The 1960 to 1972 sequence shows settlements whose instruments, commissions, formulas, instalments and templates, were drawn each time from the repertoire the 1956 settlement had built, executed by governments that retained the initiative the deferral had preserved. That is what it means, in the dry language of implementation, for a strategy to complete itself.

## The Commission on Tour: Evidence, Memoranda and the Manufacture of a Workable Map

Between its appointment on 29 December 1953 and the submission of its report on 30 September 1955, the States Reorganisation Commission conducted the largest consultative exercise the Union had yet attempted on its own internal structure. Fazl Ali, Kunzru and Panikkar toured the regions in dispute, sat in state capitals and district towns, received memoranda by the thousand from language associations, bar councils, chambers of commerce, caste and community bodies, political parties and district boards, and took evidence from the very movements whose claims they would have to disappoint in part. The tour was not decoration. It was the mechanism by which the Commission learned, region by region, where a boundary would be obeyed and where it would be resisted, and that knowledge is written into the report's fourfold test: unity, viability and planning stand beside language in the test precisely because the hearings had shown the Commission how quickly a linguistically perfect boundary could become an administrative or communal impossibility. The Commission's members did not sign a report of false unanimity; individual notes appended to the report recorded reservations on particular dispositions, and the cabinet that received the document in October 1955 received it as a negotiated instrument already, one whose internal compromises mapped the external ones the government would have to strike.

The hearings also performed a function the Nehru and Fazl Ali correspondence lets us name. Every memorandum submitted, every deputation heard, every regional claim entered into the record was time spent inside an official process, and time inside the process was time not spent in a fast or a morcha. The Commission's twenty-one months were a holding device for the whole country, not only for the three deferred cases, and the government that had created Andhra State under duress in 1953 purchased with the Commission's tour the right to face the remaining claims in an order of its own choosing. Robert King's account in Nehru and the Language Politics of India (1997) of Nehru's preference for institutional channels over street settlement finds its fullest confirmation here: the Commission was simultaneously an inquiry, a cooling mechanism and a drafting resource, and the Act of 1956 inherited all three functions in finished form.

## State by State: What the Enacted Map Did to the Units It Touched

The enacted map's least-noticed achievement is the number of founding-era units it quietly abolished. Hyderabad State, the largest of the princely inheritances, ceased to exist as a unit on 31 October 1956, its territory divided three ways: Telangana to Andhra Pradesh under section 3, Marathwada to Bombay under section 8, and the Kannada districts to Mysore under section 7. Travancore-Cochin likewise disappeared, divided between Kerala and Madras by sections 4 and 5. Saurashtra, the union of Kathiawar princely states formed in 1948, was absorbed into Bombay. Madhya Bharat and Vindhya Pradesh, the two princely unions of the centre, were dissolved into Madhya Pradesh under section 9. The Patiala and East Punjab States Union went into Punjab. Coorg went into Mysore. The 1956 Act therefore closed the princely-union experiment of 1948 completely, eight years after Sardar Patel's States Ministry had improvised it, and the federation that emerged on 1 November 1956 was the first in the Union's history whose units were all, in origin, either former British provinces, merged provincial territories or the single continuing case of Jammu and Kashmir, whose position the Act carried forward undisturbed within the asymmetric-federal design the Constitution had built for it.

Madras State's experience shows the settlement's texture at the level of a single continuing unit. Madras lost Malabar and South Kanara's Kasaragod taluk to Kerala and Mysore's new configuration, lost the Telugu districts' successor claim entirely with Andhra already separated in 1953, and gained the Tamil-speaking southern taluks of Travancore-Cochin under section 4, the territory that forms the Kanyakumari district. K. Kamaraj, Chief Minister of Madras from 1954, accepted the transfers with a public discipline that the Union government conspicuously lacked in Bombay, and Madras emerged smaller and more linguistically coherent, the model of what the Act intended a state to become. Bihar surrendered the Purulia portion of Manbhum to West Bengal and otherwise stood; Orissa stood almost entirely; Uttar Pradesh stood with minor boundary adjustments. The gradient of change ran from total abolition at Hyderabad to near-total continuity at Lucknow, and the gradient followed politics, not cartography: the units that changed most were the units whose internal settlements, princely, communal or linguistic, had never closed.

## Govind Ballabh Pant and the Congress Organisation: The Human Machinery of Implementation

Statutes do not implement themselves, and the Nehru government's implementation capacity in 1956 rested on two human instruments the summaries rarely name. The first was the Union Home Minister, Govind Ballabh Pant, who held the Home portfolio from January 1955 and carried the reorganisation through cabinet and Parliament as its political manager; Pant, a former Chief Minister of the United Provinces with a reputation for patience in committee, was the minister who negotiated with the Akali leadership over the Regional Formula, handled the Bombay amendments through the Joint Select Committee stage and answered the August 1956 debates beside Nehru. The second instrument was the Congress organisation itself. The party's provincial committees had been organised on linguistic lines since 1920, an irony the purist reading rightly savours: the Congress had conceded in its own constitution, thirty-six years before the Act, the principle it spent the 1950s administering cautiously in the state. In 1956 and 1957 the party realigned its organisation to the new map, and the chief ministers who took office on or near the appointed day formed the settlement's first operating layer: Yashwantrao Chavan in Bombay, Neelam Sanjiva Reddy in Andhra Pradesh, S. Nijalingappa in Mysore, Partap Singh Kairon in Punjab, Ravi Shankar Shukla in Madhya Pradesh, K. Kamaraj in Madras, Bidhan Chandra Roy in West Bengal, Sri Krishna Sinha in Bihar, Bishnu Ram Medhi in Assam, Harekrushna Mahatab in Orissa, Mohan Lal Sukhadia in Rajasthan and Sampurnanand in Uttar Pradesh. Kerala stood as the exception that proved the machinery's limits: the new state began under President's rule, and its first elected ministry, the Communist ministry of E. M. S. Namboodiripad sworn in on 5 April 1957, was the opposition's, a fact the Congress centre absorbed without disturbing the settlement that had made the state.

The Home Ministry's own succession work, supervised through the ministry's states desk in the months after 1 November 1956, is where Granville Austin's implementation emphasis in Working a Democratic Constitution (1999) bites hardest. Allocations of the Indian Administrative Service and Indian Police Service cadres to successor states, the division of public debt between divided units, the transfer of pending litigation, the continuation of inter-state river and electricity arrangements under Part Nine of the Act: this unglamorous docket, cleared without a single successor state repudiating its share, was the practical content of the claim that the Nehru government knew how to implement. It also explains the government's confidence in deferral. A state that can execute a fourteen-state succession cleanly can promise Patiala, Bombay and Shillong a later statute and be believed; the deferred cases were postponed by the one government in the Union's early history whose administrative credit made postponement read as scheduling rather than evasion.

## The Settlement's First Audit: The General Elections of 1957

The second general elections, held in February and March 1957, audited the settlement four months after the appointed day, and the results map the deferral strategy's costs with electoral precision. In the enacted regions, the Congress harvested the settlement: the party carried Andhra Pradesh, Mysore, Madras, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Orissa, Assam and West Bengal, governing every successor state the Act had created by agreement. In Kerala, as recorded, the Communist Party of India won the first election and formed the ministry of April 1957, a defeat for the Congress that nonetheless confirmed the settlement's premise, since the new state's politics proceeded through its legislature from its first day. The deferral costs appeared exactly where the Act had declined to settle. In bilingual Bombay, the Samyukta Maharashtra Samiti and the Mahagujarat Janata Parishad converted the statehood campaigns into parliamentary and assembly strength across Maharashtra and Gujarat, punishing the Congress in both regions for the composite it had imposed. In Punjab, Partap Singh Kairon's Congress held the state, but the Akali Dal's separate existence as the vehicle of the unmet Suba demand survived the Regional Formula intact, awaiting the morchas of 1960. The 1957 map of Congress strength is thus a photographic negative of the deferral map: strength where the Act had enacted, organised opposition where it had postponed. A government reading its own results could see, as Nehru's government plainly did, that the deferred cases had not been deleted from politics but converted into standing opposition organisations with a single, winnable demand each, a conversion the pragmatist reading counts as a cost honestly incurred for the sake of the fourteen-state succession that the same elections ratified.

## The Sixth Schedule and the Frontier: The Instruments of the Northeast Deferral

The Northeast's holding devices deserve their own account because they were constitutional instruments, not mere inaction, and the 1956 Act's choice was to continue them rather than replace them. The Sixth Schedule, written into the Constitution on the recommendations of the advisory sub-committee chaired by Gopinath Bordoloi, gave the tribal areas of Assam autonomous district councils with legislative, judicial and revenue powers over land, forests, shifting cultivation, village administration and customary law: the United Khasi and Jaintia Hills, the Garo Hills and the Lushai Hills districts worked through such councils, and the founding had designed the device precisely to govern hill societies whose consent to plains administration could not be assumed. The Naga Hills stood in a still more exceptional position, administered with the Governor's special responsibility and, from 1957, reorganised with Tuensang as the Naga Hills Tuensang Area under central administration. The North-East Frontier Agency was administered by the President through the Governor of Assam with the External Affairs Ministry's hand on it, a frontier arrangement that treated the territory as a buffer to be held rather than a population to be enfranchised into statehood. When the Act of 1956 preserved Assam and left these instruments standing, it was choosing a layered exceptionalism over the linguistic grid, and the later settlement inherited the layers: the autonomous state of Meghalaya inside Assam in 1970 was the Sixth Schedule's logic graduating one step, and the statehood settlements of 21 January 1972 were drafted as modifications of the exceptional structure, not as applications of the 1956 mainland pattern. Deferral in the Northeast therefore differed in kind from deferral in Punjab and Bombay. There, holding devices bought time for a known resolution type, a boundary. In the hills, the holding devices were themselves the evolving settlement, and the resolutions of 1963 and 1972 look less like the completion of the 1956 map than like the exceptional structure reaching, case by case, the point at which statehood became one of its available layers.

## The Long Arc: Deferral as the Nehruvian Era's Repeatable Technique

Louise Tillin's Remapping India: New States and their Political Origins (2013) closes the evidentiary arc by showing that the technique outlived its inventors. The creations of Chhattisgarh, Uttarakhand and Jharkhand in November 2000 were accomplished by the same sequence the 1956 settlement pioneered: a demand matured over decades, a national government satisfied that the parent state's politics and the Union's arithmetic could carry the change, a statute under Article 3, and successor-state machinery of allocation and apportionment copied from the 1956 template. Tillin's account of the post-2000 reorganisations as continuations of the 1956 logic is the secondary literature's confirmation that Act 37 of 1956 founded a method and not merely a map. Telangana's separation from Andhra Pradesh in 2014, which undid section 3's merger after the Gentlemen's Agreement safeguards had decayed across two generations, belongs to the same arc and carries the deferral argument's final irony: the one case the 1956 government enacted against its commission's hesitation became, fifty-eight years later, the case that had to be re-settled, while the three cases it deferred were each settled once, on terms that held. Enactment without a durable compact proved less permanent than deferral with one.

The House Thesis locates the 1956 Act inside the second of the founding tensions, the question of who governs whom, fought here over the vertical division of the Union itself. The founding had answered that question with a strong centre and plastic boundaries; the Nehruvian era's contribution was to discover, between the Fazl Ali Commission's appointment in December 1953 and the appointed day of November 1956, how that plasticity could be used without breaking the Union it was meant to serve. The answer the era gave, enact the enactable and defer the rest under holding devices of deliberate design, became the standing grammar of Indian boundary politics. Every later reorganisation, from Haryana in 1966 to Telangana in 2014, has been argued in that grammar by governments that inherited it, usually without naming it. Naming it is the work this article set out to do, and the name is the claim with which it began: the 1956 reorganisation worked because it was deliberately incomplete, and the deferred cases were the price, and the proof, of its implementability.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How did the 1956 States Reorganisation operationalise linguistic states?

The Act operationalised linguistic states by converting the Fazl Ali Commission's map into statutory territory transfers, district by district and taluk by taluk, effective from a single appointed day, 1 November 1956. Sections 3 to 17 of Act 37 of 1956 formed or re-formed each state, the First Schedule was rewritten to match, and the Constitution (Seventh Amendment) Act, 1956 abolished the Part A, Part B, Part C and Part D classification at the same moment. Language supplied the organising criterion for most boundaries, but the Commission's fourfold test had subordinated it to unity, administrative viability and planning, so the operational map was linguistic in its main lines and pragmatic in its exceptions, with Punjab, Bombay city and the Northeast deliberately left outside the operative clauses.

### Q: How many states were created by the 1956 Act?

From the appointed day, 1 November 1956, the Union consisted of fourteen states and six union territories. The fourteen states were Andhra Pradesh, Assam, Bihar, Bombay, Jammu and Kashmir, Kerala, Madhya Pradesh, Madras, Mysore, Orissa, Punjab, Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal. The six union territories were the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Delhi, Himachal Pradesh, the Laccadive, Minicoy and Amindivi Islands, Manipur and Tripura. Several of the fourteen were enlarged or consolidated versions of existing units rather than creations from nothing: Andhra Pradesh united Andhra State with Telangana, Mysore gathered Kannada districts from three parent units, and Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh and Punjab each absorbed princely unions or territories abolished by the same statute.

### Q: Why was Punjab not reorganised in 1956?

Punjab was not reorganised because the Punjabi Suba demand entangled language with religion in a way the Nehru government judged unresolvable by boundary in 1956. A Punjabi-speaking state would have been a Sikh-majority state, Hindi-speaking Hindus in the region disputed the linguistic claim itself, and the Fazl Ali Commission found the demand short of general support across the Punjabi-speaking region read as a whole. The government instead merged the Patiala and East Punjab States Union into Punjab under section 11 and offered the Regional Formula of 1956, Punjabi and Hindi regional committees inside one legislature, as a holding device. The demand returned through the morchas of 1960 to 1961 and was settled only by the Punjab Reorganisation Act, 1966, which created Haryana and the reorganised Punjab from 1 November 1966.

### Q: What is the Fazl Ali Commission?

The Fazl Ali Commission is the States Reorganisation Commission, appointed by a resolution of the Ministry of Home Affairs dated 29 December 1953, after Potti Sreeramulu's death and the creation of Andhra State had made a general inquiry unavoidable. Its chairman was Fazl Ali, a former Supreme Court judge and former Governor of Orissa; its members were H. N. Kunzru and K. M. Panikkar. The Commission toured the disputed regions, received memoranda and evidence from movements, parties and associations across India, and submitted its report on 30 September 1955. It accepted language as a legitimate basis for state boundaries, rejected one language, one state as an absolute rule, and weighed each case against unity, administrative and financial viability and the requirements of planning, a fourfold test that shaped the Act of 1956.

### Q: How was Bombay State reorganised after 1956?

The Bombay State created by section 8 of the 1956 Act was a bilingual composite of Marathi and Gujarati districts, with Saurashtra, Kutch, Vidarbha and Marathwada joined to the old Bombay territories. The composite lasted four years. The Samyukta Maharashtra movement and the Mahagujarat Andolan, which had opposed the arrangement from its first day, converted their campaigns into electoral strength in 1957, and the Union government conceded the bifurcation it had declined to enact in 1956. The Bombay Reorganisation Act, 1960, Act 11 of 1960, effective 1 May 1960, divided the state into Maharashtra, which received Bombay city as its capital, and Gujarat, with Ahmedabad as its capital. The apportionment of assets, services and legislators between the two successors followed the machinery the 1956 Act itself had normalised.

### Q: Did the 1956 Act cover the Northeast?

The Act covered the Northeast mainly by preserving it. Assam's boundaries stood substantially unchanged, the hill districts continued under the Sixth Schedule's autonomous district councils, the frontier tracts remained under central administration, and Manipur and Tripura continued as union territories. No Naga, Mizo or Khasi state was created, and the linguistic principle that organised the mainland map was not applied to the hills, where the Union government governed through exception on security and consent grounds the Fazl Ali Commission had endorsed. The deferred resolutions came later and piecemeal: Nagaland attained statehood on 1 December 1963 under the State of Nagaland Act, 1962, and the North-Eastern Areas (Reorganisation) Act, 1971 raised Manipur, Tripura and Meghalaya to statehood and created Mizoram and Arunachal Pradesh as union territories from 21 January 1972.

### Q: Why did Nehru change his position on linguistic states?

Nehru's position changed because the politics underneath it changed. As a member of the JVP Committee in 1949 he had held that linguistic provinces threatened consolidation and should wait. Potti Sreeramulu's fast and death in December 1952, the violence that followed and the creation of Andhra State on 1 October 1953 demonstrated that refusal could no longer contain the demand; every language region in India could organise on the Andhra precedent. Nehru's response was not conversion to the principle as doctrine. His correspondence with Fazl Ali during the Commission's work shows a Prime Minister instructing an inquiry toward a politically implementable scheme, and Robert King's Nehru and the Language Politics of India (1997) reads the whole episode as management rather than conversion: Nehru accepted linguistic states once he could control their sequence, their criteria and their exceptions.

### Q: What did Part Three of the Act create through the zonal councils, and what were the councils meant to do?

Part Three of the Act constituted five zones, Northern, Central, Eastern, Western and Southern, and established a zonal council for each zone. Each council brought together the Union Home Minister as common chairman, the chief ministers of the states in the zone, two further ministers from each state and the administrators of any union territories in the zone. The councils were advisory. Their remit covered economic and social planning, border disputes, linguistic minority questions and inter-state transport, the recurring friction points the new boundaries were expected to generate. Nehru's design deliberately left executive authority with the cabinets and gave the councils the work of habitual consultation, an institutional admission that linguistic states would quarrel and that the quarrels deserved a standing forum short of litigation or agitation.

### Q: What was the appointed day under the Act, and why did the whole settlement turn on a single date?

The appointed day was 1 November 1956, the date section 1 of the Act fixed for the territorial scheme to take legal effect. On that single morning, the fourteen states and six union territories came into being, the First Schedule's new tables governed, legislatures and high courts changed composition, services were allocated to successor states, and the Part A, Part B, Part C and Part D classification ceased to exist, the Constitution (Seventh Amendment) Act, 1956 taking effect the same day. A single appointed day was an administrative necessity: debt, contracts, court jurisdiction and public employment could not be left in suspense between an old map and a new one, so the statute concentrated the entire succession into one dated instant and wrote its machinery of apportionment around that instant.

### Q: How did the Act divide the assets and liabilities of the states it split?

Parts Seven and Eight of the Act apportioned the assets and liabilities of the divided Part A and Part B states and of the Union between the successor states. Treasuries and cash balances, public debt, state loans, pension obligations, contracts, land, buildings and institutional property were assigned by the statute's own schedules and principles, with the Union government empowered to settle the residues and disputes the schedules could not foresee. Part Six authorised expenditure from the consolidated funds of the successor states for the transitional period so that no new government began life unable to pay its staff, and Part Nine preserved corporations and inter-state agreements across the boundary changes. The completeness of this machinery, debt and pensions included, is a large part of why the fourteen-state succession of November 1956 proceeded without a successor state repudiating its share.

### Q: What did the Act provide for government servants whose states were divided or abolished?

Part Ten of the Act carried the services across the reorganisation. Members of the All India Services, the Indian Administrative Service and the Indian Police Service above all, and members of the state services were allocated among the successor states to which their territories had gone, so that a district officer serving in a transferred district awoke on 1 November 1956 in the service of the successor state. Conditions of service were protected: allocation could not be used to worsen an officer's pay, seniority or prospects, and the Union government retained authority over the allocation process and its disputes. The provision mattered beyond administration. A settlement that had stranded or penalised the permanent services of abolished units such as Hyderabad, Travancore-Cochin and the princely unions would have manufactured an aggrieved official class inside every new state.

### Q: How did the Act settle the high courts of the new and divided states?

Part Five of the Act performed for the judiciary the same succession it performed for legislatures and treasuries. High courts were constituted or continued for the successor states, Kerala receiving its high court at Ernakulam, Mysore at Bangalore, Madhya Pradesh at Jabalpur and Rajasthan at Jodhpur, with the existing Bombay and Punjab high courts continuing for the composite Bombay state and the enlarged Punjab. Judges were transferred or appointed to the successor benches by rule, jurisdiction followed the new territories, and proceedings pending before a predecessor court on 31 October 1956 passed to the court that inherited the territory, so that no litigant lost a forum because the map had moved. The Rajasthan arrangements recorded in the case law of the period, a principal seat fixed at Jodhpur by presidential order under the Act in October 1956, show the machinery operating at the level of a single court's seat.

### Q: What happened to Saurashtra and Kutch under the 1956 Act?

Saurashtra and Kutch both disappeared as separate units into the composite Bombay state formed by section 8. Saurashtra, the union of the Kathiawar princely states constituted in 1948 with Rajkot as its capital, had been a Part B state with its own legislature and ministry; the Act dissolved it into Bombay's Gujarati districts. Kutch, a Part C state administered from the centre after its ruler's accession, went the same way. The two territories therefore joined Gujarat's districts in the bilingual state of 1956 and passed into the state of Gujarat when the Bombay Reorganisation Act, 1960 took effect on 1 May 1960. Their absorption illustrates the Act's general treatment of the princely unions: none survived 1 November 1956 as a unit of the federation.

### Q: How was Kerala formed under section 5 of the Act?

Section 5 formed the new state of Kerala from two sources. The first was the existing state of Travancore-Cochin, minus the territories transferred to Madras by section 4, the Tamil-speaking southern taluks of Thovala, Agastheeswaram, Kalkulam and Vilavancode with the Shencottah area, which honoured the Tamil claim on the far south. The second was territory taken from Madras: the Malabar district, excluding the Laccadive and Minicoy islands, and the Kasaragod taluk of South Kanara district, which the section constituted as part of a separate Malabar district inside Kerala. The result, from 1 November 1956, was a Malayalam-speaking state running the length of the Malabar coast. Kerala began under President's rule, and its first elected government, the Communist ministry of E. M. S. Namboodiripad, took office on 5 April 1957.

### Q: How did section 7 of the Act assemble the enlarged Mysore State?

Section 7 assembled the enlarged Mysore from five sources. It took the existing state of Mysore entire; from Bombay, the districts of Belgaum apart from Chandgad taluka, Bijapur, Dharwar and Kanara; from Hyderabad, the districts of Gulbarga apart from the Kodangal and Tandur taluks, Raichur apart from Alampur and Gadwal, and Bidar apart from three named taluks; from Madras, the South Kanara district apart from the Kasaragod taluk assigned to Kerala and the Kollegal area; and the Part C state of Coorg entire. The exceptions inside the transfers are as instructive as the transfers: the drafters worked at taluk level, moving Kodangal, Tandur, Alampur and Gadwal on linguistic and administrative findings rather than by district blocks. The enlarged, Kannada-speaking Mysore that resulted was renamed Karnataka only in 1973.

### Q: Why did Ajmer merge into Rajasthan in 1956?

Ajmer merged into Rajasthan under section 10 of the Act because the Fazl Ali Commission's logic left the small Part C state no defensible separate existence. Ajmer, the former British province of Ajmer-Merwara, was a Hindi and Rajasthani-speaking enclave entirely surrounded by Rajasthan, administered from the centre with its own small legislature since 1950, and its economy, communications and politics ran through the surrounding state. The Act added Ajmer to Rajasthan together with the Abu Road taluka transferred from Bombay and the Sunel enclave from Madhya Bharat, while surrendering Rajasthan's Sironj sub-division to Madhya Pradesh, a tidying of enclaves in both directions. Rajasthan's resulting shape, the largest state in the Union by area, has endured without a serious internal challenge since 1 November 1956, one of the settlement's quietest and most complete successes.

### Q: What status did the Act give Manipur, Tripura and Himachal Pradesh?

The Act continued all three as union territories administered by the President through a chief commissioner. Manipur and Tripura were princely states integrated into the Union in 1949 and administered as Part C states; Himachal Pradesh had been assembled from the Punjab hill states in 1948. The Fazl Ali Commission's viability test bears directly on the decision: each territory was small in population and revenue, and Manipur and Tripura sat on sensitive frontiers where the Union government preferred direct administration while the Northeast's larger questions remained unsettled. Graduation came later and at different speeds. Himachal Pradesh attained statehood on 25 January 1971 under its own statehood Act of 1970, and Manipur and Tripura became states on 21 January 1972 under the North-Eastern Areas (Reorganisation) Act, 1971.

### Q: Why was Vidarbha placed inside Bombay State rather than made a state of its own?

Vidarbha, the Marathi-speaking eastern region of the old Central Provinces and Berar centred on Nagpur, had a documented claim to separate statehood: it possessed a distinct political tradition, its own capital city and advocates inside the reorganisation debates, and the States Reorganisation Commission's deliberations had entertained a Vidarbha state seriously. The Union cabinet weighed the claim and declined it, pricing the precedent a Vidarbha state would set for sub-regional claims elsewhere and judging that Marathi unity argued for keeping Vidarbha with the rest of the Marathi country. Section 8 therefore placed Vidarbha inside the bilingual Bombay state, and the Bombay Reorganisation Act, 1960 carried it into Maharashtra, where Nagpur received the compensating status of second capital and the winter seat of the state legislature. The Vidarbha statehood demand has recurred in Maharashtra politics ever since without attaining the compact that made Andhra enactable in 1953.

### Q: How did the Act redistribute seats in the Council of States and the House of the People?

Part Four of the Act rewrote representation to match the new map. The Fourth Schedule to the Constitution, which allocates seats in the Council of States among the states, was recast for the fourteen states, seats in the House of the People were reallocated among states and union territories, and the composition of each state legislative assembly and legislative council was specified in the Act's own provisions. Sitting members of the predecessor legislatures were assigned to the successor legislatures so that every new state possessed a functioning legislature from the appointed day, and delimitation machinery was provided to translate the allocations into territorial constituencies for the elections that followed. The general elections of February and March 1957 were therefore fought on the reorganised map, the settlement's first democratic audit, four months after it took effect.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court decide in Babulal Parate v State of Bombay in 1960?

In Babulal Parate v State of Bombay, decided in 1960, a Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court upheld the creation of the composite Bombay state against a challenge to Parliament's power under Article 3 of the Constitution. The petitioner contested the bilingual state's creation; the Court held that Parliament's power to form new states and alter boundaries extended to the composite state the States Reorganisation Act, 1956 had enacted, and that the procedural requirements of Article 3 had been met. The judgment's recital of the legislative history, Nehru's statement of 22 December 1953, the Commission's appointment on 29 December 1953, the Bill introduced on 18 April 1956 and the presidential assent of 31 August 1956, stands as the official chronicle of the sequence. The bilingual state it upheld was divided by statute the same year.
