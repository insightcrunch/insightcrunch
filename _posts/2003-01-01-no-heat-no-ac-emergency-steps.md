When the furnace dies in a cold snap or the air conditioner quits in a heat wave, the household turns to you and the clock feels like it is running. A no heat or no AC emergency is one of the few home problems where the temptation to grab a phone and dial the first flashing number is strongest, and where that instinct costs the most. The advice that follows is built around a single sequence, ordered the way a stressed person actually needs it: rule out the one genuinely dangerous problem, run the handful of checks that fix most false alarms, keep the people in the house safe while you arrange help, decide whether this is a call-now or a wait-until-morning problem, and only then reach for a technician, on your terms rather than the after-hours market's.

That order matters more than any single tip, because the two ways people get hurt in these moments pull in opposite directions. One household calls a premium emergency line and pays a night rate over a tripped breaker or a dead thermostat battery. Another household smells something odd, decides to poke around the equipment, and troubleshoots straight through a gas leak. The sequence below is designed to prevent both. Call it the check-safe-then-simple rule: before you dial an emergency number, rule out a gas or carbon monoxide hazard, then check the thermostat, the breaker, the switch, and the filter, because a large share of no-heat and no-AC panics turn out to be a setting or a tripped breaker, not a breakdown.

This is the calm that the ad-choked emergency search result never gives you. Search a phrase like "furnace stopped working" at midnight and the top of the page is a wall of lead-generation forms and paid numbers, all engineered to convert your panic into a dispatched truck at the highest rate the moment allows. None of it tells you the thing that saves you money and, in the gas case, possibly your life: what to do in the first five minutes, in what order, and where the line sits between a real emergency and a fixable annoyance. That is the whole job of this guide.

## Rule out the dangerous problem first: gas and carbon monoxide

Everything else in this guide waits behind one priority. Before you touch the thermostat, before you look at the breaker, before you dial anyone, your first job is to rule out a fuel-gas leak or carbon monoxide. These are the only failures in a heating system that can hurt or kill the people in the house in minutes, and they are the one situation where the correct response is to stop investigating and leave.

### What should you do first if you smell gas or suspect carbon monoxide?

Get everyone out, including pets, and call from outside or a neighbor's. Do not flip switches, light anything, or hunt for the source, because a spark can ignite leaked gas. Once safely outside, call your gas utility's emergency line and the fire department, and let them clear the house before anyone returns.

That short answer is the whole rule, but it is worth understanding why each part of it is non-negotiable, because under stress people talk themselves out of the leaving step. Natural gas and propane are delivered to furnaces, boilers, and many water heaters through a fuel line, and a leak in that line, at a fitting, or inside the appliance releases a gas that is explosive at surprisingly low concentrations. Utilities add a sulfur-like odorant, the rotten-egg smell most people recognize, precisely so a leak is detectable before it reaches a dangerous level. If you smell that odor, the safe assumption is a real leak, not a passing whiff, and the safe action is to treat the house as hazardous until a professional says otherwise.

The reason you do not flip switches or use the phone indoors is that any electrical action, a light switch, a garage door opener, a thermostat, even answering a landline, can throw a small spark, and a small spark in a gas-filled room is all it takes. This is also why you do not relight a pilot yourself when there is any gas smell in the air. Open a door on your way out if it is directly on your path, but do not go room to room opening windows and creating a project out of ventilation; the priority is distance, not airflow. Once outside and clear, call the gas utility's dedicated emergency number, which is staffed around the clock, and the fire department. Both will treat a reported gas smell as urgent and will come without an argument about hours or fees.

Carbon monoxide is the quieter cousin of the gas leak and, in a no-heat scenario, often the more insidious one. Carbon monoxide is a colorless, odorless gas produced by incomplete combustion, and a furnace with a cracked heat exchanger, a blocked flue, or a venting problem can pump it into living space while the system appears to run normally. You cannot smell it, which is why detection depends on two things: a working carbon monoxide alarm and an awareness of the symptoms. If a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, treat it exactly like a gas smell: get everyone out, including pets, and call from outside. Do not assume it is a false alarm because the air seems fine, since the whole danger of carbon monoxide is that it feels like nothing until it is a medical emergency.

The symptoms of carbon monoxide exposure mimic the flu and a hangover, which is what makes them dangerous: headache, dizziness, nausea, weakness, confusion, and drowsiness, often affecting several people in the house at once and easing when they go outside. A pattern where everyone feels sick indoors and better in the fresh air, especially with a fuel-burning furnace running, is a carbon monoxide pattern until proven otherwise. If anyone is unconscious or you strongly suspect poisoning, get them into fresh air and call for emergency medical help immediately; this is a call-now beyond any question of HVAC repair. The through-line here connects to the broader crisis playbook: a home emergency triage sequence always puts life-safety hazards ahead of property and comfort, and the same logic that governs a general [home emergency triage](/2030/02/01/home-emergency-triage/) governs this one. Gas and carbon monoxide jump the entire queue.

One practical note that belongs in this section because it prevents the most common tragedy of a no-heat night: the alternative heat sources people reach for when the furnace is down are themselves the leading cause of carbon monoxide poisoning. A charcoal grill, a camp stove, a gas oven left open for warmth, or a generator run in an attached garage or near a window all produce carbon monoxide in enclosed or semi-enclosed space, and every winter these improvised measures put people in the hospital. The stabilize-the-household section below covers the safe ways to stay warm, and the single rule that governs all of them is that nothing that burns fuel indoors is a safe heat source unless it is a permanently installed, properly vented appliance. If your plan to survive a cold night involves combustion in an enclosed room, it is not a plan, it is the emergency.

## The simple homeowner checks that fix most false alarms

Once the house is clear of any gas or carbon monoxide concern, the second half of the check-safe-then-simple rule takes over. A large fraction of the calls that emergency HVAC lines take at night are not breakdowns at all; they are a thermostat in the wrong mode, a dead thermostat battery, a tripped breaker, a switched-off furnace, or a filter so clogged the system has shut itself down to protect itself. None of these needs a technician, and all of them can be verified in a few minutes by a homeowner with no tools. The point of running them first is not to turn you into a repair person. It is to avoid paying a night rate for a five-minute reset, and to give any technician you do call an accurate picture instead of a panicked one.

### Why do so many no-heat calls turn out to be a setting or a breaker?

Because the modern system has several small points of failure that mimic a total breakdown. A thermostat with a drained battery goes dark. A breaker trips and cuts power silently. A cleaning crew or a family member flips the furnace switch by accident. Each looks like a dead system, yet each is a reset, not a repair.

Start with the thermostat, because it is the most common culprit and the easiest to fix. Confirm the mode first: it should be set to heat when you want heat and cool when you want cool, and it is genuinely common for a thermostat to be left on the wrong setting after a mild stretch of weather, so the system is idle because it was told to be. Confirm the temperature setpoint is actually above the room temperature for heating, or below it for cooling, since a setpoint that has drifted or been nudged can leave the system with no reason to run. If the screen is blank or faint, the batteries are the likely problem on any battery-powered thermostat; a fresh set of the correct batteries brings a dead-looking system back to life more often than any other single fix. If your thermostat is the newer wired-in kind with a screen that will not wake, that points at a power problem upstream, which loops you back to the breaker and switch checks below. A thermostat that is set correctly, powered, and calling for heat or cool but getting no response tells you the problem is downstream in the equipment, which is useful information to hand a technician.

Next, the electrical supply. An HVAC system usually draws from one or more dedicated circuits, and a tripped breaker cuts power to the furnace, the air handler, or the outdoor unit without any dramatic sign beyond the system going quiet. Find your electrical panel and look for a breaker that has moved out of line with the others or sits in a middle position; the ones serving heating and cooling are often labeled, though labels are unreliable in older homes. Resetting a breaker is a reasonable homeowner action: push it firmly to the full off position, then back to on. If it holds and the system runs, you have found a nuisance trip, possibly from a momentary surge. If the breaker trips again immediately or within a few minutes, stop resetting it. A breaker that trips repeatedly is doing its job, telling you there is a fault in the circuit or the equipment drawing too much current, and repeatedly forcing it back on risks overheating the wiring. That is a call-a-pro situation, not a keep-trying situation, and the same caution that governs any electrical fault applies here.

The third check is the furnace's own power switch. Most furnaces have a switch that looks exactly like an ordinary light switch, usually mounted on or near the unit, sometimes at the top of the basement stairs. It is switched off by accident more often than anyone would guess, by a person doing laundry, a child, or a service tech who forgot to flip it back. If the furnace is completely dead, this switch is a five-second check worth making before you conclude anything is broken. While you are at the unit, if it is a gas furnace and you have already ruled out any gas smell, look for whether a pilot light or ignition system appears to be functioning; on older units a pilot that has gone out is sometimes the whole problem, though relighting one is a task to approach only per the manufacturer's printed instructions on the unit and never when there is any gas odor present.

The fourth check is the air filter, and it is the one people skip because it seems too minor to matter. It is not minor. A filter clogged with months of dust chokes the airflow through the system, and both furnaces and air conditioners have safety controls that shut the equipment down when airflow drops too far, to prevent the heat exchanger from cracking or the coil from freezing. A system that runs briefly then quits, or will not start at all, with a filter that has not been changed in a long time, is frequently a filter problem wearing the costume of a breakdown. Replacing a filter is squarely a homeowner task; a fresh filter of the correct size, installed with the airflow arrow pointing toward the equipment, sometimes resolves the whole issue and always improves the odds that the system runs well once it is back up. Keeping a spare filter on hand is one of the cheapest forms of insurance against a false-alarm emergency, which is exactly the kind of small readiness the maintenance side of this trade is built on.

### The furnace-specific checks when there is no heat

Beyond the shared checks, a no-heat furnace has a few tells worth reading before you call. If the furnace runs, the blower turns, but only cold air comes out, the burners may not be igniting, which on a gas unit can trace to a safety lockout, a flame-sensor issue, or a gas-supply problem, none of which are homeowner repairs but all of which are useful to describe accurately on the phone. If the furnace tries to start and then shuts off in a repeating pattern, often called short cycling, the causes range from that clogged filter to a blocked flue to a failing component, and forcing it to keep trying does not help. A furnace that is silent with the thermostat calling, the switch on, and the breaker holding has an internal problem that a technician will need to diagnose. What you are doing with these observations is not fixing the furnace; you are gathering the two or three facts that let a technician arrive prepared and that let you tell a call-now problem from one that can hold. For the fuller catalog of what each furnace and AC symptom means and how urgent it is, the diagnosis-focused companion on [AC and furnace warning signs](/2002/01/15/hvac-warning-signs/) is the place that owns that territory, and reading it on a calm day is worth more than any midnight search.

### The AC-specific checks when there is no cooling

A no-cooling system has its own short list. If the indoor blower runs and pushes air but the air is not cold, and you can see the outdoor unit, check whether the outdoor fan is spinning; an outdoor unit that sits dead while the indoor side runs often points at a tripped outdoor breaker, a failed capacitor, or a contactor problem, and the breaker is the only part of that you should touch. If the airflow from the vents is weak, the filter and the return path are the first suspects, and a coil that has frozen into a block of ice from a dirty filter or low airflow will not cool until it thaws, which means turning the system to off and the fan to on to melt the ice before anything else can help. If you see ice on the refrigerant lines or the indoor coil, that thaw step is the right first move and running the compressor against a frozen coil risks expensive damage. As with the furnace, none of this is a repair you perform; it is a set of observations that tells you whether you are looking at a reset, a thaw-and-wait, or a genuine failure that needs a pro, and it keeps you from paying an emergency premium for something a filter change and an hour of patience would have solved.

One honest caveat runs through all of these checks: they are deliberately limited to actions that carry no real hazard, a thermostat, a labeled breaker, an accessible switch, a filter, and a look at the outdoor fan. Anything past that line, opening the sealed cabinet, touching the gas train, testing a capacitor, or working on the refrigerant circuit, is a licensed-technician job, both because the failure modes include shock, fire, and gas, and because the diagnosis takes tools and training you are not expected to have. The value of the homeowner checks is entirely in ruling out the false alarms so that when you do spend money, you are spending it on a real problem.

## Stabilize the household while you arrange help

If the checks did not restore the system and you are now waiting on a technician, or waiting for morning, the next priority shifts from the equipment to the people. Keeping the household safe and reasonably comfortable buys you the time to make a calm hiring decision instead of a desperate one, and for a vulnerable household it is the part of this whole sequence that matters most. The measures differ for cold and heat, but the governing principle is identical: use safe, passive methods first, escalate to safe active methods, and never reach for a solution that trades a comfort problem for a carbon monoxide or fire problem.

### How do you keep a vulnerable household safe without heat or AC?

Prioritize the people most at risk: infants, older adults, anyone with a heart or breathing condition, and anyone on medication affected by temperature. Move them to the most stable room, layer or hydrate them by season, and if the indoor temperature is heading toward a dangerous range, relocate them rather than riding it out.

That priority ordering is the heart of it, because healthy adults tolerate a cold night or a hot afternoon that is genuinely dangerous for an infant, a frail older person, or someone with a chronic condition. Their bodies regulate temperature less effectively, they may not communicate distress clearly, and the tipping point from uncomfortable to medical arrives faster. So the first stabilize move is to identify who in the house is most at risk and make their safety the measure of how urgent the situation is. If keeping them safe in place is not working, the right answer is often to leave: a relative's house, a friend's, a hotel, or in genuine extremes a public warming or cooling center. Choosing to relocate is not an overreaction, it is frequently the smartest and cheapest response, since it converts a potential medical emergency into an inconvenience and lets you handle the repair on a normal schedule the next day.

Staying warm when the heat is out starts with trapping the warmth you have. Close off unused rooms and keep everyone in a single interior room, ideally one with few exterior walls and windows, because a smaller space with more bodies holds heat far better than a whole house. Close blinds and curtains at night to slow heat loss through the glass, and open south-facing curtains during the day to let in whatever solar warmth is available. Layering clothing beats a single heavy garment, since trapped air between layers is the insulation, and hats and thick socks matter more than people expect because the head and feet shed heat quickly. Warm drinks help, blankets and sleeping bags help, and gathering everyone, including pets, into one bed or one small room overnight is a genuinely effective way to ride out a cold night. If you have safe, permanently installed and vented backup heat, a properly maintained wood stove or a vented gas fireplace, that is a legitimate heat source; a decorative fireplace or anything that vents into the room is not.

The dangerous shortcuts are worth naming plainly because they are the ones that hurt people. A gas oven or range left open for warmth, a charcoal or propane grill brought indoors, a camp stove, or a generator run in the garage, a breezeway, or near an open window all produce carbon monoxide, and using any of them to heat living space is how a cold night becomes a poisoning. Portable electric space heaters are the acceptable active option, and even they carry a fire caveat: keep them at least a few feet from anything that can burn, place them on a hard level surface, never run them on an extension cord or power strip, and never leave one running unattended or while everyone sleeps. A generator, if you have one, belongs outdoors and well away from doors, windows, and vents, feeding the house through a properly installed transfer setup rather than a daisy-chain of cords; running a generator to keep a furnace's blower and controls powered can be reasonable, but only when the generator itself is outside and clear.

The cold case carries one extra hazard that has nothing to do with comfort: your plumbing. When the heat is out in a hard freeze, the pipes are on a clock of their own, and a burst pipe turns a no-heat night into a no-heat-plus-flood disaster. If the indoor temperature is dropping toward freezing and help is hours away, the freeze-prevention playbook, letting faucets on exterior walls drip, opening cabinet doors under sinks to let warmth reach the pipes, and knowing where your main water shutoff is in case a pipe does let go, comes straight from the plumbing side of the house, and the guide on [winterizing your plumbing to prevent freezes](/2001/02/15/winterizing-your-plumbing/) owns that territory in full. In a genuine deep freeze with no heat and a vulnerable household, the combined risk to people and pipes is one of the clearest call-now situations there is.

Staying cool when the AC is out follows the mirror-image logic, and the danger it guards against, heat illness, can escalate faster than most people realize. The first line of defense is the body, not the building: drink water steadily even before you feel thirsty, avoid alcohol and heavy meals that add internal heat, wear light loose clothing, and use cool showers or damp cloths on the neck, wrists, and forehead to shed heat. Then shape the space: close blinds and curtains on the sun-facing side during the day to keep radiant heat out, open windows and run fans at night when the outside air is cooler to flush the day's heat, and create cross-ventilation where you can. Fans help by moving air across skin, but there is a threshold worth knowing: once the air itself is very hot, a fan can push heat onto the body rather than cooling it, so in extreme heat a fan is not a substitute for actually cooler air. The single most reliable move in a serious heat wave with no cooling is to relocate the vulnerable members of the household to somewhere air-conditioned, a relative's house, a store, a library, a public cooling center, before anyone shows signs of heat illness.

Recognizing heat illness is the reason speed matters here. Heavy sweating, muscle cramps, weakness, dizziness, headache, and nausea point to heat exhaustion, which calls for cooling down, resting, and hydrating immediately. The graver stage, heat stroke, shows as a high body temperature, hot skin that may be dry, confusion, slurred speech, or loss of consciousness, and it is a medical emergency that warrants an immediate call for emergency help while you cool the person however you can. As with the cold, the smartest stabilize decision in real heat is frequently to leave rather than to endure, and choosing to relocate early, before the vulnerable person is in distress, is the move that keeps a broken AC from becoming a hospital visit.

## Is this a call-now or a wait-until-morning problem?

Not every dead furnace or silent air conditioner is a true emergency, and treating every one as if it were is exactly what the after-hours market counts on. The honest triage separates the situations where waiting endangers people or property from the ones where waiting only costs comfort, and the difference is usually large enough to save you an emergency premium if you read it correctly. The overarching rule: a hazard to life or a threat of fast property damage is a call-now, while a comfort loss in survivable conditions is a wait-until-morning, and most no-heat and no-AC events are the second kind dressed up as the first.

### Is no heat always an emergency?

No. No heat is a true emergency when it endangers people or pipes: a vulnerable household in a hard freeze, an indoor temperature falling toward the danger zone, or any gas or carbon monoxide concern. In milder conditions with a healthy household and no freeze risk, a dead furnace is urgent but survivable and can wait for a scheduled visit.

The clearest call-now cases share a common thread of genuine danger. Any suspected gas leak or carbon monoxide reading is a call-now to the utility and fire department before anything else, full stop. A hard freeze with a vulnerable person in the house, or an indoor temperature already dropping toward the point where pipes freeze and health is at risk, is a call-now because both people and plumbing are on a clock. A severe heat wave with an infant, an older adult, or someone with a medical condition in a house that is heating up fast is a call-now for the same reason: the exposure itself is the emergency, regardless of the equipment. And any burning smell, smoke, sparking, or repeated breaker trips at the equipment is a call-now, because those point at fire risk rather than a simple failure.

The wait-until-morning cases are the more common ones, and recognizing them is how you avoid the night rate. A furnace that quits on a mild evening with a healthy household, a house that holds a safe temperature overnight with the passive measures above, an air conditioner that fails on a warm-but-not-dangerous day, or a system you have already stabilized so that everyone is comfortable enough, none of these requires a technician at midnight. They require a call first thing in the morning, when standard rates apply and when you can actually compare who is available rather than taking whoever answers a paid line at 2 a.m. Choosing to wait is not neglect; it is the financially rational move whenever the conditions are survivable, and the whole point of the stabilize step is to make more situations survivable so that more of them can wait.

There is a middle category worth naming: the problem that is not dangerous tonight but should not sit for a week. A furnace that is short cycling, an AC that froze and thawed and cooled again, a system throwing an intermittent fault, these are not midnight emergencies, but they are signs of a system in trouble that deserves a prompt scheduled visit before the intermittent failure becomes a total one at the worst possible moment. The skill is matching your response to the actual risk: the true hazard gets the immediate call, the survivable comfort loss gets the morning call, and the warning-sign problem gets a scheduled visit soon. Reading which is which is the same triage judgment that the general home emergency triage guide applies across every household crisis, and the HVAC version simply plugs into it: safety first, property second, comfort third.

## Finding urgent HVAC help fast without the after-hours gouge

When the situation genuinely warrants a technician now, the challenge flips from diagnosis to sourcing, and this is precisely the moment the emergency search engine is designed to exploit. The results that greet a midnight search for urgent furnace or AC help are dominated by lead-generation pages and paid placements whose only goal is to route your panicked call to whoever pays the most for it, at whatever rate the moment will bear. Finding good help fast means having a short, deliberate method that does not depend on trusting the top of a search page.

The strongest position is one you set up before the emergency, which is why the maintenance-minded approach to this trade pays off at exactly the wrong-time moments. If you already have a company you have used and trust, their after-hours line is your first call, because an established relationship gives you a known rate structure and a technician who may already know your system. Keeping that contact, along with your equipment's make, model, and age, saved somewhere you can reach it in a crisis is the kind of small preparation that turns a frantic search into a single confident call. If you have no existing relationship, the goal is fast vetting under time pressure rather than no vetting at all. Even in a hurry, a few seconds of scrutiny separates a legitimate operator from an opportunist: confirm the company actually services your area and your type of equipment, get a clear statement of the after-hours dispatch or diagnostic fee before the truck rolls, and note whether they will give you a license or business identity you can hold them to rather than a nameless dispatch. There is also a public-utility angle that people forget in the heat of the moment. Some gas and electric utilities offer their own emergency response for suspected leaks and certain hazards, and that response is not a sales call; it is a safety service. When the problem is a suspected gas leak or a downed-power situation affecting your heat, the utility's emergency line is the right first number, and it is free. Reserve the paid emergency HVAC dispatch for genuine equipment failures that need a technician, and route the hazard situations to the utility and fire department, who will not upsell you and will treat the safety issue as the priority it is.

## How not to be overcharged in an HVAC crisis

The after-hours premium is real and, within limits, legitimate: a technician getting out of bed to drive to your house at 2 a.m. on a holiday weekend costs more than one on a Tuesday afternoon, and expecting otherwise is not reasonable. The problem is not that emergency work costs more; it is that a crisis is the ideal setting for overcharging, because a cold or overheated household with a scared family is a customer in no position to negotiate or comparison-shop. Protecting yourself is about keeping the few forms of leverage a crisis leaves you.

The single most protective habit is to separate the diagnosis from the repair. Insist on knowing the diagnostic or trip fee up front, let the technician diagnose the actual problem, and then get the proposed fix and its price before you authorize the work, rather than approving an open-ended "whatever it takes." Emergency diagnostic fees and after-hours rates vary widely by region and market and change over time, so the honest guidance is not a number but a method: ask what the visit costs before dispatch, ask what the repair costs before it starts, and treat any refusal to quote as the warning it is. Get local quotes on your normal-hours repairs so you know the going range in your area, because the best defense against a crisis overcharge is already knowing what the non-crisis version of the job costs.

The crisis also amplifies the classic HVAC upsell, and the most expensive version is the midnight push to replace the whole system. A technician who arrives for a no-heat call and, within minutes, is pressuring you to authorize a full furnace or AC replacement on the spot, tonight, at an emergency price, is running the play the consumer-protection guide on [HVAC scams and overcharging](/2002/11/01/hvac-scams-to-avoid/) documents in detail. A genuine emergency repair gets you safely through the night; the replace-or-repair decision is a daylight decision made with multiple quotes and a clear head, not a pressured 2 a.m. signature. Unless the equipment is a proven safety hazard that cannot be made safe, the correct response to a full-replacement pitch during a crisis is to authorize only what restores safe function now and to schedule the bigger decision for normal hours. ## Preventing the next no-heat or no-AC emergency

The best emergency is the one that never happens, and the majority of midnight HVAC failures are not bolts from the blue; they are the end of a story the system had been telling for weeks or months. A furnace that finally dies in January was usually short cycling, making new noises, or struggling in November. An air conditioner that quits in a July heat wave was often blowing warm, cycling oddly, or leaking water in June. Learning to read those early signals is what converts a future emergency into a scheduled repair on a mild day at a standard rate, and the guide on AC and furnace warning signs owns that catalog of what each sign means and how urgent it is, so a calm read of it now is an investment against a panicked search later.

The other half of prevention is upkeep, because most of the failures that strand a household on the worst night trace back to skipped maintenance. A filter changed on schedule prevents the airflow shutdowns that masquerade as breakdowns. An annual professional service catches the failing igniter, the weak capacitor, the low refrigerant, or the flue problem before it strands you, and it is also the check that finds a cracked heat exchanger, the carbon monoxide risk you cannot see. The seasonal rhythm of when to service, what to check, and what each task prevents is the province of the [HVAC maintenance schedule guide](/2002/07/01/hvac-maintenance-schedule/), and following it is the difference between a system that fails predictably during a tune-up and one that fails catastrophically during a cold snap. Prevention is not glamorous, but it is where the leverage is: a modest, predictable maintenance habit displaces the expensive, dangerous, panicked emergency almost entirely.

Preparation is the final layer, the part that assumes some emergency will eventually slip through despite good maintenance. Keeping a spare filter on hand, knowing where your breaker panel and furnace switch and main water shutoff are before you need them in the dark, having a working carbon monoxide alarm on every level of the home, and keeping your trusted company's contact and your equipment's details somewhere reachable all belong to this layer. The households that come through a no-heat or no-AC emergency well are almost never the ones that improvised brilliantly under pressure; they are the ones that did a small amount of ordinary preparation on a calm day and then simply ran the sequence.

## Reading the failure so you describe it accurately

You are not diagnosing the equipment yourself, but the few things you can observe change how urgent the situation is and how prepared a technician arrives, so it pays to read the failure rather than just registering that something is wrong. The distinction that matters most in a no-heat call is whether the system is completely dead or running but not heating, because those point at different kinds of problems and different levels of urgency.

A furnace that is entirely silent, with the thermostat calling for heat, the switch on, and the breaker holding, has lost power somewhere internally or hit a control-board or safety-lockout condition. That is a technician's diagnosis, but knowing it is fully dead rather than partially working tells the technician to bring different parts and helps you judge that this is unlikely to fix itself. A furnace that runs, with the blower pushing air, but delivers only cold air is a different story: the system has power and controls but the heat is not being made, which on a gas unit commonly traces to an ignition or flame-sensing issue, a safety lockout after failed ignition attempts, or a gas-supply interruption. None of those are homeowner repairs, but reporting cold air from a running system is far more useful to a dispatcher than saying only that the heat is out.

The repeating start-and-stop pattern, where the furnace fires up, runs briefly, then shuts down and tries again, is the one worth flagging because it has a homeowner-fixable cause hiding among the technician ones. That clogged filter can trigger it by starving airflow until a safety switch trips, so a filter swap is worth trying, but a blocked flue, an overheating limit switch, or a failing component can also drive it, and none of those should be pushed by forcing the system to keep cycling. If a clean filter does not stop the pattern, that is a prompt-service problem: not necessarily a midnight emergency in mild weather, but a system telling you it is in trouble and will likely fail completely at the worst time if ignored.

On the cooling side, the equivalent reads are just as useful. An outdoor unit that sits dead while the indoor blower runs points at the outdoor side losing power or a component failure out there, and the only part of that you should touch is a tripped breaker. Weak airflow from the vents points back at the filter and the return path. A system that runs constantly but never reaches the setpoint on a very hot day may simply be undersized for the extreme, or it may be low on refrigerant or fighting a dirty coil, and a coil that has iced over will not cool until it thaws. The catalog of what every one of these symptoms means, and precisely how urgent each is, belongs to the AC and furnace warning-signs guide, and this emergency guide leans on that owner rather than restating it; the job here is only to help you turn a vague "it's broken" into the two or three specific facts that speed a fix.

## The emergency premium and what a fair crisis charge looks like

Understanding the money side of a crisis call protects you from both overpaying and, less obviously, from refusing a legitimate service because the after-hours rate surprised you. Emergency HVAC work costs more than scheduled work for reasons that are real: a technician is coming out at night, on a weekend, or during a holiday, often into worse conditions, and that labor commands a premium the same way any after-hours professional service does. The honest position is not that emergency rates are a scam; it is that a crisis is where a dishonest operator has the easiest time turning a legitimate premium into an illegitimate gouge, and your protection is knowing how the charges are structured.

Emergency service typically carries some combination of a higher hourly labor rate, a dispatch or trip fee to get the truck moving, and a diagnostic fee to identify the problem, with parts on top of that. Because these vary enormously by region, by market conditions, and over time, no honest guide hands you a fixed dollar figure; what a guide can give you is the method for judging whether a specific charge is fair. The method is to get local quotes on ordinary, scheduled versions of common repairs while you are not in a crisis, so you know the normal-hours range in your area, and then to understand that the emergency version sits above that range by a premium, not at a wholly different order of magnitude. A charge that is modestly higher than the daytime version reflects a real after-hours premium; a charge that is several times the daytime version, or a demand to authorize a large sum before any diagnosis, is a signal to slow down.

The most reliable protective habits cost nothing. Insist on knowing the trip and diagnostic fees before the truck is dispatched, because a company confident in its pricing will state them and one that dodges is telling you something. Once the technician has diagnosed the problem, get the specific repair and its price before authorizing the work rather than approving an open-ended effort. Keep the diagnosis and the repair as separate decisions, so you are paying to find out what is wrong and then making an informed choice about the fix, not signing a blank check at your most vulnerable hour. Where a repair price seems steep, remember that the crisis limits but does not eliminate your options: a genuine emergency repair only needs to restore safe function through the night, after which the larger decisions can wait for daylight and comparison. The full anatomy of crisis-rate padding, phantom findings, and the pressure tactics that recur across every trade lives in the emergency service overcharging guide, and the HVAC-specific version of the pattern is documented in the HVAC scams and overcharging guide; both are worth reading before you ever need them, because the leverage they describe only works if you carry it into the moment.

## Renters, owners, and who is responsible in an HVAC emergency

Who fixes the problem, and who pays, depends heavily on whether you own or rent, and knowing your position before the crisis changes your first move. For a homeowner, the responsibility and the decision are yours, which is what the entire sequence above assumes: you run the checks, you decide urgency, you source and authorize the repair. Your protection is the fee-first, repair-not-replacement discipline and the record-keeping that lets you act fast.

For a renter, the calculus is different and usually more favorable, because in most residential rental situations the landlord is responsible for maintaining essential systems, and heat in cold weather is typically treated as an essential service under local housing standards. That means a no-heat emergency in a rental is often the landlord's or property manager's problem to fix, and your first call in many cases should be to them or to the emergency maintenance line the lease provides, not to a private HVAC company you would pay out of pocket. The safety checks still apply to you as the occupant: if you smell gas or a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, you get out and call the utility and fire department regardless of who owns the building, because life safety does not wait on a lease question. But for the repair itself, a renter's leverage runs through the landlord's legal obligation to maintain habitable conditions, and the specifics of what counts as an emergency, how fast a landlord must respond, and what recourse you have if they do not vary by state and locality, so confirming your local tenant rights and your lease terms before a crisis is the renter's version of preparation.

Both positions benefit from the same documentation habit for different reasons. An owner keeps equipment records, maintenance history, and contractor contacts to source and judge a repair quickly. A renter keeps a record of the outage, the time they reported it, the landlord's response, and the conditions in the unit, because if a landlord fails to restore an essential service, that documented timeline is the foundation of any remedy. In either case, the household most likely to come through an HVAC failure with the least cost and stress is the one that knew its responsibilities and had its information ready before the equipment quit.

## When the power is out: heat and cooling in an outage

A surprising share of no-heat and no-AC emergencies are not equipment failures at all; they are power outages, and treating an outage like a breakdown wastes a call on a problem no HVAC technician can fix. If the whole neighborhood is dark, or if lights and other appliances are also out, the problem is the grid, not your system, and the response shifts from repair to riding out the outage safely. Even a gas furnace will not run without electricity, because the blower, the ignition, and the controls all need power, so a fuel-heated home goes cold in an outage just as an electric-heated one does.

During an outage, the stabilize steps become the whole plan. In cold weather, that means the passive warmth measures, closing off rooms, layering, gathering into one space, protecting the pipes, and protecting the vulnerable, plus the safe-heat rules that outages make even more important, because outages are exactly when people reach for the dangerous improvised heat sources that cause carbon monoxide poisoning. A generator, used strictly outdoors and connected safely, can keep a furnace's blower and controls running; a grill or camp stove brought inside cannot be used for heat under any circumstances. In hot weather, an outage means the cooling measures, hydration, shade, cross-ventilation at night, and early relocation of at-risk people to somewhere with power and air conditioning, since a fan does nothing without electricity and the house will heat up steadily.

The utility, not an HVAC company, is your information source in an outage: reporting the outage and checking the estimated restoration time tells you whether this is a two-hour inconvenience or an overnight event that warrants relocating a vulnerable household. If your heat or cooling depends on medical equipment, an outage becomes a higher-priority situation, and many utilities maintain a medical-priority registry that you can sign up for in advance so that households with life-supporting equipment are flagged for faster restoration and advance notice of planned outages. That advance registration is another piece of calm-day preparation that pays off precisely when the power is out and the temperature is turning against you.

## Cold-climate and hot-climate emergencies are not the same

The same dead furnace or failed AC carries different stakes depending on where you live, because the climate sets how fast a comfort problem becomes a danger. In a cold climate, a no-heat night can move toward genuinely hazardous indoor temperatures and pipe-freezing risk within hours, so the margin for waiting is thinner and the freeze-prevention steps for your plumbing move up the priority list. Cold-climate households also tend to lean harder on combustion heating, which raises the carbon monoxide stakes and makes a working alarm and an annual heat-exchanger check non-negotiable rather than optional.

In a hot climate, the danger runs the other direction and can escalate even faster, because a house without cooling in a severe heat wave can reach dangerous indoor temperatures for infants, older adults, and people with health conditions in a short time, and heat stroke is a faster-moving medical emergency than most cold exposure. Humid climates add a further wrinkle, since high humidity both makes the heat feel worse and stresses cooling systems harder, so a marginal AC is more likely to fail exactly when it is needed most. The relocation-early rule carries more weight in a serious heat wave than almost anywhere else in this guide.

Home age and construction shift the picture too: an older, less-insulated house loses heat and gains heat faster than a tight, well-insulated one, shrinking the safe window in both directions, while a newer home may buy you more time to arrange a calm repair. The detailed treatment of how climate, region, and home characteristics change what your heating and cooling need in the first place belongs to the guide on HVAC needs by climate, which owns that territory; the emergency lesson that draws from it is simpler. Know your climate's failure clock. If you live where cold or heat turns dangerous quickly, your triage should lean toward acting sooner and relocating earlier, because the same failure that is a survivable annoyance in a mild region is a genuine emergency in an extreme one.

## Protecting pets and special-needs households

The stabilize step assumes a specific household, and two groups deserve their own attention because they are easy to overlook under stress. Pets feel temperature extremes as acutely as people and often cannot escape them, so they belong in the same shelter room during a cold night and the same cooling plan during a heat wave, with fresh water always available and with awareness that very young, very old, and short-nosed animals tolerate heat especially poorly. Bringing pets outside during a gas or carbon monoxide evacuation is not an afterthought; it is part of getting everyone out.

Households that depend on medical equipment, oxygen concentrators, powered mobility, refrigerated medication, or anything that fails without electricity or climate control, need a plan that treats an HVAC or power failure as a higher-tier emergency from the first minute. That plan includes registering with the utility's medical-priority program in advance where one exists, having a backup power arrangement worked out safely before it is needed, knowing where to relocate quickly, and keeping the household's essential-equipment details with the rest of the emergency information. For these households, the wait-until-morning category shrinks and the call-now or relocate-now response applies to more situations, because the equipment's needs, not just comfort, are on the line. The general principle that safety and health outrank property and comfort is the same one that governs every home crisis in the broader triage playbook; these households simply sit higher on it by default, and building their plan on a calm day is the single most protective thing they can do.

## The thermostat, the single biggest false alarm

Of every check in the sequence, the thermostat deserves the closest look, because it causes more false-alarm emergency calls than any other single item and because its failures are the easiest to mistake for a dead system. A thermostat sits between you and the equipment, and when it misbehaves the symptoms look identical to a breakdown: the system will not run, or runs at the wrong time, or does nothing while the house drifts cold or hot. Almost none of that requires a technician.

Start with the mode, because the most embarrassing and most common cause is simply the wrong setting. After a mild stretch, a thermostat left on cool when the weather turned cold, or on heat when it turned hot, sits idle by design, and the fix is a single tap. Confirm the setpoint next: the target temperature has to be above the current room reading for the system to call for heat, or below it to call for cooling, and a setpoint nudged by a curious child or bumped during cleaning can leave the system with no reason to run. Confirm whether the fan is set to auto or on, since a fan running on its own while no heating or cooling happens confuses people into thinking the system is working when only the fan is.

Power is the next layer. Battery-powered thermostats simply die, and a blank or faint screen is the tell; a fresh set of the correct batteries revives a dead-looking system more often than any repair. Wired thermostats draw power from the system itself through what is often called a common wire, and when that connection is marginal a thermostat can behave erratically or go dark, which loops back to the breaker and switch checks because it usually means power is not reaching the thermostat rather than a fault in the thermostat itself. Smart and programmable thermostats add their own failure modes: a schedule can hold the house at a setback temperature when you expected comfort, a program can override a manual change after a short hold, and a connected thermostat that has lost its network connection may stop responding as expected. Knowing whether yours is running a schedule, and how to override it to a simple hold, prevents a programmed setback from being mistaken for a broken furnace.

The reason all of this matters at 2 a.m. is leverage. A thermostat problem is the one failure in this whole guide you can very likely fix yourself in under two minutes, at no cost, with no tools and no hazard, and it is also the one an opportunistic operator is happy to let you pay an emergency rate to have a technician tap a button. Ruling the thermostat in or out first is the cheapest insurance against a wasted call there is, and if a correctly set, powered, calling thermostat still produces no response from the equipment, you have learned something genuinely useful: the problem is downstream, in the system itself, which is worth a technician's time.

## What a legitimate emergency visit looks like

Knowing what a competent emergency call actually involves lets you tell an honest technician from one cutting corners or padding the bill, and it steadies you to know roughly what should happen once help arrives. A legitimate visit begins with the technician confirming the safety picture, checking for gas or combustion issues, then diagnosing rather than assuming. They should look at the same points you did, thermostat, power, filter, and then go further into the equipment you should not open: the ignition system, the flame sensor, the control board, the capacitor, the contactor, the refrigerant charge, the airflow, depending on the symptoms. A technician who names a failed part and can show you why it failed is doing the job; one who declares a vague catastrophe and reaches straight for a replacement contract is not.

The honest emergency outcome is usually a repair that restores safe function through the night, a replaced igniter or flame sensor, a new capacitor, a reset of a tripped safety, a thawed and restarted coil, a cleared blockage, with any larger decision deferred. A competent technician will tell you plainly when the fix is temporary and a fuller repair or a replacement conversation should happen in daylight, and will not manufacture urgency around a system-replacement decision that does not need to be made at midnight. The corner cuts to watch for are the mirror image: skipping the diagnosis and jumping to the most expensive conclusion, quoting a replacement before establishing that a repair is impossible, adding parts or services that were not discussed, or pressuring you to sign for major work while you are cold, tired, and scared.

You keep the upper hand by staying in the loop. Ask what the technician found, ask to see the failed part where practical, ask what the specific repair is before it happens, and ask what it costs. A trustworthy operator answers all four without friction, because their business depends on the next call and the referral, not on maximizing this one crisis. The detail of what a good job looks like across the trade, and how upsells and corner cuts show up, connects to the broader vetting and consumer-protection material, but the emergency-night version reduces to this: a real fix, clearly explained, priced before it happens, with the big decisions held for daylight.

## After the emergency: records, recovery, and stopping the repeat

When the crisis passes, a short amount of follow-through turns a bad night into protection against the next one and, sometimes, into money recovered. Write down what happened while it is fresh: the date, the symptom, what you checked, who you called, what they found, what they charged, and what parts they replaced. That record matters for more than memory. If the failed component is under a manufacturer's warranty, documentation of the failure and the repair is what supports a claim, and many HVAC parts carry warranties that go unclaimed simply because no one kept the paperwork. If the emergency involved a rental and a landlord's slow response, the timeline is the foundation of any tenant remedy. If it involved a possible carbon monoxide event, the record and a professional inspection of the heat exchanger and venting are safety essentials, not optional.

Recovery also means confirming the fix held and the root cause is understood. An emergency repair that got you through the night is not always the end of the story; a component that failed because of an underlying problem, a chronic airflow restriction, a venting issue, an aging system near the end of its service life, will fail again if only the symptom was addressed. This is the point to have the daylight conversation the crisis deferred: whether this system is worth continuing to repair or whether a planned replacement, decided with multiple quotes and a clear head rather than under midnight pressure, is the smarter path. That repair-or-replace judgment is its own decision with its own logic, and making it calmly, on your schedule, is exactly what refusing the crisis-night replacement pitch preserved for you.

The most valuable follow-through is turning the emergency into prevention. A failure is a data point about your system's health, and the households that stop having emergencies are the ones that respond to a scare by getting on a maintenance rhythm, learning the warning signs their system shows before it quits, and preparing the information and supplies that make the next event a controlled sequence instead of a panic. Keeping the repair record, the equipment details, the maintenance schedule, and the trusted-contractor contacts together in a home-records companion means the lesson of this emergency is retained and ready, so that if something does fail again, you meet it as someone who has a plan rather than someone starting a frantic search from zero. The whole arc of this guide, safety first, simple checks, stabilize, triage, protected sourcing, and prevention, is at its most powerful when the last step feeds back into the first, and each emergency you handle well makes the next one less likely and less costly.

## Why systems fail on the first cold day and the first hot day

There is a reason emergency lines light up on the first hard freeze of the season and the first serious heat wave, and understanding it helps you avoid being one of those calls. Heating and cooling equipment often sits idle for months, and the first heavy demand of the season is the moment a marginal component finally gives out. A furnace that limped through the previous cold season, or an air conditioner that was low on refrigerant when it was shut down, does not announce the problem while it rests; it announces it the first time it is asked to run hard, which is precisely when the weather makes the failure most dangerous and when every technician in the area is already swamped.

That clustering is why the single most effective emergency-avoidance move happens off-season. A furnace serviced in early fall, before the first freeze, gets its failing igniter, weak flame sensor, cracked heat exchanger, or airflow problem caught while the weather is mild and technicians are available at standard rates. An air conditioner serviced in spring, before the first heat wave, gets its low charge, tired capacitor, or dirty coil addressed before it is asked to run against a hundred-degree afternoon. The households that never seem to have midnight emergencies are usually not lucky; they are the ones who put their system through a check-up during the shoulder seasons, so that the component destined to fail did so on a technician's bench in October rather than in your living room in January. The seasonal rhythm of what to service and when is owned by the HVAC maintenance schedule guide, and the emergency payoff of following it is enormous: it moves the failure from the worst possible night to a scheduled, cheap, safe daytime visit.

The same seasonal logic should shape your preparation calendar. Before the first cold, confirm your carbon monoxide alarms work, replace the furnace filter, locate the furnace switch and the water shutoff, and make sure your trusted contractor's after-hours number is saved. Before the first heat, replace the filter again, clear debris from around the outdoor unit, and identify where you would relocate a vulnerable household member if the AC failed during a heat wave. This is not busywork; it is the difference between meeting the season's first stress test prepared and meeting it at 2 a.m. with a search engine. Treating the shoulder seasons as your emergency-prevention windows is the calm-day discipline that quietly eliminates most of the crises this guide exists to help you survive.

## What to say when you make the call

When you have decided the situation genuinely needs a technician, the call itself goes better and cheaper when you run it deliberately rather than leading with panic. Open by stating the safety picture, that there is no gas smell, no carbon monoxide alarm sounding, no smoke or sparking, because a responsible dispatcher wants to know immediately that this is an equipment call and not a hazard evacuation. Then describe the failure with the specifics you gathered: whether the system is completely dead or running but not heating or cooling, what you already checked, thermostat, breaker, switch, filter, and any pattern you noticed, such as short cycling or ice on the coil. Those facts let the company send the right technician with the likely parts and shorten the visit.

Before you agree to a truck, get the money clear. Ask what the after-hours dispatch or trip fee is and what the diagnostic fee is, and ask for those in plain numbers, not a vague "we'll take care of you." A company that answers directly is showing you good faith; one that dodges the fee question is telling you to be careful. Confirm the company services your area and your type of equipment so you are not paying a trip fee for a truck that cannot help, and get the business name and, where relevant, a license or identifying detail so you are dealing with an accountable operator rather than an anonymous dispatch. If the person on the phone pushes you to authorize a major repair or a full replacement before anyone has even seen the equipment, treat that as the warning it is and consider calling someone else.

When the technician arrives, keep the same posture: let them diagnose, then ask what they found, ask to understand the specific repair, and get its price before the work starts. Authorize the repair that restores safe function, and defer any large replacement decision to daylight unless the equipment is a proven hazard that cannot be made safe. This is not about being difficult; a good technician expects an informed customer and works comfortably with one. It is about carrying your small store of crisis leverage, the fee-first rule, the diagnosis-before-repair rule, the repairs-now-decisions-later rule, all the way through the call, so that when the night is over you paid a fair price for a real fix rather than an inflated one for a manufactured emergency. Keeping a simple version of this call script with your emergency information, so you are reading from a plan rather than improvising while cold or overheated, is the last piece of preparation that turns the worst night into a managed one.

## The one rule to carry into the crisis

If everything else fades under stress, hold onto the check-safe-then-simple rule, because it is the whole guide compressed into a single sentence you can act on: before you dial an emergency number, rule out a gas or carbon monoxide hazard, then check the thermostat, the breaker, the switch, and the filter, because a large share of no-heat and no-AC panics are a setting or a tripped breaker, not a breakdown. That rule protects you from the two failures that hurt households, the deadly one of troubleshooting through a gas leak and the expensive one of paying a night rate for a five-minute reset, and it does so in the order a stressed person can actually follow.

Everything downstream of that rule is judgment you can now make with a clear head. If the checks restore the system, you saved a call. If they do not, you stabilize the people first, protecting the vulnerable and the pipes, then decide honestly whether the danger to life or property makes this a call-now or whether survivable conditions let it wait for morning and normal rates. When you do call, you source help on your terms, fee before dispatch, repair price before authorizing, big decisions held for daylight, so the crisis market cannot turn your fear into an inflated bill. And when it is over, you record what happened and turn it into prevention, because the maintenance rhythm and the early warning signs are what keep the next season's first cold or hot day from becoming another midnight scramble.

A dead furnace in a freeze or a failed AC in a heat wave will always feel like an emergency, and sometimes it genuinely is one. The difference between the households that come through these nights safely and cheaply and the ones that get scared, overcharged, or hurt is almost never nerve or luck. It is a sequence, learned on a calm day and run when it counts, that puts safety first, simple checks second, people third, the decision fourth, and the call last. Keep the sequence where you can find it, keep your information ready, and the worst night becomes a managed one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What should you do in an HVAC emergency?

Work one sequence in order. First, rule out a gas leak or carbon monoxide; if either is present, get everyone and pets outside and call the gas utility and fire department from outside, without flipping switches. If the air is safe, run the simple checks: thermostat mode and batteries, the breaker, the furnace switch, and the filter, since these resolve most calls at no cost. If the system is still dead, stabilize the household, protecting infants, older adults, and anyone with a medical condition first, then decide whether the situation endangers people or pipes. A true hazard is a call-now; a survivable comfort loss can wait for morning and normal rates. Only then call for help, confirming the fee before dispatch and the repair price before authorizing work.

### Q: What do you do right now if your furnace stops working?

Before assuming a breakdown, confirm it is not a false alarm or a hazard. If you smell gas at any point, stop, leave, and call from outside. Otherwise, check the thermostat first: correct mode, a setpoint above room temperature, and fresh batteries if the screen is dim or blank. Next, look at the electrical panel for a tripped breaker and reset it once; if it trips again, leave it and treat it as a pro problem. Then check the furnace's light-switch-style power switch, which gets bumped off surprisingly often, and inspect the air filter, since a clogged one triggers a safety shutdown. These four checks fix a large share of no-heat events. If the furnace is still dead with everything set right, stabilize the household and decide whether the conditions make this an immediate call or a morning one.

### Q: What do you do if your AC quits during a heat wave?

Treat the heat itself as the emergency, not just the equipment, because heat illness moves faster than people expect. Get water into everyone, move at-risk people, infants, older adults, and anyone with a health condition, to the coolest space, and relocate them somewhere air-conditioned before they show distress if the house is climbing. Then run the quick checks: thermostat set to cool below room temperature, a reset of any tripped breaker, and the filter, since a choked filter can freeze the coil. If you see ice on the coil or lines, switch the system off with the fan on to thaw it before running it again. If none of that restores cooling and the heat is dangerous, that is a call-now; in survivable heat with everyone safe, a morning call at standard rates is the smarter move.

### Q: How do you stay warm if your furnace fails?

Trap the heat you have and add only safe warmth. Close off unused rooms and gather everyone, including pets, into one small interior room with few windows, since bodies and a smaller space hold heat far better than a whole house. Layer clothing rather than wearing one heavy piece, because trapped air between layers is the real insulation, and keep heads and feet covered. Close curtains at night, open sun-facing ones by day, and use blankets and warm drinks. A properly installed and vented wood stove or gas fireplace is a legitimate heat source; a portable electric space heater is acceptable with care. Never burn anything unvented indoors, no oven, grill, or camp stove for heat, because that is how a cold night becomes carbon monoxide poisoning. If the cold is turning dangerous for anyone vulnerable, relocating to a warm place is the smartest move.

### Q: How do you stay cool if your AC breaks down?

Cool the body first, then the space. Drink water steadily before you feel thirsty, skip alcohol and heavy meals, wear light loose clothing, and use cool showers or damp cloths on the neck and wrists. Block the sun by closing blinds and curtains on the sunny side during the day, and open windows with fans at night when outside air is cooler to flush the day's heat. Fans help by moving air over skin, but once the air itself is very hot they push heat rather than relief, so a fan is not a substitute for genuinely cooler air in extreme conditions. The most reliable move in a serious heat wave is to move vulnerable people to somewhere air-conditioned, a relative's home, a store, a library, or a public cooling center, before anyone shows signs of heat exhaustion or heat stroke.

### Q: Is a furnace that stops in winter an emergency?

It depends on who is in the house and how cold it is getting. A dead furnace becomes a true emergency when it endangers people or property: a vulnerable household in a hard freeze, an indoor temperature falling toward the range where pipes freeze, or any gas or carbon monoxide concern. In those cases, act now. But a furnace that quits on a mild evening with a healthy household that can hold a safe temperature overnight with layering and a closed-off room is an urgent-but-survivable problem, not a midnight crisis. That version can wait for a morning call at standard rates, which also lets you compare who is available rather than taking whoever answers a paid after-hours line. The deciding factors are the severity of the cold, the freeze risk to your pipes, and the vulnerability of the people relying on the heat.

### Q: Who do you call for an emergency furnace repair at night?

Your first call should be a company you already know and trust, using their after-hours line, because an existing relationship means a known rate structure and a technician who may already understand your system. If you have no such contact, vet a new company fast rather than not at all: confirm they service your area and your equipment, get the after-hours diagnostic or dispatch fee stated clearly before the truck rolls, and make sure you are dealing with a nameable, accountable business rather than an anonymous dispatch. If the real problem is a suspected gas leak or carbon monoxide, the correct first call is not a paid HVAC line at all but your gas utility's emergency number and the fire department, both of which respond around the clock as a safety service and will not try to sell you a system in the middle of the night.

### Q: What should you check before calling for emergency HVAC help?

Run the safety check and the four simple checks first, because they resolve most calls and they let you describe the problem accurately if you do need a pro. Safety comes first: no gas smell, no carbon monoxide alarm, no burning smell or sparking. Then confirm the thermostat is in the right mode, set past room temperature, and powered, replacing the batteries if the screen is dim. Check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker and reset it once. Check the furnace's power switch, which is easy to bump off. Inspect the air filter and swap a clogged one for a clean one. If the system is still dead after all of that, you have a real service issue, and you will also have the specific facts, what runs, what does not, what you already tried, that let a technician arrive prepared instead of guessing.

### Q: How long can a house stay warm without heat before pipes freeze?

There is no single number, because it depends on outdoor temperature, how well the house is insulated, where the pipes run, and how fast the heat is lost, so treat it as a risk to monitor rather than a countdown you can trust. Pipes in exterior walls, unheated basements, crawl spaces, and attics are the first to freeze, and the danger climbs the longer the indoor temperature sits below freezing. Rather than guess at hours, take the protective steps: let faucets on exterior walls drip to keep water moving, open cabinet doors under sinks so warmth reaches the pipes, and know where your main water shutoff is in case a pipe bursts. In a hard freeze with heat out and pipes at risk, that combination of people and plumbing danger is a clear reason to call now or relocate, and the plumbing-side freeze-prevention guide covers the details.

### Q: Is it safe to use a space heater when your furnace fails?

A portable electric space heater is the acceptable active heat source in a no-heat situation, but only with real care, because space heaters are a leading cause of home fires. Keep it at least a few feet from anything that can burn, including bedding, curtains, and furniture, and place it on a hard, level surface, not carpet or a rug that can trap heat. Plug it directly into a wall outlet, never into an extension cord or power strip, which can overheat and start a fire. Never leave a space heater running unattended or while everyone sleeps, and keep children and pets away from it. What is not safe is any fuel-burning heater used indoors without proper venting, a kerosene heater in a closed room, a gas oven left open, or a grill brought inside, all of which produce carbon monoxide. Electric and careful is the rule.

### Q: Can you power a furnace with a portable generator during an outage?

Sometimes, but only if the generator is used safely, and the safety rules are strict because generators kill people every year through carbon monoxide. A generator must run outdoors, well away from doors, windows, and vents, never in a garage, breezeway, or basement, even with the door open, because its exhaust is carbon monoxide and it accumulates fast in enclosed space. Feeding a furnace's blower and controls from a generator can be reasonable during an outage, but the connection should go through a properly installed transfer switch rather than a tangle of extension cords, and the furnace's electrical draw has to be within the generator's capacity. If you are unsure how to connect it safely, that setup is worth arranging with a licensed electrician before an outage, not improvising during one. Run outdoors, connect correctly, and keep a carbon monoxide alarm working inside.

### Q: What should you do right now if you suspect carbon monoxide?

Treat it as immediate and get out. If a carbon monoxide alarm sounds or several people feel sick indoors, headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, or drowsiness, and better in fresh air, get everyone and pets outside right away and call from outside. Do not wait to confirm it, because carbon monoxide is colorless and odorless and feels like nothing until it is a medical emergency. If anyone is unconscious or badly affected, move them to fresh air and call for emergency medical help immediately. Once outside, call the fire department or your gas utility's emergency line and let them clear the house before anyone returns; do not go back in to open windows or hunt for the source. Carbon monoxide comes from incomplete combustion, so a furnace with a cracked heat exchanger or a blocked flue can produce it while appearing to run normally, which is exactly why a working alarm on every level matters.

### Q: What belongs in a home HVAC emergency plan?

The information and supplies that turn a panicked search into a calm sequence. Keep a note of your trusted HVAC company's after-hours contact, plus your equipment's make, model, and age, somewhere you can reach in the dark. Know the locations of your electrical panel, the furnace power switch, and the main water shutoff before you need them. Have a working carbon monoxide alarm on every level of the home and a spare air filter of the correct size on hand. Keep the action sequence itself, safety check, thermostat, breaker, switch, filter, stabilize, assess, call, written where the household can find it. For heat, know your safe warmth options; for cooling, know the nearest place with air conditioning. Storing all of this together in a home-records tool means the plan exists before the crisis and you are reaching for it rather than assembling it under stress.

### Q: Should you keep your AC breaker off after it trips repeatedly?

If a breaker trips once, reset it a single time; a nuisance trip from a momentary surge can happen. But if it trips again right away or within a few minutes, leave it off and stop resetting it, because a breaker that keeps tripping is doing its job by cutting power to a real fault. Repeatedly forcing it back on can overheat the wiring and create a fire risk, so a repeat trip is a stop-and-call situation, not a keep-trying one. The underlying cause could be a failing compressor or capacitor, a short, a ground fault, or a circuit drawing more current than it should, and diagnosing that safely takes a licensed technician, since the failure modes here include shock and fire. Leave the breaker off to keep the circuit de-energized, run your cooling stabilization steps for the household, and schedule a professional to find and fix the fault rather than fighting the breaker.
