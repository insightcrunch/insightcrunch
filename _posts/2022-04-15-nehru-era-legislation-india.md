Nehru-era legislation is usually described as if it were a single project, the building of a planned economy, and the description is not wrong so much as it is incomplete to the point of distortion. Between the commencement of the Constitution in January 1950 and the death of Jawaharlal Nehru in May 1964, the Indian Parliament and the provisional legislature that preceded it were running four distinct legislative programmes at the same time, each with its own statutes, its own opposition and its own timetable. The first programme adjusted the Constitution itself, amending the fundamental rights when they obstructed the rest of the agenda. The second built the planned economy, a structure of licensing, control, public ownership and fiscal extraction. The third reformed society through law, codifying the personal law of the majority community and giving penal force to the constitutional abolition of untouchability. The fourth built the state itself: a reorganised map of States, an electoral machine, a citizenship code, a detention power and the legal shell of a strategic science establishment.

The four programmes were not sequential phases. They were simultaneous, and they interfered with one another constantly. Land reform belonged to the economic programme, but it could not proceed until the constitutional programme had neutralised the property right that the courts were using to strike abolition statutes down. The codification of Hindu law belonged to the social programme, but it consumed the parliamentary time and political capital of the same legislature that was passing the First Five Year Plan's supporting statutes. The reorganisation of the States belonged to state building, but it required a constitutional amendment of its own and redrew the constituencies through which every other programme had to be legislated. A reader who holds the four programmes apart, and can say which statute belongs to which, understands the period's statute book in a way that the single label of the planned state does not permit.

That is the test this article sets itself. It is a legislative record, not an assessment of a man or of a party. Nehru appears in it as the head of the government that piloted these measures, and where his stated reasons matter they are attributed to him; the arguments against the record are stated in the terms their proponents used, with names attached. What the article claims, and what its closing sections argue from the record itself, is a finding about durability. The economic model of the period, licensing, planning and public ownership as the organising ideas of the economy, has been substantially dismantled. The statutes that carried the model have largely not been repealed. And the social and state building legislation of the same years, the personal law codes, the citizenship statute, the representation of the people statutes and the reorganisation settlement, remains the operative law with amendments. The period's legislative output has proved far more durable than its economic programme, and that asymmetry is visible only when the four programmes are separated.

## Nehru-era legislation and the four programmes

The first programme, constitutional adjustment, was forced on the government rather than chosen in the abstract. The Constitution that commenced on 26 January 1950 made property a fundamental right, guaranteed freedom of speech in terms the Supreme Court read narrowly, and prohibited discrimination in terms that a court applied against caste-based reservation in admissions. Within eighteen months each of those positions had produced a judgment or a line of litigation that blocked a settled government policy. The response was the Constitution (First Amendment) Act of 1951, which widened the permissible restrictions on speech, inserted an enabling clause for special provision for backward classes, and created the device of the Ninth Schedule to place specified laws beyond fundamental rights challenge. The programme continued with the Fourth Amendment of 1955, which took the adequacy of compensation for compulsory acquisition out of the courts, and the Seventh Amendment of 1956, which rewrote the constitutional map to accompany the reorganisation of the States. Constitutional amendment, in this period, was not an exceptional instrument reserved for rare settlements. It was a routine tool of governance, used whenever a judicial reading of the text obstructed a programme the government intended to carry through.

The second programme, the planned economy, was the one the period is named for, and its instruments are the most familiar. The Industries (Development and Regulation) Act of 1951 subjected the establishment and expansion of scheduled industry to licence. The Essential Commodities Act of 1955 armed the executive with a general power to control the production, supply and distribution of listed commodities. The Companies Act of 1956 rebuilt the law of the corporate form through which private capital was permitted to operate. The Mines and Minerals (Regulation and Development) Act of 1957 declared Union control over the mineral estate. The Income-tax Act of 1961, the Wealth-tax Act of 1957 and the Estate Duty Act of 1953 built the fiscal frame, and the Employees' Provident Funds Act of 1952 attached a measure of social security to industrial employment. Over the whole structure stood the Planning Commission, directing the allocation of investment through Five Year Plans, and it is the period's most striking institutional fact that this body was created by a resolution of the Cabinet in March 1950 and was never given statutory status at any point in its existence.

The third programme, social reform, was the one that cost the government the most politically relative to its size. Its centre was the attempt to codify Hindu personal law: a single code drafted before independence, introduced in 1948, stalled by opposition inside and outside the ruling party, and finally enacted after the first general election as four separate statutes in 1955 and 1956, covering marriage, succession, minority and guardianship, and adoptions and maintenance. Beside it stood the Untouchability (Offences) Act of 1955, which converted the constitutional abolition of untouchability into punishable offences, and the Special Marriage Act of 1954, which provided a civil form of marriage open to any two persons irrespective of religion. The programme's limits are as much a part of the record as its achievements. The reform statutes applied to Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs and not to other communities, and the directive principle that contemplates a uniform civil code was not acted upon, so the period codified the personal law of one part of the population and left the rest where it stood.

The fourth programme, state building, supplied the machinery the other three presupposed. The Representation of the People Acts of 1950 and 1951 constructed the electoral system and made the first general election administrable. The Citizenship Act of 1955 filled in the code that the Constitution had deliberately left to Parliament. The States Reorganisation Act of 1956, with the Seventh Amendment, replaced the inherited patchwork of provinces and princely unions with States drawn substantially on linguistic lines. The Preventive Detention Act of 1950 gave the new republic a power to detain without trial, hedged, significantly, with an expiry date. And the Atomic Energy Act of 1948, inherited from the Dominion legislature and carried into the republic, placed the strategic mineral and research estate under state control, administered by a commission that, like the Planning Commission, rested on executive resolution rather than statute.

Read as one list, these statutes look miscellaneous. Read as four programmes, they form a coherent account of what a founding legislature thought it had to do: settle the constitutional terms on which reform could proceed, construct an economy the state could direct, reform the social law of the majority community, and equip the republic with the ordinary machinery of a state. The sections that follow take the conditions first, then each programme in turn, and then the two assessments of the whole record that have competed ever since, before the article states the verdict the record supports.

## The conditions: a republic legislating from scarcity

The legislature that enacted this programme was not working from a position of institutional comfort. The Constitution had commenced in January 1950, two and a half years after a partition that had divided the administrative services, the irrigation systems and the food-producing regions of the undivided country, and had driven millions of displaced persons across the new borders into the care of a state that had to house, feed and settle them. The economy the legislators inherited was overwhelmingly agrarian, with low rates of capital formation, a narrow industrial base concentrated in a few cities, and a chronic dependence on food imports that made the supply of grain a question of state survival rather than of trade policy. The agenda itself had a lineage older than the Constitution. The Karachi Resolution of 1931, by which the Indian National Congress at its Karachi session committed itself to a charter of fundamental rights and an economic programme, had promised civil liberties, a living wage, state ownership or control of key industries and services, and the protection of labour, two decades before the party was in a position to legislate any of it. The Directive Principles of State Policy, which the Constituent Assembly had placed in Part IV of the Constitution as instructions that were fundamental in the governance of the country though not enforceable by any court, read in consequence less like aspirations than like a legislative agenda awaiting a statute book: the state was directed to secure a social order promoting welfare, to distribute the ownership and control of material resources to subserve the common good, and to prevent the concentration of wealth. The programmes described in this article are, in large part, those instructions translated into Acts.

The institutional vehicle was itself improvised. Until the first general election, the Constituent Assembly doubled as the legislature of the Dominion and then of the republic, so a substantial part of the founding statute book, including the Preventive Detention Act of 1950, the Representation of the People Acts and the First Amendment itself, was enacted by a body that had been elected indirectly, on a restricted franchise, for the different purpose of framing a Constitution. That fact was not lost on contemporaries and it returns in the assessments section of this article: the President, Rajendra Prasad, questioned whether an unelected provisional Parliament was the proper body to enact a measure as contested as the Hindu Code Bill, and critics of the First Amendment made the same point about the amendment of fundamental rights by a legislature no voter had chosen for that purpose. The government's answer, in substance, was that the work could not wait for an election that itself depended on statutes the provisional legislature had to pass first, since the electoral rolls, the delimitation of constituencies and the conduct of elections all required the Representation of the People Acts. The circularity was real, and the record does not dissolve it; it simply shows which way the founding generation resolved it.

The first general election of 1951 and 1952 changed the footing of the programme without changing its content. Held on universal adult franchise under the new statutes, it returned the Congress party with a large majority in the Lok Sabha and Nehru campaigned in it, among other things, on the promise to carry the Hindu code through Parliament. The social reform programme's second life, in 1955 and 1956, is unintelligible without that electoral fact: measures that could not be carried by an unelected assembly were carried by an elected one claiming a mandate for them. The economic programme, by contrast, had never waited for a mandate. The Planning Commission was constituted in March 1950, the First Five Year Plan ran from 1951, and the licensing statute was enacted in October 1951, all before the first votes under the Constitution had been counted. The sequence matters for the assessments. The economic architecture was the work of the provisional period and of executive decision, subsequently ratified by electoral success; the social codification was the work of the elected Parliament; the constitutional adjustments were divided between the two, the First Amendment belonging to the provisional legislature and the Fourth and Seventh to the elected one.

One more condition shaped the style of the whole record: the government legislated against judicial decisions in something close to real time. The interval between the Supreme Court's judgment in the Champakam Dorairajan case in April 1951 and the enactment of the First Amendment in June of the same year was a matter of weeks. The Fourth Amendment followed within a year of the Bela Banerjee decision on compensation. This was possible because the amending power was understood at the time, and was confirmed by the Supreme Court in the Shankari Prasad case in 1951, to extend to the fundamental rights themselves, and because the ruling party's majority in the amending Houses was not in doubt. The legislative style of the era, its confidence that any obstruction in the text could be removed by amendment, its preference for schedules and lists over case by case defence, and its readiness to legislate first and litigate afterwards, all belong to this condition. When later Parliaments found the amending power fenced by the basic structure doctrine, the style became impossible; but the statutes enacted while it lasted remained on the book, which is why the era's record has to be read with its conditions in view.

The first call on the new legislature, before any of the four programmes could begin, was the human residue of partition. The Administration of Evacuee Property Act of 1950, Act 31 of 1950, placed the property left behind by those who had migrated in the hands of a Custodian, and the Displaced Persons (Compensation and Rehabilitation) Act of 1954, Act 44 of 1954, built the machinery by which displaced persons were compensated from the evacuee property pool and settled on land and in townships across northern and western India. These statutes belong to no programme and to all of them: they were the republic's first welfare legislation, administered by a dedicated ministry, and they consumed legislative and administrative energy on a scale that the cleaner programme statutes never had to face. They also established, at the very start, the era's working assumption that displacement, scarcity and entitlement were matters for statute and tribunal rather than for the market or for charity, an assumption the four programmes then generalised to the whole economy and society.

## The legislative style of the era

A word about method, because the period's legislative style is part of its record and explains the shape of everything the programmes produced. The era legislated in long sessions and at length: major Bills were introduced with detailed statements of objects and reasons, were frequently referred to select committees or joint committees of the two Houses, and emerged from committee with their architecture altered, the Hindu Code Bill passing through a select committee under its own Law Minister and the companies legislation being preceded by an expert committee under H. C. Bhabha before it reached the House. The ordinance power of Article 123 was available and was used, but the characteristic instrument of the period was not the ordinance; it was the full-dress Act, debated over sessions, because the government possessed the majority to pass Acts and preferred the permanence and legitimacy of enacted law for the structures it was building. Delegated legislation grew inside the Acts rather than instead of them: the control orders under the essential commodities statute, the schemes under the provident fund statute and the rules under the licensing statute formed a second tier of law-making that the Acts authorised in advance, and much of what a citizen experienced as the control regime was this second tier, executive law under statutory licence.

Two further features of the style deserve record. The first is the use of the schedule as a legislative instrument in its own right: the First Schedule of industries under the licensing Act, the schedules of commodities, the schedules of the Constitution itself, and at the summit the Ninth Schedule, all express the same preference for governing by list, for settling a class of cases by enumeration rather than by the litigation of each member of the class. The second is the presence, throughout the period, of a Law Ministry and a drafting establishment inherited from the colonial legislative department, which gave the era's statutes a technical finish that their political contents did not always deserve but that contributed to their durability; a well-drafted statute is harder to displace than a badly drafted one, because every replacement must first match its precision. The era's statutes were, for the most part, carefully drawn instruments, and the care is part of why so many of them are still in the hands of the courts.

## Programme one: constitutional adjustment

The First Amendment is the founding document of the adjustment programme, and its contents are best understood as three repairs made in a single operation, each answering a specific judicial obstruction. The first repair concerned the freedom of speech. Article 19(2), as commenced, permitted reasonable restrictions on the freedom of speech and expression only in relation to a short list of subjects, and it did not include public order. In 1950 the Supreme Court, in Romesh Thappar v State of Madras and in Brij Bhushan v State of Delhi, struck down restrictions on publication that the governments of the day had imposed in the name of public order, holding in substance that the freedom could be restricted only on the grounds the clause actually named. The amendment rewrote the clause. It made the restrictions expressly reasonable, which imported judicial review of their proportionality in the form the doctrine then took, and it added public order, friendly relations with foreign States, and incitement to an offence to the permitted grounds, while recasting the older ground of the security of the state. The second repair concerned equality. The Madras government maintained a communal order apportioning seats in state medical and engineering colleges among communities, and in State of Madras v Champakam Dorairajan, decided in April 1951, the Supreme Court held the order void as discriminating on grounds of caste and religion under Articles 15(1) and 29(2). The amendment inserted Article 15(4), providing that nothing in Article 15 or in Article 29(2) prevents the state from making any special provision for the advancement of any socially and educationally backward classes of citizens or for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes. The third repair concerned property, and it was the most consequential of the three.

The property repair answered the litigation that threatened the centre-piece of the government's social and economic programme, the abolition of the zamindari and comparable intermediary tenures. The States had enacted abolition statutes in the first years of the republic, Bihar among the earliest, and the former intermediaries challenged them under Article 31, which guaranteed that no person be deprived of property save by authority of law and that compulsory acquisition be for a public purpose and on payment of compensation. The Patna High Court struck the Bihar statute down on the ground of discrimination in the compensation it offered to different classes of proprietor. Appeals from Bihar, Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh were pending together, and the pattern of the litigation made the government's difficulty plain: abolition would have to be defended statute by statute, provision by provision, in litigation that could run for years while the tenures it had abolished remained in legal suspension. The amendment attacked the problem at two depths. Article 31A protected specified categories of law, acquisitions of estates and rights in estates, the taking over of management of property for a limited period, the amalgamation of corporations and the modification of the rights of their officers and shareholders, and the extinguishment or modification of mining leases, from challenge under Articles 14, 19 and 31. Article 31B went further and is examined in the next section. A fourth change, quieter than the others, amended Article 19(6) to make clear that the freedom to carry on a trade or business is no obstacle to a law creating a state monopoly in any trade or business, a provision the economic programme would rely on when it moved to nationalisation. The amendment received assent on 18 June 1951. Its remaining provisions, less argued about since, adjusted the working of the legislatures themselves, recasting Articles 85 and 174 so that six months could not intervene between the last sitting of a session and the first sitting of the next, and confining the President's and the Governors' special addresses under Articles 87 and 176 to the first session after each general election and the first session of each year; and it touched the specification of the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes under Articles 341 and 342. The debate on the Bill in the provisional Parliament set the terms in which the programme has been argued ever since. Nehru, moving the measure, argued that the Constitution could not be treated as a fixed and permanent text standing above the elected will of the community, and that a judiciary which struck down the social legislation of Parliament on the strength of the fundamental rights was assuming a role the Constitution had not given it; his opponents in the House answered that a legislature which could amend the rights could empty them, and that the first use of the power, within eighteen months of the commencement and by a body the voters had not chosen for the purpose, fixed a precedent of exactly that kind. When the amendment's validity was challenged, the Supreme Court in Shankari Prasad Singh Deo v Union of India upheld the power of Parliament to amend the fundamental rights by the procedure of Article 368, and the adjustment programme had its legal foundation.

The Fourth Amendment of 1955 returned to property, and its subject was the price of acquisition rather than the fact of it. In State of West Bengal v Bela Banerjee, decided in 1954, the Supreme Court held that the compensation which Article 31(2) required for compulsory acquisition meant a just equivalent of what the owner had been deprived of, and that a statute which fixed compensation by reference to the market value of the land at a date years before the acquisition was not providing compensation in that sense. The reading threatened every acquisition statute that fixed compensation by formula rather than by market value at the date of taking, which is to say most of the acquisition machinery through which the development programme was to be executed. The Fourth Amendment, which came into force on 27 April 1955, substituted Article 31(2) so that a law providing for acquisition must either fix the amount of compensation or specify the principles on which it is to be determined, and added that no such law shall be called in question in any court on the ground that the compensation provided is not adequate. The adequacy of the price was thereby removed from judicial review, leaving the courts the narrower questions whether the principles specified were relevant to compensation at all or a disguise for confiscation. The same amendment widened Article 31A, adding categories that included the taking over of management of industrial undertakings and other property in stated circumstances, and inserted Article 31(2A) to separate compulsory acquisition, in which ownership or possession passes to the state, from other deprivations of property, so that the compensation guarantee attached only to the former. The sequence from judgment to amendment, Bela Banerjee in 1954 and the Fourth Amendment in 1955, is the adjustment programme in its typical rhythm: the Court read the text, the government rewrote the text, and the programme proceeded on the rewritten terms.

The Seventh Amendment of 1956 belongs to the programme by form rather than by controversy. It was the constitutional instrument of states reorganisation, and its detail belongs with the reorganisation statute in the state building section of this article. What places it here is the pattern it confirms. Reorganisation was not executed by ordinary legislation under Article 3 alone; the distribution of seats in Parliament, the composition and jurisdiction of High Courts, the classification of States in the First Schedule and the linguistic minority safeguards inserted as Articles 350A and 350B all required the text of the Constitution itself to be opened and rewritten, and the government opened it without treating the operation as exceptional. Between 1951 and 1956 the Constitution was amended seven times. The frequency is itself the finding. The founding generation treated its own Constitution as a working instrument of the programmes it had been elected, or had appointed itself, to carry out, and the later amendments that extended the Ninth Schedule, taken up in the next section, show the adjustment programme outliving the emergencies that had called it into being.

### Why did the government protect land reform by listing laws rather than defending each one?

Because each abolition statute faced separate challenge in a different High Court on the compensation clauses it happened to contain, and a defence statute by statute would have taken years with inconsistent results, whereas listing a law in a schedule converted its validity into a single constitutional fact that no court could re-examine on fundamental rights grounds.

The listing technique deserves a full explanation, because it is the period's most consequential constitutional device and the one most often misdescribed by being associated with its later uses. Article 31B provides that, without prejudice to the generality of Article 31A, none of the Acts and Regulations specified in the Ninth Schedule, nor any of their provisions, shall be deemed to be void, or ever to have become void, on the ground that the Act, Regulation or provision is inconsistent with, or takes away or abridges, any of the rights conferred by Part III, and that this holds notwithstanding any judgment, decree or order of any court or tribunal to the contrary. Every element of that formula carries weight. The protection is against Part III as a whole, not only against the property articles. It is retrospective in the strongest available form, covering laws already struck down as well as laws under challenge. It operates by specification: a law is protected because it is named in a list, and its protection does not depend on the merits of its provisions, which no court is invited to examine. And the list is amendable by the same amending power that created it, so protection could be extended to further statutes by the political decision to add their names.

The Schedule began, in 1951, with thirteen entries, and every one of them was a land reform statute: the abolition and tenancy laws of Bihar, Bombay, Madras, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Assam and the other States whose agrarian programmes were in litigation or in prospect of it. The device was created for land reform, and specifically for the zamindari abolition statutes, not for the miscellaneous purposes to which it was afterwards put. Its growth is a separate story that the constitutional cluster of this series owns, and it is stated here in outline only. The Fourth Amendment added further entries, bringing the total to twenty. The Seventeenth Amendment of 1964 added forty-four, bringing it to sixty-four, and by then the entries already extended beyond agrarian reform in the strict sense. Later amendments, most of them after the period covered by this article, carried the Schedule to two hundred and eighty-four entries, covering subjects as remote from zamindari abolition as insurance nationalisation, monopolies legislation and election law. The final chapter of the outline is the judicial response. In Kesavananda Bharati, decided on 24 April 1973, the Supreme Court held the amending power itself limited by the basic structure of the Constitution; in Waman Rao the Court fixed the consequence for the Schedule, holding that laws placed in it after the date of the Kesavananda judgment stand open to challenge if they damage that structure; and a nine-judge bench in I. R. Coelho, in 2007, confirmed that position. The amendment that created the device is analysed in [the article on the First Amendment and the land reform settlement](/2010/01/15/first-amendment-act-1951-explained/), which owns the detail; what this era article records is the device's origin in a concrete litigation emergency, its extension by repeated use, and the quarter-century delay before the courts fenced it.

One further amendment of the period shows the adjustment programme at full stretch, because it reached back to repair a definition. Article 31A protected laws for the acquisition of estates, and the States' second-generation land reforms, tenancy protection and ceilings on holdings, turned on whether the ryotwari settlements of the south and west counted as estates within the article. When the Supreme Court in Karimbil Kunhikoman v State of Kerala, in 1962, struck down the Kerala Agrarian Relations Act, the reasoning exposed agrarian legislation outside the original zamindari pattern to the same property challenge the First Amendment had been passed to end. The Seventeenth Amendment of 1964 answered by widening the definition of estate in Article 31A to include land held under ryotwari settlement and land held for purposes ancillary to agriculture, and it placed forty-four further State agrarian statutes in the Ninth Schedule in a single operation. The episode completes the programme's portrait. The founding amendment of 1951 had protected a class of laws by category and a list of laws by name; thirteen years later the same instruments were being used, by a Parliament of the same party and in the same style, to protect a second generation of agrarian legislation whose drafters had not foreseen the judicial reading that undid it. Between the First Amendment and the Seventeenth, the Constitution was amended seventeen times in fourteen years, and the frequency is the surest evidence that constitutional adjustment was not an episode of the period but one of its four continuous programmes.

## Programme two: building the planned economy

The planned economy rested on a distinction that the period's critics and defenders both tend to blur: the distinction between the plan and the law. The plan was not a statute. The Planning Commission was constituted by a resolution of the Union Cabinet on 15 March 1950, as an advisory body charged with assessing the material, capital and human resources of the country and formulating a plan for their most effective and balanced utilisation, with the Prime Minister as its chairman. It had no statutory charter, no statutory functions and no statutory tenure; it existed because the executive had resolved that it should, and it could have been dissolved the same way. The National Development Council, created in August 1952 to associate the States with plan formulation and to approve the plans the Commission drafted, rested on a resolution of the same character. When the Commission was replaced in 2015, its successor, the NITI Aayog, was constituted by a Cabinet resolution of 1 January 2015, so the institution that directed the economic policy of India for six decades and the institution that replaced it share the same legal nature: neither has ever had a statute. The planned state, at its apex, was an executive construction.

What made the plan effective was therefore not the plan document but the statutes through which its priorities were enforced on persons who had never agreed to them. A plan allocation became real in a factory owner's affairs when the licensing statute refused him permission to build outside it; in a trader's affairs when a control order under the essential commodities statute fixed what he could hold, move and charge; in a company's affairs when the companies statute prescribed how it could be constituted and managed. The legislative history of the planned economy is the history of those enforcement instruments, and they were enacted early and thickly. The First Five Year Plan, which ran from 1951 to 1956, concentrated on agriculture, irrigation and the rehabilitation of a partitioned economy, and its statutory demands were correspondingly modest. The Second Five Year Plan, which ran from 1956 to 1961 and was framed around the model associated with the statistician P. C. Mahalanobis, shifted the weight to heavy industry and to the public sector as the engine of accumulation, and it is in the Second Plan's opening years that the economic statute book of the era was substantially completed.

### What changed in the legislative programme when the Second Five Year Plan began?

The emphasis moved from agriculture and rehabilitation to heavy industry held in public ownership, and the legislative programme followed the model into sectors the First Plan had left to ordinary law: insurance was nationalised, company law was rebuilt, mineral development was declared a Union concern and commodity control was generalised.

The Industrial Policy Resolution of April 1956 expressed the Second Plan's settlement in policy form. Like the plan, it was not a statute; it was a statement of the government laid before Parliament, replacing the first such resolution of 1948. It divided industries into three schedules. Seventeen industries, including arms and ammunition, atomic energy, iron and steel, heavy plant and machinery, coal, mineral oils, and aircraft and railways, were placed in the first schedule as the exclusive responsibility of the state. A second schedule of twelve industries, including machine tools, fertilisers, synthetic rubber and road and sea transport, was marked for progressive state ownership, with private enterprise supplementing the state's effort. The remainder was left to private enterprise, operating under licence and under the declared intention that the state retained the power to enter any field. The resolution is a central document of the era's legislative method precisely because of its legal character: the most important allocation of economic roles in the period was effected by an executive statement, and the statutes were then used to police the boundaries the statement had drawn.

The policing instrument was the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act of 1951, Act 65 of 1951, which received assent in October 1951 and was brought into force in May 1952. Its architecture is the architecture of the control regime in miniature. A schedule to the Act listed the industries to which it applied, and for those industries the Act required registration of existing undertakings and a licence from the Central Government for the establishment of a new industrial undertaking, for the manufacture of a new article by an existing undertaking, for a substantial expansion of capacity, and for a change of location. Around the licence the Act built a supervisory jurisdiction: the government could investigate the affairs of an undertaking whose output had fallen, whose quality had deteriorated or whose prices had risen without justification, could issue directions to it, and could in stated circumstances take over its management. Development councils for groups of industries advised on standards, efficiency and production targets. The licensing statute's own history, its schedules and its long amendment trail, are treated in [the article devoted to the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act 1951](/2020/01/15/idra-1951-explained/); what belongs to this era article is the Act's place in the programme, which was to convert the plan's priorities and the policy resolution's schedules into permissions and refusals addressed to individual firms.

### How did a licence under the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act 1951 control an industrial firm?

The licence was a continuing permission rather than a single clearance: a firm in a scheduled industry needed the Central Government's licence to set up, to expand substantially, to move, or to make a new product, and the government retained powers to investigate the undertaking, direct its conduct and take over its management.

The Essential Commodities Act of 1955, Act 10 of 1955, in force from 1 April 1955, supplied the second enforcement instrument, addressed not to capacity but to circulation. It replaced a line of wartime and post-war control legislation that had been kept alive by temporary enactments, and gave the control power a permanent statutory home. The genealogy matters to the era's character. Commodity control had entered Indian law as an emergency technique under the Defence of India Rules, and after the war the Essential Supplies (Temporary Powers) Act of 1946, Act 24 of 1946, had continued the powers for a country facing shortage and inflation, renewed from year to year on the footing that they remained exceptional. A decade of renewals had made the exception ordinary, and the Act of 1955 simply acknowledged in law what practice had settled: in an economy of chronic scarcity, the power to direct the production and movement of food, fuel, cloth and steel was not an emergency expedient but a standing department of government. The decision to make the control permanent, taken without serious parliamentary resistance in 1955, is among the most revealing of the era, because it shows the planned economy's instruments being normalised one by one, each conversion from the temporary to the permanent passing with less debate than the last. Its central provision empowered the Central Government, where it was of opinion that it was necessary or expedient for maintaining or increasing supplies of an essential commodity, for securing its equitable distribution and availability at fair prices, or for securing foodstuffs for the defence of India and the efficient conduct of military operations, to provide by order for regulating or prohibiting the production, supply and distribution of the commodity and trade and commerce in it. The classes of essential commodity were listed in the Act, among them cattle fodder, coal, component parts and accessories of automobiles, cotton and woollen textiles, drugs, foodstuffs including edible oilseeds and oils, iron and steel, and paper. Control orders made under the Act fixed prices, required licences for dealing and storage, limited stocks and directed movement, and the statute's penalty and forfeiture provisions gave the orders their teeth. The Act's durability is part of this article's larger finding: the control regime it served has been pruned repeatedly, most substantially by an amendment in 2020 that confined the power to impose stock limits and price measures on most foodstuffs to extraordinary circumstances, yet the Act itself remains the standing instrument to which governments return in every supply emergency.

The corporate and extractive frames followed. The Companies Act of 1956, Act 1 of 1956, replaced the Companies Act of 1913 on the recommendations of the Company Law Committee chaired by H. C. Bhabha, and it regulated the private capital that the policy resolution had licensed to operate: incorporation and memorandum, prospectus and allotment, share capital, the management of companies including the managing agency system that the Act restricted and set on the path to abolition, accounts and audit, investigation of a company's affairs, and winding up. It was the statute under which Indian business was organised for nearly six decades, until the Companies Act of 2013 replaced it. The Mines and Minerals (Regulation and Development) Act of 1957, Act 67 of 1957, assented to on 28 December 1957, declared in its second section that it was expedient in the public interest that the Union should take under its control the regulation of mines and the development of minerals to the extent the Act provided, and it built the concession system of prospecting licences and mining leases for major minerals under central rules while leaving the rule-making for minor minerals to the States. Between the licensing Act for factories, the control Act for commodities, the companies Act for the corporate form and the minerals Act for the extractive estate, the planned economy had by the end of 1957 a statutory instrument for each of the points at which a private decision could diverge from the plan.

The fiscal statutes completed the frame, and they show the programme reaching for revenue and for redistribution at the same time. The Estate Duty Act of 1953, Act 34 of 1953, charged duty on the capital value of property passing on a death. The Wealth-tax Act of 1957, Act 27 of 1957, charged an annual tax on net wealth above a threshold. The Gift-tax Act of 1958 closed the obvious route around the other two by charging gifts. The Income-tax Act of 1961, Act 43 of 1961, consolidated and replaced the Income-tax Act of 1922 after the work of the Law Commission and of the Direct Taxes Administration Enquiry Committee chaired by Mahavir Tyagi, and became the durable centre of the direct tax system. The Employees' Provident Funds and Miscellaneous Provisions Act of 1952, Act 19 of 1952, dated 4 March 1952, belonged to the same programme from the worker's side: it required provident fund contributions in the factories and establishments brought within its scheme, creating a compulsory savings institution inside industrial employment decades before pension reform became a general subject. Its durability carries the series' standing caution about repeal schedules: the Code on Social Security of 2020 lists the Act among those it will repeal when its corresponding provisions are brought into force, and the Act continues in operation meanwhile, listed for repeal and still the governing law of provident funds. The provident fund statute acquired companions at the period's end, as the welfare perimeter of the planned economy was widened statute by statute: the Apprentices Act of 1961, Act 52 of 1961, placed the training of apprentices in industry on a statutory footing, and the Maternity Benefit Act of 1961, Act 53 of 1961, prohibited the employment of women in defined periods before and after childbirth and secured maternity benefit as a legal entitlement in the establishments it covered. Neither statute belonged to the heroic phase of the programme, and that is their interest: by 1961 the legislative machine built in the early nineteen fifties was producing, as a matter of routine, the incremental social legislation of an industrialising state, one gap in the perimeter at a time.

The protection of the industries the licensing system sheltered had its own statutory adviser in the Tariff Commission, constituted under the Tariff Commission Act of 1951, Act 50 of 1951, which replaced the ad hoc Tariff Board of the colonial period with a standing body charged with enquiring into claims for protective duties and into the prices and costs of Indian industry. The Commission's reports formed the evidentiary base on which protection was granted, continued or withdrawn, and its existence completes the picture of the control regime as a system of institutions rather than a set of ministerial habits: a licensing authority for entry and capacity, a control statute for commodities, a tariff body for the border, and a plan for the allocation of investment among the claims the other three admitted.

### What happened to the wealth tax and estate duty enacted in this period?

Both were abolished by later finance legislation without their parent programme being repealed around them: estate duty was withdrawn by the Finance Act of 1985 for deaths after the middle of March 1985, and wealth tax was abolished by the Finance Act of 2015, while the Income-tax Act of 1961 outlasted both.

Public ownership, the third enforcement instrument, was built by nationalisation statutes aimed at single sectors. The State Bank of India Act of 1955, Act 23 of 1955, transferred the undertaking of the Imperial Bank of India to a new statutory corporation, the State Bank of India, which began business on 1 July 1955 with the Reserve Bank holding the majority stake; the object was an instrument of banking policy with a branch network the state could direct into the countryside. The Life Insurance Corporation Act of 1956, Act 31 of 1956, dated 18 June 1956, went further: it nationalised the life insurance business of the country, transferring the business of some two hundred and forty-five insurers and provident societies to a single corporation which began business on 1 September 1956 and held the exclusive privilege of carrying on life insurance business in India. Each statute is a specimen of the era's method: a sector was not regulated into the plan but taken into a statutory corporation created for the purpose, on the authority that the First Amendment's change to Article 19(6) had confirmed, that a state monopoly in a trade or business is no infringement of the freedom to carry it on. Both corporations outlasted the doctrine that created them. The Life Insurance Corporation's exclusive privilege was ended by the insurance reforms at the turn of the century, but the Corporation and its parent Act remain, and the State Bank of India Act remains the charter of the country's largest bank.

Around these central instruments stood a ring of financial and infrastructural corporations created by statute, a form the era used whenever it wanted a permanent instrument of policy with a commercial shape. The Reserve Bank of India had been taken into public ownership by the Reserve Bank (Transfer to Public Ownership) Act of 1948, Act 62 of 1948, with effect from the start of 1949. The Industrial Finance Corporation, established under its Act of 1948, and the State Financial Corporations under their Act of 1951, channelled long-term credit to industry outside the commercial banking system. The Damodar Valley Corporation Act of 1948 created the first of the multi-purpose river valley corporations, combining flood control, irrigation and power in a single statutory authority on the model of the Tennessee Valley Authority. The Air Corporations Act of 1953 nationalised air transport into two statutory corporations, Indian Airlines for domestic routes and Air-India International for external ones. The pattern is worth naming because the assessments of the era so often miss it: the planned economy was not administered by ministries in the ordinary way, but by a proliferating family of statutory corporations, each created by its own Act, each holding its assets and borrowing on its own balance sheet, and each surviving, in many instances, long after the policy that created it had been abandoned. The corporation, not the department, was the era's chosen legal technology for economic action.

One part of the economic programme was deliberately conducted without a central statute at all, and its absence from the statute book is instructive. Agriculture and land stood in the State List, and the agrarian transformation the plans presupposed, tenancy reform, ceilings and consolidation, was legislated by the States under the protection the adjustment programme had built for them. What the Centre supplied was organisation and money through executive programmes: the Community Development Programme, inaugurated on 2 October 1952, and the National Extension Service that followed it in 1953, carried the development block, the village level worker and the co-operative society into the countryside as administrative constructions resting on State co-operative and panchayat legislation of varying vintage. The contrast with industry is exact. Where the Centre held the legislative entry, it legislated comprehensively and permanently; where it did not, it governed by programme, grant and exhortation. The era's reputation for statutory omnicompetence survives contact with the Seventh Schedule no better than any other government's, and the uneven fate of land reform, strong in abolition and weak in tenancy, is in part the legislative map expressing itself.

The dismantling, when it came, was executed in the era's own style: by executive action under the era's own statutes. The industrial policy statement of 24 July 1991, again a statement and not a statute, announced the abolition of industrial licensing for all but a short list of industries, and the abolition was effected by notification under the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act itself, the licensing statute serving as the instrument of its regime's withdrawal. The detail of that settlement, and of the wider statutory changes of 1991 in foreign exchange, trade and finance, belongs to [the article on the economic reforms in statute](/2022/05/15/1991-economic-reforms-legislation/); the point for this article is the asymmetry it exposes. Delicensing did not repeal the Act of 1951. The control orders under the Act of 1955 were pruned, not the Act. The plan was abandoned without the Planning Commission being abolished by law, because there was no law constituting it to repeal. The era's economic programme was dismantled at the level of policy and notification, while its statutory skeleton was left standing, much of it in force, some of it still in daily use.

## Programme three: social reform in the statute book

The social reform programme had the longest gestation of the four, because its central measure began as an imperial enquiry. The campaign behind the draft had a history of its own, which the women's organisations of the period pressed on the committee at every stage. Statutory reform of Hindu women's position had begun in fragments: the Child Marriage Restraint Act of 1929, Act 19 of 1929, fixed minimum ages below which a marriage was punishable though not void, and the Hindu Women's Rights to Property Act of 1937, Act 18 of 1937, gave the widow of a deceased coparcener an interest in the joint family property, though a limited one terminable on her death or remarriage. The All India Women's Conference and allied bodies had demanded more than fragments, a single complete code, throughout the nineteen thirties, and their memoranda and deputations form a continuous thread from the Rau Committee's appointment to the debates of the nineteen fifties. In 1941 the Government of India appointed the Hindu Law Committee, chaired by Sir B. N. Rau, to examine the working of the recent Hindu women's property legislation and to consider the codification of Hindu law; the committee was revived in 1944, toured the country taking evidence, and produced a draft code with its final report in 1947. The draft proposed to do in one instrument what no Indian legislature had attempted: to state the whole of the personal law of Hindus, marriage and divorce, succession, minority and guardianship, adoption and maintenance, as a single enacted code, displacing the commentaries, the schools of Mitakshara and Dayabhaga and the body of custom that together constituted Hindu law as the courts administered it. After independence the draft passed to the first Law Minister, B. R. Ambedkar, who revised it and piloted it as the Hindu Code Bill in the Constituent Assembly sitting as legislature, where it was introduced in 1948 and referred to a select committee.

The Bill's passage history is the era's clearest demonstration that a statute book records defeats as faithfully as victories. The measure met sustained opposition from orthodox organisations, which campaigned against it as an assault on the Hindu family and on religion itself; from within the ruling party, where a substantial body of members shared the objections or feared their electoral cost; and from the President, Rajendra Prasad, who recorded his view that a provisional Parliament, elected for constitution-making on a limited franchise, lacked the mandate to remake the personal law of the majority community, and who raised with the government the question of his own position in assenting to it. The government first reduced the Bill, proposing to take only the marriage and divorce portions in the provisional Parliament and to leave the remainder to the elected legislature. Even that reduced measure could not be carried in the time available, and when the Bill was allowed to lapse in September 1951, Ambedkar resigned from the Cabinet, stating in the explanation he laid before Parliament in October 1951 that the treatment of the Hindu Code Bill, and the government's failure to secure its passage, were principal reasons for his decision. The code as a single instrument died with that resignation. What survived was the draft, the political question, and Nehru's undertaking, given in the general election campaign that followed, that a returned Congress government would enact the reform.

The elected Parliament enacted it in pieces. Splitting the code into separate Bills allowed each subject to be debated on its own opposition and carried by its own majority, and between May 1955 and December 1956 four statutes completed the programme. The Hindu Marriage Act of 1955, Act 25 of 1955, dated 18 May 1955, codified the conditions and ceremonies of marriage, made monogamy the rule with bigamy punishable, and introduced judicial separation and divorce into a body of law in which marriage had been, in the dominant sacramental understanding, indissoluble. The Hindu Succession Act of 1956, Act 30 of 1956, dated 17 June 1956, codified intestate succession, gave the widow, the daughter, the mother and other female heirs of the first class a defined share, and by its Section 14 converted every limited estate held by a female Hindu into an absolute ownership, striking at the doctrine of the widow's restricted interest that had defined women's property under the older law. The Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act of 1956, Act 32 of 1956, dated 25 August 1956, codified the law of natural and testamentary guardians while expressly supplementing rather than replacing the Guardians and Wards Act of 1890. The succession Act's central compromise deserves separate statement, because the durability argument turns on it. Section 6 of the Act as enacted preserved the Mitakshara coparcenary itself: a male Hindu's interest in coparcenary property continued to devolve by survivorship on the surviving coparceners, and only where he left a female heir of the first class, or a male heir claiming through her, did the section direct that his interest be computed by a notional partition immediately before his death and devolve by succession instead. The daughter took a share in her father's separated portion, but she was not herself a coparcener, could not demand a partition, and acquired no birthright in the joint estate. The settlement was deliberate: the parliamentary majority that would enact Section 14's conversion of the widow's estate would not enact the daughter's coparcenary, and it took the amendment of 2005, half a century later, to complete the reform the Rau Committee's draft had contemplated, which is why the cross-linked article on that amendment is the necessary continuation of this section. The Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act of 1956, Act 78 of 1956, dated 21 December 1956, codified adoption, opening it on conditions to female Hindus in their own right, and stated the obligations of maintenance owed to wives, children and aged parents as enforceable legal duties. The succession statute's subsequent history is continued elsewhere in this series: its most consequential later alteration, the amendment of 2005 that made daughters coparceners by birth in Mitakshara joint family property, is examined in [the article on the Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act 2005](/2016/10/15/hindu-succession-amendment-2005/), and it is noted here only to mark how long the codification remained the working text into which reforms had to be inserted.

Each of the four Acts carried the codifier's essential instrument in its fourth section: an overriding-effect clause providing that any text, rule or interpretation of Hindu law, and any custom or usage forming part of that law, in force immediately before the Act's commencement, should cease to have effect in respect of any matter for which the Act made provision, save as the Act otherwise expressly provided. The clause is what turned four statutes into a code in substance. Where Parliament had spoken, the older sources of law, the commentaries, the school doctrines and the proved customs that courts had applied for two centuries, fell silent on the subject. The courts applied the clause as it was written: a Division Bench of the Punjab and Haryana High Court in Smt. Banso v Charan Singh, for example, read Section 4 of the succession Act as giving the Act overriding effect against the rules of succession under Punjab customary law in cases where succession opened after the Act came into force. Codified provision displacing the customary rule it replaces is therefore not a gloss on the programme; it is the programme's own mechanism, stated in its text and enforced in its litigation.

### How did the Hindu code Acts displace custom where the two conflicted?

Each Act opened with an overriding-effect clause, its Section 4, under which any text, rule or interpretation of Hindu law and any custom or usage in force before the Act ceased to have effect on any matter the Act covered, except where the Act expressly saved it, so the statute and not the custom governed.

The limits of the programme were written into the same statutes. Each Act defined the persons to whom it applied: Hindus by religion in any of its forms, including followers of the Brahmo, Prarthana and Arya Samaj movements, and persons who are Buddhists, Jains or Sikhs by religion, together with a residual class of persons domiciled in India who are not Muslims, Christians, Parsis or Jews, unless it is proved that they would not have been governed by Hindu law. Members of the Scheduled Tribes were excluded unless the Central Government directed otherwise by notification. The personal law of Muslims, Christians, Parsis and Jews was left entirely as it stood, administered under its own statutes and sources. Article 44 of the Constitution, which directs the state to endeavour to secure for the citizens a uniform civil code throughout the territory of India, stood in Part IV as a non-justiciable instruction, and the period produced no legislation under it. The government's stated position, repeated by Nehru, was that a common code should not be imposed on a community that had not consented to it, and that codifying the law of the community whose reformers had demanded codification was the measure of the politically and morally possible. The result was a structural asymmetry that the statute book still carries: the majority community's family law was remade by Parliament in four Acts, and every other community's family law was left to its inherited texts, its own older statutes and its internal reform movements.

Two further statutes of the period belong to the social programme and extend it beyond personal law. The Untouchability (Offences) Act of 1955, Act 22 of 1955, dated 8 May 1955, implemented Article 35 read with Article 17 of the Constitution, which abolishes untouchability and forbids its practice in any form: the Act prescribed punishments for preventing a person, on the ground of untouchability, from entering a place of public worship, from using a sacred tank, well, spring or watercourse, from admission to hospitals, dispensaries, educational institutions and hostels, and for a schedule of comparable disabilities in shops, restaurants, occupations and the use of public conveyances and premises. It was renamed the Protection of Civil Rights Act by the amending Act of 1976, Act 106 of 1976, which also strengthened its penalties, and under that name it remains the penal statute behind the constitutional abolition. The Special Marriage Act of 1954, Act 43 of 1954, dated 9 October 1954 and in force from 1 January 1955, took the opposite approach to reform from codification: instead of remaking any community's law, it offered an exit from all of them, a form of marriage solemnised before a Marriage Officer, available to any two persons regardless of the religion either professed, together with registration of marriages celebrated in other forms and a divorce jurisdiction for marriages under the Act. It replaced the Special Marriage Act of 1872, which had made a comparable civil form available only to persons prepared to declare that they did not profess the Hindu, Christian, Jewish, Muslim or other named religions; the Act of 1954 dropped the renunciation, and the civil form became genuinely open. For a couple marrying across the lines the personal laws policed, the Act of 1954, and not any provision of the codes, was the period's operative reform.

The content of the marriage code shows how negotiated the reform was. As enacted in 1955, the Hindu Marriage Act fixed the minimum age at eighteen years for the bridegroom and fifteen years for the bride, ages raised only by the amendment of 1978 to twenty-one and eighteen; it required no registration of marriages, leaving proof of marriage to ceremony and repute; and it preserved the sacramental vocabulary of the older law, validating marriages by customary rites and ceremonies, including the saptapadi where that rite formed part of the custom, even while it subjected the resulting union to a statutory regime of monogamy, restitution, judicial separation and divorce. The dowry legislation that closed the period's family programme, the Dowry Prohibition Act of 1961, Act 28 of 1961, prohibited the giving and taking of dowry and the demanding of it, with penalties that contemporaries criticised as slight and an enforcement design dependent on complaint; its subsequent amendment history, strengthening the offence and adding evidentiary and cruelty provisions in the following decades, belongs to the social justice cluster, but its presence at the end of the period matters here, because it shows the social programme still legislating on the family in the era's final years, and still by prohibition addressed to a practice the personal law codes had not touched.

The untouchability statute's design rewards the same attention. Its offences were built around access: to places of public worship, to sacred tanks, wells, springs and watercourses, to hospitals, dispensaries, educational institutions and hostels maintained for the general public, to shops, public restaurants, hotels and places of public entertainment, to occupations and trades, and to public conveyances and premises. The penalties of the original Act were modest, imprisonment extending to six months or a fine extending to five hundred rupees or both for the principal offences, and later offences included molestation and boycott; the 1976 amendment that renamed the Act enhanced the penalties and treated repeat offences with increasing severity. The statute's modesty as a penal instrument and its importance as a constitutional statement are both part of the record: it converted the abolition declared by Article 17 into a schedule of prosecutable acts, and in doing so it defined, for the first time in Indian legislation, the practical content of the freedom the Constitution had announced.

The social programme's penal wing had one more member. The Suppression of Immoral Traffic in Women and Girls Act of 1956, Act 104 of 1956, enacted to give effect to the international convention for the suppression of the traffic in persons signed at New York in 1950, penalised the keeping of brothels, living on the earnings of prostitution, the procuring and detaining of persons for prostitution and prostitution in the vicinity of notified public places, and built a machinery of protective homes and corrective institutions for the women and girls it removed from the trade. The statute, renamed the Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act by amendment in 1986, illustrates a strand of the social programme distinct from both codification and untouchability: reform by international obligation, in which the content of the domestic statute was substantially settled by the convention the republic had signed, and Parliament's work was to fit the treaty text to Indian institutions. Between the four codes, the civil marriage Act, the untouchability statute and the traffic Act, the social programme touched the family, the caste order and the sexual economy of the period, and it is not surprising that it generated the era's most personal politics, in the House and outside it.

The assessment of this programme divides along the line the brief for the period predicts, and both sides are best stated by their named proponents. The case for the codification as an advance rests on its text: the legal scholar and campaigner for the code within government, Ambedkar himself, presented it as the application of constitutional equality inside the family, giving women enforceable rights to divorce, to adopt, to inherit and to hold property outright, in place of rights mediated by custom and by male guardians; women's organisations, prominently the All India Women's Conference, campaigned for the code on those terms, and members of the Constituent Assembly such as Hansa Mehta argued the wider position that equality would remain incomplete while personal law stood outside it. The qualifying case is that the enacted Acts were a settlement diluted from the Bill: the legal scholar Flavia Agnes has argued that the codification preserved the Mitakshara coparcenary, the joint family institution through which male birthright in ancestral property ran, and left the daughter outside it for a further half-century, so that the code rationalised Hindu law more than it equalised it. The orthodox opposition, for its part, contested the programme at its root, denying both the need for the reform and the competence of the state to legislate the religious law of a community. A reader of the statute book can hold the three positions apart without confusion: the code changed the legal position of Hindu women substantially and measurably; it did so by compromise and left structures standing that its own drafters had proposed to remove; and it was confined, by a decision the government defended openly, to the communities named in its application clauses.

## Programme four: state building

State building in the period was not a metaphor. The republic inherited a territory assembled from the provinces of British India and more than five hundred princely states acceded in the three years after 1947, a franchise that had never been operated on universal suffrage, a population whose citizenship the Constitution defined only for the moment of commencement, and a security situation that included a war in Kashmir, a communist insurgency in Telangana and the communal aftermath of partition. The statutes of the fourth programme are the answers to those problems, and they were enacted with the least controversy of the four programmes because their necessity was the common ground of government and opposition alike.

The electoral machine came first, because everything else in the republic's legality depended on it. The Representation of the People Act of 1950, Act 43 of 1950, provided for the allocation of seats in Parliament and the State Legislatures, the delimitation of constituencies, the preparation of electoral rolls and the qualifications and disqualifications of voters and members in the first instance. The Representation of the People Act of 1951, Act 43 of 1951, provided for the conduct of the elections themselves: the election machinery, the notification of elections, nominations, polling, counting, the declaration of results and the trial of election petitions. On those two statutes the first general election was conducted across the winter of 1951 and 1952, at the time the largest democratic election held anywhere, with an electorate of some one hundred and seventy-three million persons, most of them voting for the first time and many unable to read the ballot they cast, under an Election Commission constituted under Article 324 and headed by its first Chief Election Commissioner, Sukumar Sen. The rolls had to be built before the election could be held, and their construction was itself a statutory operation of the programme: house-to-house enumeration on the basis of adult franchise under the Act of 1950, followed by the delimitation of constituencies, for which the Delimitation Commission Act of 1952 supplied the machinery after the census of 1951. The two Acts, heavily amended, remain the frame within which every Indian election is conducted. Their companion in time, the Citizenship Act of 1955, Act 57 of 1955, dated 30 December 1955, discharged the task the Constitution had expressly left open: Articles 5 to 10 had settled citizenship at the commencement of the Constitution, and Article 11 provided that nothing in them derogates from the power of Parliament to make any provision with respect to the acquisition and termination of citizenship. The Act of 1955 supplied the continuing code, citizenship by birth, by descent, by registration, by naturalisation and by incorporation of territory, together with the modes of termination and deprivation, and it too remains, with amendments, the operative statute.

The redrawing of the internal map was the programme's largest single operation. Demands for provinces organised by language predated independence, and the Congress had accepted the linguistic principle in its own organisation while deferring it in government; the death of Potti Sriramulu in December 1952, after a fast for a Telugu-speaking State, and the creation of Andhra State in 1953 made deferral impossible. The government appointed the States Reorganisation Commission in December 1953, chaired by Fazl Ali and sitting with H. N. Kunzru and K. M. Panikkar, and the Commission reported in 1955. The States Reorganisation Act of 1956, Act 37 of 1956, implemented the settlement: with effect from the appointed day, 1 November 1956, the States of the republic were reconstituted substantially on linguistic lines, fourteen States and six Union Territories emerging from the reorganisation in place of the First Schedule's classification of Part A, Part B and Part C States inherited from the colonial and princely order. The reorganisation statute's own provisions, its boundary schedules and its long afterlife in later State bifurcations are the subject of [the article on the States Reorganisation Act 1956](/2018/05/15/states-reorganisation-act-1956-explained/); for the era record, the point is the scale of what was attempted by statute within a decade of independence, a negotiated dissolution and reassembly of the internal units of the Union, carried out through law rather than conquest or conference.

### What did states reorganisation require Parliament to settle besides boundaries?

Reorganisation ran through the Constitution itself: the Seventh Amendment rewrote the First Schedule, abolished the Part A, Part B and Part C classification, adjusted seats in Parliament and the composition and jurisdiction of High Courts, allocated services, assets and liabilities between successor States, and inserted safeguards for linguistic minorities as Articles 350A and 350B.

The map had an external edge as well as an internal one, and the period legislated for that too, in an episode that joins the state building programme to the adjustment programme. The boundary settlement with Pakistan negotiated in 1958, the Nehru-Noon Agreement, provided for the division of the Berubari Union and the exchange of enclaves between West Bengal and East Pakistan, and its implementation raised a question the Constitution had not answered in terms: whether Indian territory could be ceded to a foreign state by executive action and ordinary legislation, or only by constitutional amendment. Referred to the Supreme Court under Article 143, the question was answered in the Berubari Union opinion of 1960: a cession of territory required an amendment of the Constitution under Article 368, and the Ninth Amendment of 1960 was enacted to give the settlement legal effect, with the Acquired Territories (Merger) Act of 1960, Act 64 of 1960, adjusting the territories on the other side of the exchanges. The episode is a fitting part of the era's record because it shows the adjustment habit applied to the state's very extent: where the text stood in the way of a settlement the government had made, the text was amended, and the amendment, not the agreement, was treated as the operative act of state.

The final territorial operations of the period closed the integration the reorganisation had left incomplete, and they were carried through by the same combination of executive action and constitutional instrument. Dadra and Nagar Haveli, administered by a local body after the Portuguese were expelled in 1954, was incorporated as a Union territory by the Tenth Amendment of 1961. Goa, Daman and Diu, taken in December 1961, were placed under a statutory administration by the Act of 1962 and incorporated into the Union by the Twelfth Amendment of the same year. With those instruments the state building programme reached the whole of the subcontinent's post-Partition territory except the enclaves whose exchange with Pakistan the Ninth Amendment had authorised, and the method of the completions repeated the method of the whole programme: the fact was created on the ground, and the Constitution was then amended to receive it.

The coercive instruments of the new state were legislated in the same years and in a characteristic form. The Preventive Detention Act of 1950, Act 4 of 1950, dated 25 February 1950, among the first statutes of the republic, empowered the Central and State Governments to detain a person to prevent him from acting in a manner prejudicial to the defence of India, its relations with foreign powers or its security, to the security of a State or the maintenance of public order, or to the maintenance of supplies and services essential to the community. The Act was challenged immediately, and in A. K. Gopalan v State of Madras, decided in 1950, the Supreme Court upheld it save for its Section 14, which had barred the disclosure of the grounds of detention to any court. The statute's own architecture carried its apology: its first section fixed the date on which it would cease to have effect. Extended by amendment again and again as its successive terminal dates approached, it was in the end allowed to run to the last of them, ceasing to have effect on 31 December 1969, and its lineage continued in the Maintenance of Internal Security Act of 1971 and the National Security Act of 1980, which belong to later eras and other articles of this series. The form, a detention power granted with an expiry date and renewed only by fresh legislation, is the period's legislative signature on the security state: the power was asserted, and the assertion was made temporary in the text itself, so that each continuation required the government of the day to defend it again in Parliament.

The coercive map had a second instrument beside the detention Act, aimed not at individuals in the plains but at territory in insurgency. The Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act of 1958, Act 28 of 1958, enacted first for the Naga Hills district of Assam, empowered the Governor or the Central Government to declare an area disturbed and authorised commissioned officers, in such an area, to fire upon persons acting in contravention of prohibitory orders, to arrest without warrant and to enter and search premises, with protection against prosecution except with the sanction of the Central Government. Its passage through the era's Parliament adds a shade to the state building portrait that the detention Act alone would leave too simple: alongside the sunset clause of 1950, the period also produced a special powers statute with no expiry date, territorially confined at enactment and repeatedly extended in application since. The two instruments together, one temporary and general, one permanent and territorial, frame the security legislation of the republic at its origin.

The strategic estate completed the programme. The Atomic Energy Act of 1948, Act 29 of 1948, was enacted by the Dominion legislature before the Constitution commenced, vesting control over atomic minerals and the development of atomic energy in the Central Government, and the Atomic Energy Commission was constituted in August 1948, on the initiative of the physicist Homi J. Bhabha, by executive resolution rather than under the Act; the parallel with the Planning Commission, an apex body of the era resting on a resolution while a related statute stood beside it, is exact. The Act of 1948 was repealed and replaced by the Atomic Energy Act of 1962, which remains the governing statute of the sector. Two further statutes of the programme are easily overlooked because they concern the state's own personnel and its own language. The All India Services Act of 1951, Act 61 of 1951, enacted under Article 312 of the Constitution, gave Parliament's authority to the Indian Administrative Service and the Indian Police Service, the successors to the colonial services, regulating their recruitment and conditions so that a single cadre, allotted between the Centre and the States, would staff the higher administration of both. The decision to retain an all-India service, defended in the Constituent Assembly by Sardar Patel as the steel frame of the new Union, was thus carried into statute within two years of the Constitution, and the Act of 1951 remains the basis of the services. The Official Languages Act of 1963, Act 19 of 1963, answered the Constitution's own timetable: Article 343 had provided for Hindi in the Devanagari script as the official language of the Union with English continuing for fifteen years, and as 1965 approached the Act of 1963 provided for the continued use of English in addition to Hindi for the official purposes of the Union after that date, converting a constitutional deadline that threatened a confrontation with the non-Hindi States into an open-ended bilingual settlement that the amendment of 1967 then entrenched. Both statutes exhibit the programme's instinct: where the Constitution left a structural question open, whether the services or the language of the Union, the period answered it with an Act designed to be permanent.

Between the electoral Acts, the citizenship code, the reorganisation settlement, the detention statute and the atomic energy frame, the fourth programme gave the republic its operating machinery, and of the four programmes it is the one whose outputs have been least disturbed since: elections, citizenship and the State map are still administered under statutes of this period, amended but recognisably the same instruments.

## The era in the courts

The statute book the four programmes produced was tested in the courts from the moment of its enactment, and the judicial record of the period is the adjustment programme's mirror image: nearly every major constitutional decision of the first fifteen years is a judgment on a statute of one of the four programmes. The property line has been traced in the adjustment section, from the zamindari litigation through Bela Banerjee to the Fourth Amendment, with the Supreme Court in 1952, in State of Bihar v Kameshwar Singh, upholding the Bihar abolition Act in its main design while striking particular provisions of its compensation scheme, a distribution of wins that taught the government to protect by amendment what it could not be sure of winning by argument. The liberty line opened with A. K. Gopalan, in which the Court, upholding the preventive detention statute in 1950, read the guarantee of life and personal liberty in Article 21 as satisfied by any procedure established by law, a reading that confined judicial review of detention and of much else for a generation, until the Court displaced it in the Maneka Gandhi decision of 1978, well outside the period. Between those poles the early Court did the quieter work of building the doctrine of reasonable restrictions: in State of Madras v V. G. Row, in 1952, it struck down a provision empowering the executive to declare associations unlawful, for want of procedural safeguards and objective standards, and in doing so began the practice of weighing the nature of the right, the purpose of the restriction and the procedure that attended it, which became the standard grammar of rights adjudication. The personal law statutes produced their own jurisprudence of a different kind, on the displacement of custom by code, examined in the social reform section: the courts' task there was not to test the statutes against the Constitution but to apply the statutes against the older law, and Section 4 of each of the four Acts gave them a text with which to do it.

The pattern across these lines is the one this article has drawn from the legislative side. The courts of the period neither obstructed the programmes in the round nor surrendered to them; they decided case by case, striking a compensation formula, saving an abolition Act, voiding a detention provision's secrecy clause, and the government answered the decisions it could not accept by amendment rather than by defiance. That exchange, judgment and amendment in alternation, is the constitutional history of the era in miniature, and its most important product was not any single doctrine but the settled expectation, on both sides, that the exchange would continue within the forms of the Constitution. Later developments, the basic structure doctrine above all, changed the terms of the exchange; the statutes enacted while the old terms held were already on the book, and they stayed there.

The final row repays a slow reading. Constitutional adjustment survives trivially, because an amendment is the Constitution and is displaced only by another amendment; but its central subject, the fundamental right to property around which the First and Fourth Amendments were built, was removed from Part III altogether in 1978, so the programme's text endures while its battleground vanished. The planned economy shows the opposite pattern in its purest form: the statutes stand and the programme they executed has been withdrawn from beneath them. Social reform and state building show the strongest survival of all, statute and programme together: the codes of 1955 and 1956 and the machinery statutes of 1950 to 1956 are not merely unrepealed, they are the working law under which marriages are dissolved, estates are inherited, elections are run and citizenship is determined every day. The two exceptions in the state building column are instructive rather than embarrassing: the detention statute lapsed because it was written to lapse, and the atomic energy statute was replaced by a successor in the same field, which is the ordinary metabolism of a statute book. Death by sunset and death by replacement are both different in kind from the planned economy's fate, which was death of the policy with survival of the law.

## The two assessments of the record

The period is argued about continuously, and the argument is rarely about the legislative record itself, which is not in dispute in its particulars. It is about what the record means, and the two standing assessments are best given in their proponents' terms, because each is strong exactly where the other is silent.

The favourable assessment is an assessment of outcomes under constraint. Its historians, among them Bipan Chandra and his co-authors in the standard post-independence history of the period, argue in substance that a country that began with very low capital formation, an overwhelmingly agrarian economy, a partitioned administration and a refugee emergency built, within fifteen years, an industrial base including steel, heavy machinery, machine tools and a capital goods sector that had not existed; a legal framework for a democratic republic, including universal franchise operated at continental scale; a codified body of family law for the majority community; and an institutional apparatus of planning, public enterprise and scientific establishments, of which the atomic energy and space programmes were the visible peaks. On this account the constitutional amendments were not vandalism of a founding text but necessary responses to judicial decisions that would otherwise have blocked land reform and the redistribution the electorate had been promised: the First Amendment answered Champakam and the zamindari litigation, the Fourth answered Bela Banerjee, and a government that had accepted those judgments as final would have accepted the indefinite continuation of the intermediary tenures and of admission by communal quota. The assessment adds that the amendments were carried by the prescribed majorities, were defended in Parliament at length, and were upheld by the Supreme Court when challenged, so that the procedure of the Constitution was honoured even where its initial text was altered.

The record also contains an assessment from inside the programme itself, and it is the most damaging to any simple verdict because it cannot be dismissed as opposition. The abolition of intermediaries, the land reform the constitutional programme had been built to protect, was carried through for the most part: the zamindari and jagirdari tenures were extinguished across the States and the revenue relationship between the state and the cultivating holder was made direct. The second stage of the agrarian programme, the protection of tenants and the imposition of ceilings on holdings, fared worse, and the Planning Commission's own Panel on Land Reforms, reporting within the period, recorded that tenancy legislation was being widely evaded through the resumption of land for personal cultivation and through surrenders extracted from tenants, so that the class the statutes were written to protect was losing its land under the forms of the law itself. The lesson generalises across the four programmes and the assessments both understate it: the era was markedly better at enacting structural law, abolitions, nationalisations, codifications, reorganisations, than at the tenancy-by-tenancy, licence-by-licence administration on which the finer distributive promises depended. Its statute book is a record of structures transformed and of outcomes that lagged the structures, and a fair reading has to hold both columns.

The critical assessment is an assessment of method and of its long costs. Its economic wing is associated with C. Rajagopalachari, who founded the Swatantra Party in 1959 substantially on this ground, and whose phrase for the licensing system, the permit-licence raj, outlasted the party: the argument is that the licensing and planning frame, whatever its founding intentions, produced slow growth, allocated investment by administrative discretion rather than by productivity, and converted the state's gatekeeping powers into a source of rent for those who controlled the gates, so that the economy the statutes built underperformed the economy the country's savings and enterprise could have sustained. Its constitutional wing argues that the protected schedule established a device for insulating legislation from judicial review which, once normalised for land reform, was used for purposes the founding emergency could not justify, and that the habit of amending the fundamental rights when they proved inconvenient, the speech restrictions widened in 1951 and the property right progressively hollowed in 1951 and 1955 before its removal a generation later, set a precedent that later governments with worse causes inherited and used. On this account the era's deepest legacy is not any statute but a technique: the demonstration that a determined parliamentary majority can rewrite the terms of rights themselves, and that a schedule can be made to do quietly what open repeal would have to defend in public.

The two assessments are not symmetrical in kind, and the reader is not obliged to choose between them wholesale. The first is largely about outcomes: what was built, under what handicaps, in what time. The second is largely about method: what the building did to the constitutional habits of the republic and to the incentives of its economy. A reader may consistently hold that the industrial base was a real achievement and that the licensing system that delivered it also bred the rent-seeking its critics named; that land reform required constitutional protection and that the Ninth Schedule was a dangerous instrument to invent for it; that the Hindu code advanced the legal position of women and that its confinement to one part of the population created a differentiated citizenship in family law that remains contested ground. The statute book supports each of those paired holdings, because they answer different questions. What the record does not support is either of the lazy versions: that the period legislated nothing of consequence outside the economy, which the social and state building programmes refute by their survival; or that its statutes can be read off from its ideology, which the Planning Commission's missing statute and the detention Act's expiry date alike contradict.

## What the era did not legislate

A legislative record is defined by its omissions as well as its Acts, and the period's omissions follow a pattern that confirms the reading offered in this article. The uniform civil code was not attempted, for the reasons the social reform section records. No central statute attempted the second stage of land reform: tenancy protection and ceilings remained State subjects in law and State failures in much of practice, the Centre's role confined to the constitutional shelter of the Ninth Schedule and to the exhortations of the plans. The press received a statute for the service conditions of working journalists, the Working Journalists Act of 1955, but the Press Council that the Press Commission of 1954 had recommended arrived only in 1965, under Nehru's successor. The right to work, the living wage and the free and compulsory education promised in the Directive Principles received no implementing statute in the period at all; the education promise waited until the amendment of 2002 and the Act of 2009. The pattern across the omissions is consistent with the pattern across the achievements. The era legislated where a structure had to be created or a judicial obstruction removed, and it deferred where a programme required either the consent of communities it had decided not to coerce or the sustained administrative delivery it repeatedly proved unable to supply. Its statute book is therefore not the measure of its ambitions, which were wider; it is the measure of its method, which selected for the enactable.

The general criminal law tells the same story from the opposite side. The era that rewrote the Constitution seven times in six years left the Indian Penal Code, the Code of Criminal Procedure and the Indian Evidence Act, the three colonial codes on which the daily administration of criminal justice rested, substantially as it found them. Reform of the codes was referred to the Law Commission, whose reports accumulated through the period, but the wholesale revision of the criminal law belonged to later generations, and the codes of 1860, 1898 and 1872 outlasted the era untouched in their architecture while special statutes multiplied around them. The omission was not inadvertence. The programmes that commanded the era's legislative energy were the programmes of the Directive Principles and of the plans, and the ordinary criminal law served every government of the period well enough, in the form in which it stood, to be left alone. A statute book, like a government, is known by what it does not trouble to change.

The federal balance sheet belongs to the same reckoning. Planning drew fiscal power toward the Centre by a route the Constitution had not designed for it: the Finance Commission under Article 280 distributed tax revenues by constitutional formula, while plan assistance flowed to the States as grants under Article 282, on the Planning Commission's recommendations and in amounts that came to rival the constitutional channel, so that the States' development spending depended substantially on a body that owed its existence to a Cabinet resolution. The criticism that this arrangement bypassed the constitutional scheme was recorded in terms by the Sarkaria Commission on Centre-State Relations a generation later. Within the period it was the price of the programme: the States accepted plan discipline because the plans carried the money, and the reorganisation settlement of 1956, which gave the linguistic principle its victory, was negotiated inside a Union whose fiscal centre of gravity the planning system had already fixed.

## The statutes outlasted the model

The finding this article was written to state can be put plainly. The economic architecture of the period, licensing as the gate to investment, planning as the allocator of resources, and public ownership as the presumptive form for heavy industry and finance, has been substantially dismantled: licensing was abolished for all but a short list of industries by notification in 1991, the plan as an allocative instrument faded with the Planning Commission itself, and the public sector monopolies in insurance and elsewhere were opened to private entry by later statutes. The statutes that carried that architecture have largely not been repealed. The Industries (Development and Regulation) Act of 1951 remains in force, its licensing chapters spent. The Essential Commodities Act of 1955 remains the standing emergency instrument of supply policy. The Mines and Minerals Act of 1957 remains the frame of mineral concessions, amended in 2015 to an auction regime its drafters would not have recognised but enacted as amendment, not replacement. The Income-tax Act of 1961 remains the direct tax code. The State Bank of India Act and the Life Insurance Corporation Act remain the charters of the institutions they created. A statute, it turns out, is much harder to kill than a policy: repeal requires a parliamentary decision that someone must take responsibility for, while a policy can be abandoned by the simple expedient of ceasing to use the powers the statute confers.

The mechanism of survival deserves a sentence of its own, because it is uniform across the programmes and explains more than the merits of any individual statute. The era's Acts survived by being amended rather than replaced. The Citizenship Act of 1955 was amended by the Acts of 1986, 1992, 2004, 2005, 2015 and 2019, each Parliament adjusting the code for its own migration politics while leaving the structure of 1955 intact beneath the alterations. The Income-tax Act of 1961 absorbed an annual Finance Act for six decades, accumulating amendments by the hundreds without ever being consolidated away. The Representation of the People Acts were amended for every change in electoral practice, from the voting age to the voting machine. Amendment is the path of least resistance in a busy legislature: it preserves the case law, the rules, the forms and the departmental practice built on the original text, and it asks of Parliament only the clauses that change. A founding statute that is drafted as a complete code therefore tends to become permanent, not because later generations revere it, but because replacing it costs more than amending it, year after year, indefinitely. The era's drafters, who wrote complete codes, built that ratchet into their work whether or not they intended it.

The social and state building legislation of the same years presents the stronger half of the finding, because there the programme survives with the statute. The Hindu Marriage Act and Hindu Succession Act govern the marriages and inheritances of the majority of the population in every district court in the country; the succession Act was amended in 2005 to complete a reform its original text had deferred, which is the behaviour of a living code, not a monument. The Representation of the People Acts conduct every election. The Citizenship Act of 1955, amended repeatedly and most controversially in 2019, remains the code under which citizenship is acquired and lost. The reorganisation settlement of 1956 set the template, linguistic States within a strong Union, inside which every later reorganisation, from the bifurcation of Bombay in 1960 to the creation of Telangana in 2014, has been negotiated. Even the period's coercive and strategic instruments show the pattern at one remove: the detention statute lapsed by its own terms, but the power it normalised passed into successor statutes; the atomic energy statute of 1948 was replaced in 1962 by a successor that still governs. What follows from the record is offered as general information and analysis drawn from the legislative history; it is not advice on any live question arising under these statutes, several of which remain in daily operation and fall to be applied to individual facts by the courts and authorities charged with them.

The series of which this article forms part has documented, cluster by cluster, a recurring feature of Indian legislating: a statute outlives the policy it was enacted to carry. The colonial codes outlived the empire; the control statutes of the war years outlived the war; the repeals and replacements of one decade become the inherited furniture of the next. This article's contribution is to show that the pattern does not begin with later backsliding or administrative inertia. It begins with the first legislative programme under the Constitution, in the work of the founding generation itself, and it is therefore a structural feature of the system rather than a defect of any period of it. The generation that wrote the Constitution also wrote, in its first fifteen years, a statute book whose largest part it would bequeath intact to successors who repudiated much of its economic creed. Aspirants and students who organise the period for examination purposes can [set out the four programmes statute by statute as slides in the ReportMedic presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), which is a serviceable way to hold the map in mind; but the map is not the territory, and the territory is the surviving text.

For the reader who works with the period's texts, a practical consequence follows, and it is the payload the student and the researcher share. The era cannot be studied from summaries of its policies, because the policies and the law diverged, at the start by the gap between a plan and a statute, and at the end by the survival of the statute after the policy. It has to be studied in the Acts themselves, in the four Hindu code Acts with their fourth sections, in the licensing Act with its schedule, in the detention Act with its terminal date, and in the constitutional amendments with the judgments they answered lying beside them. Read that way, the period stops resolving into a single reputation and becomes what it was while it was being made: four programmes, pursued at once by a legislature that believed a statute could settle almost anything, and was right often enough to fill the modern statute book with the evidence.

The verdict, then, is the namable claim with which this article began, earned from the record. Judged by its economic model, the Nehru era is a closed period, studied as history. Judged by its statute book, it is not closed at all: it is the layer of Indian law on which the living law still stands, in the family courts, in the electoral rolls, in the citizenship register and in the schedules of the Constitution itself. The four programmes were simultaneous, but they were not equal in fate. Constitutional adjustment consumed itself into the text. The planned economy was dismantled in place, leaving its instruments standing like scaffolding around a building that was redesigned while occupied. Social reform and state building simply continued, amended, renamed in places, litigated constantly, and never replaced. A reader who wants to understand why Indian legislation looks the way it does could start with later and louder periods; the record suggests starting here, where the habits were formed: amend the Constitution when it obstructs, protect the programme by listing it, build the institution by resolution where a statute would take too long, put an expiry date on the powers that embarrass, and let the statute book accumulate. The accumulation is the era's true monument, and it is still in force.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What defined the legislative programme of the Nehru years?

Four programmes pursued simultaneously between 1950 and 1964. The government adjusted the Constitution itself, amending the fundamental rights when judgments obstructed its policies; built a planned economy of licensing, commodity control, public ownership and taxation; reformed social law, codifying Hindu personal law and penalising the practice of untouchability; and built the state, with electoral statutes, a citizenship code, a reorganised map of States, a preventive detention power and an atomic energy frame. The Directive Principles in Part IV of the Constitution supplied much of the agenda, and the programmes constantly interfered with one another, land reform depending on constitutional amendment and social codification on electoral arithmetic.

### Q: Which constitutional amendments came first and why?

The First Amendment of 1951 came first, answering three judicial obstructions at once: it widened the permissible restrictions on free speech after the Romesh Thappar and Brij Bhushan judgments, inserted Article 15(4) to protect special provision for backward classes after the Champakam Dorairajan judgment, and inserted Articles 31A and 31B with the Ninth Schedule to protect land reform statutes from property challenges. The Fourth Amendment of 1955 followed the Bela Banerjee decision by removing the adequacy of compensation for compulsory acquisition from judicial review. The Seventh Amendment of 1956 rewrote the constitutional map to accompany the reorganisation of the States.

### Q: What protection does the Ninth Schedule give?

Article 31B provides that none of the Acts and Regulations specified in the Ninth Schedule, nor any of their provisions, shall be deemed void, or ever to have become void, on the ground that they are inconsistent with or abridge any right in Part III of the Constitution, notwithstanding any judgment or order to the contrary. The protection attaches by listing: a law is immune from fundamental rights challenge because its name stands in the Schedule, without any court examining its provisions. The immunity is not unlimited for later entries, since the Supreme Court has held that laws placed in the Schedule after 24 April 1973, the date of the Kesavananda Bharati judgment, are open to challenge if they damage the basic structure of the Constitution.

### Q: What did the Hindu Code Bills change?

Enacted as four separate Acts rather than as the single code originally drafted, the measures remade the personal law of Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs. The Hindu Marriage Act of 1955 imposed monogamy and introduced judicial separation and divorce. The Hindu Succession Act of 1956 codified intestate succession, gave female heirs of the first class defined shares, and by Section 14 converted a female Hindu's limited estate into absolute ownership. The Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act of 1956 codified guardianship, and the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act of 1956 codified adoption, opening it to women in their own right, and stated maintenance obligations as enforceable duties. Each Act, by its Section 4, displaced texts, customs and usages on matters it covered.

### Q: Which economic statutes built the planned state?

The Industries (Development and Regulation) Act of 1951 subjected scheduled industry to licensing for establishment, expansion, new products and relocation. The Essential Commodities Act of 1955 empowered control orders over the production, supply, distribution and prices of listed commodities. The Companies Act of 1956 rebuilt corporate law, and the Mines and Minerals (Regulation and Development) Act of 1957 declared Union control over mines and minerals and built the concession system. The Income-tax Act of 1961, the Wealth-tax Act of 1957 and the Estate Duty Act of 1953 formed the fiscal frame, the Employees' Provident Funds Act of 1952 attached compulsory social security to industrial employment, and the State Bank of India Act of 1955 and the Life Insurance Corporation Act of 1956 carried banking and life insurance into public ownership.

### Q: Was the Planning Commission a statutory body?

No. The Planning Commission was constituted by a resolution of the Union Cabinet on 15 March 1950, with the Prime Minister as its chairman, to assess the country's resources and formulate Five Year Plans for their effective and balanced use. It was never created or governed by any Act of Parliament, had no statutory functions or tenure, and existed throughout its life as an advisory body resting on executive resolution. The body that replaced it, the NITI Aayog, was likewise constituted by a Cabinet resolution, dated 1 January 2015, so the institution that directed India's planned economy for six decades never had a statutory basis at any point in its existence.

### Q: What social legislation was enacted in that period?

The centre of the programme was the codification of Hindu personal law in four Acts of 1955 and 1956 covering marriage, succession, minority and guardianship, and adoptions and maintenance. Beside it stood the Untouchability (Offences) Act of 1955, which gave Article 17 of the Constitution penal force by punishing the enforcement of disabilities on the ground of untouchability in worship, water, education, hospitals, shops and public premises, and was later renamed the Protection of Civil Rights Act. The Special Marriage Act of 1954 provided a civil form of marriage open to any two persons regardless of religion. The Dowry Prohibition Act of 1961 closed the period's family legislation by prohibiting the giving and taking of dowry.

### Q: What does the Nehru era legislative record show?

It shows a founding legislature running four programmes at once and inventing durable techniques to carry them: amending the Constitution as a routine instrument of governance, protecting reform by listing statutes in a schedule beyond judicial review, directing an economy through an apex body that had no statute, and writing an expiry date into the one power, preventive detention, that sat uneasily with the republic's principles. It also shows an asymmetry of survival. The economic model of the period was dismantled while its statutes largely remain in force, and the social and state building legislation remains operative law with amendments, so the record's deepest feature is that its statutes outlasted its policies.

### Q: Which Nehru-era statutes have been repealed, and which remain in force?

The clearest losses are the Preventive Detention Act of 1950, which lapsed by its own terms at the end of 1969; the Atomic Energy Act of 1948, repealed and replaced by the Atomic Energy Act of 1962; and the Companies Act of 1956, replaced by the Companies Act of 2013. The wealth tax and estate duty were abolished by finance legislation in 2015 and 1985 respectively. Against those stand the survivors: the industrial licensing, essential commodities, minerals and income-tax statutes; the four Hindu code Acts; the Special Marriage Act; the Protection of Civil Rights Act under its later name; the Representation of the People Acts; the Citizenship Act; and the States Reorganisation settlement, all in force with amendments.

### Q: What replaced the Planning Commission in 2015?

The NITI Aayog, the National Institution for Transforming India, constituted by a resolution of the Union Cabinet dated 1 January 2015. The mode of creation repeated the original exactly: an executive resolution, not a statute, so the successor body has the same non-statutory character as the Commission it replaced. The stated design differed, substituting a Governing Council of Chief Ministers and Lieutenant Governors and the declared method of cooperative federalism for the Commission's centralised formulation of Five Year Plans, and the Five Year Plan cycle itself was discontinued after the Twelfth Plan. The change illustrates the period's institutional lesson in reverse: a body created by resolution could be, and was, ended and replaced by resolution.

### Q: How many laws did the Ninth Schedule begin with, and how did it grow?

The Schedule began in 1951 with thirteen entries, and every one was a State land reform statute, the zamindari abolition and tenancy laws whose defence in the courts had prompted the First Amendment. The Fourth Amendment of 1955 brought the total to twenty. The Seventeenth Amendment of 1964 added forty-four entries, bringing the total to sixty-four, and by then the Schedule already reached beyond agrarian reform in the strict sense. Later amendments, most of them after the period, carried it to two hundred and eighty-four entries, covering subjects far removed from land reform, among them nationalisation statutes and election law, until the Supreme Court's basic structure jurisprudence fenced the entries added after April 1973.

### Q: What did the Fourth Amendment of 1955 change about compensation for property?

The Supreme Court in State of West Bengal v Bela Banerjee had read the compensation guaranteed by Article 31(2) as a just equivalent of what the owner was deprived of, exposing every acquisition statute that fixed compensation by formula. The Fourth Amendment substituted the clause so that an acquisition law must either fix the amount of compensation or specify the principles by which it is to be determined, and barred any court from calling the law in question on the ground that the compensation provided is not adequate. It also widened the protected categories in Article 31A and inserted Article 31(2A), separating compulsory acquisition, where ownership or possession passes to the state, from other deprivations to which the compensation guarantee does not attach.

### Q: Which communities did the Hindu code Acts not apply to?

The four Acts applied to Hindus by religion in any form, to followers of the Brahmo, Prarthana and Arya Samaj movements, and to Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs, with a residual class for other persons domiciled in India who were not Muslims, Christians, Parsis or Jews and who would otherwise have been governed by Hindu law. They did not apply to Muslims, Christians, Parsis or Jews, whose personal law was left to its own statutes and sources, and members of the Scheduled Tribes were excluded unless the Central Government directed the Acts' application to them by notification. The Special Marriage Act of 1954 remained open to members of every community as an optional civil form.

### Q: Why was the uniform civil code in Article 44 not enacted in this period?

Article 44 stands in Part IV of the Constitution among the Directive Principles, which are not enforceable by any court, so it instructed the state to endeavour to secure a uniform civil code without obliging any government to enact one. The governments of the period spent their reform capital on codifying the law of the majority community, whose own reform movement had produced the draft code, and the stated position of the government, repeated by Nehru, was that a common code should not be imposed on a community that had not consented to it. The codification of one community's law and the deferral of the uniform code were two sides of the same decision, and the differentiated position in family law that resulted remains part of the period's legacy.

### Q: Which economic controls of the Nehru period were dismantled by the reforms of 1991?

The industrial policy statement of 24 July 1991 abolished industrial licensing for all but a short list of industries, and the abolition was effected by notification under the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act of 1951 itself, so the licensing statute carried out the withdrawal of its own regime. The schedules of industries reserved for the public sector, which the Industrial Policy Resolution of 1956 had set at seventeen in the first schedule, were cut in stages to a small residue. Commodity controls under the Essential Commodities Act were progressively pruned, and the wider settlement opened trade, foreign investment and finance. The detail belongs to the reforms legislation; the era point is that the dismantling proceeded by notification and policy, leaving the parent statutes in force.

### Q: What did the Industrial Policy Resolution of 1956 settle without being a statute?

The Resolution of April 1956, a statement of government policy laid before Parliament rather than an enactment, divided industry into three schedules. Seventeen industries, including arms, atomic energy, iron and steel, heavy machinery, coal, mineral oils, aircraft and railways, were reserved as the exclusive responsibility of the state. Twelve further industries were marked for progressive state ownership, with private enterprise in a supplementary role. The rest was left to private enterprise operating under licence. For three decades this executive document, and not any Act, fixed the boundary between the public and private sectors, while the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act of 1951 supplied the licensing machinery that policed the boundary firm by firm.

### Q: Why was the single Hindu Code Bill split into separate Acts?

The single code, drafted by the Rau Committee, revised by Ambedkar and introduced in 1948, attracted a combined opposition of orthodox organisations, members of the ruling party and the President's doubts about the provisional Parliament's mandate, and it could not be carried as one measure; its lapse in 1951 was followed by Ambedkar's resignation. Splitting the code into four Bills on marriage, succession, guardianship, and adoptions and maintenance allowed each subject to be debated separately and to assemble its own majority in the elected Parliament, where the marriage Bill was taken first. The technique succeeded where the single instrument had failed: between May 1955 and December 1956 all four parts became law.

### Q: Why did the era's preventive detention statute carry an expiry date?

The Preventive Detention Act of 1950 authorised detention without trial on the executive's satisfaction, a power that sat in visible tension with the safeguards of Article 22 and with the new republic's claims about liberty; the government of the day, piloting the measure through the Home Minister, Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, presented it as a temporary necessity for a disturbed transition rather than a permanent feature of the legal order. Its first section accordingly fixed the date on which it would cease to have effect. The sunset had real consequences: each continuation required a fresh amending Act and a fresh parliamentary defence, and when no further extension was enacted the Act lapsed at its final terminal date, 31 December 1969.

### Q: When did the States Reorganisation Act of 1956 take effect?

The appointed day under the States Reorganisation Act of 1956 was 1 November 1956. On that date the States of India were reconstituted substantially on linguistic lines, fourteen States and six Union Territories replacing the First Schedule's inherited classification of Part A, Part B and Part C States and the patchwork of former provinces and acceded princely unions. The Seventh Amendment of the Constitution, which rewrote the First Schedule, adjusted parliamentary seats and High Court arrangements and abolished the old classifications, operated with the same settlement. The date remains the reference point of the modern Indian map, every later creation or bifurcation of a State being measured against the reorganisation it completed.

### Q: Which laws of this period are asked about most often in the civil services examination?

The examination staples are the First Amendment and the Ninth Schedule, including the thirteen original entries, Articles 31A and 31B and the basic structure limit fixed by the Kesavananda Bharati line of cases; the Hindu code Acts of 1955 and 1956 and their difference from the single Bill that preceded them; the non-statutory character of the Planning Commission; the States Reorganisation Act and the linguistic principle; and the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act with the Industrial Policy Resolution of 1956 as the frame of the planned economy. The Citizenship Act of 1955 and the two Representation of the People Acts recur as machinery questions, usually asked as distinctions rather than as narrative.
