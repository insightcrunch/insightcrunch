The Farm Laws Repeal Act 2021 did something Indian legislatures almost never do: it took a flagship economic reform of a government with a comfortable majority, a reform defended in Parliament, in court and in eleven rounds of negotiation for over a year, and erased it from the statute book in a single working day. Repeals of major legislation are rare in India, and repeals extracted by street pressure are rarer still. Yet the genuinely unusual feature of this repeal is not that Parliament reversed itself. It is that Parliament reversed itself by exactly the procedure that had been the central objection to the original enactment. The three farm laws of 2020 were passed by voice vote, over a demand for a counted division, without examination by any parliamentary committee. The Act that repealed them in 2021 was passed without a word of debate, over a demand for discussion, without examination by any committee either. Both the making and the unmaking skipped the deliberative stage entirely, and understanding that symmetry is the key to understanding what the repeal did, what it did not do, and why almost nothing about the underlying dispute was settled by it.

![The Farm Laws Repeal Act 2021 and the repeal of the three farm laws, showing the mirrored passage of September 2020 and November 2021 - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

A repeal article in this series asks two questions: what ended, and what replaced it. The first answer here is precise. Three statutes enacted in September 2020 ceased to exist, and a fourth and older statute, the Essential Commodities Act of 1955, was restored to the form it had before 2020 by the deletion of a single sub-section. The second answer is equally precise and much stranger. Nothing replaced them. No alternative marketing law was enacted, no price assurance framework was substituted, no statutory guarantee of the minimum support price was created, and the one constitutional question the episode had squarely raised, whether Parliament was competent to legislate on agricultural trade in the manner it chose, was left undecided when the challenges to the farm laws collapsed for want of a subject. The repeal is therefore best read as a restoration rather than a settlement: it returned Indian agricultural marketing law to the position of June 2020, and it left every substantive argument that had been running since June 2020 exactly where it had been.

## The three laws the Farm Laws Repeal Act 2021 repealed

The repealing Act cannot be understood without a compact account of what it repealed, stated here in summary because the content of the three statutes belongs to a companion article. The full section-by-section treatment of the trade, contract farming and essential commodities statutes is set out in [the account of the farm laws of 2020 and what each of them changed](/2014/04/01/farm-laws-2020-explained/), and this article confines itself to the identity of the three Acts and the manner of their making, which is the part of the record the repeal mirrors. The first was the Farmers' Produce Trade and Commerce (Promotion and Facilitation) Act, 2020, numbered 21 of 2020, which created a space for trade in farmers' produce outside the physical premises of the markets regulated under state agricultural produce marketing legislation, barred state governments from levying market fees on such outside trade, and provided for electronic trading platforms. The second was the Farmers (Empowerment and Protection) Agreement on Price Assurance and Farm Services Act, 2020, numbered 20 of 2020, which built a national framework for farming agreements between farmers and buyers, fixing the price or a pricing formula before production, and routed disputes through the sub-divisional administration rather than the civil courts. The third was the Essential Commodities (Amendment) Act, 2020, numbered 22 of 2020, which inserted a new sub-section (1A) into section 3 of the Essential Commodities Act, 1955, so that the supply of specified foodstuffs, including cereals, pulses, potatoes, onions, edible oilseeds and oils, could be regulated, and stock limits imposed, only in extraordinary circumstances such as war, famine, extraordinary price rise or a natural calamity of a grave nature, with price triggers fixed for any stock limit in ordinary times.

None of the three began life as a bill. Each began as an ordinance promulgated by the President on 5 June 2020, in the middle of the first year of the pandemic, when Parliament was not in session. The ordinance route is constitutionally available and frequently used, but it meant that the reform programme entered the law without any prior parliamentary scrutiny at all, and when the replacing bills came before Parliament in the monsoon session of September 2020 the scrutiny that might have corrected that beginning did not occur either. The bills were not referred to a select committee or a standing committee. The two principal bills passed the Lok Sabha on 17 September 2020. In the Rajya Sabha on 20 September 2020, where the government's majority was not assured, opposition members demanded a division, a counted vote, on the trade and commerce bill. The Deputy Chairman in the chair, Harivansh Narayan Singh, proceeded by voice vote amid disorder in the House, members in the well of the House, and a demand that the bills first be sent to a select committee; the bills were declared passed. The Essential Commodities (Amendment) Bill completed its passage through the Rajya Sabha on 22 September 2020. On the day after the Rajya Sabha sitting, eight opposition members were suspended for the remainder of the session for unruly conduct during the passage, and a no-confidence notice against the Deputy Chairman was not admitted. The President's assent to the three bills followed within days, and the Acts were notified on 27 September 2020.

Two features of that passage matter for everything that followed, because the protests and the eventual repeal both fastened on them. The first is that a reform restructuring the terms on which the produce of several hundred million people is sold was enacted without a recorded vote in the House of the states and without committee examination in either House. Whatever the merits of the three Acts, and serious economists defended them on their merits, the manner of their passage denied the government the political ownership that deliberation confers and handed the opposition a procedural grievance that was entirely separable from the substance. The second is that the competence question was visible from the start. Agriculture is Entry 14 of the State List in the Seventh Schedule, and markets and fairs are a state subject as well, while the Union defended the statutes principally under its power over inter-state trade and commerce and the Concurrent List entry on trade and commerce in, and the production, supply and distribution of, foodstuffs and other specified goods. That contest over legislative competence was the ground on which the statutes were challenged in the Supreme Court within weeks, and it is the ground the repeal eventually left vacant.

## The ordinance origin: a reform announced as a package and enacted in a pandemic session

The manner of the farm laws' birth repays a fuller telling, because the repeal's symmetry begins here and not in September 2020. The three measures were first announced to the public not as agricultural policy debated on its own terms but as one component of the Union's economic response to the pandemic: in May 2020, in the series of announcements presenting the self reliant India package, the Finance Minister set out a set of agricultural reforms including amendments to the Essential Commodities Act, a central law to free the trade in farm produce from the state marketing regime, and a framework for farming agreements, presented as long pending structural changes whose moment had come. The ordinances followed on 5 June 2020. The ordinance power exists for situations requiring immediate action when Parliament is not sitting, and its use for considered structural reform, of a kind that committees of experts had debated for two decades, was the episode's first procedural choice and its most consequential one, because it fixed the sequence in which everything else happened: the law existed before the legislature saw it, the government's public commitment hardened around a text no parliamentarian had examined, and the farmers of the procurement belt encountered the reform as a finished fact to be resisted rather than a proposal to be shaped. When the bills to replace the ordinances reached Parliament in September, the monsoon session itself was a pandemic artifact, shortened, sitting in shifts, and conducted without Question Hour, the daily hour in which ministers answer for their departments on the floor, a curtailment the government defended on grounds of time and safety and the opposition attacked as part of the same pattern. The bills were introduced, the demand for reference to a select committee was made and refused, and the passage described above followed within days. Every deliberative instrument the system possesses, prior consultation in the public domain, committee scrutiny, question hour, the counted vote, was therefore absent from the making of these three Acts at least once and most of them at every stage, a completeness of absence that no single decision explains and that the repeal, by replicating the pattern in miniature, confirmed as habit rather than accident.

There is a further point about the ordinance origin that a repeal article should record, because it distinguishes the farm laws from most contested statutes in this series. An ordinance is constitutionally temporary: it lapses unless approved within six weeks of Parliament's reassembly, and the government that promulgates one begins, from that day, in a posture of defending a fait accompli against a deadline. The politics of the farm laws were set by that posture. The unions could present the reform as something done to them in a lockdown, without their representatives in the room, and the government could not answer by pointing to any deliberative moment at which the farming interest had been heard and answered, because there had been none. Later, when the government offered amendments, committee examination by a joint panel was among the things the unions demanded as a condition of trust at various points, and the government's reluctance to reopen the text through a committee, intelligible as tactics, read to the other side as confirmation that the text had never been meant to be examined. The repeal cannot be understood as a simple response to the laws' content; it was a response to a legislative biography, ordinance to voice vote to stay to encampment, in which each stage's procedural shortcut raised the price of the next stage's settlement, until the only settlement left was surrender of the whole.

## The design objections: what the critics said was wrong with the three laws

A repeal closes a statute, but the objections that killed it remain the best guide to what any successor would have to answer, and they were objections to the design of the laws as much as to their ideology. The trade and commerce Act attracted the heaviest fire, on four grounds that its critics, who included the state governments of the procurement belt and a substantial body of agricultural economists, pressed in a consistent order. It created the fee free trade area without writing any protection of the support price into the statute, so that the fear that procurement would wither as trade migrated out of the taxed mandis, whose fees funded the procurement infrastructure, had no textual answer. It displaced the civil courts for disputes arising under it in favour of the sub divisional administration, a design its critics read as sending the weakest party in Indian agriculture, the individual farmer, to the executive officer of the very government promoting the reform for justice against a corporate buyer. It was, in the critics' framing, a central statute about the most local of transactions, the sale of a crop in a village, enacted over the heads of the states whose subject agriculture is. And it arrived by ordinance in a pandemic, which allowed the suspicion that speed was the point. The Act's defenders, a body of economists at least as distinguished, answered each ground in terms: the support price system was untouched by the text and procurement volumes in the years of the agitation's approach had been rising; an additional marketing channel cannot harm a seller who remains free to use the mandi; the dispute mechanism was designed for speed, with appeal upwards within the administration; and state marketing law had failed for decades to reform itself, which was the vacancy the Centre had entered. Both cases were arguable, and the tragedy of the episode's procedure is that they were never argued in the forum built for arguing them. No committee took evidence on the fee question. No clause by clause consideration tested the dispute design. The objections therefore accumulated in the street with their answers unexamined beside them, and the repeal eventually disposed of the statute without disposing of a single one of the arguments, which survive intact for the next draftsman.

The price assurance Act, the contract farming statute, divided opinion differently. Its framework was the most conventional of the three, modelled on practices and model legislation that had circulated for years, and several states already had contract farming provisions of their own. The objections that attached to it were narrower: that a written agreement between a farmer and a sponsor is a contract between unequals whatever the statute says about good faith; that the Act's protections, including the bar on recovery of dues from the farmer's land, would be tested only in disputes the farmer could not afford to run; and that the same administrative dispute route, conciliation board to sub divisional magistrate to appellate authority, kept the courts out of the farmer's most consequential commercial relationship. Its defenders replied that the Act for the first time gave the farming agreement a statutory floor, a price or pricing formula fixed before sowing, a bar on the sponsor acquiring any interest in the farmer's land, and protection of the farmer's position if the sponsor defaulted, and that contract farming existed across Indian agriculture with none of those protections before 2020. The Essential Commodities amendment stood apart from both. It had the widest expert support of the three measures, because the proposition that stock limits should be exceptional and price triggered rather than routine had been recommended by committees for years, and its inclusion in the repeal was the clearest demonstration that the repeal was a political settlement rather than a considered legislative judgment: Parliament in November 2021 struck down the one measure of the three that much of the expert community, including economists otherwise critical of the package, thought worth keeping in some form, and it did so in the same voice vote and for the same reason, that the three Acts stood or fell together as the symbol the agitation had made of them. A repeal driven by a movement answers the movement's demand, which was for the three laws as a set; it is not required to discriminate among them, and this one did not.

## The states answer back: counter-legislation and its constitutional fate

The farm laws were central statutes on a state subject, and the first institutional resistance to them came not from the street but from state legislatures, in an episode that belongs to the repeal's prehistory because it tested, and exposed the limits of, the federal answer to a centralising statute. In October 2020 the Punjab Vidhan Sabha, meeting in a special session, passed bills amending each of the three central Acts in their application to Punjab, restoring the primacy of the state marketing framework, writing in protections for the support price, and amending the state's own marketing legislation alongside. Rajasthan and Chhattisgarh, governed by the principal opposition party, passed comparable counter measures in their assemblies in the weeks that followed. The constitutional route for such state legislation runs through the repugnancy doctrine: where a state law conflicts with a central law on a Concurrent List subject, the state law can prevail in that state if it has been reserved for the President's consideration and has received the President's assent. The Punjab bills were accordingly sent forward for assent. The assent never came. The bills waited with the Governor and then in the Union's processes while the protest, the stay and the negotiation ran their course, and the repeal of the parent Acts in November 2021 extinguished the subject matter on which the state amendments were to operate, leaving the counter legislation a dead letter without a decision ever having been taken on it in terms.

The episode deserves its place in the repeal's record for what it shows about the structure of the dispute. The states that objected to the farm laws had a constitutional remedy, and they used it, and the remedy depended entirely on the assent of the very executive whose statutes were being resisted, so that the federal check operated, in practice, as a request addressed to the author of the measure. The repeal rendered the question moot before it could be answered, as it rendered the Supreme Court's competence question moot, and the two mootnesses together describe the episode's constitutional signature: every institutional forum in which the farm laws were contested, the Rajya Sabha, the courts, the state legislatures, the committee system, was prevented by the manner of the contest from delivering a decision, and the only forum that produced an outcome was the street, followed by an afternoon's voice votes that ratified it. A legal system can survive such an episode; what it cannot do is cite it as precedent for anything, because the episode decided nothing that a later dispute can use.

## A year at the borders of Delhi: protest, negotiation and a suspended statute

Opposition to the ordinances began in Punjab within weeks of their promulgation and hardened as the bills moved through Parliament. After the Acts were notified, farmer unions, principally from Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh, marched on Delhi in late November 2020 in the movement they called Dilli Chalo, were stopped at the borders of the capital, and settled into encampments at Singhu, Tikri, Ghazipur and other entry points that they then held, through a winter, a summer and a second winter, for a little over a year. The Samyukt Kisan Morcha, a coalition of farmer unions formed to coordinate the agitation, spoke for the encampments and conducted the negotiations. The demand was never amendment. From the first round of talks it was repeal of all three Acts, together with a statutory guarantee that the minimum support price, the price at which government agencies procure specified crops, would be a legal entitlement rather than an administrative practice.

Alongside the talks the government conducted a public persuasion campaign of a kind that itself became part of the record. In December 2020 the Agriculture Minister published an open letter to the farmers of the country, setting out the government's case at length, assuring them that the support price system and the mandis would continue, and appealing to the protesting unions to return to the negotiating table without the precondition of repeal. The letter stated the government's difficulty honestly, as its authors saw it: the reforms had been demanded for decades by committees and experts, they took nothing away that already existed, and the government's fear was that misinformation had filled the space where its explanation should have been. The unions read the same letter as proof that the government intended to explain rather than to listen, and the talks that followed it hardened both accounts into the positions that held until November 2021. The episode matters to the repeal's history because it forecloses a later simplification. The government did not refuse to engage; it engaged continuously, at ministerial level, in writing and across eleven sittings, and failed anyway, because the one concession the other side would accept was the one concession engagement was designed to avoid.

The government negotiated extensively without conceding the central demand. Between December 2020 and 22 January 2021 ministers held eleven rounds of talks with union representatives. The government's offers moved in stages: amendments to meet stated concerns, then a written assurance on the continuation of support price procurement, and finally, in January 2021, an offer to keep the three Acts in abeyance for eighteen months while a committee discussed the issues. The unions rejected each stage because none of them was repeal, and the eleventh round ended without a date for a twelfth. The arithmetic of the deadlock was simple and stayed unchanged for ten months: the government would discuss everything except the existence of the statutes, and the unions would discuss nothing until the statutes ceased to exist.

### How many rounds of talks did the government hold with the farmer unions?

Eleven rounds of ministerial talks were held between December 2020 and 22 January 2021. The government's final offer, made in January 2021, was to suspend the operation of the three Acts for eighteen months while a joint committee examined the issues. The unions rejected the offer because it left the statutes alive, and no twelfth round was ever fixed.

Events then moved onto two tracks that ran in parallel until the repeal. On the street, the protest acquired the incidents that fixed it in public memory. A tractor march into Delhi on Republic Day, 26 January 2021, deviated from the routes agreed with the police and ended in clashes and the occupation of the Red Fort, an episode the union leadership disowned in its violent parts while refusing to withdraw the movement. At Lakhimpur Kheri in Uttar Pradesh on 3 October 2021, violence connected with a ministerial convoy left eight people dead, four of them protesting farmers, and a Union minister's son among the accused in the criminal case; the killings sharpened the confrontation in the state that was about to elect a new assembly. The number of protesters who died across the year of encampment itself became a contested figure. The Samyukt Kisan Morcha dedicated its eventual victory to around 715 dead, a figure its leaders repeated in December 2021. The government's position, stated by the Agriculture Minister Narendra Singh Tomar in answer to a parliamentary question on 1 December 2021, was that the Ministry of Agriculture and Farmers Welfare held no records of deaths of protesting farmers and that the question of compensation therefore did not arise. Those two positions, a named toll on one side and an absence of records on the other, were never reconciled, and any honest account has to carry both.

In the Supreme Court, the challenges to the three Acts were heard in a batch of petitions of which Rakesh Vaishnav v. Union of India was the lead matter. The petitions divided into those attacking the Acts as beyond Parliament's competence and harmful to farmers, those supporting them as valid and beneficial, and those filed by residents of Delhi and its neighbourhood complaining of the blockades. On 12 January 2021 a three-judge bench headed by the Chief Justice, S. A. Bobde, with A. S. Bopanna and V. Ramasubramanian JJ, did something the Court very rarely does in a constitutional challenge: it stayed the implementation of all three Acts until further orders, without first holding that any provision was unconstitutional, saying in terms that a stay might assuage the hurt feelings of the farmers and encourage them to come to the negotiating table in good faith. The same order constituted a committee of four, the agricultural economists Ashok Gulati and Pramod Kumar Joshi, the farmer leader Anil Ghanwat of the Shetkari Sanghatana, and Bhupinder Singh Mann of the Bharatiya Kisan Sangh, to hear the stakeholders and report to the Court. Mann recused himself within days, saying he stood with the farmers. The protesting unions refused to appear before the committee at all, on the ground that its remaining members had publicly supported the farm laws. The committee nevertheless heard other farmer organisations, state governments and stakeholders, and submitted its report to the Court on 19 March 2021. The Court never published it and never resumed the hearing to a conclusion.

### Were the farm laws ever actually implemented?

Only in name. The three Acts stood on the statute book from September 2020, but their implementation was stayed by the Supreme Court on 12 January 2021, three and a half months after notification, and the stay was never lifted. The trade areas, the farming agreement framework and the stock limit regime never operated in the ordinary course.

The consequence deserves emphasis because it shapes what repeal meant in practice. When Parliament repealed the three Acts in November 2021, it was repealing statutes that had been frozen for ten of their fourteen months of existence. The regulated mandi system they were designed to bypass had continued throughout, since the trade Act had added an outside option rather than abolishing the mandis and the stay had suspended even that addition. Procurement at support prices had continued throughout, since nothing in the three Acts had touched the procurement system in the first place. The statutory position on the ground in November 2021 was therefore already very close to the statutory position the repeal would produce, which is one reason the repeal could be drafted in three sections: there was almost nothing in operation to unwind, no licences to cancel, no functioning machinery to dismantle, and only a thin layer of transactions entered into during the short un-stayed window to worry about.

## Why persuasion failed: the interests inside the argument

The Prime Minister's concession that the government had failed to convince a section of farmers invites the analytical question of why the convincing failed, and the answer matters to the repeal because it determines what the repeal was a response to: not ignorance of the laws' benefits, as the government's own rhetoric sometimes suggested, but a collision between the laws and a working system of interests that their text did not accommodate. At the centre stood the economics of the procurement belt. In Punjab and Haryana the support price is not an abstraction; it is the price at which the state, through its agencies, reliably lifts the paddy and wheat harvests, purchased through regulated mandis in which licensed commission agents advance credit to farmers across the season and are repaid at the sale. The mandi fees and rural development charges levied on those transactions fund both the market infrastructure and a significant stream of state revenue. The trade Act's design, a fee free channel outside the mandis with no statutory floor price written into it, threatened every strand of that arrangement at once: if trade migrated out of the mandis, the fees that funded the system would shrink, the commission agent's role and credit would be displaced by buyers answerable to no local structure, the state's revenue would fall, and the procurement operation that depended on the mandi's machinery would be left standing in a market hollowed out around it. A farmer in that system did not need to believe the government intended to abolish the support price, a step no text proposed, to reason that a law which starved the infrastructure of procurement while promising its continuation by executive assurance alone was a risk he had not been consulted about and need not accept. The government's answer, that the mandis and procurement continued untouched in the text and that an additional channel cannot injure a seller free to ignore it, was a good answer to the text. It was not an answer to the system, because the system's fear was dynamic, about what migration of trade would do over five seasons, and dynamic fears are not met by pointing at a static page. The repeal, on this analysis, was the price of a reform designed as an addition and experienced as a subtraction, by the one region organised enough to make its experience decisive.

The persuasion failure had a second layer, of trust rather than interest, and it explains why even farmers outside the procurement belt, with less to lose, did not rally to the laws in numbers that might have changed the negotiation. The government argued that reforms of this family had been recommended for decades, and the record supported it: expert committees, economic surveys and the manifestos of parties across the spectrum, including the party leading the opposition in 2020, had at various times proposed freeing farm trade, reforming the Essential Commodities regime and encouraging contract farming. The unions' response to that record was not to dispute it but to discount it, on the ground that a recommendation is not a mandate and that reforms proposed for consultation cannot be enacted by ordinance without the consultation and retain the authority of the proposal. Each procedural shortcut thus consumed some of the substantive goodwill the underlying ideas possessed, and by the time ministers sat down to explain the laws' merits in the winter of 2020 they were explaining them to an audience that had already decided what the manner of enactment said about the matter's good faith. The repeal's lesson for future reformers is written into that sequence and is worth stating as this series states its namable claims: in Indian agricultural legislation, the process is not packaging for the policy; it is the first clause of the policy, and a government that will not spend deliberation on a reform will eventually spend the reform itself.

## The announcement of 19 November 2021

The reversal, when it came, came from the top and without warning to Parliament. On the morning of 19 November 2021 the Prime Minister, Narendra Modi, addressed the nation on television and announced that the government had decided to repeal the three farm laws in the winter session of Parliament that was about to begin. The date was Gurpurab, the birth anniversary of Guru Nanak, a festival of the first order in Punjab, the state where the protest had begun and from which a large share of the encamped farmers came. Two elements of the address became the canonical record of the government's reasons. The Prime Minister said that the government had been unable to convince a section of the farmers of the benefits of the laws, that this was a failure he acknowledged, and he apologised to the country for it, while continuing to maintain that the laws themselves had been enacted with honest intent for farmers' welfare. He also repeated the government's framing that the protest represented a section of farmers rather than the farming community as a whole. In the same address he announced that a committee would be constituted to work on making the support price system more effective and transparent, and on allied questions including zero budget natural farming and changes in cropping patterns, with representatives of the Centre, the states, farmers, agricultural scientists and agricultural economists on it. That promise, made in the announcement itself, is the origin of the committee that was eventually notified in July 2022 and of the demand that survived the repeal in its most concrete form.

### Why was the repeal announced on Guru Nanak Jayanti?

The announcement came in an address on 19 November 2021, which was Gurpurab, the birth anniversary of Guru Nanak and a leading festival in Punjab, the state at the centre of the protest. The Prime Minister framed the decision as reconciliation, apologised for failing to persuade a section of farmers, and promised a committee on support prices.

The timing had a second dimension, which is a fact about the calendar and should be stated as such without dressing it as a proved motive. Assembly elections were due within months in five states, among them Punjab, where the protest was a dominant political issue, and Uttar Pradesh, the largest state, where the protesting districts of the west were electorally significant and where the Lakhimpur Kheri killings had kept the farm question at the front of the campaign. Opposition speakers made the electoral connection expressly and repeatedly, in Parliament and outside it; the Leader of the Opposition in the Rajya Sabha, Mallikarjun Kharge, said on the day of the repeal's passage that the government had taken the laws back in view of the upcoming elections, after more than seven hundred deaths during the agitation. The government never accepted that characterisation. Its stated reason, in the Prime Minister's address and in the Statement of Objects and Reasons of the repeal bill, remained the failure to persuade: the laws were good, the explanation had not carried, and in the seventy fifth year of independence the need was to take everyone along. A reader weighing the two accounts should notice that they are not strictly contradictory. A government can fail to persuade, can regret the failure, and can still choose the moment of its concession with an election in view, and the record establishes the concession and the calendar beyond dispute while leaving the weight of each consideration where the evidence leaves it, inside the government's own deliberations.

It is also worth recording what the announcement did not contain, because the gaps determined the politics of the following fortnight. The Prime Minister did not concede a statutory guarantee of the support price; he promised a committee. He did not announce the withdrawal of the criminal cases registered against protesters during the agitation, or compensation for the families of those who had died; those questions were left to the negotiation that followed the announcement and were answered only in the government's letter to the Samyukt Kisan Morcha three weeks later. And the unions, for their part, did not treat the announcement as the end. The Samyukt Kisan Morcha welcomed the decision, said the protest would continue until the repeal was carried through Parliament in law, and restated the surviving demands, so that the period between 19 November and the passage of the repeal bill was not a formality but a short, watchful interval in which neither side stood down.

## The repealing statute: three sections and nothing else

The Farm Laws Repeal Act, 2021 is one of the shortest consequential statutes in the Indian code. It is Act 40 of 2021, and the whole of its operative text fits in three sections. Section 1 gives the short title alone; the Act carries no commencement clause, so it took effect on the President's assent and its publication in the Gazette on 30 November 2021. Section 2 does the repealing, in one sentence: the Farmers (Empowerment and Protection) Agreement on Price Assurance and Farm Services Act, 2020, the Farmers' Produce Trade and Commerce (Promotion and Facilitation) Act, 2020 and the Essential Commodities (Amendment) Act, 2020 are repealed. Section 3 does the restoring, also in one sentence: in section 3 of the Essential Commodities Act, 1955, sub-section (1A) shall be omitted. That is the entire statute. There is no preamble beyond the standard long title, which describes an Act to repeal the three 2020 Acts and to amend the Essential Commodities Act, 1955; there is no savings clause; there is no schedule; there is no provision about the support price, about the cases against protesters, about compensation, or about anything that had happened while the repealed Acts stood on the book.

### How many sections does the Farm Laws Repeal Act 2021 have?

Three. Section 1 gives the short title. Section 2 repeals the three farm statutes of 2020 by name and number. Section 3 amends the Essential Commodities Act, 1955 by omitting the sub-section (1A) that the 2020 amendment had inserted into its section 3. The Act contains no savings clause, no schedule and no provision on support prices.

The architecture rewards a moment's attention, because the pairing of section 2 and section 3 shows the draftsman dealing with a subtlety that summary accounts of the repeal almost always miss. Two of the three repealed Acts were free standing statutes, and repealing them removed them entire. The third was an amending Act: its whole work had been to insert sub-section (1A) into the Essential Commodities Act and to attach conditions to the regulation of foodstuffs under that Act. Under the ordinary principles governing the repeal of an amending statute, repealing the amendment does not by itself undo the amendment's work on the parent text; an amendment once incorporated into the principal Act is generally treated as having become part of that Act, and a later repeal of the amending Act leaves the incorporated change standing unless the repealing statute says otherwise. If Parliament had stopped at section 2, the deregulation of cereals, pulses, potatoes, onions, edible oilseeds and oils would therefore arguably have survived the repeal of the statute that created it, an absurd outcome that nobody intended. Section 3 exists to foreclose that argument: by omitting sub-section (1A) from section 3 of the 1955 Act in terms, it restored the parent Act to its pre-2020 shape, and with it the Union's unrestricted standing power to regulate the supply and distribution of the foodstuffs concerned and to impose stock limits without waiting for war, famine or an extraordinary price rise. The Essential Commodities dimension of the episode is examined in the [guide to the Essential Commodities Act 1955 and the powers collected in its section 3](/2014/03/01/essential-commodities-act-1955-guide/), and the point here is confined to the mechanism: the repeal restored the 1955 Act by express amendment, not by mere repeal, and that choice is the one piece of careful drafting in an otherwise bare instrument.

What the Act omitted is as important as what it contained. A repeal statute is the natural place for a savings clause, the standard provision preserving rights accrued, liabilities incurred and proceedings pending under the repealed enactments, and for transitional directions about anything done while the repealed law operated. The farm laws repeal has none. The gap was visible enough that it was put to the government in Parliament: a member asked whether the repeal Act dealt with the agreements entered into and acts done under the three Acts between their enactment and the repeal, and if not, why not. The Agriculture Minister's written answer was that the implementation of the farm laws had been stayed by the Supreme Court in January 2021, that the repeal bill had been passed by both Houses on 29 November 2021 repealing all three laws, and that agreements entered into and acts done under them would be governed by the relevant legal provisions. In practical terms that answer leans, without naming it, on the general savings doctrine of section 6 of the General Clauses Act, 1897, which preserves accrued rights and liabilities on a repeal unless a contrary intention appears, and on the smallness of the un-stayed window in which anything could have accrued at all. The account given in this article is legal information about a concluded episode rather than advice on any individual transaction, and anyone whose rights turn on an agreement made while the repealed Acts were in force needs advice on those specific facts, because the answer depends on which law governed the act when it was done.

The Statement of Objects and Reasons appended to the bill, signed by the Agriculture Minister on 24 November 2021, is a longer document than the bill it accompanied, and its argument is worth stating fairly because it is the government's own account of the repeal. It recites the government's interventions for farmers, the budgetary allocations, the soil health cards, the direct transfers and the fixing of support prices at at least one and a half times the cost of production. It describes the three Acts as enactments made for the socio economic development of farmers after extensive consultations and in response to a demand made for years by farmers, experts and farmer organisations. It then concedes the political fact in a sentence that was much quoted: even though only a group of farmers are protesting against these laws, the government has tried hard to sensitise the farmers on the importance of the farm laws and explain their merits, and, the operation of the laws having been stayed by the Supreme Court, in the seventy fifth year of independence everyone had to be taken together on the path of inclusive growth and development. Supporters of the repeal read that sentence as a graceful concession. Its critics read the opening clause, only a group of farmers, as evidence that the government conceded the policy while withholding recognition from the movement that had defeated it. Both readings are available on the text, and the text is the best evidence of the government's position: a repeal offered as an act of national inclusion, by a government that continued to believe the repealed laws had been right.

For a reader assembling the primary record of this episode, the bill as introduced, the Statement of Objects and Reasons, the Gazette text of Act 40 of 2021 and the two days' proceedings in each House form a compact set of documents, and laying them side by side in [the ReportMedic presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) makes the contrast between the long justification and the three operative sections immediately visible. The contrast is the lesson. Parliament was asked to repeal on the strength of an explanatory memorandum, not on the strength of a debate, and the statute it produced answers the political question completely while answering almost no legal question at all.

## Repeal as a legislative instrument, and why this repeal is not an ordinary one

Repeal is the plainest act a legislature performs, and the Indian statute book is full of it, which is why the abnormality of this instance needs the ordinary background to be seen against. Parliament repeals statutes constantly and almost always without controversy. The great bulk of Indian repeal legislation consists of housekeeping: the Repealing and Amending Acts, passed in batches across the decades, which strike out enactments that have spent themselves, been superseded, or survived from a colonial code nobody has opened in generations. The government in office from 2014 made a programme of this housekeeping and repealed obsolete central Acts by the hundred, in omnibus bills that passed with minimal debate because there was nothing in them to debate: the statutes removed were dead letters, and their removal changed no living person's rights. A second, smaller class consists of repeal and replacement, where an old statute is struck down and a successor erected on its site in the same instrument or a companion one, the pattern this series treats in its repeal articles generally, in which continuity questions dominate and savings clauses do the heavy work. The Farm Laws Repeal Act belongs to neither class. It is the rare third thing: the repeal of a living, contested, signature policy of the very government that had enacted it, carried against the government's own stated conviction that the policy was right, with no replacement of any kind. The statute book offers no close modern parallel at the Centre for a repeal of that character in the economic field, which is the answer in embryo to the question the ledger asks about whether Parliament has ever repealed a major economic law in this way: governments have let contested measures lapse, have amended them into different shapes, have allowed courts to strike them, and have repealed each other's enactments after a change of power, but a governing majority erasing its own flagship reform under street pressure, in a single afternoon, stands essentially alone.

The legal mechanics of repeal explain the instrument's spareness. A repeal requires no special majority and no special procedure; the power to enact carries the power to repeal, and a statute made by simple majority falls by simple majority, which is why the entire constitutional apparatus of the episode could be contained in an ordinary bill passed by voice vote. What repeal does to the world the statute created is governed, in the absence of express provision, by section 6 of the General Clauses Act, 1897, which provides that a repeal does not revive anything not in force when the repeal takes effect, does not affect the previous operation of the repealed enactment or anything duly done under it, does not affect rights, privileges, obligations or liabilities acquired or incurred under it, and does not affect penalties or proceedings in respect of offences under it, unless a different intention appears. The farm laws repeal expressed no different intention, and the design of the episode made the default rules almost costless to rely on: the stay had prevented the repealed Acts from generating the dense web of accrued rights that makes an unsavings repeal dangerous, and the one incorporated amendment that repeal alone could not reach was dealt with by section 3 in terms. The contrast with a repeal like that of a taxing or licensing statute in full operation, where the savings clause is the most fought over provision in the bill, measures how unusual the farm laws' posture was. These were statutes that had lived their whole lives under a judicial freeze, and they could be killed with a sentence because, in operational terms, they had barely lived at all.

One further mechanical point completes the picture, because it determines what kind of act the announcement of 19 November 2021 was in law. A prime ministerial announcement repeals nothing. Between the announcement and the assent on 30 November 2021 the three Acts remained in force, stayed but valid, and had the winter session failed to pass the bill the announcement would have had no legal consequence at all. The unions' refusal to vacate the borders on the strength of the announcement, and their insistence that the protest would end only when the repeal was law, was therefore not obstinacy but a correct reading of the constitutional position, and it is a reading worth preserving in an article about a repeal: in a parliamentary system a reform dies the way it was born, by an Act, and everything before the Act is weather.

The transition the repeal had to manage was, for the reasons given, small, but it was not nothing, and its smallness should not be allowed to conceal the general point it illustrates about repeals of market legislation. During the un-stayed months between September 2020 and January 2021 the trade Act was law, and trade could lawfully be conducted in reliance on it: produce could be sold outside the mandis without the state fee, electronic platforms could be and were prepared, and farming agreements could be drafted on the price assurance Act's template even if the harvest cycle meant few matured. A repeal that ignored that residue would have created a class of transactions lawful when made and orphaned when the statute vanished. The Indian law's answer, in the absence of the savings clause Parliament did not write, is the General Clauses Act default described above, which preserves the previous operation of the repealed enactment and rights and obligations accrued under it, so that a sale completed in the window is not reopened and a liability incurred in the window is not forgiven. The government's parliamentary answer, that agreements and acts done would be governed by the relevant legal provisions, is a compressed statement of exactly that default. The deeper reason the residue caused no litigation worth recording is the stay: by freezing implementation ten months before the repeal, the Supreme Court had, unintentionally, performed the transition management that a careful repealing statute would have had to perform for itself, and the repeal arrived to find the patient already anaesthetised. Drafters of future repeals of operating statutes will not be able to count on a court having done that work for them, and the farm laws repeal, read as a drafting precedent, is a warning disguised as an example: its three sections sufficed only because history had emptied the statutes before Parliament emptied the book.

## Passed and repealed the same way: 29 November 2021

The winter session of Parliament opened on 29 November 2021 in an atmosphere that belonged to the same story. On that first day the Rajya Sabha suspended twelve of its opposition members for the remainder of the session, for unruly conduct during the monsoon session that had preceded it, so that the House which took up the repeal in the afternoon did so with a dozen opposition benches emptied by a disciplinary vote about a previous session's disorder. The suspension was a distinct proceeding from the repeal and the two should not be conflated, but a reader reconstructing the day should hold them together, because they compose the day's parliamentary weather: a session opening under the sign of suspension, a repeal demanded by the suspended members' parties being passed in the same hours without the discussion those parties sought, and a government majority managing the chamber by the instruments of discipline and timetable rather than by argument. The repeal was the first business of the session. The Farm Laws Repeal Bill, 2021 stood at the head of the Lok Sabha's list of business. The House met at noon. After obituary references and the swearing in of two newly elected members, the Agriculture Minister, Narendra Singh Tomar, moved the bill for consideration shortly after twelve. Opposition members were already in the well of the House demanding a discussion, on the support price, on the deaths during the agitation and on the manner of the bill's taking up. The Speaker, Om Birla, said a discussion could be held if members returned to their seats and the House was in order. The sloganeering continued. The Speaker put the bill to a voice vote and declared it passed. Contemporary reporting timed the Lok Sabha's disposal of the bill at a matter of minutes from its introduction, eight minutes by one widely carried account, and the whole exercise in the House was over well inside the first hour of the session.

The bill went the same afternoon to the Rajya Sabha, which took it up at two o'clock. There the presiding officer allowed the Leader of the Opposition, Mallikarjun Kharge, a few minutes to state the opposition's position before the bill was moved: the opposition welcomed the repeal, Kharge said, while attributing it to the coming elections and the deaths of more than seven hundred farmers, and it wanted a discussion. The Agriculture Minister's answer when he moved the bill stated the government's position in its most compressed form: since the opposition parties had themselves been demanding the repeal of the laws, and the government was itself repealing them, there was no need for a discussion. The bill was put to a voice vote in a noisy House and declared passed. From the first movement of the bill in the Lok Sabha to its passage in the Rajya Sabha, contemporary reporting put the elapsed time at about two hours. Three days of legislative time had not been consumed; a single afternoon had.

The speed had a further feature that distinguishes it even from other hurried Indian legislation. A bill ordinarily passes through distinguishable stages on distinguishable days: introduction, circulation to members, consideration, clause by clause voting, and the interval between the two Houses in which the text can be read by the public whose law it will become. The repeal bill compressed the whole cycle into a single day's sittings because there was, by design, nothing to read: three sections, the operative one a single sentence, a text whose entire content a member could absorb between the lobby and the chamber. That is the final element of the symmetry and in some ways the most instructive. In 2020 the compression of stages had concealed a programme of genuine complexity, three statutes restructuring a sector, from the scrutiny that might have improved or legitimised it. In 2021 the same compression concealed nothing, because there was nothing to conceal, and the opposition's demand for discussion was correspondingly not about the text at all: no member needed an afternoon to understand section 2. The discussion demanded was about everything the text omitted, the support price, the deaths, the minister at Lakhimpur, the government's reasons, and the demand was refused for exactly that reason, because the omitted subjects were the only ones a debate could have reached. A legislature's control over its own agenda is the control over which questions get asked in the country's name, and on 29 November 2021 that control was exercised to ensure that the largest legislative reversal of the decade was accompanied by the smallest possible record of reasons.

Set the two passages side by side and the mirror is exact enough to be tabulated, which is why the comparison is this article's findable artifact. In September 2020 the government had the votes, or believed it had them, and chose not to count them, because a counted vote in the Rajya Sabha was the one outcome it could not be sure of controlling and because counting would have required the House to be in order. In November 2021 the government had the outcome it wanted within reach and chose not to debate it, because debate would have put the support price, the deaths and the Lakhimpur Kheri case on the parliamentary record at the head of an election season. In 2020 the opposition's demand was for a division and for a select committee, and it was refused. In 2021 the opposition's demand was for a discussion, and it was refused. In neither year was any committee of Parliament asked to look at the text. The bills of 2020 and the bill of 2021 are connected by more than subject matter: they are products of a single, consistent practice in which the forms of Parliament are observed, motions are moved, voices are called for, the ayes are declared to have it, and the deliberative stages that exist to test a text and to put disagreement on record are skipped whenever they threaten inconvenience. The protest and process critique of the whole farm laws episode, including the argument that this practice and not any single statute was the deeper injury, is developed in [the companion account of the farmers' protest and its legislative lessons](/2014/05/01/farmers-protest-legislative-lessons/), and the table below states the parallel in its barest form.

| Element of the passage | September 2020: the three farm bills | November 2021: the Farm Laws Repeal Bill |
|---|---|---|
| Route into Parliament | Ordinances first, on 5 June 2020, replaced by bills in the monsoon session | A single bill introduced on the first day of the winter session, 29 November 2021 |
| Examination by a parliamentary committee | None; a demand to refer the bills to a select committee was not accepted | None; the bill was passed on the day of its introduction |
| Discussion on the floor | Truncated by disorder in the Rajya Sabha; no structured debate on the clauses | None in either House; passed amid sloganeering in the Lok Sabha and after a brief opposition statement in the Rajya Sabha |
| Division, a counted vote | Demanded by the opposition in the Rajya Sabha and not held; the bills were declared passed by voice vote | Not demanded to a conclusion; the bill was declared passed by voice vote in both Houses |
| Time taken in the Houses | The principal bills passed the Rajya Sabha in a single sitting on 20 September 2020; the Essential Commodities amendment completed passage on 22 September 2020 | The whole passage in both Houses was completed in about two hours on 29 November 2021 |
| The opposition's central demand at the moment of passage | A division and reference to a select committee | A discussion, principally on the support price and the deaths during the agitation |
| The government's stated reason for the shortcut | The House was not in order and members would not return to their seats | The opposition itself wanted repeal, so there was nothing to discuss |
| Consequence in the following days | Eight opposition members suspended in the Rajya Sabha; a no-confidence notice against the Deputy Chairman not admitted | The session continued into a confrontational winter sitting; the repeal itself was not further debated |

The table supports the claim around which this article is built, and the claim should be stated without varnish. The process was never the point of contention for either side. The objection to the passage of the farm laws in 2020, pressed by the opposition for a year, was that Parliament had not deliberated: no committee, no division, no considered debate. The repeal of those laws in 2021 was carried without deliberation by a government that saw no advantage in a debate, and it was accepted by an opposition that wanted the outcome more than it wanted the argument, since its members voted with their voices for a bill whose manner of passage repeated the offence they had been denouncing. The episode therefore produced a complete reversal of policy and no change whatever in the practice that had been objected to. No rule was amended. No convention was strengthened. Nothing in the record of 29 November 2021 makes a repetition of 20 September 2020 in some future session any less likely, and that, more than the fate of the three statutes, is the durable significance of the repeal for the legislative process.

## Valid in law, empty in deliberation: the legal status of a passage without debate

Because this article's central complaint is procedural, precision about what the procedure's defects do and do not amount to in law is essential, and the distinction cuts against any easy conclusion that either passage was illegal. The Constitution prescribes the form of lawmaking in outline: a bill passed by both Houses and assented to by the President becomes an Act, questions in a House are determined by a majority of the votes of members present and voting, and the presiding officer's declaration of the result of a voice vote is the ordinary and lawful mode of ascertaining that majority unless a division is duly demanded and granted under the rules. A division is a right governed by the rules of the House, not by the Constitution in terms, and the rules condition its exercise on order in the House; the Deputy Chairman's position on 20 September 2020, defended by the Chairman on the following day, was that he had repeatedly offered a division to members who returned to their seats, and that members in the well demanding a vote while disrupting the sitting could not have one. The opposition's counter position, argued in print by constitutional commentators in the days that followed, was that a demand for division from members in their seats, which at least some members said they had made, obliged the chair to count the House, and that disorder created in part by the refusal could not be prayed in aid to defeat the demand. That argument was never tested, because the Constitution also provides, in Article 122, that the validity of proceedings in Parliament shall not be called in question on the ground of any alleged irregularity of procedure, and that no officer or member in whom powers are vested for regulating procedure is subject to the courts' jurisdiction in respect of the exercise of those powers. Whatever happened in the Rajya Sabha on 20 September 2020 was therefore, for legal purposes, the passage of three bills; the grievance it generated was political and constitutional in the small c sense, a matter of convention, legitimacy and the health of the institution, and it could be vindicated only politically. It was, in the event, vindicated politically, by the repeal, and by nothing else.

The passage of the repeal bill a year later sits on the same legal footing and illustrates the same gap between validity and deliberation from the other side. No provision of the Constitution and no rule of either House requires that a bill be debated before it is put to the vote. A House that wishes to pass a bill in silence, amid noise, in two hours, on the first day of a session, is exercising powers the rules give it: the minister moves, the presiding officer puts the question, the ayes have it. The opposition's complaint on 29 November 2021 was not that the passage was void, and no serious voice claimed it was; the complaint was that a government which had spent a year saying the farm laws were among its most considered reforms declined to spend an afternoon saying why it was withdrawing them, and that subjects of obvious public consequence, the support price guarantee, the deaths, the Lakhimpur Kheri case, were thereby kept off the record of the only forum whose record the executive cannot edit. Rahul Gandhi's formulation outside the House that day, that the government was terrified of the discussion and did not have the courage to defend its actions, was polemic, but it identified the real stake correctly: debate in Parliament is not a courtesy extended to the opposition but the mechanism by which the executive's reasons become a public record against which it can later be held. A repeal carried without that record leaves the reasons where the executive prefers them, in a televised address and a Statement of Objects and Reasons, unexamined by any question on the floor. Readers of this series will recognise the pattern as the farm episode's deepest continuity with the rest of the Indian legislative record examined in these articles: the displacement of deliberation from the floor of the House to instruments the government controls outright, the ordinance, the explanatory memorandum, the address to the nation. The farm laws were unusual only in that the displacement was total at both ends of their lives, and in that, for once, it failed to protect the policy it was protecting.

## What the repeal changed, and what it deliberately left standing

The legal consequences of the repeal can be enumerated completely, which is itself unusual for a major legislative event, because the statute did so little. First, the two free standing farm statutes, the trade and commerce Act and the price assurance Act, ceased to exist as law from 30 November 2021. The national framework for farming agreements went with the second of them, and the fee free trade area outside the regulated markets went with the first. Secondly, by the omission of sub-section (1A) of section 3 of the Essential Commodities Act, 1955, the conditional deregulation of the specified foodstuffs ended: the Union's power to regulate their production, supply and distribution, and to impose stock limits, again runs in ordinary times and not only in war, famine, extraordinary price rise or grave natural calamity, subject to the other provisions of the 1955 Act as they always stood. Thirdly, and by silence, everything the 2020 Acts had not touched stayed untouched. The three Acts had never repealed, amended or displaced the state agricultural produce marketing statutes; they had built a parallel, fee free channel beside the state mandis and forbidden the states to tax that channel. With the parallel channel abolished, the state statutes simply resumed the exclusive occupancy of the field they had never in law vacated. The mandis of Punjab, Haryana and every other state continued under the same state Acts, the same market committees and the same fee structures that had governed them in 2019.

The geography of what continued deserves one further sentence, because it explains the geography of the protest. Public procurement at the support price has never been uniformly spread across India; it is deep in Punjab and Haryana, where the mandi system, the commission agent network and state procurement machinery grew together over decades, substantial in a handful of other states for paddy and wheat, and thin or absent for most crops in most districts, where the support price functions as a reference rate rather than as a floor any agency stands ready to enforce. The 2020 trade Act threatened the deep procurement belt's arrangements first and most, since it was there that fee funded mandis, licensed intermediaries and assured offtake formed a single working system into which farming households had organised their credit, their labour and their cropping choices. That concentration is why the encampments at Delhi's borders drew their numbers overwhelmingly from three states while the laws applied to twenty eight, and it is also why the repeal, which restored that belt's system intact, settled the question completely for the region that had mobilised and left it untouched, in both its strengths and its well documented inefficiencies, for everywhere else.

Fourthly, and most consequentially for the politics that followed, the repeal created no right of any kind. It conferred no entitlement to sell at the minimum support price, which before 2020, during the farm laws and after the repeal alike remained what it had always been: a price announced by the executive on the recommendation of the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices, at which government agencies conduct procurement operations of varying depth for a limited set of crops, principally paddy and wheat, in a limited set of states. The support price's legal status, the procurement machinery that gives it effect, and the long argument for converting the announcement into an enforceable guarantee are flagged here only to fix the boundary of the repealing Act: a reader who opens Act 40 of 2021 looking for the price settlement that the agitation demanded will find three sections, and the price is not in any of them. Nor did the Act say anything about the criminal cases against protesters, about compensation for deaths, about the stubble burning liability that had been written into the air quality legislation for the capital region, or about the electricity legislation the unions also objected to. Every one of those questions was settled, to the extent it was settled at all, outside the statute, in the executive negotiation recorded in the government's letter of 9 December 2021. The repeal was a complete answer to the demand that three named statutes be removed from the book, and it was no answer at all to the four demands that travelled with it.

## The courts after the repeal: a question left permanently open

A repeal does not merely end a statute; it ends the litigation about the statute, and in this instance it ended the litigation at the one point where a decision would have mattered beyond the farm sector. The writ petitions in the Supreme Court attacked the three Acts principally on the ground of legislative competence: agriculture and markets are state subjects, the petitioners argued, and the Union had dressed a law on agriculture in the vocabulary of inter-state trade to enter a field the Constitution reserves to the states. The Union's answer rested on the Concurrent List entry covering trade and commerce in foodstuffs and on its inter-state trade power, and the question was a real one, with serious argument on both sides and implications for every future central statute that touches the farm economy, from marketing to warehousing to contract enforcement. The Court stayed the Acts in January 2021 without deciding that question, in an order that was avowedly an instrument of negotiation rather than an interim assessment of merits. When the Acts were repealed in November 2021, the petitions lost their subject matter. A court cannot pronounce on the validity of a statute that no longer exists, and challenges to the farm laws accordingly became infructuous: the competence question was never argued to a conclusion, never answered, and remains open in its entirety for any future Parliament that attempts a similar enactment. The most durable legal consequence of the whole episode is therefore a non decision. The boundary between the Union and the states in agricultural marketing stands exactly where it stood before June 2020, mapped by the text of the Seventh Schedule and untested by any judgment, and a future government drafting central farm legislation inherits the same uncertainty the 2020 draftsmen faced, with the addition of a political warning about the cost of resolving it by ordinance and voice vote.

The petitions the Court was managing deserve one more word of description, because their composition explains the shape of the Court's intervention. When the batch was organised in the stay order, the Court itself sorted the petitions into three families: those challenging the farm laws as unconstitutional, those supporting them as valid and beneficial, and those brought by residents of Delhi and the surrounding districts whose complaint was not about the laws at all but about the blockades the protest had created on the highways into the capital. A court facing a single question, is this statute valid, can answer it. A court facing three questions pointing in three directions, validity, benefit and the management of an ongoing siege, is being asked to govern an event rather than decide a case, and the stay and committee were the instruments of a court that had accepted, or been assigned, the governing role. The repeal relieved the Court of all three questions simultaneously, and it is at least arguable that this was the outcome the interim arrangement had been drifting toward from January 2021: the laws suspended by the judiciary, the negotiation conducted under the suspension's shelter, and the political branches left to find the exit that the Court, having no mandate to repeal anything, could not itself supply.

One strand of the litigation did produce observations of continuing relevance, though not on the validity of the Acts. The petitions by residents and commuters affected by the blockades at the capital's borders raised the competing rights of protest and movement, and in the course of those proceedings in October 2021 the Court recorded the position it had taken in the earlier protest litigation: the right to protest is fundamental, but it does not extend to occupying public roads indefinitely and inconveniencing commuters who have their own rights of free movement. Nothing in those observations decided anything about the farm laws themselves, and they are noted here only to prevent a misreading of the record. When the repeal came, every live question in the Supreme Court concerning the three Acts, their competence, their operation and their committee, expired with them; the protest and blockade questions, which concerned the conduct of the agitation rather than the content of the statutes, were the only part of the litigation with a subject capable of surviving the repeal, and even they lost their practical setting when the borders were vacated in December 2021.

The stay itself had been contested from the moment it was granted, and the controversy over it is part of the judicial record the repeal froze in place. A stay of a statute's operation, granted without any prima facie finding of unconstitutionality, sits at the edge of the Court's interim powers, since a duly enacted law carries a presumption of constitutionality and legislatures are not ordinarily suspended while their work is examined. The Attorney General, K. K. Venugopal, appearing for the Union, had opposed a stay on precisely the practical ground that farmers and traders had already begun to act on the new laws and that a stay would accomplish indirectly what the petitioners had not yet earned directly, a judicial veto in advance of judgment. The bench's order answered that concern not with doctrine but with purpose: the stay was granted because it might assuage hurt feelings and bring the unions to the table, an expressly therapeutic justification that commentators across the legal spectrum found remarkable, some welcoming the candour of a court managing a political crisis it could not decide, others objecting that the management had displaced the adjudication the Court existed to perform, and that a stay granted to encourage negotiation had no natural terminus if the negotiation failed. The repeal supplied the terminus from outside. Because the petitions then became infructuous, the Court was never required to defend, qualify or withdraw the stay's reasoning in a final judgment, and the order of 12 January 2021 survives as an unexamined precedent: a three judge bench's interim order, resting on reasons of statecraft, which any future bench confronting a challenge to an unpopular statute will have to distinguish or follow without the assistance of the final word that never came. That, too, is part of what the repeal left undecided, and it concerns the judicial power itself rather than the farm sector at all.

The committee episode belongs to the judicial record and has no parallel in the Court's modern practice. The committee constituted on 12 January 2021 did its work in the absence of the protesting unions, heard the stakeholders who would appear before it, and submitted its report to the Court on 19 March 2021. The Court, which had described the committee as an instrument to give it a clearer picture, never published the report, never relied on it in any order, and never brought the petitions on for the merits hearing at which the report might have been used. The report remained sealed in the Court's record while the laws it assessed were stayed, announced for repeal and repealed. In the months after submission, Anil Ghanwat, one of the three members who completed the work, wrote repeatedly to the Court asking that the report be released. Receiving no response, he released it himself at a press conference on 21 March 2022, a year after its submission, saying that the report had become a dead letter and that it retained educational value for farmers and policymakers. The released report recorded that the committee had invited 266 farmer organisations and interacted with 73, and that of those organisations a large majority, 61 organisations said to represent over three crore farmers, supported the Acts, some with modifications, while a small minority did not. Its central recommendation was against the course events had already taken: a repeal or a long suspension of the farm laws, the committee had advised, would be unfair to the silent majority who supported them, and it proposed flexibilities in implementation instead, including leaving a legal support price mechanism to the states.

Every part of that paragraph needs its attribution kept visible. The figures are the committee's figures, from a consultation from which the principal protesting coalition stayed away, and they measure the views of the organisations that chose to engage with a committee the unions had rejected as composed of supporters of the laws. The committee's defenders, Ghanwat among them, argued that the report's suppression deprived the public of evidence that the laws commanded wider support than the encampments suggested, and that its release during the protest might have changed the negotiation. The unions' answer was that a consultation boycotted by the movement it concerned could not measure the movement's claims, and that the members' prior public positions had disqualified the exercise before it began. What is not contested is the institutional sequence, and it is the sequence that a legal reader should retain: a court stayed a statute to promote a negotiation, appointed a committee to inform itself, received the committee's report, and then neither used it nor released it, so that the only public version of the document exists because a member published it on his own authority after the statute under review had ceased to exist.

## The demands that survived the repeal

The repeal disposed of the first demand of the agitation and none of the others, and the afterlife of the episode is the story of those surviving demands. The Samyukt Kisan Morcha's charter, beyond repeal, asked for a statutory guarantee of the minimum support price for all farmers and all produce, the withdrawal of the cases registered against protesters during the movement, compensation for the families of those who died, the removal of the penal provision on stubble burning from the legislation governing air quality in the national capital region, and consultation before the electricity amendment legislation was taken up. The [framework governing the support price and procurement, and the form a statutory guarantee would have to take](/2014/07/01/msp-procurement-legal-framework-india/), is treated in its own article; the repeal's aftermath is recorded here as a matter of what the government promised, what it did, and what remained undone.

The immediate settlement was executive, not legislative. Through the first week of December 2021 the government and the Morcha exchanged drafts, and on 9 December 2021 the Agriculture Secretary, Sanjay Agrawal, signed a letter to the Morcha recording the government's commitments. The governments of Uttar Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Himachal Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh and Haryana had agreed to withdraw the cases arising from the agitation with immediate effect, and cases filed by the government and its agencies in Delhi and the Union territories would likewise be taken back. On compensation, the letter recorded that the governments of Uttar Pradesh and Haryana had agreed in principle to compensate the families of the deceased, as the government of Punjab had already done. On stubble burning, the farmers' liability had already been removed in the enacted air quality legislation. On electricity, the government committed that the amendment bill would be discussed comprehensively with all stakeholders, including the Morcha, before being piloted in Parliament. On the support price, the letter repeated the Prime Minister's announcement of a committee that would include farmer representatives and would deliberate on how farmers could be assured the support price. On the strength of that letter the Morcha announced on the same day that the agitation stood suspended, describing the outcome in its own words as a battle won with the war for farmers' rights continuing, and the encampments at the borders were vacated from 11 December 2021, the protesters leaving in victory processions a little over a year after they had arrived.

Of the surviving demands, the support price guarantee was the largest and the committee was its designated vehicle, and the vehicle's history is a study in a promise kept in form and unkept in substance. The committee promised in November 2021 and repeated in the December letter was notified by the Ministry of Agriculture and Farmers Welfare on 12 July 2022, by a notification that quoted the Prime Minister's announcement as its charter. It was chaired by Sanjay Agrawal, the former Agriculture Secretary who had signed the December letter and who had been the ministry's secretary through the period of the farm laws themselves. Its remit, as notified, was to suggest ways of making the support price more effective and transparent, to promote natural farming, to suggest changes in cropping patterns in light of the country's changing needs, and to address the autonomy of the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices. The notification reserved three seats for nominees of the Samyukt Kisan Morcha, to be added on receipt of their names. The names never came. The Morcha rejected the committee outright within days of its notification, declining to nominate any representative, on grounds it stated publicly: the terms of reference asked how to make the support price effective and transparent but did not ask the committee to consider a legal guarantee, which was the demand; the committee was populated by officials and by persons who had advocated the repealed laws, beginning with its chairman; and the questions the Morcha had put about the committee's chairmanship, timetable and the status of its recommendations had gone unanswered. The committee met without the Morcha, constituted sub groups on its assigned subjects, and continued to meet; the government's answers to Parliament in later sessions described its meetings as being held regularly and its subjects as under deliberation. No statute conferring a legal guarantee of the support price was enacted, and the support price continued on its administrative footing, announced crop by crop and season by season, exactly as it had operated before 2020. The demand that had outlasted a year of encampment thus ended the repeal's aftermath in the condition the repeal itself exemplified: formally answered, substantively open.

One demand of the aftermath period never entered the December letter at all and is recorded here so the settlement is not misdescribed as complete. The Morcha had demanded the dismissal of the Union Minister of State for Home Affairs, Ajay Kumar Mishra, whose son was among the accused in the Lakhimpur Kheri killings of October 2021, and the demand was pressed at the height of the repeal fortnight as a condition of the movement's full satisfaction. The minister was not dismissed, the criminal case proceeded through the courts on its own track, and the Morcha's statements through the following year continued to list the unmet demand among the defaults of the settlement. Its exclusion from the letter, against the inclusion of the committee promise and the case withdrawals, marks the boundary of what the government was prepared to concede in December 2021: the policy, the prosecutions arising from the protest, and a process on prices were negotiable; the composition of the Council of Ministers was not. A repeal narrative that ends at the statute will miss that boundary entirely, which is why this article has treated the repeal and its surrounding settlement as one event with two instruments, an Act of three sections and a letter of five assurances, of which the second carried almost everything the first omitted.

The other demands closed unevenly. The withdrawal of cases proceeded as a matter for state governments and prosecuting agencies rather than as any single legal event, and the Morcha's review meetings through 2022 recorded its continuing dissatisfaction with the pace of withdrawals and with the delivery of compensation, which rested with the states under the December letter and was never converted into a central scheme. The government had told Parliament, in the same answer of 1 December 2021 in which it disclaimed any record of deaths, that compensation for the families of deceased farmers was a matter for the concerned state governments, and that answer defined the ceiling of the Centre's engagement with the question. The agitation's demand structure had been aimed at the Union throughout, since only the Union could repeal the Acts and only the Union could enact a national price guarantee; its residue, compensation and cases, lay in the hands of the states, and the repeal's settlement is best understood as the Union discharging the two demands that were exclusively its own, repeal and a committee, and recording state assurances on the rest.

## The restored law in operation, and the return of the unanswered demand

The sincerest test of a repeal is what the restored law does next, and on that test the Essential Commodities restoration proved to be no formality. The power that sub-section (1A) had fenced off, the power to regulate the supply and distribution of foodstuffs and to cap the stocks that traders and processors may hold in ordinary times, returned to the Union's hands on 30 November 2021, and the Union used the restored armoury in the years that followed in exactly the situations the 2020 amendment had been designed to take out of official discretion. When food inflation and the disruption of Black Sea grain exports followed the invasion of Ukraine, the government prohibited wheat exports in May 2022 and went on to impose stock limits on wheat held by traders, wholesalers, retailers and processors, measures squarely within the pre-2020 understanding of section 3 of the 1955 Act; restrictions on broken rice exports, duties and prohibitions touching onions, and stock disclosure and limit regimes for pulses followed the same pattern as prices dictated. A reader comparing the statute book with the market will therefore find the repeal's most tangible legal legacy not in the farm laws' absence but in the 1955 Act's presence: the regulatory state in foodstuffs that the 2020 amendment had set out to retire resumed its ordinary operations, and the debate the amendment had tried to settle, about whether the threat of stock limits deters the private investment in storage and supply chains that Indian agriculture lacks, resumed with it, unresolved in both directions. Supporters of the 2020 settlement cite each new stock limit as evidence of the old regime's reflexes. Its defenders cite the same orders as evidence that the power exists for the price emergencies in which it is in fact used. The repeal decided which side held the power; it did not decide, and could not have decided, which side holds the argument.

One element of the 2020 design vanished so completely with sub-section (1A) that its absence is worth a sentence of its own, because it was the amendment's most technically accomplished feature. The inserted sub-section had not merely restricted stock limits to emergencies; it had defined the emergency in numbers, permitting limits on horticultural produce only when retail prices had doubled and on non perishable foodstuffs only when they had risen by half, measured against the preceding year's prices or the average of the preceding five years, and it had exempted processors and value chain participants holding stock within their installed capacity or against export demand. Whatever the merits of that architecture, and its defenders valued precisely its conversion of an official discretion into a rule a trader could price, the omission of the sub-section removed the thresholds, the exemptions and the rule bound character together, restoring the open textured discretion of the unamended section 3 in which the government's power over food stocks is as wide as the words production, supply and distribution can be made to run. The repeal thus did not merely restore an older statute; it restored an older philosophy of the statute, in which the food economy is administered by a power held in reserve and exercised at need, rather than by a code of triggers published in advance. India's experience since 2021, of export bans and stock limits arriving by notification as prices moved, is the philosophy in operation, and the argument between the two philosophies, discretion against rule, is the one part of the farm laws debate that the repeal genuinely decided for its generation, by restoring the discretion and retiring the rule.

The support price demand, the repeal's great survivor, also returned in the form the repeal's logic predicted. In February 2024 farmer organisations, including unions associated with the earlier movement, marched on Delhi again under the banner of a legal guarantee of the support price for all crops, together with the other unfinished items of the 2021 charter, and were stopped at the Punjab and Haryana border at Shambhu, where the confrontation settled into a second, smaller encampment rather than a second siege of the capital. The 2024 mobilisation is part of this article's record for one reason: it demonstrates the exact shape of the repeal's incompleteness. The demand that could not be met by repealing statutes in 2021 could not be met by anything short of enacting one, and enactment is the thing that had not happened in the interval, the committee of July 2022 notwithstanding. The legal question the 2024 protesters posed was the question the repeal had declined to touch, whether Parliament will convert an executive price announcement into a statutory entitlement and, if it does, how such an entitlement can be drafted to survive contact with the market, since a legal duty to buy everything offered at the support price, a legal duty merely to ensure that market prices do not fall below it, and a deficiency payment scheme that bridges the difference are three different statutes wearing one slogan. None had been introduced. The repeal had answered the question of 2020 completely and the question of 2021 not at all, and the politics of the farm sector continued to be organised around the unanswered one.

The mirror image of the returning demand is the returning reform, and here the repeal's lesson for drafters is constitutional rather than political. Nothing in the repeal, and nothing in the undecided competence question, prevents the substance of much of the 2020 programme from being enacted again, because the greater part of it never needed a central statute in the first place. Agricultural marketing is a state subject, and the regulated market system the trade Act bypassed is a creature of state legislation; a state legislature can amend its own marketing Act to free trade outside the mandis, to permit private markets and electronic platforms, to rationalise fees, or to go further, as Bihar did when it repealed its agricultural produce marketing Act outright in 2006 and left trade to the general law. Several states had liberalised parts of their marketing regimes before 2020, Maharashtra's removal of fruits and vegetables from compulsory mandi sale being the best known instance, and the Union's own model marketing legislation, circulated to the states in 2017, had invited exactly the reforms the 2020 Acts later imposed from the Centre. The ordinances of June 2020 chose the central route partly for uniformity and partly for speed, and the repeal restored the state route to its previous position as the constitutionally unremarkable path. Whether any future reform travels it depends on state politics rather than on anything Act 40 of 2021 did, which is the last of the repeal's negations: it did not even settle where the next attempt would be made.

## Two readings of the repeal, and the problem both leave standing

Two interpretations of the repeal compete in the literature of the episode, and the stance this series requires is that each be given in its strongest form before either is judged. The first reads the repeal as a democratic victory of a rare and instructive kind. On this reading, pressed by the Morcha and its supporters and by commentators who followed the movement closely, a peaceful, disciplined and extraordinarily sustained mobilisation by the citizens most affected by a policy compelled a government with a large parliamentary majority to withdraw a reform it believed in, something that almost never happens in Indian legislative practice, where statutes once enacted are amended at the margins and essentially never surrendered. The conditions that made it work are visible in the record: the demand was single and binary, repeal or nothing, and could not be dissolved by concessions; the protesters held ground at the capital's gates for over a year without giving the state a disorder serious enough to justify dispersal; the coalition held together across states, crops and organisations; and the concession, when it came, cost the government a policy but not its majority. The repeal on this reading is evidence that the legislative process has a corrective outside itself, and that a Parliament which declines to deliberate may find the deliberation conducted in the street instead, at far higher cost to everyone.

The second reading treats the repeal as a capitulation to a sectional interest, and it too has a serious form that deserves a serious answer. The protest, on this account, was concentrated in the procurement belt of Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh and among the crops and commission agent networks that the existing support price and mandi system serves best; most Indian farmers sell most of their produce outside that system and many supported, or had no quarrel with, the reforms, as the Supreme Court committee's consultations suggested; and a veto exercised by the best organised fraction of a sector over reforms addressed to the whole of it is a troubling precedent for any government contemplating agricultural change. The government's own Statement of Objects and Reasons lent this reading its central phrase when it described the protesters as only a group of farmers. The answer to the strong form of this reading is not that concentration delegitimises a protest, because concentrated interests mobilise in every democracy and diffuse interests rarely can; it is that the government's difficulty was a product of its own procedure. A reform carried through committee, debated, and voted on by division would have carried a legitimacy that could have been set against the encampments; a reform carried by ordinance and voice vote had nothing to set against them except insistence, and insistence failed. The sectional character of the protest explains who mobilised. It does not explain why the state, holding a majority and the statute book, was the party that yielded, and the explanation for that lies in the manner of the original enactment and in the electoral calendar, not in any defect of the protesters' standing to object.

Against both readings stands the fact neither disputes, which is the proper verdict on the repeal as legislation. The repeal left the underlying problem exactly where the ordinances of June 2020 had found it. The marketing system the reforms targeted, state regulated mandis of uneven quality, a procurement apparatus concentrated in a few states and crops, interposed layers of intermediation, continues, because the repeal restored it and nothing has replaced it. The support price still has no statutory basis. No alternative reform, central or coordinated, has been enacted in the repealed Acts' place, and the constitutional uncertainty that hung over the 2020 experiment hangs unresolved over any successor, because the one court that could have mapped the boundary let the question lapse with the statutes. There is a precedent inside the same government's tenure for a contested economic reform being abandoned under pressure, and the comparison is instructive precisely because the mechanisms differ: in 2015 the government of the day allowed [its land acquisition ordinance, thrice re-promulgated and blocked in the Rajya Sabha, to lapse](/2013/11/15/land-acquisition-ordinance-2015/) rather than force the amendment of the 2013 acquisition law, announcing the decision in a radio address and salvaging the compensation provisions by executive order, so that the reform died without Parliament ever voting to kill it. In 2021 the reform had already been enacted, and killing it required Parliament to act, which it did, in an afternoon, without debate. The two episodes bookend a decade's lesson about contested economic legislation in India: such measures are far easier to announce than to carry, and the point of failure is rarely the drafting. It is the absence of the deliberative and consultative work that alone makes a reform survivable, work that was skipped in 2015 at the ordinance stage and in 2020 at every stage, and skipped again in the repeal that closed the second episode.

What, then, did the Farm Laws Repeal Act 2021 accomplish? As a statute, the minimum consistent with its purpose: it removed three Acts and restored a fourth, cleanly and completely, in three sections whose only sophisticated feature is the care taken to undo the Essential Commodities amendment in terms. As a political act, a great deal: it ended a year long siege of the capital's borders, closed the most serious confrontation of its government's tenure, and demonstrated that sustained protest can reverse enacted law. As a precedent for legislative practice, nothing, and that is the finding this article exists to record. A repeal that had been accompanied by a debate, a committee report, a counted vote, could have been the occasion on which Parliament repaired the habits of September 2020. Instead the repeal ratified them. The farm laws were passed the way they were repealed, and repealed the way they were passed, and the distance between those two afternoons is the measure of the episode: fourteen months, one of the largest protests in the country's legislative history, and no change at all in how the country's laws are made.

For the drafter of some future repeal, and every contested statute in this series will eventually supply one, the episode yields a short code of practice that the farm laws repeal honoured mostly in the breach and survived only because circumstance excused it. A repeal of operating legislation should carry its savings on its face rather than leaving accrued rights to a general default, because the parties who acted under the dying statute are entitled to learn their position from the instrument that kills it. It should deal expressly with any amendment the repealed statute made to other living statutes, as section 3 of this Act, to its credit, did. It should be accompanied by a record of reasons tested in debate, because the reasons for a repeal are the surest guide the next legislature will have to the ground the repeal was meant to clear, and a Statement of Objects and Reasons that no one was permitted to question is a frail foundation for that reliance. And where the repeal is the settlement of a dispute with demands attached, the statute or its accompanying instruments should say, in terms, which demands the settlement answers and which it does not, so that the survivors of the settlement are not left, as the support price demand was left, to discover their status from the silences of a three section Act and a committee's unanswered correspondence. None of this would have changed the politics of November 2021. All of it would have changed the quality of the record November 2021 left behind, and records are what legislatures bequeath.

## Reading the record correctly: the three errors this episode attracts

Because the episode is constantly invoked in political argument, it is constantly misdescribed, and three errors recur often enough in print to be worth correcting in one place, since this series exists to give the journalist and the examination candidate a record that can be relied on. The first error is chronological: the repeal is dated to 19 November 2021. That was the date of the Prime Minister's announcement, a political act of great consequence and no legal effect. The repeal in law is the work of the Farm Laws Repeal Act, 2021, passed by both Houses on 29 November 2021 and assented to on 30 November 2021, and the three Acts lived in law until the second of those dates. Any account that has the laws repealed on Gurpurab has confused the promise with the instrument, a confusion the protesting unions themselves were careful not to make, since they stayed at the borders until the instrument existed. The second error is substantive: the claim that the repeal guaranteed the minimum support price. It did not, in any text, at any stage. The Act contains no price provision; the announcement contained a promise of a committee; the December letter repeated the promise; the committee was notified seven months later with terms that did not include a legal guarantee; and no statute on the subject followed. The distance between a committee on a question and a guarantee of its answer is the whole of the surviving dispute, and accounts that collapse it misdescribe both the victory the unions won and the demand they carried away unmet. The third error is judicial: the claim that the constitutional challenge to the farm laws was decided, in either direction. It was not. The Supreme Court stayed the Acts as an interim, negotiation promoting measure, received its committee's report without publishing or adopting it, and never delivered a judgment on competence, on the Seventh Schedule question, or on any other ground of challenge, because the repeal removed the statutes the judgment would have been about. The correct statement of the judicial record is that there is no judicial record on the merits, and any future account that cites the farm laws litigation as authority for a proposition about Parliament's power over agricultural trade is citing a decision that does not exist. An episode that produced a statute of three sections, a sealed report and an unanswered constitutional question is, in the strictest sense, an episode whose official texts say less than the arguments built on them; the discipline of reading it correctly is the discipline of not letting the arguments supply what the texts omit.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: When were the farm laws repealed?

The farm laws were repealed in law by the Farm Laws Repeal Act, 2021, which Parliament passed on 29 November 2021 and which received the President's assent and was published in the Gazette on 30 November 2021. The date often given, 19 November 2021, was the day the Prime Minister announced the decision in a televised address; an announcement has no legal effect, and the three Acts of 2020 remained in force, under the Supreme Court's stay, until the repealing Act took effect at the end of November. The unions treated the distinction as essential and stayed at the Delhi borders until the repeal was enacted.

### Q: How quickly did Parliament pass the farm laws repeal bill?

The whole passage took about two hours on a single day, 29 November 2021, the first day of the winter session. The Agriculture Minister moved the bill in the Lok Sabha shortly after the House met at noon, and it was declared passed by voice vote within minutes, contemporary reporting timing the disposal at around eight minutes, after the Speaker declined a discussion while the House was not in order. The bill went to the Rajya Sabha the same afternoon, where the Leader of the Opposition was allowed a brief statement before it was again passed by voice vote. There was no debate and no committee stage in either House.

### Q: What did the repeal of the farm laws restore?

It restored the position as it stood before June 2020. The two free standing farm statutes, on trade in produce and on farming agreements, ceased to exist. The Essential Commodities Act, 1955 was restored to its pre-2020 form by the omission of sub-section (1A) from its section 3, reviving the Union's general power to regulate the supply and distribution of the specified foodstuffs and to impose stock limits in ordinary times rather than only in war, famine or extraordinary price rise. The state marketing statutes, which the farm laws had bypassed but never repealed, resumed their exclusive operation, and support price procurement continued on its administrative footing, unchanged by the repeal.

### Q: Has Parliament ever repealed a major economic law the way the farm laws were repealed?

Essentially never in the same way. Parliament repeals statutes constantly, but almost always as housekeeping of dead enactments, as repeal and replacement by a successor government, or as amendment. Governments have abandoned contested economic measures by other routes: the land acquisition ordinance of the same government was allowed to lapse in 2015 rather than being carried, and reforms have been diluted, deferred or struck down by courts. The farm laws repeal combined features with no close parallel: a governing majority repealing its own flagship reform, within fourteen months of enacting it, under sustained street pressure, in a single day, without debate and without any replacement. The combination, rather than any single element, is what makes the episode singular.

### Q: What did farmers demand after the farm laws were repealed?

The demands attached to the repeal survived it, because the Samyukt Kisan Morcha's charter had always run beyond the three statutes. The principal surviving demand was a statutory guarantee of the minimum support price as a legal entitlement for all farmers and all produce, rather than an administrative announcement. Behind it stood the withdrawal of the criminal cases registered against protesters during the agitation, compensation for the families of farmers who had died during the movement, removal of the penal provision on stubble burning, consultation before the electricity amendment legislation was introduced, and the dismissal of the minister whose son was accused in the Lakhimpur Kheri killings. Of these, the December 2021 government letter addressed the cases, compensation and consultation; the price guarantee was referred to a committee.

### Q: Did the farm laws repeal end the Supreme Court proceedings?

Yes, by removing their subject rather than by any judgment. The petitions challenging the three Acts, led by Rakesh Vaishnav v. Union of India, attacked the Acts principally as beyond Parliament's legislative competence. The Court had stayed the Acts' implementation on 12 January 2021 as an interim measure to promote negotiation, without deciding their validity. Once Parliament repealed the Acts, there was no statute left whose validity could be pronounced on, and the challenges became infructuous. The competence question, whether the Union may legislate on agricultural trade as it attempted in 2020, was therefore never decided and remains open for any future enactment.

### Q: What happened to the Supreme Court committee report on the farm laws?

The committee the Supreme Court constituted in January 2021, which completed its work under Ashok Gulati, Pramod Kumar Joshi and Anil Ghanwat after Bhupinder Singh Mann recused himself, submitted its report to the Court on 19 March 2021. The Court never published the report, never relied on it in any order, and never brought the petitions to a merits hearing at which it could have been used. After the repeal, Anil Ghanwat, who had written repeatedly to the Court seeking the report's release, published it himself on 21 March 2022. The released report recorded that most farmer organisations that engaged with the committee supported the Acts and advised that repeal or long suspension would be unfair to that majority.

### Q: Could similar agricultural reforms return in another form?

Yes, and the most available form needs no central statute at all. Agricultural marketing is a state subject, and a state legislature can amend its own marketing law to free trade outside the mandis, license private markets and electronic platforms, or restructure fees, as several states had partially done before 2020 and as Bihar did most radically by repealing its marketing Act in 2006. The Union could also return with central legislation, but it would face the same undecided competence question the 2020 Acts raised and the same political warning. What the repeal forecloses is nothing in law; what it changes is the demonstrated cost of attempting the reform by ordinance and voice vote.

### Q: What is the Farm Laws Repeal Act 2021?

It is Act 40 of 2021, the short statute by which Parliament repealed the three farm laws of 2020. It contains only three sections. Section 1 gives the short title. Section 2 repeals the Farmers (Empowerment and Protection) Agreement on Price Assurance and Farm Services Act, 2020, the Farmers' Produce Trade and Commerce (Promotion and Facilitation) Act, 2020 and the Essential Commodities (Amendment) Act, 2020. Section 3 amends the Essential Commodities Act, 1955 by omitting the sub-section (1A) that the 2020 amendment had inserted. The Act has no savings clause, no schedule and no provision on prices, cases or compensation, and it was passed by both Houses without debate on 29 November 2021.

### Q: What does section 3 of the Farm Laws Repeal Act 2021 change in the Essential Commodities Act 1955?

Section 3 omits sub-section (1A) from section 3 of the Essential Commodities Act, 1955. That sub-section, inserted by the Essential Commodities (Amendment) Act, 2020, had confined the regulation of specified foodstuffs, including cereals, pulses, potatoes, onions, edible oilseeds and oils, to extraordinary circumstances such as war, famine, extraordinary price rise or grave natural calamity, and had tied stock limits to fixed price triggers, a doubling for horticultural produce and a rise of one half for non perishable foodstuffs. The omission was legally necessary because repealing the amending Act alone would not reliably have undone the amendment it had incorporated into the 1955 Act. With the sub-section gone, the general power in section 3 runs as it did before 2020.

### Q: Why did the government repeal the farm laws after defending them for over a year?

The government's stated reason, in the Prime Minister's address of 19 November 2021 and in the repeal bill's Statement of Objects and Reasons, was the failure to persuade: the laws were presented as beneficial, but a section of farmers could not be convinced of their merits, the Prime Minister acknowledged the failure and apologised, and in the seventy fifth year of independence the need was to take everyone along. Opposition parties attributed the decision to the assembly elections due in five states including Punjab and Uttar Pradesh and to the political cost of a year long siege of the capital's borders. The record establishes the concession and the electoral calendar; the weight of each consideration inside the government's deliberations is not a matter the public record can settle.

### Q: What did Prime Minister Narendra Modi say when he announced the repeal on 19 November 2021?

In a televised address to the nation on Gurpurab, the birth anniversary of Guru Nanak, the Prime Minister announced that the government had decided to repeal the three farm laws in the coming winter session of Parliament. He maintained that the laws had been enacted with honest intent for farmers' benefit, said that the government had been unable to convince a section of the farmers of that benefit despite its efforts, acknowledged the failure as his own and apologised to the country for it. In the same address he announced that a committee would be constituted, with representatives of the Centre, the states, farmers, agricultural scientists and agricultural economists, to make the support price system more effective and transparent and to work on natural farming and cropping patterns.

### Q: Did the Farm Laws Repeal Act 2021 give farmers a legal guarantee of minimum support price?

No. The Act contains three sections, and none of them touches the support price. It repealed the two free standing farm Acts, repealed the Essential Commodities amendment of 2020 and omitted the sub-section that amendment had inserted into the 1955 Act. A statutory guarantee of the minimum support price, the demand that the protesting unions ranked immediately after repeal itself, would have required a new enactment creating an enforceable entitlement, and no such provision was in the repeal bill or followed it. The support price remained what it had been before 2020: a price announced by the executive on the recommendation of the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices, at which government agencies procure, carrying administrative force but no legal right a farmer can enforce.

### Q: What did the government's letter of 9 December 2021 promise the Samyukt Kisan Morcha?

The letter, signed by the Agriculture Secretary Sanjay Agrawal after an exchange of drafts, recorded the commitments on which the agitation was suspended the same day. The governments of Uttar Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Himachal Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh and Haryana had agreed to withdraw the cases arising from the agitation with immediate effect, and cases filed by the government and its agencies in Delhi and the Union territories would also be taken back. Uttar Pradesh and Haryana had agreed in principle to compensate the families of deceased farmers, as Punjab had done. Farmers' liability for stubble burning had already been removed. The Electricity (Amendment) Bill would be discussed with all stakeholders, including the Morcha, before being piloted in Parliament. On prices, the letter repeated the promise of a committee including farmer representatives.

### Q: What happened to the criminal cases registered against farmers during the protest?

Withdrawal of the cases was one of the demands the government's letter of 9 December 2021 answered, and the mechanism was necessarily decentralised, because the cases had been registered by different authorities in different jurisdictions. The letter recorded that five state governments, Uttar Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Himachal Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh and Haryana, had agreed to withdraw the agitation cases with immediate effect, and that cases filed by the government and its agencies in Delhi and the other Union territories would be taken back likewise. Withdrawal of a prosecution is an executive and judicial process case by case, not a single legal event, and the Samyukt Kisan Morcha's review meetings after the suspension recorded its continuing dissatisfaction with the pace at which withdrawals were actually completed.

### Q: Were the families of farmers who died during the protest compensated after the repeal?

Compensation was promised at the level of the states, not the Centre, and the two positions on the deaths themselves were never reconciled. The Samyukt Kisan Morcha dedicated its victory to around 715 dead and the opposition cited a toll of over seven hundred in Parliament. The Agriculture Minister told Parliament on 1 December 2021 that his ministry held no records of deaths of protesting farmers, that the question of compensation therefore did not arise at the Centre, and that compensation was a matter for the concerned state governments. The government's letter of 9 December 2021 recorded that Uttar Pradesh and Haryana had agreed in principle to compensate the families, as the government of Punjab had already done, which left delivery to state schemes rather than to any commitment in the repeal settlement itself.

### Q: What did the Statement of Objects and Reasons of the Repeal Bill say about the protests?

The Statement, signed by the Agriculture Minister on 24 November 2021, made the government's case in a form longer than the bill it accompanied. It recited the government's expenditure and schemes for farmers, described the three Acts as reforms demanded for years by farmers, experts and farmer organisations and enacted after extensive consultation, and then conceded the political fact in a sentence that defined the episode's official history: even though only a group of farmers are protesting against these laws, the government had tried hard to sensitise farmers to their merits, the laws stood stayed by the Supreme Court, and in the seventy fifth year of independence the need was to take everyone together on the path of inclusive growth. Supporters read the sentence as a graceful concession; critics fastened on its opening clause as a refusal to recognise the movement.

### Q: How did the opposition react to the Repeal Bill being passed without any discussion?

The opposition parties supported the repeal and objected to its manner, a combination that defined the day. In the Lok Sabha opposition members entered the well demanding a discussion, principally on a statutory guarantee of the support price, the deaths during the agitation and the Lakhimpur Kheri case, and the bill was passed by voice vote amid the sloganeering after the Speaker said discussion required an orderly House. In the Rajya Sabha the Leader of the Opposition, Mallikarjun Kharge, was permitted a brief statement in which he welcomed the repeal, attributed it to the coming elections and cited the deaths of more than seven hundred farmers. The Agriculture Minister answered that since the opposition itself had demanded repeal, there was no need for a discussion. Rahul Gandhi told reporters the government was terrified of the discussion it had refused.

### Q: What was the committee on minimum support price constituted in July 2022 asked to do, and what came of it?

The committee, notified by the Agriculture Ministry on 12 July 2022 and chaired by the former Agriculture Secretary Sanjay Agrawal, was asked to suggest how to make the minimum support price more effective and transparent, to promote natural farming, to suggest changes in cropping patterns and to address the autonomy of the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices. Its terms did not include recommending a legal guarantee of the support price, which was the unions' demand. Three seats were reserved for nominees of the Samyukt Kisan Morcha, which rejected the committee and declined to nominate anyone. The committee met and formed sub groups on its subjects, and the government later told Parliament its meetings were being held regularly. No statute giving the support price legal force resulted from its work.

### Q: Does the Farm Laws Repeal Act 2021 operate retrospectively on transactions under the repealed laws?

The Act says nothing about transactions at all, because it contains no savings or transitional clause. The position is therefore governed by the general rule in section 6 of the General Clauses Act, 1897: a repeal does not, unless a contrary intention appears, affect the previous operation of the repealed enactment, or rights, liabilities and proceedings accrued under it. In practice the exposure was small, since the Supreme Court's stay of 12 January 2021 had frozen the Acts' operation for most of their life, and the government confirmed in answer to a parliamentary question that agreements entered into and acts done under the three Acts before the repeal would be governed by the relevant legal provisions. The repeal extinguished the statutes for the future; it did not rewrite the legal character of the short period in which they had operated.
