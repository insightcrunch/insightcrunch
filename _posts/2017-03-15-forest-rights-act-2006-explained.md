The Forest Rights Act is the most persistently misdescribed statute in Indian environmental law, and the misdescription is in the verb. Commentary says the Act grants land to forest dwellers, gives forests to villages, or distributes pattas as a welfare measure. The Act does none of those things, and its own long title says so: it is an Act to recognise and vest rights that forest dwelling communities held for generations but that were never recorded when the forests around them were consolidated into state forests. Everything distinctive about the statute follows from that single design choice. A government that confers a benefit may attach conditions, set quotas, and withdraw the benefit when policy changes. A government that recognises a pre-existing right is doing something closer to correcting a land record: the enquiry is not whether the claimant deserves the right but whether the right exists. That is why rejection under this Act is not a refusal, why the procedure by which rejections are made carries more legal weight than it would in a benefit scheme, and why the Supreme Court's intervention of February 2019 turned entirely on whether reasons had been recorded and appeals heard, and not at all on whether the claimants were sympathetic.

The statute's formal identity carries a trap of the same kind that runs through this family of legislation, and it is worth clearing at the start. The short title is the Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006, yet the Act is Act 2 of 2007. Both halves are correct. Parliament passed the Bill in December 2006 and the President assented to it on 29 December 2006, but the Act was published in the Gazette of India on 2 January 2007, and the annual numbering of Acts follows the year of publication, so a statute named for 2006 sits in the statute book as the second Act of 2007. The Act then waited almost a full year for commencement: it was brought into force on 31 December 2007, and the Rules that operate it, the Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Rules, were notified on 1 January 2008 and substantially amended by the Amendment Rules of 2012, notified on 6 September 2012. A reader who treats the 2006 in the title as the year the machinery started will misdate every claim decided in the first years of the Act's life.

The claim this article defends, and returns to at the close, is the one the brief for the statute itself advances: rejection is a finding, not a refusal. Because the statute recognises rights that already exist rather than conferring new ones, a rejected claim is a determination that a person has no right at all, and a determination of that character demands a process that hears the claimant, records reasons, and permits an appeal. Where that process has been skipped, the rejection proves nothing about the right; it proves only that the machinery failed. The implementation record, the amendment of the Rules in 2012, and the judicial episode of 2019 are all, on examination, variations on that point.

## The historical injustice the preamble records

Most Indian statutes begin with a preamble that does little work. This one begins with an admission, and the admission is the interpretive key to everything that follows. The preamble recites that the forest rights on ancestral lands and habitats were not adequately recognised in the consolidation of state forests during the colonial period as well as in independent India, resulting in historical injustice to the forest dwelling Scheduled Tribes and other traditional forest dwellers, and that it became necessary to address the long standing insecurity of tenurial and access rights, including of those who were forced to relocate their dwelling due to state development interventions. Legislatures rarely describe the state's own record-keeping as the source of the wrong. Here the wrong is specified with precision: not that forest dwellers were given too little, but that what they had was never written down.

The mechanism of the original failure deserves a full account, because the Act's procedure is unintelligible without it. When forests were reserved under the colonial forest legislation, most completely under the Indian Forest Act of 1927 and its provincial predecessors, a settlement process was supposed to enquire into and record the rights of people living in and using the forest before the reservation took final effect. A forest settlement officer was to proclaim the proposed reservation, receive claims, enquire into rights of pasture, produce, cultivation and passage, and either record them as admitted rights or acquire them. In very large areas that enquiry was perfunctory, hurried, or never genuinely completed. Communities whose cultivation was shifting, whose use was seasonal, whose tenure was communal rather than individual, or whose villages lay inside unsurveyed forest found that the settlement record either reduced their rights to concessions recorded at the pleasure of the forest department or omitted them entirely. What the record omitted, later law treated as non-existent: unrecorded occupation became encroachment, unrecorded collection became theft of government property, and unrecorded grazing became trespass. The legal personality of the forest dweller was thus created by a clerical omission, and then reproduced by every subsequent statute that took the record as its starting point.

Independent India inherited the record and, for decades, reproduced the omission. Forest villages established by forest departments for labour remained departmental settlements rather than revenue villages. Working plans treated customary use as a burden on the forest rather than as a right in it. The Forest (Conservation) Act of 1980, a genuinely necessary statute that centralised diversion decisions and slowed the conversion of forest land, nonetheless completed the structure from the dweller's point of view: the land was recorded as forest, the forest could not be diverted without the Centre's approval, and the people inside it had no recorded right that any of the machinery was bound to consult. When eviction drives against encroachment were launched, most notoriously in the years after 2002, the category of encroacher swept up households whose presence in the forest predated the record that condemned them, alongside genuine recent occupation that no legal system could be expected to absorb. The political pressure that produced the Forest Rights Act came from the recognition, pressed by a national mobilisation of forest dwellers' organisations under banners such as the Campaign for Survival and Dignity, that a legal order which cannot distinguish between the two has failed at the elementary task of a land record.

The legislative route mattered too. The Bill introduced in 2005 was examined by a Joint Parliamentary Committee, and the committee's scrutiny widened the Bill in respects that survive in the enacted text: the inclusion of other traditional forest dwellers alongside forest dwelling Scheduled Tribes, the extension of rights beyond land to community rights over resources, and procedural safeguards around eviction and resettlement. The Bill passed the Lok Sabha on 15 December 2006 and the Rajya Sabha on 18 December 2006 on the National Commission for Scheduled Tribes' account of the passage record, received assent on 29 December 2006, and then, unusually, slept for a year while the Rules were prepared and the states constituted the committees the Act requires. That year's gap between enactment and commencement is a small specimen of a recurring pattern in this statute's life: the text moves first and the machinery follows slowly behind it, and the people the text concerns live in the gap.

One distinction inside the settlement record deserves emphasis because the Act's vocabulary answers it directly. The colonial forest settlement did not treat all recorded use alike: it separated rights, which the settlement officer admitted as burdens on the forest that the state had to respect or extinguish with compensation, from concessions and privileges, which were recorded as indulgences the administration extended at its pleasure and could withdraw. Across successive settlements the tendency ran one way, with uses that villages experienced as ancestral entitlements progressively reclassified downward from right to concession to unrecorded tolerance, until the same act of grazing, gathering or cultivation carried a different legal character on either side of a survey line drawn through identical country. The Forest Rights Act's catalogue can be read as the reversal of that classification exercise item by item. Nistar, the very word the settlement records used for the privileged use of forest produce for domestic need, appears in clause (b) as a right. Produce held at departmental pleasure reappears in clause (c) as ownership. Grazing tolerated as a concession reappears in clause (d) as community right. The conversion clause answers the settlements that were never completed; the rehabilitation clause answers the removals that were never compensated. A reader who keeps the rights-versus-concessions distinction in view understands why the Act's drafters insisted on the vocabulary of recognition throughout, and why the communities' own descriptions of the statute, in the movement literature that accompanied its passage, treated the change of legal category, from tolerated presence to held right, as the substance of the reform rather than its form.

## A statute that recognises rather than confers

The declaratory character of the Act is not a rhetorical flourish in its preamble; it is engineered into its operative sections, and three features of the drafting show the engineering. The first is the vocabulary of the vesting provision. Section 4 recognises and vests the forest rights in the two claimant categories, subject to the Act's conditions, and it does so notwithstanding anything contained in any other law in force. The rights are not created by the section in the way a licence is created by a licensing provision; the section takes rights identified in Section 3, which are framed as rights the communities hold, and gives them statutory recognition and a record. The second feature is retrospectivity by design. Because the rights pre-date the Act, the Act measures entitlement against a past state of affairs, occupation and dependence as they stood on a fixed historical date, and not against the claimant's position at the date of application. A conferring statute looks forward from commencement; this statute looks backward to a cut-off. The third feature is the structure of the determination itself. The process Section 6 creates is an enquiry into the nature and extent of rights, conducted in the first instance by the village assembly of the very community whose customary arrangements are in question, which is the institutional form a legal system gives to fact-finding about its own past. None of this makes the Act self-executing, and the gap between recognition in principle and titles in hand is the subject of the implementation sections below. It does mean that every procedural question under the Act has to be answered in light of what the procedure is for. A process for deciding whether to give something may legitimately be summary. A process for deciding whether a right exists may not.

### Does the Forest Rights Act grant new land to forest dwellers?

No. The Act recognises and vests rights that forest dwelling Scheduled Tribes and other traditional forest dwellers already held over forest land and resources but that were never recorded during forest consolidation. A successful claim produces a title recording an existing right; it does not allot land, and a rejected claim is a finding that the right does not exist.

That distinction controls the two questions readers most often ask about the Act. The first is whether recognition can be capped or withdrawn as policy, the way a scheme can be closed. It cannot, in the same way: the state may amend the Act, as it may amend any statute, but within the Act as enacted there is no quota of rights awaiting distribution, and an authority that has run out of budget or enthusiasm has no lawful ground to stop recognising rights that exist. The second question is whether a claimant needs the Act's permission to have the right, or only needs the Act's process to prove it. The Act's own answer is the second one, and Section 4's bar on eviction, examined below, is drafted on exactly that footing: a person in occupation is protected until the recognition and verification procedure is complete, because until that procedure concludes, nobody knows whether an eviction would be removing an encroacher or dispossessing a right holder. The burden the statute places on the state is therefore epistemic before it is distributive. The state must find out who holds rights in its forests, a thing it declined to find out at settlement, and the entire procedural architecture exists to force that finding to happen carefully.

There is a further consequence that matters when the conservation argument arrives later in this article. A declaratory statute does not balance rights against conservation by weighing the claimant's interest against the forest's interest at the point of recognition, because the recognition stage is not where the balance is struck. The balance is struck inside the rights themselves, which are recognised subject to duties of protection in Section 5, and at the points where the Act expressly provides for it, above all in the critical wildlife habitat provisions of Section 4(2). Readers who expect the claim process to refuse recognition wherever conservation would prefer empty forest are asking the process to do something the statute deliberately assigned elsewhere, and much of the administrative behaviour criticised in the implementation record consists of exactly that displacement: conservation judgements smuggled into a fact-finding process that was never given the legal standard by which to make them.

## From bill to commencement: how the Act was made

The making of the Act explains several of its features that otherwise read as accidents. The Scheduled Tribes (Recognition of Forest Rights) Bill, as introduced in the Lok Sabha in December 2005, was a narrower instrument than the Act that emerged: it was addressed to Scheduled Tribes, and its rights catalogue and procedural protections were thinner. Two features of the introduction matter for reading the finished statute. The first is the cut-off date. The date of 13 December 2005, which governs the occupation enquiry throughout the Act, corresponds to the Bill's introduction in Parliament, so the recognition exercise was from the beginning anchored to the moment the legislative promise was made, and occupation after that moment could not be dressed as reliance on the promise. The second is that the Bill was sent to a Joint Parliamentary Committee, chaired by V. Kishore Chandra Deo, whose examination through 2006 heard the forest dwellers' movement, the forest administration and the conservation establishment, and whose recommendations reshaped the Bill in the directions the enacted text displays. The category of other traditional forest dwellers, absent from a Bill framed for Scheduled Tribes alone, entered through this scrutiny, bringing with it the three-generation test that committee and Parliament devised as the price of the category's inclusion. The community rights, the habitat right and the protections around eviction and resettlement were likewise deepened between introduction and enactment, so that the gap between the introduced Bill and the enacted Act, the analytical heart of any legislative history, is in this statute unusually wide and unusually instructive: almost everything that makes the Act distinctive was added when Parliament looked at the draft closely.

The passage itself, once the committee had reported, was swift and substantially consensual in form: the National Commission for Scheduled Tribes' account records passage in the Lok Sabha on 15 December 2006 and in the Rajya Sabha on 18 December 2006, assent followed on 29 December 2006, and the controversy that might have been expected attended the implementation rather than the enactment. The year of sleep between assent and commencement, with the Rules notified on 1 January 2008 and the state committees constituted under Section 6 in the interval, set the pattern the implementation record repeats. A recognition statute requires an apparatus of a kind India did not possess: village-level committees elected for a land function, interdepartmental committees at two levels, a monitoring committee in each state and a nodal ministry compiling returns. Building that apparatus took time the right holders did not have, in the sense that every month of delay was a month in which the eviction bar protected occupation on paper while the machinery that would convert occupation into title did not yet exist to be invoked. The Rules of 2008 then supplied the procedural detail, the evidence list, the committee forms, and the first version of the quorum requirement whose impracticality the 2012 amendments corrected. The statute a claimant actually uses is therefore a composite instrument: the Act of 2006 for the rights and the architecture, the Rules of 2008 for the forms, and the Amendment Rules of 2012 for the evidentiary and quorum standards that govern a properly conducted claim. Advice or commentary that works from the Act alone, or from the Rules unamended, will misstate the process in particulars that decide claims.

One more feature of the making deserves record because it conditions the Act's later judicial life. The Act was passed against the background of the Supreme Court's continuing supervision of forest matters in the Godavarman proceedings, in which the Court had for a decade treated the forest estate as a subject of judicially monitored administration and had repeatedly characterised unrecorded occupation as encroachment to be cleared. The political branches were legislating a different theory of the same estate, that much apparent encroachment was unrecorded right, at a time when the judiciary's working assumption ran the other way. The 2019 collision between the Act's machinery and a bench directing eviction on the strength of rejection figures was, in this light, not an accident of one case but the meeting of the two theories the legal system had been carrying in parallel since 2006, and the stay of 28 February 2019 is best read as the point at which the Court required the administrative theory to be proved before the judicial theory could act on it.

## What the Forest Rights Act 2006 recognises: the thirteen rights of Section 3

Section 3(1) contains the Act's catalogue, thirteen clauses lettered (a) to (m), and the catalogue is best understood in three groups: rights over land, rights over resources and community life, and rights that repair specific historical situations. The clauses secure individual or community tenure or both, and they apply on all forest lands, a phrase the definitions section makes deliberately wide: forest land means land of any description falling within any forest area, including unclassified forests, undemarcated forests, existing or deemed forests, protected forests, reserved forests, sanctuaries and national parks. The Act therefore reaches inside protected areas, which is precisely why its wildlife provisions had to be written with care.

The land rights come first. Clause (a) recognises the right to hold and live in forest land under individual or common occupation for habitation or for self-cultivation for livelihood. This is the right around which most claims, and most of the Act's public reputation, are organised, and its limits are as important as its content: the right attaches to land in occupation, it is for habitation or self-cultivation, and it serves bona fide livelihood needs, a term the Rules define by reference to sustenance of self and family through production or sale of produce from self-cultivation. Clause (f) recognises rights in or over disputed lands under any nomenclature in any state where claims are disputed, a clause aimed at the large class of cases in which the settlement and survey record itself is contested. Clause (g) recognises rights for the conversion of pattas, leases or grants issued by a local authority or a state government on forest land into titles, regularising paper that earlier administrations issued but the forest record refused to honour. Clause (h) recognises rights of settlement and conversion of all forest villages, old habitations, unsurveyed villages and other villages in forests, whether recorded, notified or not, into revenue villages, which converts departmental settlements and unrecorded habitations into ordinary villages of the revenue administration, with everything that follows for schools, ration shops, roads and the franchise's practical machinery.

The resource and community rights are, in aggregate reach, larger than the land rights, and they are the part of the Act least reproduced in summaries. Clause (b) recognises community rights such as nistar, by whatever name called, including those used in erstwhile princely states, zamindari or other intermediary regimes; nistar is the customary entitlement of villagers to take forest produce for domestic needs, and the clause deliberately preserves its local names and forms rather than forcing it into a uniform definition. Clause (c) recognises the right of ownership, access to collect, use and dispose of minor forest produce that has been traditionally collected within or outside village boundaries. Minor forest produce is defined to include all non-timber forest produce of plant origin, and the definition names bamboo, brush wood, stumps, cane, tussar, cocoons, honey, wax, lac, tendu or kendu leaves, medicinal plants and herbs, roots and tubers. The inclusion of bamboo in a statutory list of non-timber produce was a deliberate reversal of the older forest law's treatment of bamboo as a tree, and the word ownership in the clause is equally deliberate: the gatherer does not receive a revocable collection licence but owns the produce, with the right of disposal, which the 2012 Rules glossed to include local processing, value addition and transport. Clause (d) recognises other community rights of use or entitlement, such as fish and other products of water bodies, grazing, both settled and transhumant, and traditional seasonal resource access of nomadic or pastoralist communities, a clause that reaches mobile livelihoods the settlement record was worst at capturing. Clause (i) recognises the right to protect, regenerate, conserve or manage any community forest resource which the community has been traditionally protecting and conserving for sustainable use. That is the community forest resource right, the provision with the greatest potential territorial reach in the entire Act, because it recognises not a use but a jurisdiction: the community's authority over a forest it has customarily protected. Clause (e) recognises rights including community tenures of habitat and habitation for primitive tribal groups and pre-agricultural communities, the category the administration calls particularly vulnerable tribal groups, whose relationship to a habitat is collective in a way individual pattas cannot express. Clause (j) recognises rights recognised under any state law or the laws of any autonomous district council or autonomous regional council, or accepted as rights under the traditional or customary law of the concerned tribes. Clause (k) recognises the right of access to biodiversity and the community right to intellectual property and traditional knowledge related to biodiversity and cultural diversity. Clause (l) is the residuary clause: any other traditional right customarily enjoyed, not mentioned in the preceding clauses, but excluding the traditional right of hunting or trapping or extracting a part of the body of any species of wild animal. The exclusion is express and total, and it marks the boundary Parliament drew between customary use and customary taking of wildlife.

Clause (m) belongs in a group of its own. It recognises the right to in situ rehabilitation, including alternative land, where forest dwelling Scheduled Tribes or other traditional forest dwellers were illegally evicted or displaced from forest land of any description without receiving their legal entitlement to rehabilitation before the 13th day of December 2005. The clause converts past unlawful displacement into a present statutory entitlement, and it connects this Act to the general law of acquisition and displacement, whose full scheme of compensation, rehabilitation and resettlement is treated in the series guide to [land acquisition law in India](/2013/10/01/land-acquisition-law-india-guide/). The connection matters practically: displacement from forest land for a development project does not stand outside the land acquisition framework merely because the land is recorded as forest, and clause (m) ensures that a displaced forest dweller whom that framework failed does not lose the claim with the passing of the project.

Two structural features of the catalogue deserve emphasis before leaving it. The first is that Section 3(2) immediately qualifies the conservation statute's gate for a defined social purpose, and it is dealt with in its own section below. The second is the character of the rights once recognised. Under Section 4, the rights conferred are heritable but not alienable or transferable, and they are to be registered jointly in the names of both spouses in the case of married persons, and in the name of the single head of a household headed by a single person. The inalienability is a protective device with a long pedigree in tribal land law: a right that cannot be sold cannot be bought out from under an illiterate holder by a creditor or a speculator, and the price of that protection is that the title cannot be mortgaged or realised as capital in the ordinary way. Reformers argue about that price; the statute has chosen its side of the argument, and a claimant who expects a freely tradeable title will misunderstand what the record gives.

### The conversion and rehabilitation rights: clauses (h) and (m)

Two clauses of the catalogue operate on the past rather than the present occupation, and they are the clauses through which the Act settles accounts with specific administrative wrongs. Clause (h) recognises rights in or over disputed lands under any nomenclature, together with the conversion of all types of tenures the state itself had handed out in incomplete forms: pattas, leases and grants issued by a state government or a local authority on forest land, and the conversion of forest villages, old habitations and unsurveyed villages, whether recorded, notified or not, into revenue villages. Each item in that list is a category of administrative limbo. The leaseholder whose lease the department granted and whose status a later administration disputed; the lineage settled in a forest village for the department's own forestry work, drawing rations rather than holding rights, governed as a labour settlement a century after its founders were recruited; the unsurveyed village that appears in no revenue record because the survey never reached it: clause (h) converts these statuses into rights, and the conversion into a revenue village changes a settlement's entire administrative future, since a revenue village acquires the ordinary institutions of local governance, the tax and record framework, and eligibility for the general run of rural programmes from which a forest village stood excluded. The conversion right is therefore the catalogue's administrative conscience: it recognises that a large part of forest illegality was manufactured by the state's own unfinished paperwork.

Clause (m) reaches further back and further out. It recognises the right to in situ rehabilitation, including alternative land, in cases where a tribal or other traditional forest dweller has been illegally evicted or displaced from forest land of any description without receiving the legal entitlement to rehabilitation, before 13 December 2005. The clause is the Act's remedy for the displacement history of the conservation and development estate: families removed for sanctuaries, dams and projects in the decades when removal required no one's consent and generated no enforceable rehabilitation debt, provided the removal was illegal or the rehabilitation entitlement was never delivered. Its conditions are demanding, since the displacement, its illegality or the missing entitlement, and the identity of the displaced must all be established at the distance of years, and the clause's yield in recognised rights has accordingly been small; but its presence in the catalogue matters to the statute's theory. The Act does not merely stop the historical wrong at the cut-off date. It carries a provision for the wrong's surviving victims, and the presence of that provision is the clearest textual evidence that Parliament understood the Act as reparative legislation in a strict sense, addressing not only rights never recorded but wrongs already completed.

## Minor forest produce: the economics inside clause (c)

Clause (c) of Section 3(1), the produce right, deserves a section of its own because it is where the Act most directly changes household economics, and where the distance between the text and the market is most visible. The produce in question is not marginal. Tendu leaf for bidi rolling, mahua flower and seed, sal seed and leaf, chironji, lac, honey, bamboo, tamarind, and a long list of medicinal plants and herbs move through forest villages in quantities that, for many households, exceed the cash value of the cultivated holding the land right secures. Before the Act, the gatherer's legal position in most states was that of a labourer or licensee within a state-controlled trade: commercially valuable produce was frequently nationalised, meaning it could be sold only to the state or its federation, collection rates were fixed administratively, and movement of produce required transit permits issued under the forest administration's authority, so that a villager carrying headloads of produce the village had gathered could be stopped and the produce seized as forest property. The Act's word ownership displaces that structure at its root. The right recognised is a right of ownership, access to collect, use and dispose of produce traditionally collected within or outside village boundaries, and the 2012 Rules completed the thought by defining disposal to include local processing, value addition and transport, and by shifting the transit pass for produce movement to the committee constituted by the Gram Sabha. The National Committee chaired by N. C. Saxena had recommended in the same direction, urging the abolition of forest corporation monopolies in the produce trade and the exemption of produce movement from the transit permit regime, and the amendments of 2012 carried much of that programme into the Rules.

The bamboo question concentrates the whole history into one species. Bamboo is, botanically, a grass, and for forest communities it is simultaneously a building material, a craft input and a cash crop. The Indian Forest Act of 1927 had nonetheless classified bamboo among trees, which brought its felling and transit within the permit regime even when it grew on private or community land, while the Forest Rights Act lists bamboo within minor forest produce, produce of plant origin that is not timber. A community holding a recognised community forest resource right over a bamboo-bearing forest therefore owns, on the Act's text, a resource that the older statute's categories had made it a supplicant for, and the celebrated early assertions of the produce right, including the sale of bamboo by Gram Sabhas in Gadchiroli district in Maharashtra, were politically charged precisely because they enacted that reversal in public. Parliament later amended the Indian Forest Act to remove bamboo grown in non-forest areas from the definition of tree, narrowing the contradiction from the other side. The remaining friction is administrative rather than conceptual: transit and trade rules written for a departmental monopoly do not rewrite themselves, and gatherers in many states continue to meet the old regime at the forest edge. For the claimant reader, the operative points are that the produce right is a property right and not a concession, that it extends to produce collected outside village boundaries where tradition supports it, and that a demand for a departmental transit permit for the community's own produce movement is a survival of the pre-Act structure which the amended Rules were designed to displace.

The produce right also reframes the central government's later welfare instruments. A minimum support price mechanism for listed minor forest produce, administered through the tribal affairs ministry with its federation machinery, presupposes gatherers who own what they sell and can choose between buyers; the price mechanism is an attempt to make the ownership economically real in thin markets where a single contractor's rate otherwise decides. Read together, the produce clause, the transit changes and the price mechanism show the Act operating as the foundation of an economic settlement rather than a land settlement alone, and they explain why resistance to the produce and community rights has frequently been stronger than resistance to individual titles. A title to a small holding costs the administration a record entry. Ownership of produce costs it a trade.

## The definitions that decide claims

Statute pillars are usually read for their operative sections, but claims under this Act are won and lost in Section 2, and four definitions do most of the work. Forest land is defined by inclusion rather than by record: land of any description falling within any forest area, expressly including unclassified forests, undemarcated forests, existing or deemed forests, protected forests, reserved forests, sanctuaries and national parks. The consequence is that a claimant need not show that the land is recorded as forest in any particular register; land within a forest area qualifies across the whole spectrum of forest classifications, and land the record treats casually, the undemarcated and unclassified forests of the north-east and the unsurveyed tracts of the interior, is fully inside the Act. The Gram Sabha is defined through the electoral roll: a village assembly of all adult members of a village whose names appear in the electoral rolls for the Panchayat at the village level, or, in states without Panchayats, the traditional village institutions. That definition decides hard cases in regions of dispersed hamlets, since the unit that initiates a claim is the village of the roll and not necessarily the hamlet of residence, and disputes about which Gram Sabha is competent for a claim are, on inspection, disputes about this definition. Minor forest produce is defined to include all non-timber forest produce of plant origin, with the illustrative list already noticed, and the words of plant origin carry a boundary: produce of animal origin is outside the clause, as hunting is outside the rights catalogue altogether.

The fourth definition is the one the committees actually fight over, because the Act distributes its content between two provisions. Bona fide livelihood needs, the qualifier that runs through the claimant definitions and the land right, is not elaborated in the Act itself; the Rules supply the content, tying it to the sustenance needs of the claimant and family through production or sale of produce resulting from self-cultivation of forest land under the land, produce and community-use clauses. The qualifier excludes two things at once: occupation held for commercial exploitation rather than household sustenance, and claims by persons whose connection to the forest is investment rather than dependence. Committees err in both directions with it, treating the qualifier as a general power to refuse claims that look prosperous, which it is not, and ignoring it where a claim is in substance a commercial occupation dressed in household form, which it exists to catch. The definition of a village, and of the forest village, completes the frame for the conversion right in clause (h): forest villages are settlements established inside the forest by a forest department for forestry operations, together with settlements converted into forest villages through the forest reservation process, including fixed demand holdings and taungya settlements by whatever name called, so the clause reaches the full variety of departmental settlements whose inhabitants worked the forest for generations without ever acquiring the status of villagers in law. A reader who masters these definitions can predict most claim outcomes, because the committees' questions, whose land, whose village, what produce, what need, are the definitions restated as an enquiry.

## Who may claim: two categories and one cut-off date

Eligibility under the Act runs through two definitions, and the difference between them is the single most consequential drafting choice in the statute. A forest dwelling Scheduled Tribe means the members or community of the Scheduled Tribes who primarily reside in and depend on the forests or forest land for bona fide livelihood needs, and the definition includes Scheduled Tribe pastoralist communities. For this category, membership of a tribe scheduled in relation to the state, combined with primary residence in and dependence on the forest, is the qualification; the temporal depth of the community's presence is established by the cut-off date that governs the land right, occupation as it stood on 13 December 2005, rather than by a genealogical enquiry. The second category, other traditional forest dwellers, means any member or community, other than forest dwelling Scheduled Tribes, who has for at least three generations prior to 13 December 2005 primarily resided in and depended on the forest or forest land for bona fide livelihood needs, and an explanation to the definition fixes a generation as a period of twenty-five years. Three generations is therefore seventy-five years of primary residence and dependence running back from the end of 2005, a requirement that, in practice, asks a community to evidence a connection beginning around 1930.

The asymmetry is deliberate and defensible in origin: Scheduled Tribes carry a constitutional recognition of their position that other communities do not, and Parliament chose to presume the depth of a tribal community's forest connection from its scheduled status while requiring other communities to prove theirs. But the asymmetry has an evidentiary consequence the article returns to in the implementation record. The documents that prove seventy-five years of residence, revenue records, settlement records, census entries, ration and electoral rolls across three generations, are documents that forest communities characteristically do not possess, because the same administrative history that failed to record their rights also failed to paper their lives. A category defined by proof the record rarely supplies will generate rejections at a rate that says more about the proof than about the presence, and the honest statement of the position is that whether the three-generation requirement is a principled limit or a drafting defect dressed as one is genuinely contested between those who drafted the Act and those who administer it.

The cut-off date itself does the opposite work. Occupation on 13 December 2005, the date associated with the Bill's introduction in Parliament in December 2005, is the line between occupation the Act will recognise and occupation it will not. A bright historical line is the only administrable way to run a retrospective recognition statute, since without one the recognition process becomes an open invitation to fresh occupation in the hope of regularisation, which would harm precisely the forests and the existing right holders the Act protects. The date is therefore not a judgement that occupation before it was virtuous and occupation after it is criminal; it is the boundary of the historical enquiry. Claims founded on occupation that began after the cut-off fail not because the claimant is undeserving but because the statute's question, what rights existed unrecorded, has a fixed temporal frame.

### What is the largest area an individual forest right can cover?

Four hectares. The land right in Section 3 is restricted to the area under the claimant's occupation and is in no case to exceed four hectares, so the Act recognises the holding a family actually occupies and cultivates rather than allotting a standard parcel. Most recognised individual holdings are far smaller than the ceiling.

The ceiling performs two functions. It keeps the recognition enquiry anchored in fact, since the claimant must show occupation of a specific extent rather than claim a notional entitlement, and it forecloses the most damaging misuse of the Act, the conversion of a recognition statute into a land distribution scheme for large claims by influential occupants. The distinction between area in occupation and area claimed is also one of the commonest sources of modification rather than outright rejection, where committees reduce a claim to the extent the evidence supports. A claimant reading a title for less land than was claimed is looking, very often, at that distinction at work, which is why the record of what was occupied, and the map the Gram Sabha prepares delineating each recommended claim, matter as much as the narrative of residence.

## The claim process: the Gram Sabha decides first

The provision that makes the Act structurally unusual is Section 6, and the unusualness is jurisdictional. In the ordinary architecture of Indian land administration, rights over government land are determined by revenue and forest officials, with village bodies in an advisory or evidentiary role. Section 6 inverts that order. The Gram Sabha, defined as a village assembly consisting of all adult members of a village whose names are included in the electoral rolls for the Panchayat at the village level, and in states having no Panchayats the traditional village institutions, is the authority to initiate the process for determining the nature and extent of individual or community forest rights. It receives claims, consolidates and verifies them, prepares a map delineating the area of each recommended claim, passes a resolution, and forwards the resolution to the Sub-Divisional Level Committee. The same Gram Sabha is the basic unit of self-governance that [the Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act equips with authority over village life in the Scheduled Areas](/2017/04/01/pesa-act-1996-explained/), and the Forest Rights Act builds its entire determination process on that foundation rather than on the forest bureaucracy whose records are under correction. The reason is epistemic again: the facts to be found, who has occupied what, whose grazing runs where, which grove a community protects, are facts held in the community's memory and practice, and the statute puts the first finding where the knowledge is.

Within the Gram Sabha, the working body is the Forest Rights Committee, elected by the Gram Sabha from among its members under the Rules, with a membership of not less than ten and not more than fifteen persons. The committee receives and assists claims, helps claimants assemble evidence, and supports the verification the Gram Sabha must conduct. The Rules structure the Gram Sabha's own decision conditions: the quorum for a meeting at which resolutions on claims are passed, originally two-thirds of all members under the 2008 Rules, was reduced by the 2012 Amendment Rules to one-half of all members, with requirements that at least one-third of the members present be women and that, where resolutions on forest rights claims are to be passed, at least fifty per cent of the claimants or their representatives be present, the resolution passing by simple majority of those present and voting. The 2012 amendments simultaneously raised the Scheduled Tribe representation on the Forest Rights Committee from one-third to two-thirds. Delegated legislation of this kind rarely attracts attention, but these changes record a diagnosis: the original quorum was so demanding that Gram Sabhas in dispersed forest villages could not reliably convene it, and claims were stalling at the first stage of a process designed to start there.

Above the Gram Sabha sit two committees and a monitor. The Sub-Divisional Level Committee examines the Gram Sabha's resolution and prepares the record of forest rights, forwarding it through the Sub-Divisional Officer to the District Level Committee. The District Level Committee considers and finally approves the record of forest rights, and its decision on the record is final and binding. The State Level Monitoring Committee monitors the process of recognition and vesting and submits returns and reports to the nodal agency, which Section 11 fixes as the central ministry dealing with tribal affairs, the Ministry of Tribal Affairs. Each committee consists of officers of the revenue, forest and tribal affairs departments of the state government together with three members of the Panchayati Raj Institutions at the appropriate level, appointed by those institutions, of whom two must be Scheduled Tribe members and at least one a woman. The composition tells the statute's story in miniature: the departments whose records are being corrected are inside the process, where their knowledge belongs, but they sit with elected tribal representatives and beneath a village assembly that initiates, and the final word at district level is a committee decision, not a departmental order.

The petition structure is the claimant's protection against each level, and its periods are short enough to be dangerous. A person aggrieved by the resolution of the Gram Sabha may petition the Sub-Divisional Level Committee within sixty days of the resolution, and a person aggrieved by that committee's decision may petition the District Level Committee within sixty days of the decision. No petition lies directly to the District Level Committee against a Gram Sabha resolution that has not first been preferred before and considered by the Sub-Divisional Level Committee, and no petition may be disposed of against the aggrieved person unless that person has been given a reasonable opportunity to present the case. This account states the general position under the statute and the Rules; it is information about the law rather than advice on an individual claim, and the sixty-day periods run from the date of the decision whether or not the claimant has had assistance in time, which is the strongest practical reason for a claimant or a supporting organisation to keep the committee's paperwork, and copies of everything filed, in order from the first day. Those who document claims professionally, including journalists recording the process in districts where the record itself is contested, face the same problem of keeping sensitive papers safe, and the working methods described in [how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources and their material](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) are built for exactly that discipline of private, organised custody.

Evidence is where the statute's declaratory theory meets the claimant's documentary poverty, and the Rules, particularly after 2012, are drafted to meet it honestly. The Rules list the forms of evidence a claim may rest on, including government records, documents and maps, census and survey material, research studies, records of the erstwhile princely states and intermediary regimes, physical attributes such as houses and improvements, and statements of elders. The governing ideas, hardened by the 2012 amendments into Rule 12A, are that a claim accompanied by any two of the specified forms of evidence is not to be rejected without reasons recorded in writing, that no committee may insist on a particular form of evidence, that satellite imagery and other technology are to supplement the other evidence and not to replace it, and that no authority other than the Gram Sabha, the Sub-Divisional Level Committee and the District Level Committee may reject or modify a claim at all. A rejection issued by a forest officer acting alone is therefore not an irregularity to be excused but an act by a body the statute never empowered, and the 2019 proceedings below turned in part on how frequently exactly such departures had occurred.

### Who decides whether a forest rights claim succeeds?

The Gram Sabha initiates and determines claims at the first stage, receiving and verifying them and passing a resolution on the nature and extent of each right. Its resolution is examined by the Sub-Divisional Level Committee, which prepares the record of rights, and finally approved by the District Level Committee, whose decision is final and binding.

The findable artifact below compresses the route into the form a claimant or an assisting organisation actually needs: who acts at each step, what the claimant must produce, where claims characteristically fail, and what remedy remains.

### Measurement, maps and the boundary disputes recognition creates

Every recognised right must eventually be drawn, and the drawing is where the Act meets its most technical difficulties. The Gram Sabha prepares a map delineating the area of each claim it recommends, and the record the committees approve is built on that delineation; but the village map is a sketch of customary knowledge, while the record it feeds is a legal document that must coexist with survey numbers, compartment boundaries and the maps of neighbouring villages. Individual titles have repeatedly been issued for areas differing from the occupation on the ground, sometimes because measurement at the verification stage compressed the claim, sometimes because the plot was never measured with instruments at all, and a holder who discovers the discrepancy discovers it against a record that two committees have approved. Community forest resource boundaries raise the same problem at the scale of a whole forest tract: the traditional boundary of a village's forest, a ridge, a stream, an old path, must be reconciled with the recorded boundaries of the forest compartments and with the adjoining village's own traditional boundary, and overlaps between neighbouring communities' resource claims are a regular product of a system that asks each village to draw its forest before any of them sees the others' drawings. The machinery for resolving those overlaps sits where the Act puts inter-village questions generally, in the committees above the village, with the Gram Sabhas of both villages entitled to be heard on the boundary of their own claims. The technical lesson for the pillar's reader is that recognition under this Act is not complete when the title issues; it is complete when the record, the map and the ground agree, and a good deal of the post-title litigation and departmental friction in the record is the three disagreeing. Digitisation of case records and the entry of recognised rights into the revenue and forest records, which the Ministry's review agenda names as priorities, are the administrative answer to that disagreement, and their slow progress is one reason the implementation story remains open long after a district's title figures look respectable.

### Evidence in practice: how a claim is actually proved

The evidence question decides more claims than any other, and it repays precise statement, because the popular account of the Act, that forest dwellers were asked for documents they could never have, is a half-truth which the 2012 amendments substantially changed. The Rules' list of evidence is deliberately heterogeneous. It includes public records of the kind the state itself maintains, government records, documents, maps, reports and satellite imagery; older sovereign records, documents and records of princely states, intermediary regimes such as zamindari and other local authorities; judgements and orders of courts and quasi-judicial authorities; research studies and documentation of reputed institutions; census records and survey material including maps of village boundaries; physical attributes on the ground, permanent improvements, houses, huts and other structures; quasi-judicial and judicial records including recorded offences; records of concessions, leases, licences and permits granted by governments and local authorities; records of rights in neighbouring areas; and the statements of elders of the village. The design logic is cumulative: a claim is a reconstruction of history from fragments, and the list invites the committee to assemble the fragments rather than demand the single dispositive document that the history of forest administration made impossible. The elder's statement stands in the list as a first-class form of evidence, not as a courtesy, because in unwritten communities memory is the archive.

The 2012 amendments then disciplined the committee's side of the exchange in four ways, and a claimant or adviser who does not know them cannot read a rejection order properly. First, where a claim is supported by any two of the specified forms of evidence, it is not to be rejected without the authority recording reasons in writing. Second, no committee or authority may insist that a claim be supported by one particular form of evidence, the rule against the demand for the single conclusive document. Third, technology, satellite imagery, remote sensing images and tracking of boundaries, may be used as a supplementary form of evidence and may not substitute for the other listed evidence, the rule against map-based rejection, significant because imagery shows cover rather than tenure and reads fallow cycles in shifting cultivation as vacancy. Fourth, the power to reject is confined to the Gram Sabha and the two committees, and a modification or rejection of the Gram Sabha's recommendation by the higher committees requires the claimant to be informed in writing and the petition remedy to be available. A rejection order that fails these disciplines, unexplained, unreasoned, uncommunicated, founded on a single demanded document or on imagery alone, is not a finding on the claim's merits; it is an order made in breach of the Rules, and the petition route and, after it, the constitutional courts are the remedies the legal order provides. The 2019 proceedings turned, in the end, on how widespread that species of order had become, which is why the affidavit question the Court posed was procedural rather than substantive.

## The record of rights and the title: what recognition changes

Approval by the District Level Committee is not the end of the legal story, because a forest right exists across country administered through records, and the Act's recognition has to be written into them. The record of forest rights prepared under Section 6 is the operative document: it is from the approved record that titles issue, in the statutory forms, to the right holders, and the title so issued is the holder's documentary proof in dealings with every arm of the state. For land rights the record carries the joint registration the Act requires, in the names of both spouses in the case of a married claimant and in the name of the single head in the case of a household headed by a single person. The entry of recognised rights into the revenue and forest records, and the correction of those records where they conflict, has been a persistent administrative lag: a holder may possess a title while the forest department's register continues to show the plot as departmental land and the revenue record shows nothing at all, and the Ministry's own review agenda, as stated at its 2026 regional review conference, lists digitisation of case records and incorporation of recognised rights into revenue and forest records among the standing priorities, an admission in agenda form that the writing of the record has not kept pace with the recognition of the right. Until the records converge, the title holder's practical course is to insist on the title and the approved record as the authoritative statement of the right, since the Act attaches the right to the recognition and not to the convenience of any single register.

Recognition changes the holder's position in several concrete ways beyond the title itself. Occupation that carried criminal exposure under the forest statutes becomes occupation by right, so that the daily offences of the unrecorded forest, cultivation, grazing, collection, residence, cease to be available as instruments against the holder. The produce right converts the holder's trade from tolerated extraction into ownership, with the transit consequences already traced. The community forest resource right changes the community's standing in every later proceeding about the forest: a Gram Sabha holding a recognised resource right is a party whose consent and whose management duties the diversion process and the wildlife process must both reckon with, which is why the recognition of resource rights alters the balance of the critical wildlife habitat and clearance negotiations far beyond the area titled. Recognition also has a negative content worth stating: it does not make the holder an owner in the full private law sense, since the right cannot be transferred, and it does not displace the regulatory law of the forest, since the Section 5 duties bind holders precisely because the forest remains a forest. What changes is the direction of justification. Before recognition, the holder justified presence to the administration. After recognition, the administration justifies interference to the holder, and every later exercise of power over the titled land must find its warrant in a law that survives the recognition.

### Seasonal country: pastoralists, nomads and clause (d)

Clause (d) of Section 3(1) recognises community rights such as fishing in water bodies, grazing, both settled and transhumant, and traditional seasonal resource access of nomadic or pastoralist communities, including rights to seasonal use of grazing country. The clause addresses the hardest tenure problem in the forest, because the pastoralist's right is real but episodic: a grazing ground used for six weeks a year, a migration corridor used twice, a forest patch that matters only in the dry season. Every instrument of the claim process is built for residents, the Gram Sabha of the village of residence, the Forest Rights Committee elected from its members, the verification of occupation on the ground, and the mobile community fits none of them comfortably. The Van Gujjars of the Uttarakhand and Uttar Pradesh Terai, the Bakarwals of Jammu and Kashmir, and the camel and sheep pastoralists of the western forests all hold their forest relationship through movement, and their claims have accordingly been among the slowest in the Act, frequently dependent on the Gram Sabhas of settled villages resolving claims for communities who are, for much of the year, elsewhere. The statutory text is not the obstacle: clause (d) names that seasonal use expressly, and the definition of forest dwelling Scheduled Tribes expressly includes Scheduled Tribe pastoralist communities. The obstacle is institutional design labouring against mobility, and the clause's modest yield is a measure of how completely Indian land administration, revenue and forest alike, was built for the sedentary. For the pillar, clause (d) stands with clause (e) as evidence of the catalogue's ambition: Parliament wrote seasonal and territorial tenures into a legal system whose every form assumes a fixed plot and a resident holder, and the implementation difficulties of those two clauses are the price of that ambition, payable in facilitation the system has yet fully to build.

## Habitat rights: the community tenure of the most vulnerable

Among the thirteen rights, the habitat right of clause (e) is the least exercised and the most distinctive, because it recognises a tenure over a territory rather than over plots or produce. The right runs to community tenures of habitat and habitation for the groups the Act names as primitive tribal groups and pre-agricultural communities, the categories the administration calls particularly vulnerable tribal groups, of whom seventy-five groups stand identified across the country. These are communities whose relationship with the forest is not reducible to households cultivating clearings: shifting cultivation cycles, gathering ranges, sacred geographies and seasonal movement make the community's real tenure a territory, and a title programme that recognises only the cultivated plot recognises the smallest and least characteristic part of it. The habitat right is the Act's answer to that mismatch. It vests the community's tenure over its habitat as a whole, within which the ordinary activities of the community's life are lawful by right rather than by tolerance, and it does so for the communities least equipped to fight plot-by-plot claim battles against documentary standards designed for settled agriculture.

The recognition record for habitat rights is the thinnest in the Act. Individual titles number in lakhs; habitat rights have been recognised in a small number of cases, with the Ministry reporting recognition for communities in Odisha, whose particularly vulnerable groups include some of the most studied in the country. The thinness has structural causes. A habitat claim requires the community and the machinery to delineate a territory and to describe customary tenure over it, work that fits neither the plot forms nor the committee habits the system developed for individual claims, and the communities concerned are often remote from the administrative centres where facilitation exists. The consequence is a paradox the monitoring literature records: the right designed for the most vulnerable claimants demands the most sophisticated claim-making. For the pillar's purposes the points to hold are the right's existence, its territorial character, its confinement to the named categories of community, and its place in the catalogue as proof that the Act's theory of tenure is communal at its centre and individual mainly at its evidentiary edge. A settlement pattern that reads the Act as a land distribution scheme will consistently underrate clause (e); read as a tenure statute, clause (e) is among its most honest provisions, because it admits that for some citizens the forest is not a resource to be divided but a home to be recognised whole.

## The eviction bar and the duties that come with rights

Section 4(5) contains the provision every forest dweller needs and many administrations have disregarded: save as otherwise provided, no member of a forest dwelling Scheduled Tribe or other traditional forest dweller shall be evicted or removed from forest land under occupation until the recognition and verification procedure is complete. The bar is not a stay granted by a court or a concession by a department; it is a statutory prohibition that operates by itself, and its placement in Section 4, among the vesting provisions, shows its logic. Until the procedure is complete, the legal character of the occupation is undetermined, and the state cannot treat as an encroacher a person whom its own incomplete enquiry might be about to recognise as a right holder. Eviction during pendency is therefore not merely harsh administration; it pre-empts the statute's central question, and evictions carried out while claims awaited decision, which recur in the implementation literature and in the monitoring committee record, are violations of the Act in the plainest sense the Act affords.

The other side of recognition is stated in Section 5, and it matters to the conservation debate that the duties are statutory rather than aspirational. The holders of forest rights, the Gram Sabha and the village level institutions in forest areas are empowered to protect the wildlife, forest and biodiversity of the area, to ensure that adjoining catchment areas and other ecologically sensitive areas are adequately protected, to ensure that the habitat of forest dwelling communities is preserved from destructive practices affecting their cultural and natural heritage, and to ensure that decisions taken in the Gram Sabha to regulate access to community forest resources and to stop activity that adversely affects wild animals, forest and biodiversity are complied with. Section 5 is the textual answer to the claim that the Act simply privatises the forest estate to its occupants: the rights are recognised in the same instrument that makes their holders the statutory protectors of the resource, and the community forest resource right in particular is a right to protect, regenerate, conserve or manage, a formulation in which the verbs of custody precede any verb of extraction. Whether that custody has been effective where the right has been recognised is an empirical question taken up in the debate section below; that the Act imposes it is not in doubt.

The penalty architecture is deliberately light and revealingly aimed. Section 7 provides that where an authority, committee, officer or member contravenes the provisions relating to recognition of forest rights, that person is liable to be proceeded against and punished with a fine which may extend to one thousand rupees, subject to a defence that the contravention took place without knowledge or despite the exercise of due diligence. The only offence the Act creates is an offence by the machinery against the claimant, and the fine is nominal. Both facts are worth a claimant's attention: the statute anticipated official obstruction as the characteristic violation, and then punished it at a level that functions as a declaration rather than a deterrent, leaving the real sanctions for obstruction in the petition structure, the monitoring committees, and the courts.

## Critical wildlife habitat: the safeguard built for the hardest cases

The hardest conflict the Act has to govern is the one inside protected areas, where the conservation claim for inviolate habitat is strongest and where many forest dwellers in fact live. The Act's instrument is the critical wildlife habitat, defined in Section 2(b) as such areas of national parks and sanctuaries as are required to be kept as inviolate for the purposes of wildlife conservation, determined and notified by the central environment ministry after a process of identification by an expert committee and open consultation. The concept is borrowed from, and interlocks with, the critical tiger habitat of [the Wildlife (Protection) Act 1972](/2014/10/15/wildlife-protection-act-1972-explained/), which Parliament amended in the same period to build the same safeguard structure, and the two statutes have to be read together in any actual relocation.

Section 4(2) then states the only route by which recognised rights inside such a habitat may be modified or their holders resettled, and it is a route of cumulative conditions, every one of which must be satisfied. The process of recognition and determination of rights in the area must first be complete, so no family can be moved on the footing of rights nobody has yet bothered to determine. The concerned agency of the state government must then establish that the activities or presence of the right holders are causing irreversible damage and threatening the existence of the species and their habitat, a scientific finding about identified harm, not a general preference for empty forest. The state must further conclude that coexistence by the right holders with the wildlife is not possible, which forecloses relocation as a first resort where coexistence has not been tried and found impossible. A resettlement or alternative package providing a secure livelihood for the affected individuals and communities must be prepared and communicated, in accordance with the applicable rehabilitation policy. The free informed consent of the Gram Sabhas concerned to the resettlement, and to the package, must be obtained in writing. And no resettlement may take effect until the facilities and land allocation at the resettlement location are complete, a condition aimed at the classic scandal of families moved to sites where the promised plots, water and schools did not exist. The land from which people are moved for a critical wildlife habitat is not thereafter to be diverted for other uses, which prevents the conservation rationale from serving as a clearing mechanism for commercial entry.

### What must be proved before a family can be moved from a critical wildlife habitat?

Six cumulative conditions must all be met: recognition of rights must be complete; the state must establish that the holders' presence causes irreversible damage threatening the species and its habitat; coexistence must be shown impossible; a livelihood-securing resettlement package must be prepared and communicated; the Gram Sabhas must consent freely in writing; and the resettlement site must be ready first.

The striking fact about this machinery, and the honest common ground in the debate examined below, is how little it has been used. The consent and scientific-establishment conditions require the state to do the two things its machinery is least organised to do: prove ecological harm claimant by claimant and habitat by habitat, and negotiate a package a village assembly will accept in writing. Relocation from protected areas has in practice proceeded on other footings, voluntary relocation packages under wildlife programmes operating outside the critical wildlife habitat route, with recurring controversy about how voluntary the consent obtained under them has been, while the Section 4(2) route, with its full safeguards, has remained close to unused as a formal pathway. The consequence is a debate conducted at the wrong site: instead of contesting relocation where the Act intends it to be contested, inside a structured enquiry into damage, coexistence and consent, the contest has been displaced into the claim process itself, where forest administrations doubtful about continued habitation have an incentive to resolve the question by rejecting the underlying rights. A rejected claim produces a person with nothing to relocate and nothing to consent about. That displacement is the thread connecting the habitat provisions to the rejection record and to the events of 2019.

## Diversion for village facilities and the conservation gate

Section 3(2) is the Act's own diversion provision, and it is deliberately narrow. Notwithstanding anything contained in the Forest (Conservation) Act 1980, the Central Government is to provide for diversion of forest land for a defined list of facilities managed by the government: schools, dispensaries or hospitals, anganwadis, fair price shops, electric and telecommunication lines, tanks and other minor water bodies, drinking water supply and water pipelines, water or rain water harvesting structures, minor irrigation canals, non-conventional sources of energy, skill upgradation or vocational training centres, roads, and community centres. The diversion is allowed only where the forest land to be diverted is less than one hectare in each case, where the felling involved does not exceed seventy-five trees per hectare, and where the clearance of the project is recommended by the Gram Sabha. The provision exists because the conservation gate, necessary as it is against commercial conversion, would otherwise require a full central clearance for a hamlet's drinking water pipeline or a path to its school, and because the facilities listed are the infrastructure of the very development whose absence the preamble counts as part of the injustice. A village recognised as a settlement of right holders should not have to litigate for a hand pump.

Beyond that narrow channel, the ordinary law of diversion continues to govern, and the Forest Rights Act interacts with it at the point of clearance rather than displacing it. The complete scheme of central approval for non-forest use is the subject of the series pillar on [the Forest (Conservation) Act 1980](/2014/11/01/forest-conservation-act-1980-explained/), and its contraction by the 2023 amendment, which altered the categories of land and activity the gate covers, is analysed in the companion account of [the forest conservation amendment of 2023](/2014/12/01/forest-conservation-amendment-2023/), which owns the interaction in this cluster. What belongs to this article is the Forest Rights Act side of the junction. From 2009, the environment ministry's clearance practice required evidence that the recognition process had been completed in the area proposed for diversion and that the Gram Sabhas concerned had consented, a requirement that converted the Act from a statute about titles into a procedural precondition of diversion itself. The most famous application of the Gram Sabha's voice in this setting came at Niyamgiri in Odisha, where, under the Supreme Court's direction in the Orissa Mining Corporation proceedings of 2013, the Gram Sabhas of the Dongria Khond and other villages were asked whether a bauxite mining project affected their religious and community rights, and answered that it did, an answer the clearance process then had to absorb. Niyamgiri is usually remembered as a mining story. In the architecture of this Act it is better understood as the Gram Sabha functioning exactly as Section 6 designs it, as the institution through which a community's relationship with its forest is authoritatively stated, on a question that arrived from the diversion side rather than the claims side.

For claimants, the practical distinction to hold is between the two channels. A facility on the Section 3(2) list, within the one-hectare and tree-felling limits and recommended by the Gram Sabha, travels through the Act's own simplified channel. Everything else, a mine, a dam, a highway, a resort, travels through the conservation statute's clearance, where the completed recognition of rights and the position of the Gram Sabhas are inputs the file must contain, and where the absence of recognition in a project area is not a gap in the paperwork but a failure to perform the Act's first task in the very place where its consequences are most permanent.

### The guideline years: from commencement to the 2012 settlement

Between commencement and the 2012 amendments, the Act was governed as much by executive instruction as by its text, and the period deserves record because it set the administrative common law of the statute. The environment ministry's intervention of 2009, requiring forest diversion proposals to be accompanied by evidence that the recognition process was complete and by the Gram Sabha's resolution, converted the Gram Sabha's certificate into a document of consequence in the clearance system and gave villages a bargaining strength over diversion that the Act's text alone had not spelled out; the Niyamgiri proceedings of 2013, in which the Gram Sabhas of the affected villages resolved against the mining proposal placed before them in proceedings arising from the Orissa Mining Corporation litigation, were the most visible exercise of that strength. The tribal affairs ministry's own guidance through the period, and comprehensively in 2012, worked the other side of the field, instructing states on the conduct of Gram Sabha meetings, the evidence to be accepted, the treatment of rejected claims and the composition and duties of the committees, drawing substantially on the 2010 National Committee's diagnosis. The 2012 amendments to the Rules then carried the core of that guidance into law: the reduced quorum, the two-evidence protection, the bar on single-document insistence, the supplementary status of imagery, the written reasons and communication requirements, the strengthened Forest Rights Committees and the produce transit changes. The sequence, committee report to ministerial guideline to rule amendment, is the Act's standard method of self-correction, and it explains a feature of the statute's administration that confuses outside readers: much of what is described in debate as the ministry's policy is, on inspection, the Rules as amended, and is law in the claim room whether or not a particular committee has absorbed it. The implementation record after 2012 must therefore be judged against a text that had already been repaired once in the claimants' favour, which sharpens rather than softens the criticism of the rejection practices that survived the repair.

## The implementation record: recognition and rejection at scale

The Ministry of Tribal Affairs compiles the implementation position from state returns in monthly progress reports, and the shape of the record those reports disclose is consistent across the years they cover: claims counted in tens of lakhs, titles issued for a substantial minority of them, rejections at a scale comparable to recognition, and a long tail of pendency concentrated in particular states and districts. In the Ministry's figures for the position on 31 August 2026, stated by its Joint Secretary at the second regional review conference on implementation held at Kolkata, more than 54 lakh claims had been received nationally, about 25 lakh titles had been distributed, more than 18 lakh claims had been rejected, and about 11 lakh claims remained pending at different stages, with Madhya Pradesh, Odisha and Assam named as accounting for a substantial share of the pendency. Two features of that distribution matter more than the totals. Individual land rights account for the overwhelming bulk of what has been recognised, and they have been recognised at genuine scale: millions of households hold a recorded title where the settlement record gave them nothing. And the community rights, above all the community forest resource right under clause (i), lag by an order of magnitude. The Community Forest Rights-Learning and Advocacy process, in its citizens' report Promise and Performance marking ten years of the Act, estimated the minimum potential area over which community forest resource rights could be recognised at 34.6 million hectares and found that little over 1.1 million hectares, about three per cent of that potential, had been recognised in the Act's first decade. The environment ministry itself had estimated in 2009 that about 40 million hectares of forest land could potentially come under Gram Sabha control under the Act. Between the potential and the record lies the Act's largest unperformed promise: the right that transfers not a plot but a jurisdiction, and that therefore asks the forest administration to surrender authority rather than merely to correct entries, has moved slowest precisely where it reaches furthest.

The geography of success confirms the diagnosis. Where community forest resource rights have been recognised at scale, as in parts of Maharashtra, most prominently in Gadchiroli district, where villages such as Mendha Lekha were among the earliest in the country to receive community forest resource titles and to assert the accompanying authority over bamboo and other produce, the common conditions are an organised village institution, sustained facilitation, and a district administration prepared to let the Gram Sabha's determination stand. Where those conditions are absent, the claim process meets the forest department as an interested party wearing the costume of a verifying authority, and the outcomes differ accordingly. The pattern is not one of a statute that cannot work; it is of a statute whose operation depends on the local balance between the community institution it empowers and the department it displaces, which is a fragile thing to rest a national right upon and the strongest structural criticism the record supports.

Rejection is the centre of the implementation story, and its anatomy is well documented in the monitoring and committee record. Claims have been rejected for want of documentary evidence of the kind the Rules do not insist upon, including rejections resting on the absence of satellite imagery of occupation or on a committee's treatment of imagery as conclusive against oral and physical evidence, which Rule 12A forbids. Claims have been rejected without the reasons being recorded in writing or communicated to the claimant, which the National Committee on the Forest Rights Act, the joint committee of the environment and tribal affairs ministries chaired by N. C. Saxena that reported in 2010, already identified as a systemic defect and sought to cure through guidelines and then through the 2012 amendment of the Rules. Gram Sabha resolutions have been modified or reversed by committees without the hearing Section 6 guarantees, and claims have on occasion been rejected by officials acting without the authority Section 6 confers on the three named bodies alone. Claims by other traditional forest dwellers have been rejected at markedly high rates, for the structural reason examined earlier: the three-generation test demands documentary depth that communities outside the revenue record cannot supply, and committees have too often treated the predictable absence of such documents as proof of absence of the community. Each of these is a procedural failure before it is a substantive one, which is exactly what the Act's declaratory design predicts will matter. A benefit refused on a bad process leaves the applicant where they were. A right denied on a bad process leaves the record stating, falsely or untestably, that the right never existed.

### Can a forest rights claim be rejected without written reasons?

No. After the 2012 amendment of the Rules, a claim supported by any two specified forms of evidence cannot be rejected without written reasons, the decision must be communicated to the claimant, and satellite imagery may supplement other evidence but never replace it. A rejection without recorded reasons is open to petition and challenge.

The 2012 amendments deserve to be read as the legislature's own audit of the first four years. The quorum reduction, the raised tribal representation on Forest Rights Committees, the insistence on written and communicated reasons, the two-evidence rule, the subordination of satellite imagery, the confinement of the power to reject to the three statutory bodies, and the modification of the transit permit regime for minor forest produce so that movement of produce owned by the gatherer is governed through the Gram Sabha's committee rather than the forest department's permit, all respond to failures that had been named in the Saxena committee's work and in the monitoring record. That so many of the same failures continued to be reported after 2012 is the ground on which the debate about this Act must be conducted: not whether the procedural standard is adequate, since on paper it largely is, but why a standard stated this clearly, in rules this specific, fails to govern the rooms in which claims are actually decided.

### The petitions and the writs: what happens when the machinery fails

The sixty-day petition route is the statute's only internal appeal, and its practical importance is larger than its length in the Act suggests, because it is where most defective decisions are corrected without reaching a court. A person aggrieved by the Gram Sabha's resolution may petition the Sub-Divisional Level Committee within sixty days; a person aggrieved by that committee's decision may petition the District Level Committee within sixty days. The petition is not a rehearing of the village process but a check on it: the committees must hear the petitioner, record the evidence, give reasons, and communicate the decision, and a petition that succeeds does so because the original decision failed one of those disciplines. The claimant who receives a written rejection with reasons and within time is thereby put in the position to petition intelligibly; the claimant who receives nothing learns, from the absence, that the failure was itself the violation. In practice the route's usefulness depends on awareness, which is why the ministry's guidelines treat the communication of decisions, including intimation of the petition remedy, as part of the committee's duty, and why the 2019 proceedings' demand for evidence of communication was directed at exactly this point.

Beyond the petitions lies the constitutional jurisdiction, and the writ courts' treatment of Forest Rights Act cases has become the practical last word in a substantial class of disputes. Eviction in defiance of the Section 4(5) bar, rejection without reasons, reversal of the Gram Sabha's resolution without a hearing, insistence on a single form of evidence: each has generated orders quashing or remanding the offending decision, and the body of writ law around the Act is, in substance, an enforcement of the Act's own procedures by courts that have repeatedly declined to decide the underlying right for the committees. That self-limitation is itself instructive. The courts treat the recognition of forest rights as an administrative function the statute assigned to the village and the district, and they police the function rather than performing it, which keeps the village's decision where the Act put it while denying the village and the district the power to decide arbitrarily. A claimant facing an unlawful rejection therefore has, in sequence, the petitions within the machinery, the writ outside it, and, behind both, the eviction bar while the process is alive. The route is not a grievance procedure of the modern administrative kind, with escalating tiers and time-bound disposals; it is a statutory process with one internal check and the constitutional courts beyond, and its operation in the rejection-heavy states of the record is the clearest demonstration the system offers that the Act's survival in any given village depends as much on remedies as on rights.

### The committees examined: who sits and who decides

The quality problem in the implementation record has a constitutional address inside the Act, and it lies in the composition of the deciding bodies. The Gram Sabha is a popular body: the whole village in assembly, with the claimant community present in force and the elected Forest Rights Committee doing the working. The two committees above it are, by Section 6, interdepartmental bodies: the Sub-Divisional Level Committee consists of officers of the revenue, forest and tribal affairs departments together with three members of the Panchayat Raj institutions at the block level, of whom two must be from Scheduled Tribes and at least one a woman, and the District Level Committee repeats the pattern with three members of the Zila Panchayat on the same conditions. The popular element is thus a minority in both deciding committees, and the official element includes the forest department, the institution whose recorded position the claim frequently contradicts. The design has a rationale, since records, survey capacity and funds sit with the departments and a purely popular record would lack administrative legs; but it builds the central tension of the Act into its machinery, because the body with the final word is not the body the preamble's theory trusts. The National Committee of 2010 recommended that Gram Sabhas not be bypassed in the process of recognition, and the 2012 amendments tightened the conditions under which the committees may depart from the Gram Sabha's resolutions, requiring hearing, written reasons and communication, disciplines which operate as a procedural compensation for a composition Parliament chose not to change. The working rule that emerges for readers of any claim file is this: the further the eventual decision travels from the Gram Sabha's resolution, the more of its legality depends on the quality of the reasons recorded for the journey, and a district decision that departs from the village resolution on a silent file is the Act's characteristic unlawful act.

The State Level Monitoring Committee completes the architecture and exhibits its own asymmetry. It monitors, receives reports, and answers to the nodal ministry; it neither decides claims nor hears grievances, so the state-level tier of the Act is supervisory without being appellate. Between the district's final word and the citizen there stands no statutory appeal at all, a gap the design leaves to the monitoring reports, the ministerial review conferences and, in the event, the writ courts. Whether that gap should be filled by a statutory appellate forum has been a standing item in the reform literature on the Act; the committees' defenders answer that the petition route plus monitoring keeps the process administrative and fast, while the claimants' experience answers that finality without appeal is exactly what defective rejections needed in order to harden into the aggregates of 2019. The pillar records the design as enacted and the argument as it stands: final and binding at the district, monitored at the state, enforced, when it is enforced, by constitutional courts on the statute's own procedural terms.

### How the record varies by state

The national figures conceal a federation of different records, and the Ministry's compilation laid before the Rajya Sabha, again at the position of 30 June 2026, shows how wide the variation runs. Chhattisgarh, a state built substantially on forest, had received 9,47,479 claims and issued 5,34,068 titles, a recognition effort at the scale the statute imagined. Andhra Pradesh had converted 2,88,409 claims into 2,28,489 titles, among the highest conversion rates of any large state. Bihar, by contrast, had received only 4,696 claims in nearly two decades and issued 191 titles, a record that says almost nothing about the merits of claims in Bihar and a great deal about the reach of the machinery into the state's small forest fringe. Goa shows yet another pattern, 10,377 claims received, 1,270 titles issued and 7,206 claims pending, so that the Goan story is neither recognition nor rejection but indefinite suspension. Assam's 1,12,829 pending claims, out of 2,16,644 received, is the largest pendency block in the compilation. These variations are the implementation story in its precise form. The Act is one text; its administration is twenty-eight state apparatuses of different capacities, political commitments and forest geographies, and a claimant's realistic prospects under the identical statutory standard depend heavily on which apparatus receives the claim. Any account of the Act that speaks only of national aggregates mistakes the federation's average for the citizen's experience.

The variation also locates the reform questions correctly. Where the failure is non-receipt, as in Bihar, the deficiency is outreach: claims were never filed, Gram Sabhas never activated, committees never fed, and the remedy is the administrative campaign, help desks and Gram Sabha-level facilitation that the Ministry's conference statements describe. Where the failure is pendency, as in Assam and Goa, the deficiency is throughput: the committees exist, the claims exist, and the decisions do not emerge, which is a management failure inside a functioning frame. Where the failure is rejection quality, the deficiency is legal discipline in the committees, curable by the written-reasons and communication requirements the 2012 amendments already impose and by the systematic review of rejected claims that the Ministry states it has taken up, examining whether rejections followed the Act and the Rules, whether due process was observed and whether decisions were ever communicated to the claimants. Three different failures wearing one aggregate number require three different remedies, and the first service of the state-wise record is to stop them being confused.

## February 2019: the eviction order and its stay

The proceedings in Wildlife First v. Ministry of Environment and Forests, pending since 2008 as a challenge by a conservation organisation and other petitioners, including retired forest officers, to the constitutional validity of the Act itself, produced the Act's most consequential judicial episode, and the episode is widely misremembered. On 13 February 2019, a bench of Arun Mishra, Navin Sinha and M. R. Shah JJ., recording the states' own figures of claims rejected, directed the chief secretaries of the states to ensure the eviction of claimants whose rejections had attained finality, with the Forest Survey of India to conduct a satellite survey after eviction, and treated failure to evict as a matter to be viewed seriously. Press Trust of India reporting put the number of rejected claimants exposed to eviction at some 11.8 lakh. The order did not hold the Act invalid, did not examine any individual rejection, and did not direct the eviction of any person whose claim was pending or recognised; but by converting rejection into a ground for eviction at national scale, it attached the Act's gravest consequence to the Act's least reliable output, the rejection figures of processes nobody in the courtroom had audited.

The correction came within a fortnight, and its terms matter more than the original direction. The Union of India, through the Ministry of Tribal Affairs, applied for modification, submitting that the Act is beneficial legislation to be construed liberally for claimants who are extremely poor and often unaware of their rights and of the procedure, and the Solicitor General, Tushar Mehta, argued that the rights of forest inhabitants could not be sacrificed on account of forest preservation and that the two must exist in harmony. On 28 February 2019 the same bench stayed and kept on hold the order of 13 February, and directed the states to file affidavits, through their chief secretaries, disclosing the process by which claims had been rejected: the modalities followed, whether the rejections were made after due process, whether there had been adequate communication with the claimants at all stages, and the position of the State Level Monitoring Committees. Read against the Act, the stay is the judiciary arriving at the statute's own theory. The question the Court chose to ask was not whether the rejected claimants deserved the forest, but whether the rejections on which eviction was to rest had been produced by the process the Act prescribes, with reasons recorded, communication made and appeals available. Where that question cannot be answered affirmatively, rejection cannot bear the weight the February order proposed to put on it, because under a recognition statute the rejection is the only thing standing between the claimant and the status of right holder. The proceedings have continued thereafter without the eviction direction being revived in its original form, and the matter stands as a continuing adjudication of the Act's validity and administration rather than as an operative eviction mandate. Any account that describes the 2019 order as an eviction order that stood is describing an order that survived for fifteen days.

## The Act in court beyond 2019: validity, remedy and the continuing proceedings

The Wildlife First proceedings carried two distinct challenges that the public record sometimes merges, and the pillar keeps them apart. The first was a challenge to the Act itself and to its Rules and clarifications, the petition's constitutional case being that the recognition architecture trespassed on the forest estate and on the conservation statutes; that challenge has remained pending through the years of interim orders, and the Act has operated throughout under its own authority, unstruck and unstayed. The second was the enforcement case built on rejection figures, which produced the February 2019 orders. The distinction matters for readers of the headlines: no court has held the Act invalid, and the eviction episode arose not from a ruling against the Act but from an interim direction in proceedings where the Act's validity was itself in issue, a direction the same bench then suspended on the procedural question. The proceedings have since continued on affidavits and administration, with the eviction direction never revived in its original form, and the practical law for claimants throughout has been the Act as enacted, supplemented by the disciplines the stay order reasserted: process first, findings on record, no enforcement on aggregates.

Below the Supreme Court, the Act's judicial life runs through the High Courts on writs against particular rejections and particular evictions, and this is where most of its case law is actually made. The recurring writ pattern is instructive: a claimant or community whose claim was rejected without reasons, or whose occupation was threatened while a claim stood pending, invokes the constitutional courts, and the courts respond by enforcing the statute's own procedures, remanding decisions for reasons, restraining eviction during pendency, directing committees to hear and to record. The writ jurisdiction has thus functioned as the enforcement machinery the Act did not build for itself, since Section 7's penalty is nominal and Section 6's remedies end at the District Level Committee. The lesson the case law teaches, consistent from the writ courts to the 2019 bench, is that the Act is most vulnerable where its procedure is treated as decoration and most secure where the procedure is followed: recognition programmes conducted with quorum, evidence and reasons have survived challenge, and shortcuts have not. For the institutional reader, that consistency is the practical synthesis of the Act's litigation history.

## Section 7: the offence provision and the enforcement gap

The Act's teeth are in Section 7, and they are small. Where an authority, a committee named in the Act, a government officer or a member of such a body contravenes any provision of the Act or any rule concerning the recognition of forest rights, the contravention is an offence punishable on conviction with a fine extending to one thousand rupees, and no person is liable to punishment who proves that the offence was committed without that person's knowledge or that all due diligence was exercised to prevent it. As drafted, the provision is directed at the machinery rather than at the claimant, which is the correct direction: the offences that matter under a recognition statute are official, the refusal to receive a claim, the rejection without process, the eviction in defiance of the bar. But a fine capped at one thousand rupees, recoverable only on conviction in a criminal prosecution, has proved in practice to be a provision of symbolic rather than disciplinary force, and prosecutions under it are not a feature of the implementation record. The enforcement of the Act against its own machinery has accordingly migrated to the instruments that do work: the monitoring reports, the ministerial review conferences with their state-wise scrutiny, the petition route, and the writ jurisdiction, whose quashing and remand orders are the real sanctions the system applies to a disobedient committee. The gap between Section 7's text and the Act's actual enforcement economy is worth recording in the pillar because it explains where the pressure on administrators in fact comes from, and because it marks the limit of the 2006 settlement: Parliament corrected the forest record and armed the claimant with procedure, but left the erring official to be corrected by supervision and by courts rather than by the statute's own hand. The same limit shapes the Act's relationship with the general penal law of the forest. A recognised holder who exceeds the recognised right, by hunting, by taking produce beyond the clauses, or by occupying beyond the titled extent, stands outside the Act's protection and within the reach of the wildlife and forest statutes in the ordinary way; the Act is a shield for the rights it recognises and confers no immunity beyond them, which is the necessary corollary of the declaratory design and the answer the statute itself gives to the objection that recognition is deregulation by another name. Symmetrically, an officer who treats recognised tenure as encroachment acts against the statute the officer serves, and the discipline for that conduct, weak in Section 7, is supplied in practice by the monitoring and judicial machinery described above, whose interventions uniformly take the form of returning the defaulting authority to the Act's own procedure.

## Women, joint title and the design of the household right

The Act's treatment of women is written into its tenure mechanics rather than declared in a separate chapter, and it is easy to miss for that reason. Section 4(4) requires that a recognised right be registered jointly in the names of both spouses in the case of married persons, and in the name of the single head in the case of a household headed by a single person, so that the widow, the deserted wife and the woman heading her household alone take title in her own name rather than through a male relative. Joint registration changes the title's behaviour across a generation: the holding cannot be dealt with, to the extent dealings are possible at all, nor the household's position represented, on the authority of one spouse alone, and on succession the widow is already on the title rather than a petitioner for entry. The committee design carries the same policy into procedure: the Gram Sabha process runs on quorums that include women in their own right, the claim resolution meetings require the presence of women members in the proportion the amended Rules prescribe, and the Forest Rights Committee's composition reserves representation for women. These provisions belong to the statute's definition of the household as a rights-bearing unit of two adults rather than a unit headed by one, and they gave the recognition programme a gender architecture unusual in Indian land legislation of its period, achieved not through a women's chapter but through the ordinary machinery of registration and quorum where it does its work silently.

## The conservation objection and the rights answer

The conservation objection to the Act deserves its strongest statement, because it is made in part by people with genuine records in the field and because the Act's defenders weaken their own case by caricaturing it. In its strongest form, as pressed by Wildlife First in the proceedings described above and by conservation voices who supported the challenge, the objection runs as follows. India's remaining natural forests survive in fragments under intense pressure, and the protected area network is the principal instrument by which habitat of any integrity has been retained. A statute that recognises individual occupation rights across forest land, that empowers village assemblies to determine claims in the first instance, and that recognises community authority over community forest resources, creates powerful incentives for fresh occupation in anticipation of regularisation, weakens the deterrent against encroachment, and fragments the estate within which wide-ranging species must move. The objection adds an institutional point: the Gram Sabha is a political body, not an ecological one, and its incentives run toward distributing the resource it controls among its members, so the Act replaces a flawed but conservation-mandated trustee with thousands of local principals whose interests are extractive by default. On this view, the historical injustice was real but the remedy overshoots, and the forest pays for the correction.

The rights answer, in its strongest form, as made by the Campaign for Survival and Dignity, by Madhu Sarin, who participated in the drafting of the legislation and has written its most sustained defence, and by researchers such as C. R. Bijoy, runs as follows. The forests the conservation objection treats as empty habitat are, in large part, inhabited and worked country that communities used, protected and regenerated for generations, and the state's record of fortress conservation, exclusion without recognition, produced neither consent nor effective protection outside a small protected core, while manufacturing a permanent conflict between the forest staff and the forest population. Tenure security changes incentives rather than merely distributing assets: a community with a recognised right to protect and manage a community forest resource, and with ownership of the minor forest produce it gathers, has a material interest in the standing forest that an excluded population, treated as encroachers on its own land, does not. The evidence offered for this includes the regeneration reported from areas under recognised community management, and the environment ministry's own estimate that tens of millions of hectares could pass under Gram Sabha authority, an area no conceivable expansion of the forest guard force could police against the will of the people living in it. On this view the Act does not trade forest for rights; it is the first forest legislation to notice that the two stand or fall together.

The verdict the record supports is narrower than either side's full claim, and it is the one the statute's own text anticipates. The Act contains, in Section 4(2), a complete mechanism for the genuine conflict: where a particular habitat must be inviolate, the state may establish irreversible damage, rule out coexistence, prepare a package, obtain written consent and resettle, with the vacated land barred from other uses. That mechanism transfers the argument from ideology to proof, habitat by habitat. Its near-total disuse, while the conflict is instead fought through mass claim rejection and episodic eviction directions, is the clearest failure the implementation record discloses, and it is a failure of the state rather than of either camp. A conservation policy that cannot operate its own statute's safeguarded route, and a rights policy content to let recognised jurisdiction stall at three per cent of its potential, are both, at present, positions about the Act rather than performances of it. What is settled is the text: rights are recognised, duties attach, relocation is conditioned and consent-bound. What is contested on the facts is whether community management conserves as well as exclusion does, a question the recognised areas are only beginning to answer at scale. What is contested on values, and not resolvable by evidence, is whether a forest is diminished or completed by the presence of the people who have always lived in it.

## Scheduled Areas, the Sixth Schedule and the Act's geography

The Act applies across the country's forest geography, but it operates differently in the constitutionally scheduled regions, and a pillar must register the difference. In the Fifth Schedule areas of the ten states that carry them, the Gram Sabha exercises its Forest Rights Act functions within a constitutional framework that already treats the tribal village as a unit of governance, with the Governor's special powers over land transfer and the Panchayats legislation extended with modifications; the Act's initiating authority and PESA's empowered assembly are the same body in the same hall, and the consent and consultation practices of the two instruments interlock, which is one reason the strongest assertions of Gram Sabha authority in the diversion field have come from Scheduled Area villages. In the Sixth Schedule areas of the north-east, forest administration runs through Autonomous District Councils with their own legislative competence over land and forest management, and the autonomous councils' laws are themselves a source of recognised rights under clause (k) of Section 3(1); the Act's machinery there works through the traditional village institutions the Gram Sabha definition reaches in the absence of Panchayats, and the region's large undemarcated and unclassified forests fall within the Act because the forest land definition expressly includes them. Outside the scheduled regions, in the reserved forest heartlands of the central Indian plateau where the largest claimant populations live, the Act operates without constitutional reinforcement, on its own statutory strength alone. The geography explains part of the implementation map: the same committee procedure produces different practical authority depending on the constitutional ground beneath the village, and the monitoring record's best community forest performances tend to sit where the Act's Gram Sabha and the older self-governance instruments pull together.

## The Act among its neighbours: PESA, the wildlife code and the clearance regime

No statute in the forest field operates alone, and the Act's drafters said so in its text: Section 13 provides that, save as otherwise provided in the Act itself and in the Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act, the Act's provisions are in addition to, and not in derogation of, the provisions of any other law in force. The clause is a deliberate refusal of the override. The Forest Rights Act does not repeal the Indian Forest Act, does not amend the Wildlife (Protection) Act, does not displace the Forest (Conservation) Act, and does not rewrite the Panchayats framework; it adds a rights layer across all of them, and the legal work of the following two decades has been the negotiation of that layer against each neighbour in turn. With PESA, the negotiation is internal to self-governance: the Gram Sabha that PESA empowers over village affairs and resources in Scheduled Areas is the same assembly the Forest Rights Act makes the initiating authority for claims, and the consent practice that grew around diversion in Scheduled Areas draws strength from both instruments at once, the ownership of minor forest produce recognised in the Panchayats legislation reinforcing the produce right recognised here. The two statutes are best read as one settlement in two enactments, the governance half and the tenure half.

Against the wildlife code, the negotiation is the critical wildlife habitat mechanism already examined: the Wildlife (Protection) Act supplies the protected areas and the inviolate objective, the Forest Rights Act supplies the conditions under which the people inside those areas may be asked to move, and the settlement between them is procedural rather than substantive, since neither statute yields its objective and the conditions decide which objective prevails in a given forest. Against the clearance regime, the negotiation runs through consent and prior recognition: because the Forest (Conservation) Act's gate governs the land use change and the Forest Rights Act governs the rights over the land, a lawful diversion must pass both gates, and the administrative practice of requiring the Forest Rights Act process to be completed, with Gram Sabha resolutions produced, before diversion is approved is the executive's way of sequencing two statutes that Parliament left to be read together. The 2023 amendment of the conservation statute, which recast the categories of land and activity within that Act's reach, did not amend the Forest Rights Act, and rights recognised under this Act over land of any category continue on their own statutory footing; what the amendment changed is the clearance analysis for the land use, not the existence of the rights the diversion must reckon with. Finally, against the land acquisition code, the negotiation is the displacement interface: acquisition of forest land displaces right holders whose recognised rights are interests the acquisition process must identify and compensate, and clause (m) reaches back to displacement suffered without rehabilitation before the Act. In each pairing the pattern repeats, and it is the pattern of Section 13: the Act changes the starting position of every other process in the forest without abolishing any of them, which is precisely why it is simultaneously so consequential and so dependent on the quality of its own recognition machinery.

## Assessment and verdict

Measured against the One Test for a statute pillar, the Act succeeds as drafting and falters as administration, and the distance between the two is unusually instructive. As drafting, it is coherent to an uncommon degree: a declaratory theory of rights generates the cut-off date, the Gram Sabha's primacy, the eviction bar, the conditioned relocation route and the duties of holders, each provision answering a question the theory poses. As administration, it has delivered individual titles at a scale that has changed millions of households' legal position, while leaving the community jurisdiction that is its largest idea mostly unexercised, and while tolerating rejection practices that its own Rules, twice amended to forbid them, describe in terms that leave no room for excuse. The reader who assists claimants should therefore work the procedure as written, insisting on the Gram Sabha's primacy, the two-evidence rule, written reasons and the sixty-day petitions, because the gap between the text and the practice is closed, where it has been closed, by claimants and committees invoking the text. The reader who studies the Act should treat the preamble as its ratio: a legislature that names its own record as the injustice has told the reader exactly where to look when the record is again the problem. Rejection is a finding, not a refusal, and a finding made without reasons, without a hearing and without an appeal is not a finding at all.

### Reading this statute: the route for each kind of reader

The claimant's route through the Act is procedural, and it runs in the statute's own order: confirm the category and the cut-off, file through the Gram Sabha with the evidence the Rules list in any two forms that can be assembled, insist that the resolution meeting carries its quorum, and treat every adverse step as an order that must arrive in writing with reasons, against which the sixty-day petitions run. The two provisions a claimant should be able to cite unassisted are Section 4(5), the eviction bar that protects the occupation while the process lives, and the 2012 evidence disciplines that forbid rejection on a single demanded document or on imagery alone. The practitioner's route is the same road walked in the other direction: a rejection is reviewed by asking whether the committee that made it had the power, whether the claimant was heard, whether reasons were recorded and communicated, whether the evidence rules were honoured, and whether the petition remedies were intimated; most defective orders fail on one of those questions without the merits being reached. The administrator's route is the implementation record read as instruction: the failures that recur, non-receipt of claims, pendency, silent rejection, records left unamended after titles issue, are all failures of the apparatus rather than the text, and each has a named remedy in the ministry's own review agenda. The research reader's route is to resist the aggregates. The Act's truth is located at the level of the village file and the district return, in the distance between the Gram Sabha's resolution and the district's word, and in the ratio, village by village, between the forest that exists and the forest that stands recognised. A statute that set out to correct a record can only be judged record by record, and the materials for that judgement, the titles, the rejection orders and the committee files, are the archive this Act is still in the process of creating.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What rights does the Forest Rights Act recognise?

Section 3(1) recognises thirteen categories of right. They include the right to hold and live in forest land in occupation for habitation or self-cultivation; community rights such as nistar; ownership of, access to collect, use and dispose of minor forest produce; community rights over water bodies, grazing and seasonal resource access; habitat rights for primitive tribal groups and pre-agricultural communities; rights over disputed lands; conversion of pattas, leases, grants and forest villages; the right to protect and manage community forest resources; rights under state law, autonomous council law and customary law; access to biodiversity and traditional knowledge; other traditional rights except hunting; and in situ rehabilitation for those unlawfully displaced.

### Q: Who is eligible to claim forest rights?

Two categories may claim. Forest dwelling Scheduled Tribes are members or communities of Scheduled Tribes who primarily reside in and depend on forests or forest land for bona fide livelihood needs, including pastoralist communities. Other traditional forest dwellers are members or communities outside that category who have, for at least three generations before 13 December 2005, primarily resided in and depended on the forest or forest land for bona fide livelihood needs, a generation being twenty-five years. Both categories claim through the Gram Sabha, and the land right depends on occupation as it stood on the cut-off date.

### Q: What is the cut-off date for a forest rights claim?

The cut-off date is 13 December 2005. The land right recognised by the Act is measured against occupation as it stood on that date, and the eligibility of other traditional forest dwellers is measured by three generations of residence and dependence running back from it. Occupation that began after 13 December 2005 is outside the Act's enquiry, because the statute recognises rights that existed unrecorded at that point rather than distributing land for later occupation. The date corresponds to the period of the Bill's introduction in Parliament and gives the retrospective recognition exercise a fixed historical frame.

### Q: What is a community forest resource right?

It is the right recognised by Section 3(1)(i) to protect, regenerate, conserve or manage any community forest resource which the community has been traditionally protecting and conserving for sustainable use. Unlike an individual land right, it is a right over a forest area as a whole, held by the community through its Gram Sabha, and it carries authority as well as use: the community may regulate access, stop activities harmful to the forest and wildlife, and manage the produce of the area. The right has the largest potential territorial reach in the Act and the slowest record of recognition.

### Q: How does a Gram Sabha process a forest rights claim?

The Gram Sabha is the authority that initiates the process. A Forest Rights Committee elected by the Gram Sabha assists claimants and supports verification. The Gram Sabha receives claims, consolidates and verifies them, prepares a map delineating the area of each recommended claim, and passes a resolution on the nature and extent of the right, which it forwards to the Sub-Divisional Level Committee. Claim resolutions require the quorum the Rules prescribe, including a minimum presence of claimants or their representatives, and pass by simple majority of those present and voting.

### Q: Can a forest dweller be evicted before a claim is decided?

No. Section 4(5) provides that no member of a forest dwelling Scheduled Tribe or other traditional forest dweller shall be evicted or removed from forest land under occupation until the recognition and verification procedure is complete. The bar operates by force of the statute and does not depend on a court order or a departmental concession. Its logic is that the legal character of an occupation is undetermined while the procedure is pending, so eviction during pendency would pre-empt the very question the Act requires the machinery to answer.

### Q: What happened to the eviction order of 2019?

On 13 February 2019, in the Wildlife First proceedings, the Supreme Court directed states to evict claimants whose rejections under the Act had attained finality. On 28 February 2019, on the Union government's application, the same bench stayed that direction and required states to file affidavits explaining the process by which claims had been rejected, including whether due process was followed, whether decisions were communicated, and the role of the monitoring committees. The eviction direction therefore operated for fifteen days and was not revived in its original form; the proceedings continued on the Act's validity and administration.

### Q: Why are so many forest rights claims rejected?

The documented grounds cluster around process rather than entitlement. Claims have been rejected for want of particular documents the Rules do not insist on, including on the strength of satellite imagery treated as conclusive although the Rules make technology supplementary only; without reasons recorded in writing or communicated to the claimant; by committees reversing Gram Sabha resolutions without hearing the claimant; and, for other traditional forest dwellers, because the three-generation test demands documentary proof reaching back some seventy-five years that communities outside the revenue record rarely possess. High rejection numbers therefore measure the machinery's performance at least as much as the claims' merits.

### Q: What evidence can be used to prove a forest rights claim?

The Rules list broad forms of evidence, including government records, documents and maps, census and survey material, research studies, records of erstwhile princely states and intermediary regimes, physical attributes on the ground such as houses and improvements, and statements of elders of the village. After the 2012 amendment of the Rules, a claim supported by any two of the specified forms of evidence is not to be rejected without reasons in writing, no authority may insist on one particular form of evidence, and satellite imagery and other technology may supplement the other evidence but never replace it.

### Q: What is the difference between an individual forest right and a community forest right?

An individual forest right attaches to a household and, in the case of land, covers the area in occupation up to four hectares, held heritably and registered jointly in the names of both spouses where applicable. Community rights attach to the village community acting through its Gram Sabha and cover shared resources and areas: minor forest produce, grazing, water bodies, nistar, and, at the largest scale, the community forest resource, where the right is one of protection and management over a forest. The first secures a family's tenure; the second secures a community's jurisdiction.

### Q: What happens after the Gram Sabha passes a resolution on a claim?

The resolution goes to the Sub-Divisional Level Committee, which examines it and prepares the record of forest rights, forwarding the record through the Sub-Divisional Officer to the District Level Committee. The District Level Committee considers and finally approves the record, and its decision on the record is final and binding. A person aggrieved by the Gram Sabha's resolution may petition the Sub-Divisional Level Committee within sixty days, and a person aggrieved by that committee's decision may petition the District Level Committee within sixty days, with a right to be heard before any adverse disposal.

### Q: What does the Act allow on forest land for schools, roads and drinking water?

Section 3(2) requires the Central Government to provide for diversion of forest land for thirteen listed facilities managed by the government, including schools, dispensaries or hospitals, anganwadis, fair price shops, electric and telecommunication lines, drinking water supply, minor irrigation canals, roads and community centres. The diversion is allowed only where the land is less than one hectare in each case, the felling does not exceed seventy-five trees per hectare, and the Gram Sabha recommends the project. Larger or unlisted projects travel through the ordinary forest clearance process instead.

### Q: What is a critical wildlife habitat under the Forest Rights Act?

A critical wildlife habitat is an area of a national park or sanctuary determined and notified as required to be kept inviolate for wildlife conservation, identified through a scientific and objective process with consultation. Recognised rights inside such a habitat may be modified, and holders resettled, only under the cumulative safeguards of Section 4(2): completed recognition, a scientific finding of irreversible damage, a conclusion that coexistence is not possible, a prepared and communicated resettlement package, the free informed consent of the Gram Sabhas in writing, and a ready resettlement site before anyone moves.

### Q: Can rights recognised under the Forest Rights Act be sold or transferred?

No. The rights conferred by the Act are heritable but not alienable or transferable. They may pass to heirs, and land rights are registered jointly in the names of both spouses in the case of married persons, but they cannot be sold, gifted away or otherwise conveyed to another person. The restriction protects holders against distress sales and acquisition of titles by outsiders, at the cost of preventing the title from being used as an ordinary marketable or mortgageable asset. What a holder receives is secure tenure, not a tradeable property interest.

### Q: What duties does the Forest Rights Act place on right holders and Gram Sabhas?

Section 5 empowers and obliges the holders of forest rights, the Gram Sabha and village institutions to protect the wildlife, forest and biodiversity of the area, to ensure that adjoining catchment areas and ecologically sensitive areas are adequately protected, to preserve the habitat of forest dwelling communities from destructive practices affecting their cultural and natural heritage, and to ensure compliance with Gram Sabha decisions regulating access to community forest resources and stopping activity harmful to wild animals, forest and biodiversity. The Act pairs every recognised right with a statutory responsibility for the resource it concerns.

### Q: How did the 2012 amendment of the Forest Rights Rules change claim processing?

The Amendment Rules of 2012, notified on 6 September 2012, reduced the Gram Sabha quorum for claim resolutions from two-thirds to one-half of members, raised Scheduled Tribe representation on Forest Rights Committees from one-third to two-thirds, required decisions rejecting or modifying claims to be communicated with reasons, protected claims supported by any two listed forms of evidence from rejection without written reasons, made satellite imagery supplementary rather than a substitute for other evidence, confined the power to reject claims to the Gram Sabha and the two committees, and reworked the transit permit regime for minor forest produce in the gatherers' favour.

### Q: What is the role of the Forest Rights Committee in a claim?

The Forest Rights Committee is a committee of not less than ten and not more than fifteen persons elected by the Gram Sabha from among its members, with the Scheduled Tribe representation and women's representation the Rules prescribe. It is the working body of the claim process at village level: it assists claimants in preparing and filing claims, helps assemble the evidence the Rules recognise, supports the Gram Sabha's verification of claims on the ground, and carries the process between the claimant and the Gram Sabha. A member who is personally a claimant must not participate in the verification of that claim.

### Q: Why is the Act called the Act of 2006 when it is numbered Act 2 of 2007?

The short title carries the year of enactment, and the Act number carries the year of publication. Parliament passed the Bill in December 2006 and the President assented on 29 December 2006, so the statute is named for 2006. It was published in the Gazette of India on 2 January 2007, and Acts are numbered within the calendar year of their publication, making it the second Act of 2007. The Act was then brought into force on 31 December 2007, with the Rules notified on 1 January 2008, so three different years attach to its making, numbering and commencement.

### Q: Does the Forest Rights Act apply inside national parks and sanctuaries?

Yes. Forest land under the Act means land of any description falling within any forest area, expressly including sanctuaries and national parks, and rights under Section 3 may be recognised in such areas. What differs inside protected areas is the possibility of later modification: rights in critical wildlife habitats of national parks and sanctuaries may be modified or their holders resettled only through the safeguarded process of Section 4(2), including scientific establishment of irreversible damage, exhaustion of coexistence, a resettlement package and the written free informed consent of the Gram Sabhas.

### Q: Who monitors implementation of the Forest Rights Act, and what can be done about obstruction?

At the centre, Section 11 makes the ministry dealing with tribal affairs the nodal agency, and in each state the State Level Monitoring Committee monitors the process of recognition and vesting and reports to that agency. Within the process, the petition route of Section 6 and the requirement of written, communicated reasons are the claimant's first remedies. Section 7 separately makes an authority, committee, officer or member who contravenes the recognition provisions liable to a fine extending to one thousand rupees, subject to a defence of absence of knowledge or due diligence.
