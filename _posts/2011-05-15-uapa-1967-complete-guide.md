Any UAPA 1967 complete guide has to begin with a fact that the name of the statute tends to conceal, which is that the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act is not one coherent code written at a single moment for a single purpose. It is two statutes stacked in one binding. The lower layer, enacted in 1967, is a law about associations. Its central question is whether an organisation may be declared unlawful by the Central Government, and its central machinery is a declaration, a reference to a Tribunal, and a confirmation or cancellation by a judge. The upper layer, grafted onto the first in 2004, is a law about terrorism. Its central question is whether a person has committed, conspired to commit, financed, supported or concealed a terrorist act, and its central machinery is investigation, extended detention before a charge sheet, a demanding test for bail, forfeiture of property said to be proceeds of terrorism, and punishment that can extend to death or imprisonment for life. The two layers share a cover, a short title and a place in the statute book. They do not share a logic, and a reader who confuses them will misunderstand almost everything that follows.

The difference is not merely one of subject matter. The 1967 regime works, in the main, before any criminal punishment is imposed on an individual for membership or support, and it works through a collective object, the association. The Central Government forms an opinion that an association is unlawful, issues a notification in the Gazette specifying the grounds, and must then place that notification before a Tribunal presided over by a sitting Judge of a High Court. The notification, with a narrow exception for immediate effect where reasons are recorded in writing, has no effect until the Tribunal confirms it. The 2004 regime works in almost the opposite direction. It defines a terrorist act in section 15, attaches punishment in sections 16 to 23, creates offences connected with terrorist organisations in sections 35 to 40, and then, in Chapter VII, modifies ordinary criminal procedure in ways that bite from the moment of arrest. Police custody may run to thirty days and detention before a charge sheet to ninety days, extendable to one hundred and eighty days. Anticipatory bail does not apply, bail is barred where the court finds reasonable grounds for believing the accusation is prima facie true, and prior sanction is required before a court takes cognizance. The safeguards are therefore different in kind, not only in degree, because the first half interposes a judicial Tribunal between an executive declaration and its legal effect, while the second half places its principal checks inside a criminal process that has itself been deliberately made more stringent.

That distinction supplies the one test by which the whole Act can be read without confusion. Place any UAPA case in its correct half, and the applicable procedure and the available remedies fall into place. If the case concerns a declaration under section 3, the questions are whether the Central Government specified its grounds, whether any withholding of facts was justified on the ground that disclosure would be against the public interest, whether the reference reached the Tribunal within thirty days, whether the association had its opportunity to show cause, and whether the Tribunal formed its own opinion on credible material rather than merely endorsing the opinion of the Government. If the case concerns a terrorist act under section 15, the questions are entirely different. They concern the rank of the investigating officer, the modified periods of custody and detention, the bar in section 43D(5) at the bail stage, and the prior sanction required under section 45, with the independent review of the evidence that section 45(2) requires before sanction is given. The test is simple to state and demanding to apply, because real prosecutions often draw on both halves at once, with an association declared unlawful under Chapter II, membership punished under Chapter III, and terrorist offences alleged under Chapter IV. Disentangling the layers is the first task in every such case, and it cannot be done at all if the statute is treated as a single anti-terror code.

Two common errors make that disentangling harder than it needs to be. The first is to treat the UAPA as a post-2001 anti-terror law, a product of the wave of security legislation that followed the attacks of that period in the United States and the attack on Parliament in India, and to read its every provision as though it had been designed in that atmosphere. The second, which follows from the first, is to date its terrorism provisions to 1967, as though Parliament in that year had enacted a law against bombs, terrorist funding and terrorist organisations and had simply refined it since. Both errors are contradicted by the structure of the Act itself. For its first thirty-seven years the UAPA contained no chapter on terrorist activities at all. Chapter IV, in the form that punishes terrorist acts, was substituted by Act 29 of 2004 with effect from 21 September 2004. Provisions on terrorist organisations and special procedure arrived through the amendments of 2004 and 2008, and the designation of individuals as terrorists arrived only in 2019. To project those provisions back to 1967 is to erase the long period in which the Act did a narrower and quite different job, and to miss the constitutional reason for which it was passed in the first place.

The wider field helps to make that seam visible, because Indian anti-terror law did not develop inside the UAPA alone, and [the lineage of anti-terror laws in India](/2011/04/01/anti-terror-laws-india-lineage/) shows a pattern of special statutes enacted, allowed to lapse or repealed, and then partly absorbed into the permanent statute book. Earlier special statutes had lapsed or been repealed, among them the Prevention of Terrorism Act in 2004, and Parliament did not leave the field empty. It transferred much of the substance of a terrorism code into the UAPA, an existing permanent statute, rather than enacting a fresh special law with a sunset clause. That choice has consequences that run through every later debate about the Act. Provisions that might have been debated as temporary and exceptional became part of an ordinary, permanent law that had begun life as a measure against secessionist association. Critics of the 2004 changes argued in Parliament and outside it that the repeal lost much of its meaning if the substance survived in another statute without the political visibility of a separate anti-terror law. The Central Government, defending the changes, presented them as the necessary means of dealing with terrorist activities after the repeal, within a statute whose long title was extended in 2004 to add the words "and for dealing with terrorist activities". The question is always which half is doing the work in a given case, which safeguard Parliament attached to that half, and whether that safeguard was observed.

## The constitutional space the Act was built to fill

The 1967 Act was not written on a blank constitutional page. It was written to occupy a space that Parliament had deliberately created four years earlier, through an amendment to the Constitution itself, and the shape of that space explains both the language of the Act and the limits written into its first provisions. The starting point was a committee and a concern about national integration. The Committee on National Integration and Regionalism, appointed by the National Integration Council, considered the forces that pulled against the unity of the Union and recommended, among other things, that Article 19 of the Constitution be amended so that powers would exist to preserve the sovereignty and integrity of the Union, and that the oaths taken by those who held public office or sought it be reformed to match. Those recommendations matter because Article 19 is the article that guarantees the freedoms on which the whole argument turned. Article 19(1) protects, among other freedoms, the freedom of speech and expression, the freedom to assemble peaceably and without arms, and the freedom to form associations or unions. Article 19(2), (3) and (4) then set out the grounds on which the State may impose reasonable restrictions on those freedoms. Before 1963, the sovereignty and integrity of India was not among the grounds stated in those clauses, and a statute aimed squarely at advocacy of secession therefore stood on uncertain constitutional ground.

The Constitution (Sixteenth Amendment) Act, 1963 supplied the missing ground, and the route it took illustrates [the process of amending the Constitution of India](/2010/01/01/constitution-amendment-process-india/) in a particularly clear form, because a change to the text of a fundamental right was used to prepare the way for ordinary legislation that would follow later. The Sixteenth Amendment inserted the words "the sovereignty and integrity of India" into Article 19(2), Article 19(3) and Article 19(4). Reasonable restrictions on the freedoms of speech and expression, of assembly and of forming associations or unions could thereafter be imposed in the interests of the sovereignty and integrity of India. The amendment did not itself ban anything, punish anything or declare any association unlawful. It altered the conditions under which a later law could validly restrict three connected freedoms, and it did so in language that the later law would repeat almost word for word. The definition of unlawful activity in section 2(1)(o) of the UAPA, built on cession, secession, disruption of the sovereignty and territorial integrity of India and disaffection against India, draws directly on that constitutional vocabulary. The Act and the amendment are therefore best read as a pair. The amendment answered the question of constitutional permission. The Act answered the question of machinery.

The same amendment reached beyond Article 19 and into the oaths by which public life is conducted. It amended Articles 84 and 173, which deal with the qualifications and oaths of members of Parliament and of State Legislatures, and it amended the forms of oath and affirmation in the Third Schedule to the Constitution. Those forms cover candidates for election, ministers, members of Parliament and of State Legislatures, Judges of the Supreme Court and of the High Courts, and the Comptroller and Auditor General of India. After the amendment, the person taking the oath swears or affirms to uphold the sovereignty and integrity of India. The change placed the unity of the country inside the words spoken at the threshold of public office, and made advocacy of secession difficult to reconcile with the oath that a candidate for a legislature must take. The Committee on National Integration and Regionalism had recommended oath reform alongside the amendment of Article 19, and Parliament enacted both together, treating the restriction of certain freedoms and the redrafting of public oaths as two parts of a single settlement about the terms on which the Union would be held together.

The background to that settlement was advocacy of secession, and in particular the demand for a separate Dravida Nadu that had been associated with the DMK in that era. The fact sheet of the period is one of a political movement advancing a claim to separate statehood for a region of the Union, and of a Union Government and a National Integration Council concerned that such advocacy, if left constitutionally untouched, could not be restricted even where it was directed at the dismemberment of the country. The party that had carried the demand later dropped secession, and that later change is part of the record as well. The amendment was not directed at a single act of violence, nor framed as an emergency measure. It was directed at a form of political claim, the claim that a part of India should cease to be part of India, and it proceeded by adjusting the permanent text of the Constitution so that ordinary legislation could address that claim. Supporters of the amendment presented it as a necessary protection for the integrity of the Union at a time of regional and linguistic pressure. Critics of restrictions of that kind have argued, in debates over the amendment and over the legislation that followed it, that advocacy and association are themselves protected freedoms, and that a ground as broad as the sovereignty and integrity of India can be used to restrict political speech that stops well short of violence. Both arguments were present at the creation of the constitutional space, and both have followed the Act through its later life, although the subject matter changed from secessionist advocacy to terrorism.

Into that prepared space Parliament enacted the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967, which is Act 37 of 1967 and which bears the date 30 December 1967. Its long title, in its original form, described a law to provide for the more effective prevention of certain unlawful activities of individuals and associations. Nothing in that original description spoke of terrorism, and nothing in Chapters I to III, which formed the working core of the early Act, punished a terrorist act as such. Chapter I contained the preliminary provisions and the definitions. Chapter II, sections 3 to 9, contained the machinery for declaring associations unlawful, for referring the declaration to a Tribunal, for the period for which a confirmed declaration would remain in force, and for orders about the moneys and places of a declared association. Chapter III, sections 10 to 14, contained offences and penalties connected with unlawful associations and unlawful activities, including membership of a declared association and taking part in or inciting an unlawful activity. The whole design was aimed at the association as the unit of concern. It was not a design for investigating a bombing, tracing the source of funds for an attack, or trying the members of a terrorist gang, and for thirty-seven years Parliament did not ask it to be one.

## Three decades as an association law alone

From 30 December 1967 until 21 September 2004 the UAPA operated as an association law alone, and that long first life is the strongest evidence against reading the statute backwards from its later chapters. During those decades there was no Chapter IV punishing terrorist activities, no First Schedule of terrorist organisations, and no modified bail regime under section 43D. The work of the Act was done in the Gazette and before the Tribunal, and its most consequential act was not an arrest but a notification. The Central Government would declare an association unlawful by notification specifying the grounds, relying on the definition of unlawful association in section 2(1)(p), which covers an association whose object is an unlawful activity, which encourages or aids such activity, whose members undertake it, or whose object or activity is punishable under sections 153A or 153B of the Indian Penal Code.

The testing procedure and its timetable still govern declarations under the first half of the Act. Under section 3, the notification has no effect until the Tribunal confirms it under section 4 and the order is published, unless the Central Government records reasons in writing that circumstances make immediate effect necessary, in which case the notification takes effect from publication but remains subject to confirmation. Under section 4, the Central Government must refer the notification to the Tribunal within thirty days of its publication. The Tribunal calls upon the association to show cause within thirty days of service of the notice, and it must pass an order confirming or cancelling the notification within six months from the date on which the notification was issued. Under section 5, the Tribunal consists of one person appointed by the Central Government, who must be a Judge of a High Court, and it holds civil court powers to summon witnesses, require discovery, receive evidence on affidavits, requisition public records and issue commissions. Its proceedings are judicial proceedings. Under section 6, a confirmed notification remained in force for two years during most of that early period, a period extended to five years only by Act 3 of 2013 with effect from 1 February 2013. Around that central procedure sat supporting powers, including the power under section 7 to prohibit dealings with the moneys, securities or credits of the association, with an application to the District Judge available within fifteen days, and the power under section 8 to notify places used by the association, with a District Judge route within thirty days. The pattern throughout was declaration first, judicial confirmation second, and criminal liability for membership, funding, meetings and assistance built on the foundation of a confirmed declaration.

That pattern produced a jurisprudence of its own, centred on the independence of the Tribunal rather than on the trial of terrorist offences. In Jamaat-e-Islami Hind v Union of India, decided by the Supreme Court in 1994, the Court set aside an order by which a Tribunal had confirmed a declaration, and held that the Tribunal must form its own opinion after assessing the credibility of the material placed before it. The Tribunal, on that holding, is not a mere imprimatur of the opinion of the Government. Natural justice applies even where some material is withheld on grounds of public interest, as the proviso to section 3(2) permits where disclosure would be against the public interest. The Central Government, defending declarations in such proceedings, has relied on the sensitivity of the material and on that statutory permission to withhold facts. Associations challenging declarations, and critics of the procedure, have answered that a confirmation process loses much of its protective value if the material that decides it cannot be tested by the party it condemns. The Supreme Court gave the critics of a passive Tribunal their strongest authority, while leaving the withholding provision itself in place.

The first life of the Act ended not because the association machinery failed or was repealed, but because a separate statute was repealed and a gap opened elsewhere. The Prevention of Terrorism Act, which had supplied a terrorism code since 2002, was repealed by the Prevention of Terrorism (Repeal) Act, 2004. That repeal created the legislative vacuum to which the 2004 amendment of the UAPA was the response. Parliament chose to place terrorism provisions inside a permanent statute that already existed, rather than enact another special and temporary anti-terror statute or leave terrorism to the ordinary penal law. The association law of 1967 was about to acquire a second and very different life.

## The graft of 2004

The amendment that changed the character of the Act was Act 29 of 2004. By that Act, Chapter IV was substituted with effect from 21 September 2004, sections 10 to 14 were reshaped, and the machinery for listing terrorist organisations in the First Schedule was introduced. The date was not incidental. It was the same date on which the repeal of the Prevention of Terrorism Act took effect, so that one terrorism regime ended and another began without an interval between them. The long title of the UAPA was extended at the same time to include dealing with terrorist activities, an acknowledgement in the statute's own opening words that its purpose had widened beyond the prevention of unlawful activities by individuals and associations.

### When did the UAPA become a terrorism law?

The UAPA became a terrorism law on 21 September 2004, when Act 29 of 2004 substituted Chapter IV with effect from that date, the same date on which the Prevention of Terrorism Act was repealed, grafting punishment for terrorist activities onto a statute that had operated since 1967 as an association law alone.

The detail of that substitution, the later substitutions and insertions of 2008, the changes of 2013 and the individual designation introduced in 2019 belong to the amendment history of the Act, which traces each change in sequence and in depth. What matters for the reading of the Act as a whole is the nature of the join. Nothing in Act 29 of 2004 repealed Chapters II and III or displaced the Tribunal procedure. The old machinery continued to operate alongside the new one, and it continues to be used for declarations against associations, while Chapter IV and the chapters that followed it operate on individuals, terrorist gangs and terrorist organisations through the ordinary courts under modified procedure. A single statute therefore contains a procedure in which executive action generally requires confirmation by a High Court Judge sitting as a Tribunal before it takes lasting effect, and a procedure in which an accused person may be held for up to one hundred and eighty days before a charge sheet and must surmount a prima facie bar before bail can be granted. Holding those two facts together, without allowing either to be mistaken for the other, is the discipline that the rest of any serious account of the UAPA has to observe.

## The two halves of the UAPA

Every provision in the Act belongs to one half or the other, and the halves do not share machinery. The table below sets the two regimes side by side on the points that decide a real case: what sets the regime in motion, who decides, what check stands between the decision and its effect, how long the clocks run, what offence the regime creates, what punishment follows, and where a challenge lies.

| Question | The 1967 half: unlawful associations | The terrorism half: terrorist acts, organisations and individuals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What triggers the regime? | A Central Government notification under Section 3 declaring an association unlawful, on the Section 2(1)(o) definition of unlawful activity | A terrorist act under Section 15, or a notification under Section 35 listing an organisation in the First Schedule or an individual in the Fourth Schedule |
| Who decides? | The Central Government declares; a Tribunal presided over by a sitting High Court judge must confirm | The Central Government lists and designates; courts decide guilt on prosecution evidence at trial |
| What is the safeguard? | Prior judicial confirmation: the notification has no effect until the Tribunal confirms it, after show cause to the association | No confirmation stage for listing or designation; post-decision application under Section 36 and Review Committee review under Section 37, plus sanction for prosecution under Section 45 |
| What are the time limits? | Referral to the Tribunal within 30 days, show cause within 30 days of service, Tribunal order within 6 months, ban in force for 5 years | Custody and charge sheet clock under Section 43D(2): 30 days of police custody, 90 days to the charge sheet, extendable to 180 days |
| What offence is created? | Membership and related conduct under Section 10; participation in unlawful activity under Section 13 | The terrorist act itself under Section 15; membership, support and fund raising for a listed organisation under Sections 38 to 40 |

## The first half: declaring an association unlawful

The original architecture of the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967 rests on two definitions and one executive act. The definitions identify the activity Parliament treated as unlawful and the association that could be brought within the Act because of that activity. The executive act is the declaration by the Central Government that a particular association is unlawful. The text proceeds in stages. It first describes action, then connects an association to that action, and only then empowers the Centre to declare the association unlawful by notification. Each stage carries its own language, and the language matters because the work of the Tribunal and the offences in Chapter III depend upon a declaration made in respect of an association that answers the statutory description. Read without that sequence, the power can look wider and simpler than the statute makes it.

Section 2(1)(o) defines unlawful activity by reference to action, and action is not confined to a physical act. It includes an act, words spoken or written, signs and visible representation. That starting point explains why argument about the provision turns on speech and expression as well as organised conduct. A speech, a pamphlet, a placard or a symbolic display may be considered under the same definition as an overt deed, provided that one of its limbs is satisfied. The definition does not treat every controversial statement as unlawful activity merely because it is forceful. It requires the action to answer one of the specified descriptions, directed to cession, secession, sovereignty, territorial integrity or disaffection against India. The distinction between action hostile to India in one of those specified senses and action that is merely critical, however sharply, is therefore located inside the text itself, even if disputes arise about where particular words or a particular campaign fall.

The first limb covers action that is intended to bring about the cession of a part of the territory of India or the secession of a part of that territory from the Union, action that supports a claim to bring about such cession or secession, and action that incites it. Cession and secession are not identical, and the statute names both. Cession speaks of territory being given up or transferred away from the Union. Secession speaks of a part separating itself from the Union. The verbs also do distinct work. Intention looks to purpose. Supporting a claim looks to endorsement or assistance given to an asserted entitlement to transfer or separate territory. Incitement looks outward to the effect sought upon others. A person may therefore fall within the limb without personally attempting a separation, if the action supports the claim or incites the result, but the limb still requires that territorial result to be the object of the intention, support or incitement.

Critics point to the inclusion of words, signs and visible representation, together with support and incitement, and argue that the first limb can reach advocacy rather than completed territorial change. Defenders of the scheme answer that the limb is anchored to a concrete constitutional concern, the sovereignty and integrity of India, and that it does not prohibit disagreement at large. On its own terms, the limb requires a connection to cession or secession of part of the territory of India. It is not a general prohibition on discussing federal arrangements, regional grievances, language, culture or the distribution of powers within the Union. Such subjects become relevant only if the action is intended to bring about the specified territorial result, supports a claim to bring it about, or incites it. The contested ground is the application of those verbs to particular facts, especially where the government says that speech supports a separatist claim and the speaker says it is political argument within the Union.

The second limb covers action that disclaims, questions, disrupts or is intended to disrupt the sovereignty and territorial integrity of India. Disclaiming and questioning can be done by assertion and argument. To disclaim sovereignty is to deny or repudiate it, and to question it is to call it into doubt. Disruption is different in character because it points to interference in practice, and the limb also covers action intended to disrupt even before disruption is achieved. Sovereignty concerns the authority and independence of India as a state. Territorial integrity concerns the wholeness of the territory over which that authority extends. The limb is framed in the alternative, so the government does not have to show that every verb applies to the same act. Critics argue that this is the most open textured part of the definition, because questioning sovereignty can be expressed through words alone and because the boundary between questioning a constitutional arrangement and questioning sovereignty itself may be disputed. The strongest answer from the text is that the object remains sovereignty and territorial integrity, not the reputation of a government, the wisdom of a policy or the popularity of a ruling party. Criticism of a ministry, a boundary policy or the treatment of a region does not, without more, disclaim or disrupt the sovereignty and territorial integrity of India. At the same time, the text does not require completed disruption where intention to disrupt is proved, and it does not exempt spoken or written words.

The third limb covers action that causes disaffection against India or is intended to cause disaffection against India. Disaffection describes alienation or hostility in feeling, and the limb is concerned with action that produces that state or seeks to produce it. Unlike the first limb, it names no territorial outcome. Unlike the second, it does not name sovereignty or territorial integrity as the direct object. Its object is India itself. Where actual causation is alleged, the action must cause the disaffection. Where intention is alleged, the design of the action becomes central, and proof that every listener or reader was alienated is not the statutory test the words suggest. Critics contend that hostility in feeling is difficult to measure and that governments may be tempted to treat vehement opposition to state policy as disaffection against India. Supporters respond that India and the government of the day are different objects, and that the text says disaffection against India, not disaffection against a ministry, a party or an official. That distinction is the principal restraint supplied by the words themselves. Even so, the limb plainly reaches expressive action and intended effects as well as completed effects. A careful reading neither shrinks it to physical violence nor expands it to every form of dissent. It leaves a contest in each case about the object of the action, the intention behind it and the effect alleged.

Section 2(1)(p) defines unlawful association. An association may answer that description where its object is an unlawful activity, where it encourages or aids the doing of an unlawful activity, or where its members undertake such activity. The definition also covers an association whose object or activity is punishable under sections 153A or 153B of the Indian Penal Code. The structure moves from individual action to collective identity, and it offers separate routes. Encouragement and aid are distinct from object, and the conduct of members undertaking unlawful activity is another route. An association is not made unlawful merely because it is unpopular or critical. The statutory routes require object, encouragement, aid or member conduct within the terms stated, or the specified Penal Code limb. The difference between an association whose object is unlawful activity and an association whose members undertake such activity can matter in evidence, because the first route directs attention to the purposes for which the association exists, while the member route directs attention to what members do. The Penal Code limb does not depend upon first proving unlawful activity under section 2(1)(o). It incorporates sections 153A and 153B by reference rather than restating their ingredients, and it shows that the 1967 scheme was concerned with organised activity that Parliament had already selected for punishment under specified provisions of the general criminal law, as well as with the activity defined in the UAPA itself.

Once the Centre is satisfied that an association is an unlawful association, section 3 supplies the formal act. The Central Government may, by notification in the Official Gazette specifying the grounds, declare the association to be unlawful. The requirement to specify grounds forces the executive to state in the instrument why the declaration is issued, and it creates the record against which the Tribunal later performs its function and against which the association shows cause. The discipline has an express limit. Under the proviso to section 3(2), facts may be withheld where disclosure is against the public interest. The statute thus distinguishes between grounds, which must be specified, and facts, some of which may be withheld. The government can say that the association is told why it has been declared unlawful even if particular underlying facts are not disclosed. The association can answer that grounds stripped of the facts needed to test them may leave it unable to meet the case in substance. Both arguments arise from the same arrangement, and neither feature cancels the other.

### Does a ban under the UAPA take effect immediately?

Ordinarily, no. A Section 3 notification has no effect until the Tribunal confirms it and that confirmation is published. The exception is narrow in form: where the Centre records reasons in writing that circumstances make immediate effect necessary, the notification operates from publication, subject to later Tribunal confirmation.

The general rule in section 3(3) is therefore that the notification has no effect until the Tribunal confirms it under section 4 and the confirmation is published. In the ordinary course the declaration is a step towards a ban rather than a completed ban, and the Tribunal decides whether it becomes effective. The exception is the immediate effect proviso. If the Central Government records reasons in writing that circumstances make it necessary, the notification takes effect from publication in the Official Gazette, subject to confirmation under section 4. The reasons requirement separates a preference for speed from a judgment that circumstances make immediate effect necessary. Defenders of the scheme emphasise sequence and safeguard, pointing to the definitions, the Gazette notification, the stated grounds, the ordinary need for confirmation and the written reasons required for immediate effect. Critics emphasise the width of the opening definitions, the capacity of words alone to constitute action, the place of intention where no result has occurred and the withholding of facts on public interest grounds. Both readings begin from real features of the text. The statute is neither a bare power to proscribe any organisation the executive dislikes nor a measure confined to completed acts of territorial separation. It is a structured power built around sovereignty, territorial integrity and disaffection against India, expressed in language broad enough to require the Tribunal stage to do serious work.

## The Tribunal: a judge between the government and the ban

Section 4 places a Tribunal between the Central Government and the completed declaration. The Centre must refer the notification to the Tribunal within thirty days of its publication. The Tribunal calls upon the association to show cause within thirty days of service of the notice why it should not be declared unlawful, and the Tribunal must pass an order confirming or cancelling the notification within six months from the date of issue of the section 3 notification. The timetable gives each actor a distinct part. The Centre controls the referral and must act within thirty days. The association controls its response and must show cause within thirty days of service. The Tribunal controls the decision and must complete it within six months. Those periods are short when measured against the consequence of confirmation, since a confirmed declaration remains in force for five years. For the government, speed is part of the point, because an association believed to threaten sovereignty and territorial integrity may continue the activity that prompted the notification while a slower process unfolds. For the association, speed compresses the time available to obtain instructions, gather material and answer allegations that may be stated in grounds while some supporting facts remain undisclosed. The statute resolves that tension in favour of a prompt decision, but it does not remove the need for a real decision. The order must confirm or cancel, and a Tribunal that merely repeated the executive conclusion would not perform the function section 4 assigns.

Section 5 determines who performs that function. The Tribunal consists of one person appointed by the Central Government, and that person must be a Judge of a High Court. The requirement is not satisfied by a retired official, a police officer, a legal adviser or a committee of administrators. For discharging its functions, the Tribunal has the powers of a civil court in summoning witnesses, discovery, receiving evidence on affidavits, requisitioning public records and issuing commissions. Its proceedings are judicial proceedings. The Tribunal is not a court trying an offence and it does not impose the punishments in Chapter III, yet its confirmation is the act that ordinarily makes the declaration effective and sustains it for five years. Parliament chose to give that act to a High Court Judge equipped with civil court powers and conducting judicial proceedings, rather than leave the executive declaration to stand on executive authority alone. That choice is the institutional centre of the first half of the Act. It does not answer every objection to the breadth of the definitions or the withholding of facts, but it identifies the person who must be persuaded, the form of proceeding in which persuasion is to occur and the powers available for obtaining and testing material.

Section 6 fixes duration and the possibility of earlier release. A confirmed notification remains in force for five years from the date on which the notification became effective. The period was two years and was extended to five years by Act 3 of 2013, with effect from 1 February 2013. The change altered the practical weight of every confirmation. Under a two year period the executive would need to return sooner if it wished the declaration to continue. Under a five year period a single confirmation carries the association through a much longer stretch, and the offences attached to membership and assistance retain their setting for the whole period. The Central Government may cancel a confirmed notification at any time, either of its own motion or on application. Cancellation is therefore a separate executive route that does not depend upon waiting for five years to expire. Its existence does not reduce the importance of the Tribunal decision at the outset, because the confirmed ban is the legal position unless cancellation occurs or the period runs out. Duration also explains why the quality of the Tribunal inquiry matters so much. An error or a weakness in scrutiny at confirmation does not govern a brief interval. It governs the status of the association, and the exposure of its members and supporters, for five years.

The leading judicial statement in the fact record is Jamaat-e-Islami Hind v Union of India, decided by the Supreme Court in 1994. The Court set aside a Tribunal confirmation order, holding that the Tribunal must form its own opinion after assessing the credibility of the material before it. The Tribunal is not a mere imprimatur of the opinion formed by the government. That holding captures the constitutional anxiety at the centre of the scheme. If confirmation were a formal stamp placed upon an executive decision already treated as complete, the interposition of a High Court Judge would add ceremony without adding judgment. The Supreme Court required judgment. The Tribunal must look at the material, assess whether it can be relied upon and reach its own conclusion on whether sufficient cause exists for the declaration. The decision also recognised that natural justice applies even where some material is withheld on public interest grounds. Withholding may be permitted by the statute, but it does not convert the proceeding into one in which fairness has no purchase. A Tribunal order that recites the government case and confirms without showing an independent assessment of credibility would fall short of that standard. An order that examines the material, explains the weight it accepts and reaches its own conclusion stands on stronger ground. The case does not hold that every item must be disclosed regardless of public interest. It holds that non-disclosure does not relieve the Tribunal of the duty to think independently, and that the less the association can see, the more significant the Tribunal scrutiny becomes, because the Judge may be the only person outside the executive who tests the credibility of the material.

The declaration concerning the Popular Front of India illustrates the scheme in operation. In September 2022 the Centre declared the Popular Front of India and eight associates or fronts unlawful associations with immediate effect for five years. The use of immediate effect shows the proviso to section 3(3) at work, because the ban did not wait for confirmation before it began to operate. A Tribunal presided over by Justice Dinesh Kumar Sharma of the Delhi High Court confirmed the ban in March 2023. The example brings together features that might otherwise remain abstract. The declaration was made by notification, it took effect immediately rather than after confirmation, it was expressed for five years in line with section 6, and it was then subjected to the Tribunal process. Supporters of the process can point to that sequence as evidence that immediate executive action remains subject to confirmation by a High Court Judge. Critics can point to the same sequence and observe that the association carried the practical burden of an operating ban before confirmation was given. Both observations describe different aspects of a design that authorises the sequence, and neither can be understood if immediate effect and later confirmation are treated as alternatives rather than as successive stages in the same case.

The sharpest continuing objection concerns sealed cover material. Critics object that material may be examined by the Tribunal without disclosure to the association, leaving the association to answer grounds without seeing all the factual support said to justify them. In the 2019 confirmation of the ban on Jamaat-e-Islami (Jammu and Kashmir), the Tribunal examined material in sealed cover that was not disclosed to the association. For critics, that example states the problem in concrete form. A party cannot effectively challenge the accuracy, context or credibility of material it has not seen. It cannot instruct representatives on documents hidden from it, cannot point to omissions in a file it cannot read and cannot show that an allegation rests on mistaken identity or an incomplete sequence of events. The objection gains force from Jamaat-e-Islami Hind, because that judgment insists on independent assessment and natural justice. If the association is substantially blindfolded, critics argue, the reality of an adversarial test is weakened, and the Tribunal must carry alone a burden that in ordinary judicial proceedings would be shared by challenge from the affected party. On that view, the Judge is asked not only to decide but also to supply, from within the proceeding, the scepticism that disclosure would have allowed the association to supply for itself.

The government case for non-disclosure, put in its strongest form, begins with the proviso that Parliament itself enacted. Facts may be withheld where disclosure is against the public interest, and the Tribunal system was designed with that possibility inside it. Disclosure can reveal sources, methods and the state of an investigation, expose persons who supplied information to risk, and alert persons connected with an association to the extent of official knowledge, allowing material to be destroyed, funds to be moved or activity to be reorganised. On this view the safeguard is not disclosure to the association in every instance but examination by a High Court Judge who is independent of the officials bringing the material and who must form a personal opinion on its credibility. The government also stresses the subject matter. The declaration power concerns sovereignty, territorial integrity and organised unlawful activity, fields in which the state claims a legitimate need to protect sensitive information while still submitting the declaration to a Judge. The dispute is therefore not between scrutiny and no scrutiny. It is between scrutiny by the Tribunal alone over undisclosed material and scrutiny assisted by disclosure to the association, with each side invoking a value recognised in the Act itself.

That dispute explains the uneasy position of the Tribunal in public argument. To the executive, the Judge is the answer to the charge of unchecked power, because no ordinary declaration takes effect without confirmation and no immediate declaration escapes the need for confirmation. To critics, the Judge can become the point at which executive material is insulated from challenge, especially when the association sees the grounds but not the facts and when confirmation sustains a five year ban. The statute gives material support to each concern. It appoints a High Court Judge, clothes the Tribunal with civil court powers, calls its proceedings judicial proceedings, fixes a six month decision period and empowers cancellation as well as confirmation. It also permits immediate effect on recorded reasons, permits facts to be withheld on public interest grounds and gives a confirmed notification a five year life. Jamaat-e-Islami Hind mediates between those features by insisting that the Judge must judge. The case does not dissolve the tension, but it identifies the minimum that makes the Tribunal a real institution rather than a route by which an executive opinion acquires a judicial label.

## What a confirmed ban does: funds, places and offences

Confirmation changes the legal environment around the association in ways that reach money, premises and personal conduct. Chapter II gives the Centre power to restrict dealings with the funds of the unlawful association and to act in relation to places used by it, while Chapter III creates offences that attach to membership, assistance, finance, use of notified places and participation in unlawful activity. The sequence is deliberate. First the association is declared unlawful and the declaration is confirmed or takes effect under the immediate effect route. Then funds and places can be controlled under sections 7 and 8, according to the procedure in section 9. Separately, individuals can be prosecuted for conduct described in sections 10 to 13, with section 14 making the offences cognizable. A confirmed ban therefore operates administratively, through orders about funds and places, and criminally, through offences that may be committed by members, supporters and others who deal with the association after the declaration.

### What happens to the funds of an association declared unlawful?

The Centre may prohibit any person from dealing with moneys, securities or credits belonging to or intended for the unlawful association. A person claiming an interest in what is caught by such an order may apply to the District Judge within fifteen days for relief.

Section 7 is the provision behind that answer. Where an association has been declared unlawful, the Central Government may prohibit any person from dealing with moneys, securities or credits belonging to or intended for the unlawful association. The power is prohibitory rather than confiscatory in its immediate form. It stops dealing and prevents funds from being moved, applied or handled in the ordinary way by persons who hold or control them. The language covers assets already identified with the association and resources destined for it, so a bank balance, a security or a credit may be frozen in practical effect by an order directed to the person who holds or controls it. A person claiming an interest in the moneys, securities or credits may apply to the District Judge within fifteen days. That short period is part of the design, since funds can move quickly and Parliament provided a rapid judicial route rather than a leisurely civil suit. The route also places the burden on the claimant to come forward in time and establish the interest asserted. A third party who says that money caught by the order is not the association money, or that a particular interest should not be treated as belonging to or intended for the association, must use that route within the period allowed. Its existence answers in part the concern that a prohibitory order may sweep in assets in which other persons claim rights, although the speed required of the claimant remains demanding.

Section 8 deals with places used by the unlawful association, which may be notified under the section. The provision is concerned with physical settings in which the association carries on its work, meets, stores material or otherwise functions. A place may be an office, a house, a building or other premises, and notification marks it out because of its use by or on behalf of the unlawful association. A person claiming an interest in a notified place has a District Judge route within thirty days, twice the period allowed for funds under section 7. The difference is intelligible because claims to places may involve title, tenancy, possession and the rights of persons other than the association, questions that can require fuller inquiry than the immediate question whether a dealing with funds should be stopped. Section 9 supplies the procedure for sections 7 and 8, following the procedure of the Code of Civil Procedure for the matters covered, and decisions under that procedure are final. The division of labour is significant. The Tribunal decides whether the association should be declared unlawful. The District Judge deals with particular claims that arise when the consequences of the declaration touch funds or premises in which specific persons assert interests. Confirmation turns on the association, its object and its activities. A funds or place claim turns on the rights of an identified claimant in an identified asset or premises, and finality prevents those consequences from remaining indefinitely unsettled while the declaration is in force.

The offences in Chapter III begin with section 10, in the form substituted in 2004. Under section 10(a), a person who is a member of a declared unlawful association, takes part in its meetings, contributes to or receives or solicits contributions for it, or in any way assists its operations, is punishable with imprisonment that may extend to two years and is also liable to fine. The provision reaches more than formal membership. Meetings, contributions and assistance are separately named, so a person who never appears on a membership roll may still fall within the section by taking part in a meeting, handling contributions or assisting operations. The conduct is criminal because it is conduct in relation to an association declared unlawful under the Act, and section 10(a) gives the ban its sharpest personal edge for ordinary supporters. Section 10(b) contains a much graver aggravated limb. Where a member, or a person who aids or promotes the association, is in possession of unlicensed firearms, ammunition, explosives or other instruments capable of causing mass destruction and commits an act resulting in death, the punishment is death or imprisonment for life. In other cases within that limb, the punishment is imprisonment for not less than five years and up to imprisonment for life. The aggravated penalty is linked to possession of the specified dangerous articles in the setting of membership, aid or promotion. Section 10 thus contains two very different levels of wrongdoing under one number, associational conduct punishable up to two years and armed destructive conduct punishable at its highest with death.

Sections 11 and 12 punish contraventions of the controls already described. A person who deals with funds contrary to a prohibitory order under section 7 is punishable under section 11 with imprisonment that may extend to three years, or with fine, or with both. The offence gives criminal backing to the funds prohibition, so a custodian, intermediary or other person who deals in defiance of the order faces prosecution rather than merely civil consequences. Contravention in relation to a notified place under section 8 is punishable under section 12 with imprisonment that may extend to one year and liability to fine. The lower maximum reflects the different character of conduct concerning a place rather than the movement of association funds, but the principle is the same. Administrative control after a ban is supported by offences that punish disobedience to the specific controls imposed. Without sections 11 and 12, sections 7 and 8 would depend much more heavily on voluntary compliance by persons holding money or controlling premises at the moment a declaration takes effect.

Section 13 returns to unlawful activity itself and to assistance given to a declared association. Under section 13(1), a person who takes part in or commits an unlawful activity, or advocates, abets, advises or incites its commission, is punishable with imprisonment that may extend to seven years and is also liable to fine. The verbs track the breadth of section 2(1)(o), because participation, advocacy, abetment, advice and incitement can all be modes of involvement. Under section 13(2), assisting the unlawful activity of an association declared unlawful is punishable with imprisonment that may extend to five years, or with fine, or with both. The distinction is worth preserving. Section 13(1) is directed to unlawful activity and involvement in it, with a maximum of seven years. Section 13(2) is directed to assisting the unlawful activity of a declared association, with a maximum of five years. Section 13(3) contains an exception for the treaty negotiation context specified there, a reminder that the offence is not written to sweep in the public business described by that exception. Section 14 completes Chapter III by providing that all the offences are cognizable, which affects the powers of the police in relation to arrest and investigation under the general law of criminal procedure and removes doubt about their procedural character.

Taken together, the consequences of a confirmed ban are cumulative rather than alternative. The association loses the ability to operate as a lawful body. Its funds may be made the subject of prohibitory orders, subject to claims before the District Judge within fifteen days. Places used by it may be notified, subject to claims within thirty days. Members and supporters risk prosecution for membership, meetings, contributions and assistance under section 10(a), with the aggravated firearms limb in section 10(b) carrying death or life imprisonment where the specified act results in death. Persons who defy funds or place controls risk sections 11 and 12. Persons who take part in, advocate or assist unlawful activity risk the higher maxima in section 13, seven years for the activity itself and five years for assisting a declared association. That cumulative structure is the practical meaning of the first half of the Act, and it explains why the definitions in section 2, the grounds in section 3 and the independent judgment required of the Tribunal carry weight far beyond the Gazette notification with which the process begins.

## The second half: the terrorist act and its offences

The terrorist half of the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act 1967 begins with a definition, and everything else in Chapter IV follows from it. For its first thirty seven years the Act contained no terrorism chapter at all. Chapter IV, headed Punishment for Terrorist Activities and covering sections 15 to 23, was substituted by Act 29 of 2004 with effect from 21 September 2004, the long title was extended by the same amendment to add dealing with terrorist activities, and Act 35 of 2008 later inserted into the preamble a reference to United Nations Security Council Resolution 1373. The result is a second regime inside the same Act, distinct in object and in severity from the unlawful associations regime, yet sharing the preliminary provisions, the schedules and much of the procedural machinery.

Section 15 defines the terrorist act, and its width is built from separate limbs that operate together. At its head stands intention, expressed as an act done with intent to threaten or likely to threaten the unity, integrity, security or sovereignty of India, or with intent to strike terror in the people or in any section of the people. Security in this limb includes economic security, added by Act 3 of 2013. Unity, integrity and sovereignty speak to the cohesion of India as a Union, while the terror limb speaks to the effect on the public, and either form of intended harm suffices if the remaining elements are present. The means limb then lists bombs, dynamite, explosives, inflammable substances, firearms, lethal weapons, poisonous or noxious gases, chemicals, and biological, radioactive, nuclear or other hazardous substances, before opening out to any other means of whatever nature. The catalogue therefore does not operate as a closed code, and a prosecution does not fail on means merely because the instrument was not a bomb or a firearm.

The harm limbs state the consequences caused or likely to be caused. They include death or injury to any person, loss of, damage to or destruction of property, and disruption of any supplies or services essential to the life of the community. They include damage to the monetary stability of India by way of production, smuggling or circulation of high quality counterfeit Indian currency, the counterfeit currency limb tied to the 2013 amendment and defined further through the Third Schedule. They also include damage to defence property or to government property, whether in India or in a foreign country, used or intended to be used for the defence of India or in connection with the purposes of the Government of India, a State Government or any of their agencies. Because the section speaks of harm caused or likely to be caused, it is not confined to completed devastation. An act that fails in its object may still fall within Section 15 if a specified harm was a likely consequence, read with the intention with which the act was done.

Two further forms of conduct are brought expressly within Section 15, and they show how far it travels from an explosion or an armed attack. The first is overawing by means of criminal force, or attempting to overawe, any public functionary, which connects the terrorist act to coercion of those who carry out public functions. The second is detaining, kidnapping or abducting a person and keeping that person in detention in order to compel the Government of India, a State Government, a foreign government, an international or inter governmental organisation, or any other person, to do or abstain from doing any act. Detention used as leverage is treated in the same frame as violence, because the coercive purpose supplies the terrorist character. Section 15(2) adds the treaty limb. An act within the scope of any of the treaties listed in the Second Schedule is included within the meaning of terrorist act, linking the domestic definition directly to the nine international instruments in that Schedule.

Section 16 punishes the terrorist act itself. Where the act results in the death of any person, the punishment is death or imprisonment for life, with liability to fine. In any other case the punishment is imprisonment for not less than five years but which may extend to imprisonment for life, with liability to fine. No intermediate gradation based on the particular harm limb is written into Section 16. Gradation appears instead in the surrounding offences, which punish conduct that prepares for, finances, supports or conceals a terrorist act even where the person before the court did not carry out the act itself.

Section 17, in the form substituted by Act 3 of 2013, deals with raising funds for a terrorist act. It covers raising, providing or collecting funds, or attempting to do so, from legitimate or illegitimate sources, intending that the funds shall be used, or knowing that they are likely to be used, in full or in part to commit a terrorist act, whether or not the funds are in fact used for that purpose. The punishment is imprisonment for not less than five years but which may extend to imprisonment for life. Two features deserve emphasis. Actual use of the money is not an element the prosecution must establish, and the source of the money does not clean it, because legitimate and illegitimate sources are treated alike once intention or knowledge as to terrorist use is proved.

Section 18 addresses conspiracy and facilitation. It punishes conspiring or attempting to commit, or advocating, abetting, advising or inciting, or knowingly facilitating the commission of, a terrorist act or any act preparatory to the commission of a terrorist act, with imprisonment for not less than five years but which may extend to imprisonment for life. The verbs matter. Conspiracy looks to agreement, attempt to a step towards commission, advocacy and incitement to words and encouragement, abetment and advice to assistance by influence or direction, and knowing facilitation to practical help given with knowledge of its purpose. Preparatory acts are drawn in expressly, so the section reaches back to the planning stage. Sections 18A and 18B, inserted by Act 35 of 2008, particularise two forms of preparation. Section 18A punishes organising terrorist camps, and Section 18B punishes recruiting persons for a terrorist act, each with imprisonment for not less than five years but which may extend to imprisonment for life. Training and recruitment can therefore be prosecuted without forcing them into the general language of conspiracy or facilitation, and the organiser or recruiter need not have personally performed the terrorist act for which the training or recruitment was intended.

Section 19 turns to harbouring. It punishes harbouring or concealing a person knowing that the person is a terrorist, with imprisonment for not less than three years but which may extend to imprisonment for life. The section contains a spouse exception, so the offence does not apply where the harbouring or concealment is by the spouse of the terrorist. Knowledge is the stated mental element, and the lower minimum of three years, rather than five years, distinguishes harbouring from fund raising, conspiracy, training and recruitment, while the maximum of life imprisonment preserves a high ceiling. The exception is narrow and personal. It is not a general family exception, and it does not protect other forms of assistance that may fall under a different section.

Two definitions in Section 2 govern several offences that follow. Section 2(1)(l) defines terrorist gang as an association of persons or a group of persons, other than a terrorist organisation, concerned in or involved in a terrorist act. Section 2(1)(m) defines terrorist organisation as an organisation listed in the First Schedule or an organisation operating under the same name as an organisation so listed. Listing is therefore constitutive for the second definition, while involvement in a terrorist act is the test for the first. An unlisted group may be a terrorist gang, and a listed body is a terrorist organisation by reason of its First Schedule entry. The distinction prevents the terrorism provisions from depending entirely on prior executive listing, and it supports different offences in Chapter IV and Chapter VI.

Section 20 punishes being a member of a terrorist gang or a terrorist organisation which is involved in a terrorist act, with imprisonment which may extend to imprisonment for life. The qualifying phrase is involvement in a terrorist act, and Section 20 sits between the act based offences and the organisation based offences in sections 38 to 40, which deal with association, support and fund raising for a terrorist organisation as such. Section 21 punishes knowingly holding proceeds of terrorism, described as holding property derived or obtained from a terrorist act or acquired through terrorist funds, with imprisonment which may extend to imprisonment for life. Knowledge supplies the mental element, and the property need not be money alone. Chapter V then provides a separate machinery for seizure, attachment and forfeiture of such proceeds, operating alongside Section 21 rather than replacing it.

Section 22 punishes threatening a witness, with imprisonment which may extend to three years, together with liability to fine. Its place in Chapter IV is protective, because terrorism prosecutions depend on witnesses who may be exposed to pressure from the organisation or gang involved, and Section 22 supplies an offence calibrated to that pressure rather than to the terrorist act itself. Sections 22A to 22C, inserted by Act 3 of 2013, deal with offences by companies, societies and trusts. Their insertion filled a structural gap in a chapter framed principally around natural persons, in a field where funds, property and organisational activity may be held or carried on through a company, a society or a trust. They belong to the same 2013 package that introduced economic security into Section 15, substituted Section 17 and added the schedules in their renumbered sequence, and their function is to ensure that the form in which an entity is organised does not by itself place its conduct outside Chapter IV. Section 23 provides for enhanced penalties, the closing provision of the chapter. It does not create a new terrorist act or a new ancillary offence. It operates on a case that already falls within an offence and raises the punishment consequence in the circumstances to which the enhancement applies. Read as a whole, Chapter IV moves in sequence from definition in Section 15 and punishment in Section 16, through money, conspiracy, camps, recruitment, harbouring, membership, proceeds and witness threats, to entities in sections 22A to 22C and enhancement in Section 23. It is a code within the Act for the stages by which terrorism is prepared, financed, carried out, sustained and concealed.

## Property: seizure, attachment and forfeiture

Chapter V, covering sections 24 to 34 and headed Forfeiture of Proceeds of Terrorism, provides the property machinery of the Act. It operates alongside Section 21, and it asks a different question from a prosecution. A prosecution asks whether a person committed an offence and what punishment follows. Chapter V asks whether property is proceeds of terrorism and what should happen to that property, and several of its provisions are written so that the second question does not depend entirely on the answer to the first. The chapter moves through seizure and attachment at the investigation stage, an administrative check for seized cash, a show cause process before forfeiture, an appeal to the High Court, and attachment during trial leading to forfeiture on conviction in Section 33.

Section 25 confers the power of seizure and attachment. An investigating officer may seize or attach property reasonably suspected to be proceeds of terrorism, but only with the prior approval in writing of the Director General of Police. The prior approval requirement is a condition on the power, not a formality after the event, and it places a senior officer between the investigating officer and the interference with property. Where the National Investigation Agency investigates, the approval authority is different. Following the change made by Act 28 of 2019, approval in an Agency case is that of the Director General of the National Investigation Agency, rather than the State Director General of Police. That change aligns approval with the agency conducting the investigation, and it was made in the same 2019 amendment that introduced individual designation and Inspector rank investigation by Agency officers.

Cash receives specific and time bound treatment under Section 25. Cash seized must be released within forty eight hours unless the Designated Authority orders its retention. The forty eight hour rule is a safeguard written in hours rather than in the longer periods found elsewhere in the Act, reflecting the immediate hardship and ease of movement that attach to cash. Retention is not automatic on seizure. It requires an order of the Designated Authority, and an appeal against that order lies to the court within thirty days. That thirty day appeal should be distinguished from the one month appeal to the High Court under Section 28 against a forfeiture order, because the two appeals attack different orders, go to different forums and arise at different stages of the property process.

Section 26 provides that a court may order forfeiture of property under Chapter V whether or not the person concerned is prosecuted under Chapter IV or Chapter VI. The words whether or not are the heart of the provision. Forfeiture does not have to wait for a prosecution to be launched, and it is not defeated merely because no prosecution for a terrorist act or for a terrorist organisation offence follows. That independence gives Chapter V its character as a proceeds regime rather than as a mere sentencing consequence. At the same time, Section 26 does not dispense with a court order or with the show cause procedure in Section 27, and an administrative suspicion does not become a forfeiture without notice, hearing and an order open to appeal.

Section 27 supplies the show cause stage. Before forfeiture, notice is given calling on the person affected to show cause why the property should not be declared proceeds of terrorism and forfeited, with an opportunity of hearing. The person served may explain the source of the property and contest the claim that it represents proceeds of terrorism. Section 27 also protects a bona fide transferee, meaning a transferee for value who acted in good faith and without knowledge that the property was proceeds of terrorism. The protection matters because proceeds may pass through several hands before seizure, and a regime that ignored good faith acquisition for value would punish a purchaser who had no knowledge of the terrorist source. Its limits are part of the protection. Value, good faith and absence of knowledge are the qualifying ideas, and a transferee who gave no value or who knew of the terrorist origin does not fall within the description.

Section 28 provides the appeal and the consequence of success. A person aggrieved by a forfeiture order may appeal to the High Court within one month. The appeal lies to the High Court, not to the court that hears the narrower appeal concerning the Designated Authority and seized cash under Section 25, and the difference in forum reflects the difference between an interim retention question and a final forfeiture. Where the forfeiture order is modified or annulled, or where the person is acquitted, the property must be returned, or its price with interest paid where return of the property itself is not possible, in the terms recorded for the chapter. Return on acquittal connects the property track back to the criminal track at the point of outcome. A forfeiture may be ordered without a prosecution under Section 26, but acquittal, or modification or annulment of the order itself, carries an express restitutionary consequence.

Section 33 deals with property in the setting of a trial. It provides for court ordered attachment of property during trial and for forfeiture on conviction. Attachment during trial is preservative in purpose, preventing disposal of property that may be liable to forfeiture if the trial ends in conviction, while the trial remains undecided. Forfeiture on conviction is consequential, following a finding of guilt and accompanying the sentence. Section 33 should not be merged with Section 25 or Section 26 in description. Section 25 is an investigating officer power exercised with prior senior approval and, for cash, subject to the forty eight hour rule. Section 26 is a court forfeiture power that may operate whether or not a prosecution follows. Section 33 is a trial court power that begins with attachment while a case is pending and ends, where conviction follows, with forfeiture. Together they cover investigation, independent proceeds proceedings and trial, disciplined by notice and hearing in Section 27, bona fide transferee protection, and the High Court appeal in Section 28.

## Listing terrorist organisations and the four Schedules

Chapter VI, covering sections 35 to 40 and headed Terrorist Organisations, contains the listing machinery and the offences that attach to a listed organisation. It is distinct from Chapter II, which declares an association unlawful, subject to confirmation by a Tribunal, for five years. Listing under Chapter VI places an organisation in the First Schedule as a terrorist organisation within Section 2(1)(m), and it triggers the offences in sections 38, 39 and 40. The two regimes may overlap in fact, because a body may be both a declared unlawful association and a listed terrorist organisation, but the Act keeps their procedures, tests and consequences in separate chapters.

Section 35 confers the listing power on the Central Government, which may by notification in the Official Gazette add an organisation to the First Schedule, remove an organisation from it or amend an entry, including organisations listed in the manner reflected in entries derived from United Nations listing. The power is not at large. It may be exercised only if the Central Government believes that the organisation is involved in terrorism, and involvement is explained by four alternatives. The organisation commits or participates in acts of terrorism, or it prepares for terrorism, or it promotes or encourages terrorism, or it is otherwise involved in terrorism. Any one form suffices, and the fourth phrase prevents the first three from being read as exhaustive. The standard is belief by the Central Government, a different standard from proof in a criminal trial of a terrorist act under Section 15. Listing is an executive act with criminal consequences through the offences that follow, which is why sections 36 and 37 on denotification and review are an essential part of the chapter. Section 35(4) also empowers the Centre to amend the Second Schedule or the Third Schedule by notification, and notifications under the chapter are laid before Parliament.

Section 36 provides the application for removal, or denotification. A listed organisation, or any person affected by the listing, may apply to the Central Government for removal of the entry. The Centre considers the application and may reject it. If the application is rejected, review by the Review Committee under Section 37 is available, to be sought within one month of receipt of the refusal in the timeline recorded for the chapter. The sequence is therefore application to the government that made the listing, followed on rejection by review by a statutory committee, rather than a fresh listing hearing before a tribunal of the kind used in Chapter II.

Section 37 constitutes the Review Committee and defines its method. Its chairperson must be a person who is or has been a Judge of a High Court, and where a sitting judge is chairperson, the concurrence of the Chief Justice of that High Court is required. The Committee has the chairperson and not more than three other members. It tests the rejection on judicial review principles, examining the legality of the decision rather than conducting a fresh merits adjudication of whether it would itself have listed the organisation. If the Committee allows the review, the Central Government must remove the entry from the First Schedule. Judicial headship and a judicial review method are both part of the design. The first gives the Committee a chairperson drawn from the higher judiciary, and the second defines and limits the scrutiny the Committee performs.

Section 38 punishes associating or professing to be associated with a terrorist organisation, with intent to further its activities, with imprisonment which may extend to ten years, or fine, or both. Association and professed association are both covered, and intent to further the activities of the organisation is an element. The section contains a statutory defence with two conditions. The person must show that the organisation was not listed in the First Schedule when he joined it or began to profess association with it, and that he took no part in its activities at any time while it was listed. Both conditions must be present. Joining before listing does not protect participation after listing, and non participation after listing does not cure a joinder that took place after listing. The defence protects a person who joined before listing and stood apart from activity throughout the listed period, as a matter of statutory text rather than prosecutorial discretion.

Section 39 punishes support for a terrorist organisation, with intent to further its activity, with imprisonment which may extend to ten years, or fine, or both. It covers inviting support for the organisation, where the support is other than money or property, arranging or managing a meeting to support the organisation or further its activity, or a meeting addressed by a person associated with it, assisting in arranging or managing such a meeting, and addressing such a meeting to encourage support or further the activity. Section 40 punishes raising funds for a terrorist organisation, covering inviting, receiving or providing money or other property intending that it be used, or suspecting that it may be used, for terrorist purposes, with imprisonment which may extend to fourteen years, or fine, or both. The distinction between the sections is the nature of the involvement. Section 38 covers association, Section 39 covers support and meetings, and Section 40 covers money and property. Section 40 should also be distinguished from Section 17. Section 17 punishes raising funds for a terrorist act, from five years to life. Section 40 punishes raising funds for a terrorist organisation, up to fourteen years. The object of the funds, act or organisation, determines which provision applies.

The First Schedule is the list of terrorist organisations for Section 2(1)(m) and Section 35. Its entries include Babbar Khalsa International, Lashkar-e-Taiba, Jaish-e-Mohammed, Hizb-ul-Mujahideen, ULFA, LTTE, and the Students Islamic Movement of India, together with organisations listed in the Schedule to the United Nations Prevention and Suppression of Terrorism Order, 2007. No total count is stated here, because the Schedule is amended by notification and a count would present a changing list as a fixed feature. The significance of the First Schedule lies not in its length but in its consequences, through the definition in Section 2(1)(m), through Section 20 where an organisation is involved in a terrorist act, and through sections 38 to 40.

The Second Schedule collects the treaties for Section 15(2). It lists nine instruments, from the 1970 Hague convention on unlawful seizure of aircraft to the 1997 Terrorist Bombings Convention, covering in subject matter civil aviation, hostages, nuclear material and maritime navigation, among the fields represented in the nine instruments. An act within the scope of any of those treaties is included in the definition of terrorist act, so the Schedule is an operative part of the domestic offence, incorporating treaty defined conduct by reference, and amendment by notification under Section 35(4) provides the mechanism for updating it. The Third Schedule specifies the three security features that define high quality counterfeit Indian currency, namely the watermark, the latent image and see through registration. Only counterfeit currency bearing that quality falls within the description the Schedule supports, and the Schedule therefore particularises the monetary stability limb of Section 15. Economic security in Section 15, damage to monetary stability by high quality counterfeit currency, and the Third Schedule features belong to the same 2013 package and should be read together. The Fourth Schedule lists individuals designated as terrorists, added by the 2019 amendment. Its designation story, including the standard applied and the post decision review route, is told later in this article, and it is recorded here by pointer only to complete the scheme. The First Schedule lists organisations, the Second lists treaties, the Third lists security features of currency, and the Fourth lists designated individuals, each answering to a different provision and a different legal consequence.

## Investigation and the long road to a charge sheet

Investigation under the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act 1967 begins with a rank floor that Parliament placed in Section 43, and that floor shapes everything that follows. Offences under Chapter IV, which deals with punishment for terrorist activities, and Chapter VI, which deals with terrorist organisations, may be investigated only by officers who are not below a stated seniority. In the Delhi Special Police Establishment, the investigating officer must not be below the rank of Deputy Superintendent of Police. In a metropolitan area, the officer must be an Assistant Commissioner of Police. In every other case, the officer must be a Deputy Superintendent of Police or an officer of equivalent rank. Parliament did not leave these offences to investigation at any level of the police hierarchy. It reserved them to officers of gazetted seniority, officers who carry supervisory responsibility within their own forces. The rank floor interposes senior judgement between an allegation of exceptional gravity and the coercive steps that follow arrest, search and seizure, and it marks the offences in Chapters IV and VI as a class apart from ordinary crime.

The 2019 amendment altered that floor for one investigating body without removing it for others. Officers of the National Investigation Agency who hold the rank of Inspector and above may also investigate offences under Chapters IV and VI. The change sits alongside the general rule rather than replacing it, so the position after 2019 is a divided one. A state police investigation, or an investigation by the Delhi Special Police Establishment, remains subject to the higher rank floor that Section 43 prescribes. An Agency investigation may be carried by an officer of Inspector rank. Supporters of the change within government presented it, in substance, as a matter of capacity, because a specialised agency dependent on a small cadre of senior officers will struggle across the volume and geography of the cases referred to it. Critics answered, in substance, that the change weakened the safeguard that the rank floor was meant to provide, and did so for the body that handles the gravest cases. Section 43, as amended, permits both routes, and the seniority an accused person encounters depends on which force holds the investigation. Where the Centre directs an investigation by the Agency under [the National Investigation Agency Act 2008](/2011/06/15/nia-act-2008-explained/), Agency officers carry the investigation within that statutory direction, and the takeover follows the direction of the Centre rather than the choice of the state police.

Arrest and search, once an investigation is under way, are governed by sections 43A to 43F, a group of provisions inserted by the amendment of 2008, and sections 43A to 43C deal with the first moments of coercion. Under Section 43A, an officer of the Designated Authority who knows, or who has received written information, that an offence under the Act has been committed, or that illegally acquired property is connected with such an offence, may exercise powers of arrest and search. The power may be exercised by day or by night, and Section 43A states it in direct terms, tied to knowledge or to written information received by the officer. Section 43B then imposes two duties that follow every arrest made under the Act. The arrested person must be informed of the grounds of arrest, and the arrested person must be forwarded without delay to the nearest police station. The first duty gives the arrest a stated reason that can later be tested before a court. The second duty moves the person into the ordinary station process, where production before a magistrate and the recording of custody can take their course. Section 43C completes the opening scheme by providing that the Code applies to arrest, search and seizure under the Act wherever the Code is not inconsistent with the provisions of the Act. Where the Act is silent, the Code supplies the procedure. Where the Act speaks differently, the Act prevails.

The centre of the procedural scheme, and the provision that determines how long the road to a charge sheet can be, is Section 43D(2). That provision works by modifying the application of Section 167 of the Code, the provision that ordinarily governs detention during investigation, and it modifies that provision in three connected ways. First, police custody may extend to thirty days in place of fifteen days. An investigating officer who persuades a court that police custody is required may therefore hold the accused in that form of custody for twice the period that the Code would ordinarily allow. Second, the total period of detention that may run before a charge sheet is filed is ninety days. Within that period, custody may move between police custody and judicial custody as a court directs, but the investigation is expected to conclude, and the charge sheet to be filed, inside the ninety day span. Third, a court may extend the period of detention to one hundred and eighty days. The extension is not automatic and it is not available on a bare request. It depends on a report of the Public Prosecutor that indicates the progress of the investigation and that gives specific reasons for the detention of the accused beyond ninety days. The court must act on that report, which means the prosecution must show, in a document placed before the court, both that the investigation has in fact progressed and that particular reasons, rather than general assertions about gravity, justify holding the accused for the longer period.

A further condition in Section 43D(2) governs the movement of an accused person back into police custody after a period in judicial custody. Where police custody is sought from judicial custody, the investigating officer must support the request with an affidavit that explains the reasons for seeking police custody and that explains the delay in seeking it earlier. Without that condition, an agency could hold its request in reserve, allow a long period of judicial custody to pass, and then revive interrogation at a late stage without accounting for the interval. With it, the reasons and the delay must both be sworn to and placed on record. Taken together, Section 43D(2) describes a custody clock that is longer than the ordinary clock at every point: thirty days for police custody, ninety days before a charge sheet, and one hundred and eighty days as the outer limit where the Public Prosecutor files the required report. The statute gives investigation more time than ordinary criminal procedure gives, and it prices that extra time in a reasoned prosecutorial report and, for renewed police custody, a sworn explanation.

The reach of the Act, stated in Section 1(2), explains why investigations under it can begin far from the place where an offence is alleged to have been planned or carried out. The Act extends to the whole of India and applies beyond the territory of India in defined cases. It applies to citizens of India who are outside India, to persons in the service of the Government wherever they may be serving, and to persons on ships and aircraft registered in India, wherever those ships and aircraft may be. An investigation may therefore concern conduct by an Indian citizen abroad, by a government servant outside India, or aboard an Indian registered vessel or aircraft on an international route. For the investigating officer, the extent provision removes a threshold objection, because the connection the Act requires is supplied by citizenship, by government service, or by registration, rather than by the place of the act alone. For the accused person, distance from India is not, by itself, an answer to a charge under the Act.

### Which courts try offences under the UAPA?

Offences under Chapters IV and VI are tried by the Court of Session, and where the National Investigation Agency investigates, by the Special Courts constituted under the National Investigation Agency Act 2008. No other court takes cognizance without the sanction that Section 45 requires before proceedings begin.

Trial, once investigation ends and a charge sheet is filed, takes place before the Court of Session in the ordinary course. Where the National Investigation Agency investigates a case, trial takes place before the Special Courts constituted under the legislation that governs the Agency. The distinction follows the investigation rather than the wishes of either party. A case investigated by the state police proceeds before the Court of Session, and a case investigated by the Agency proceeds before a Special Court. In either forum, the procedural modifications that Section 43D introduces, and the sanction requirement that Section 45 imposes, travel with the case, because those provisions attach to the offence and to the stage of the proceeding rather than to the identity of the court. The road that begins with a senior investigating officer, passes through an extended custody clock measured in thirty days, ninety days and one hundred and eighty days, and ends in a charge sheet, therefore ends in one of two courtrooms, and the identity of the courtroom is settled by the identity of the investigator.

## Sanction, presumptions and protected witnesses

Before any trial can begin, Section 45 places a gate across the path of the prosecution. No court shall take cognizance of an offence under the Act without the previous sanction of the government that the section specifies for each chapter. For an offence under Chapter III, cognizance requires the prior sanction of the Centre, acting through the Central Government or through an officer authorised by it. For an offence under Chapter IV or Chapter VI, cognizance requires the prior sanction of the Central Government or of the State Government, as the case may be. One exception narrows that choice: where the offence is against a foreign government, sanction must come from the Central Government alone, and a State Government cannot supply it. Sanction is therefore not a formality attached to the filing of a charge sheet. It is a prior executive decision, chapter by chapter, without which the court has no power to take cognizance at all.

The amendment of 2008 added a discipline to that executive decision in Section 45(2). A sanction under Section 45 may be given only after the sanctioning government has considered the report of an authority that has made an independent review of the evidence gathered in the investigation, and the sanction must be given within the time prescribed for that purpose. The independent review is the safeguard the provision offers against sanction granted on the file of the investigating agency alone. Defenders of the provision within government describe it as a filter that keeps weak prosecutions from reaching a court at all. Critics in legal and civil liberties debate answer that the review takes place inside the executive, on material that the accused person cannot see or answer at that stage, so the filter operates without adversarial testing. Both descriptions can be held at once. The review is independent of the investigating officer, as Section 45(2) requires, and it is also a review conducted before charge, without the participation of the person who will stand accused. This segment states the law as information and is not legal advice for any person's case, a distinction that matters because sanction, like every other provision described here, turns on the facts and documents of an individual prosecution.

Section 43E introduces a presumption that can alter the work a prosecution must do at trial in a prosecution for a terrorist act under Section 15. Where, in such a prosecution, arms or explosives or other specified material were recovered from the possession of the accused, and there is reason to believe that such arms or explosives were used in the commission of the offence, the court shall presume, unless the contrary is shown, that the accused committed the offence. The presumption also operates where a fingerprint of the accused, or other definitive evidence, links the accused to the material or to the scene in the manner the section describes. The provision does not convict on recovery alone. Its own terms require the recovery, require the reason to believe that the recovered material was used, and leave the presumption open to rebuttal where the contrary is shown. Its practical effect, however, is to shift the evidential effort at trial, because a fact the prosecution would ordinarily have to prove may be presumed once the foundation Section 43E lays down has been established. Around that presumption, Section 43F imposes a duty to furnish information. A person required, on the requisition of an investigating officer acting with the prior written approval of an officer not below the rank of Superintendent of Police, to furnish information in that person's possession in relation to an offence under the Act, must furnish it. Refusal, or furnishing information known to be false, carries imprisonment that may extend to three years.

Protection for witnesses, admissibility of intercepted material, and the closing of other doors occupy the remaining provisions of this part of Chapter VII. Section 44 empowers a court to protect witnesses, through measures that include holding proceedings in camera and keeping the identity and address of a witness secret. A breach of the secrecy the court directs carries imprisonment that may extend to three years. Section 46 deals with evidence gathered by interception. Evidence collected through the interception of communications is admissible, subject to a condition about notice: the order authorising the interception must be furnished to the accused not less than ten days before the trial. The ten day rule gives the defence a minimum interval in which to read the order and prepare any challenge to the intercepted material before the trial opens. Section 47 bars the jurisdiction of civil courts in matters the Act commits to its own authorities and courts. Section 51 permits the impounding of the passport and the arms licence of a person who has been charge sheeted for an offence under the Act. The consequence attaches at the charge sheet stage rather than at conviction, and it restricts both movement abroad and the lawful holding of arms while the prosecution runs its course.

## Bail: the rule in Section 43D(5)

Bail under the Act is governed by modifications that Section 43D makes to the ordinary law. Section 43D(4) provides that nothing in Section 438 of the Code shall apply in relation to a case involving the arrest of a person for an offence under the Act, so anticipatory bail is unavailable and a person who apprehends arrest cannot obtain in advance a direction for release in the event of arrest. Section 43D(7) adds a further restriction: a person who is not a citizen of India and who has entered India illegally shall not be released on bail, except in very exceptional circumstances and for reasons recorded in writing. For the ordinary applicant, the governing provision is Section 43D(5). Where an accused person is in custody for an offence under Chapter IV or Chapter VI, the person shall not be released on bail if the court, on a perusal of the case diary or of the report under Section 173 of the Code, is of the opinion that there are reasonable grounds for believing that the accusation is prima facie true. The Public Prosecutor must first be given an opportunity to be heard. The test is therefore cumulative: the offence must fall within Chapter IV or Chapter VI, the court must read the case diary or the charge sheet, the Public Prosecutor must be heard, and the court must form that opinion. Where the opinion is formed, the bar operates and bail must be refused, whatever view the court might have taken under ordinary bail principles in other cases.

The Supreme Court has addressed the operation of that bar in two decisions that pull in different directions, in [the judicial interpretation of UAPA bail](/2011/07/01/uapa-bail-judicial-interpretation/) that later courts apply. In National Investigation Agency v Zahoor Ahmad Shah Watali (2019), the Supreme Court held that, at the stage of bail under Section 43D(5), the court is to work from the material produced by the prosecution broadly at face value for the prima facie test, and is not to conduct a detailed weighing of the admissibility of that material, an exercise that belongs to the trial. In Union of India v K.A. Najeeb (2021), the Supreme Court held that the bar in Section 43D(5) does not remove the power of constitutional courts to grant bail where a person has undergone prolonged incarceration with no prospect of a timely trial, because such incarceration infringes Article 21 of the Constitution and the statutory restriction cannot eclipse the constitutional guarantee. Watali therefore governs the reading of the prosecution material at the threshold, while Najeeb preserves, for the constitutional courts, a separate ground founded on delay and on the length of custody already undergone.

## Membership: the question the courts answered twice

Membership is the point at which the Act reaches a person who has committed no act of violence, raised no fund and harboured no fugitive, and it is therefore the point at which the argument about the Act is at its sharpest. Section 10, as substituted in 2004, punishes membership of an association declared unlawful. Under Section 10(a), a person who is and continues to be a member of such an association, or who takes part in its meetings, or who contributes to, receives or solicits contributions for it, or who in any way assists its operations, is punishable with imprisonment that may extend to two years, and is also liable to fine. The question that reached the Supreme Court was whether member, in that setting, carried its full width, so that a person became guilty by status alone, or whether the provision had to be read down so that membership without more was not enough. In 2011, two judge benches of the Supreme Court answered in favour of the narrower reading. In Arup Bhuyan v State of Assam and in Indra Das v State of Assam, the Court read the membership provisions down and held that mere membership of a banned association, without incitement to violence and without an overt act, was not an offence. State of Kerala v Raneef reasoned along the same line. The 2011 position therefore required the prosecution to prove something beyond membership itself, an act of incitement or some overt conduct, before a conviction could stand.

In March 2023, a three judge bench of the Supreme Court, hearing a review in Arup Bhuyan v State of Assam, answered the same question the other way. The bench overruled the 2011 decisions and upheld Section 10(a)(i). Membership of an association declared unlawful is itself an offence under the Act, and the reading down the 2011 benches had adopted was not accepted. A charge under Section 10(a)(i) may therefore rest on proof that the association was declared unlawful and that the accused person was a member of it, without the additional proof of incitement to violence or of an overt act that the 2011 reading had required. Two decisions of two judge benches stood for twelve years as the governing reading, and a larger bench replaced that reading with its opposite, so the offence a member commits by membership alone depends, in the history of the provision, on which of the two answers a court is bound to follow at the time of trial.

### How is the UAPA different from preventive detention?

The UAPA is a penal statute that punishes defined offences through prosecution, charge, trial and sentence, whereas preventive detention restrains a person without trial to prevent anticipated future acts. Detention under the UAPA follows arrest for an alleged offence and remains subject to court proceedings.

Precision about which membership, and membership of what, is essential, because the Act contains three distinct provisions that differ in subject, punishment and defences. Section 10 concerns membership of an unlawful association, meaning an association declared unlawful under Chapter II after the notification and Tribunal process that sections 3 to 9 prescribe, and the punishment under Section 10(a) extends to two years with fine. Section 38 punishes a person who associates, or professes to associate, with a terrorist organisation with intent to further its activities, at up to ten years, or fine, or both. Section 38, unlike Section 10, carries an express statutory defence: a person has a defence where the organisation was not listed when the person joined and the person took no part in its activities while it was listed. Section 20 punishes a member of a terrorist gang or a terrorist organisation involved in a terrorist act, at up to imprisonment for life. The gradation is structural, and Parliament wrote a defence into the Section 38 offence that it did not write into Section 10.

The underlying argument, in its strongest form on each side, begins with the objection critics of the 2023 position press hardest. Punishing membership as such, the objection runs, is punishment for association rather than for conduct, and it exposes a person who joined an organisation for its social, religious, charitable or political work, who attended its meetings and paid its dues and committed no violent act, to conviction for the company that person kept. On that view, the declaration of unlawfulness does the decisive work, because the executive decision to declare, confirmed by a Tribunal, converts continued membership into a crime without any fresh act by the member, who must abandon the association or accept prosecution. The answer, in its strongest form from defenders of the 2023 position, is that proscription without a membership offence is a declaration without consequence. An organisation declared unlawful can, on the narrower reading, retain its members, hold their loyalty and rebuild its strength in the open, while the State must wait for each member to commit an incitement or an overt act before the law can move. On that view, membership is not a passive status but the continuing act by which an organisation exists at all, since an association has no life apart from the persons who compose it, and a member who remains after declaration, with knowledge of it, has made a choice the law is entitled to treat as participation. The Supreme Court in 2023 accepted the second argument as a matter of statutory construction and upheld the provision as written. The first argument remains the ground on which the provision continues to be criticised, and it identifies the cost of the upheld reading: the boundary of the offence is set by a declaration about an organisation rather than by proof of a violent act by the individual in the dock.

## 2019: the designated individual

For the first five decades of its life, the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act 1967 reached persons mainly through the collectives to which they belonged. An association could be declared unlawful under Section 3. An organisation could be listed in the First Schedule as a terrorist organisation under Section 35. Membership, support and fundraising directed towards those collectives could then be prosecuted under provisions such as Sections 10, 38, 39 and 40, and the perpetrator of a terrorist act defined in Section 15 could be punished under Section 16. The Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Amendment Act 2019 changed that position directly by naming an individual, by executive notification, as a terrorist in his own right. It was Act 28 of 2019, received presidential assent on 8 August 2019 and came into force on 14 August 2019, after passage by the Lok Sabha on 24 July 2019 and by the Rajya Sabha on 2 August 2019. It created a new form of executive designation, personal in its object and consequences, in machinery previously built for organisations.

The place of the 2019 Act in the longer statutory history is clearest when it is read alongside [the amendments that rebuilt the UAPA](/2011/06/01/uapa-amendments-explained/).

The first of its three changes concerned Section 35 and the schedules. Before the amendment, Section 35 empowered the Central Government, by notification, to add, remove or amend entries in the First Schedule, and the power could be exercised only if the Central Government believed that the organisation was involved in terrorism, a phrase covering an organisation that commits or participates in acts of terrorism, prepares for terrorism, promotes or encourages terrorism, or is otherwise involved in terrorism. The 2019 Act extended that machinery to individuals. An individual could be designated as a terrorist and added to the Fourth Schedule, a new schedule created for that purpose, through the Section 35 and Section 36 machinery, on the same belief standard. The amendment created no separate offence of being designated and attached no term of imprisonment to designation itself. It created a status, entered in a schedule to a central statute by notification of the Central Government, founded on executive belief rather than on a charge, a trial or a finding by a court. That distinction between status and conviction runs through the debate that followed, because much of the defence of the change and much of the criticism of it turn on how much weight the distinction can bear in practice.

The second change altered the internal balance of an investigation under Chapter V. Section 25 permits an investigating officer to seize or attach property reasonably suspected to be proceeds of terrorism, but only with prior approval at a senior level. In a case investigated by a state force, that approval comes from the Director General of Police of the state. In a case investigated by the National Investigation Agency, the 2019 Act moved the approval from the state Director General of Police to the Director General of the National Investigation Agency. Defended as alignment, it placed approval within the investigating agency chain of command. Criticised as dilution, it made an external approval internal. The surrounding Chapter V safeguards were left untouched: cash seized must be released within forty eight hours unless the Designated Authority orders retention, an appeal against its order lies to the court within thirty days, and forfeiture under Section 26 follows the show cause procedure in Section 27, with protection for a bona fide transferee for value without knowledge and an appeal to the High Court under Section 28 within one month.

The third change also concerned the National Investigation Agency. Section 43 had restricted investigation of offences under the Act to officers not below the rank of Deputy Superintendent of Police, with the corresponding rank of Assistant Commissioner of Police in metropolitan areas. The 2019 Act provided that officers of the Agency of the rank of Inspector and above may also investigate. Defended as a measure of capacity, it widened the pool of officers able to conduct an Agency investigation. Criticised as a lowering of the seniority floor, it applied to a statute where Section 43D detention and bail restrictions and Section 43E presumptions already give the investigation exceptional weight.

The new power was used within weeks of commencement. On 4 September 2019, by a release of the Press Information Bureau, the Central Government designated Maulana Masood Azhar, Hafiz Muhammad Saeed, Zaki-ur-Rehman Lakhvi and Dawood Ibrahim Kaskar. The release described the schedule as Schedule 4, and the four names became the first entries in the Fourth Schedule. Each of the four was already designated under the United Nations system, described in the verified material as designated as a global terrorist under the United Nations. That fact matters. The inaugural designations were not directed at persons known only to Indian agencies, nor at persons present in India and amenable in the ordinary way to arrest, charge and trial. They followed an international designation. A reader who tests the power by its hardest case, designation within the jurisdiction on contested material with no external designation, is not testing it by its first exercise, and a reader who defends it solely by the first four names defends it at its easiest point. The text authorises designation on the belief standard in any case meeting that standard, with no condition of a prior United Nations designation.

What designation does and does not do needs to be stated with care. Designation is not a conviction and carries no sentence. No court finds the ingredients of the terrorist act in Section 15 proved, applies the punishment in Section 16, or records a determination following trial. A designated individual has not, by reason of designation alone, been sentenced to any term at all, and designation does not substitute for a prosecution where a prosecution can be brought. Its consequences are administrative and reputational: administrative, because entry places the named person within a scheme built around proscription, terrorist organisations, proceeds of terrorism, support and fundraising; reputational, because to be named in a schedule to a terrorism statute as a person believed to be involved in terrorism is a label of the utmost gravity in public, professional and personal life. The government defence recorded in Parliament and outside it has rested on the first half of that account. The criticism has rested on the second, that the absence of a sentence does not make the label light. Neither side disputes the formal position. The dispute is over its significance in a statute whose provisions on investigation, detention, bail, forfeiture and prosecution, including sanction under Section 45 and the bail restriction in Section 43D(5), are unusually stringent.

## The Tribunal check and the missing check

The central claim of this article is precise. The older half of the Act, which bans associations, is procedurally more protective than the newer half, which designates individuals, even though the newer half attaches its label to a single identified person. The inversion is visible when the two mechanisms are set side by side, from the executive decision to the point at which an independent judicial mind must, or need not, be applied. It runs against the usual expectation that the more personal and grave a determination is, the stronger the procedure preceding it will be.

Begin with the ban on an association under Section 3. The Central Government may, by notification in the Official Gazette specifying the grounds, declare an association unlawful. Grounds must be specified, and facts may be withheld only where, under the proviso to Section 3(2), disclosure would be against the public interest. The decisive feature lies in Section 3(3). The notification has no effect until the Tribunal confirms it under Section 4 and the confirmation order is published. Confirmation is a condition of effect, not advice the government may accept or disregard. The Centre must refer the notification to the Tribunal within thirty days of publication. The Tribunal must call upon the association to show cause why it should not be declared unlawful, within thirty days of service of the notice. The Tribunal must pass its order, confirming or cancelling the notification, within six months from the date of issue of the Section 3 notification. There is a reference, a show cause stage, an inquiry, a decision to confirm or cancel, and a statutory clock governing the whole sequence.

Under Section 5, the Tribunal consists of one person appointed by the Central Government, who must be a Judge of a High Court, a sitting judge, with civil court powers to summon witnesses, require discovery, receive evidence on affidavits, requisition public records and issue commissions. Its proceedings are judicial proceedings. In Jamaat-e-Islami Hind v Union of India in 1994, the Supreme Court set aside a confirmation order and held that the Tribunal must form its own opinion after assessing the credibility of the material. It is not a mere imprimatur of the government opinion, and natural justice applies even where material is withheld on public interest grounds. A confirmed notification remains in force for five years, extended from two years by Act 3 of 2013 with effect from 1 February 2013. The procedure preceding a fully effective ban therefore includes a show cause opportunity, a sitting High Court judge, an independent opinion, and a six month limit.

Turn to designation of an individual and follow the same sequence. The Central Government, if it believes the individual is involved in terrorism, may add the name to the Fourth Schedule through the Section 35 machinery. The designation takes effect on that belief. There is no prior hearing and no call to show cause before the name is entered. There is no reference to a Tribunal, no confirmation stage at which a sitting High Court judge must form an independent opinion on the credibility of the material before the designation operates, and no statutory clock of the Section 4 kind. The notification operates because the Central Government has issued it, and it continues until it is removed. The procedural energy of the design is entirely post decisional. The person affected may apply to the Central Government itself under Section 36 for removal of the entry, so the first judge of the designation is the authority that made it. If the Central Government rejects the application, review lies to the Review Committee under Section 37, to be sought within one month of receipt of the refusal on the scheme applied through the same machinery. The Committee is chaired by a person who is or has been a Judge of a High Court, with the concurrence of the Chief Justice required where the chairperson is a sitting judge, and has not more than three other members. If it allows the review, the Centre must remove the entry. That is a real power. Its limits are structural. The Committee tests the rejection on judicial review principles, examining the legality of the decision rather than conducting a fresh merits hearing in which it substitutes its own assessment of involvement for that of the government. Beyond the Committee, the constitutional courts remain open under Article 226 in the High Court and Article 32 in the Supreme Court. That avenue is general constitutional protection against state action, not a confirmation mechanism built into the designation scheme with its own reference, timetable and independent opinion requirement.

The asymmetry therefore survives careful statement. In the association regime, independent judicial confirmation is built into the path to effectiveness, even though the subject is a collective that can act through office bearers and present an institutional answer to a show cause notice. In the individual regime, no independent confirmation is built into that path, even though the subject is a single person and the reputational consequence falls on an identified life. The older law requires the executive to persuade a sitting judge within six months, in an inquiry where its material is assessed for credibility. The newer law requires the executive to persuade no one before the label attaches, and places the burden of moving the machinery, first to the designating authority and then to a committee applying legality principles, on the person labelled. Scrutiny in the older scheme comes before full effect and is merits based. Scrutiny in the newer scheme comes after effect and is legality based. That is the precise content of the claim.

The honest qualification must be faced. The Tribunal check is not perfect. The first weakness is the immediate effect proviso to Section 3(3). Where the Central Government records reasons in writing that circumstances make it necessary, the notification takes effect from publication, subject to confirmation under Section 4. Bans are declared with immediate effect in practice. The Popular Front of India and eight associates or fronts were declared unlawful with immediate effect for five years in September 2022, and a Tribunal presided over by Justice Dinesh Kumar Sharma of the Delhi High Court confirmed the ban in March 2023. For the intervening period the association lived under an unconfirmed ban. Confirmation in such a case does not prevent operation. It time limits unconfirmed operation and subjects continuance to independent judicial approval. The second weakness is appointment, since the Tribunal is appointed by the Central Government whose notification is under inquiry, though a sitting High Court judge brings the independence of judicial office and the Jamaat-e-Islami Hind principle insists on an independent opinion. A further concern, recorded around particular confirmations, is material examined in sealed cover without disclosure to the association. In the 2019 confirmation of the ban on Jamaat-e-Islami (Jammu and Kashmir), the Tribunal examined material in sealed cover that was not disclosed to the association. A hearing in which part of the material cannot be answered is a hearing of reduced adversarial value, whatever the public interest justification for withholding.

The qualification narrows the gap but does not close it. An imperfect confirmation before a sitting judge, with a duty to form an independent opinion, a show cause opportunity and a six month deadline, is still a confirmation: the executive case must be placed before a judicial mind that can cancel the notification. Immediate effect weakens the timing without abolishing the substance, because a ban still fails if confirmation is refused. Appointment by the Centre cannot explain a preference for the newer mechanism, since the Section 37 Review Committee is also constituted within a Central Government scheme. Sealed cover concerns the quality of a hearing, not a reason to prefer no hearing at all before a label attaches. To say a safeguard is flawed is to argue for its repair, not that its absence is equivalent. One scheme has a judicial gate, however imperfect. The other has no gate before designation operates, only a path back through the designating authority and a legality review.

## The argument, taken seriously on both sides

The argument over individual designation has reasons on both sides, and they deserve statement in their strongest form, attributed to the sides that advance them, before any verdict is offered. The state case begins with a premise that critics in their strongest form do not deny. Organised armed threats are real. Organisations listed in the First Schedule include Lashkar-e-Taiba, Jaish-e-Mohammed, Hizb-ul-Mujahideen, Babbar Khalsa International, the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam, the Students Islamic Movement of India and the United Liberation Front of Asom, together with organisations listed in the Schedule to the United Nations Prevention and Suppression of Terrorism Order 2007. The terrorist act in Section 15 describes killing, injury, destruction of property, disruption of essential supplies and services, damage to monetary stability through high quality counterfeit currency, and coercion of governments through detention and abduction, by bombs, firearms, explosives and chemical, biological, radioactive, nuclear and other hazardous means. A state that could not act against networks that plan such acts would fail in a basic duty to protect life. The question in dispute is not whether the state may act. It is whether designation of a named individual on executive belief, without prior judicial confirmation, is a justified and well designed way to act, and whether safeguards proportionate to its gravity accompany it.

Within that frame, the government case in Parliament and outside it has rested on a practical observation. Organisations re-form under new names when banned. A ban addresses a name and a public identity, while the persons who direct, finance and sustain an organisation can discard the name, adopt another, and continue through a front or successor. If the law reaches only the organisation, the government argues, it remains behind a network that migrates between forms. The individuals driving it must therefore be reachable as individuals. Supporters add an international parallel: designation of individuals is familiar in United Nations Security Council practice, the first four Indian designations concerned persons already designated under the United Nations system, and proscription and financing offences have parallels in that practice, reflected in Security Council Resolution 1373, whose preamble reference was inserted by Act 35 of 2008. Designation also serves notice and coordination functions for banks, agencies, foreign governments and the public, especially where the person is beyond the reach of Indian process. On that account it is not a substitute for trial where trial is possible, but a tool where trial is not immediately possible.

The critics case, in its strongest form, starts with the text into which designation was inserted. The breadth of the underlying definitions determines the breadth of the belief that can ground a designation. Unlawful activity in Section 2(1)(o) extends to action by act, words spoken or written, signs or visible representation that is intended, or supports a claim, to bring about cession or secession, that disclaims, questions, disrupts or is intended to disrupt the sovereignty and territorial integrity of India, or that causes or is intended to cause disaffection against India. Terrorist act in Section 15 extends across a wide range of means and consequences, tied to intent to threaten or likelihood of threatening the unity, integrity, security, including economic security, or sovereignty of India, or striking terror in the people or any section of the people. A standard expressed as belief that an individual is involved in terrorism, where involvement covers committing, participating, preparing, promoting, encouraging or being otherwise involved, gives the executive a wide field within which belief may be formed. The graver the field, critics argue, the more procedure matters, and here procedure is at its thinnest. Designation rests on executive belief with no hearing before a label of the utmost gravity attaches. It is a public notification in a schedule to a statute, carrying reputational and practical consequences, issued without the person having been heard, without judicial confirmation, and without a deadline by which the state must justify it to an independent body. The review route does not cure that thinness, because it is executive constituted and legality based. The application under Section 36 goes to the Central Government that designated. The Review Committee under Section 37, though chaired by a person who is or has been a High Court judge, tests the rejection on judicial review principles rather than rehearing the merits and deciding involvement for itself. A legality review can correct a decision that is irrational, procedurally improper or beyond power. It is not designed to answer, on an assessment of credibility of the kind the Tribunal must undertake, whether the material in fact supports the belief. Constitutional challenge under Articles 226 and 32 remains, critics acknowledge, but a general constitutional remedy is not a substitute for a built in confirmation safeguard designed for the power in question.

That dispute reached the Supreme Court in the form in which such disputes ordinarily reach it. Petitions challenging the 2019 amendment were filed in the Supreme Court. Structurally, such petitions question the validity of a parliamentary amendment creating a designation power and invite the Court to test it against the fundamental rights chapter. The grounds described for the challenge rest on Articles 14, 19 and 21. An Article 14 ground directs attention to arbitrariness and to the absence of a fair procedure in a power founded on belief. An Article 19 ground directs attention to the effect of designation and of the surrounding provisions on the freedoms in that article and to whether restrictions can be justified within the permissible heads, including the sovereignty and integrity of India ground inserted into Article 19 by the Constitution (Sixteenth Amendment) Act 1963. An Article 21 ground directs attention to reputation, liberty and livelihood as aspects of life and personal liberty and to the quality of the procedure by which they are affected in practice. No outcome of those petitions is predicted here, and none should be inferred from the statement of the grounds. The petitions matter for a narrower reason. They show that the controversy is framed by those who brought it as a question about the constitutional conditions under which a grave executive label may be imposed, a framing that requires any court deciding it to address procedure, not merely purpose.

A distinction analysis should preserve is between criticisms of the text and criticisms of application, since they call for different remedies. A criticism of the text says the statute permits too much, defines its concepts too broadly, sets its belief standard too low, or omits a safeguard required in every case. Its remedy is legislative amendment, constitutional invalidation, or reading down, because sound administration cannot supply a hearing the text does not require. A criticism of application says the statute was used in a particular case on insufficient material or for an improper purpose. Its remedy is case specific: removal, quashing, or relief on the facts. Misuse in one case does not prove that no designation power could be justified, just as restraint in the first four designations does not prove the text contains adequate safeguards for a harder case. The strongest state case concerns the need for a personal tool. The strongest critics case concerns the procedure and breadth that should condition such a tool. They answer different questions.

On the design as enacted in 2019, the verdict of this article is that a designation power aimed at individuals was defensible in purpose but deficient in procedure, and that the deficiency is structural rather than incidental. A Tribunal style confirmation, adapted to individuals and operating within a short statutory period, with an independent judicial opinion on the credibility of the material, would have answered the central objection without denying the state the tool it sought, and its absence is difficult to justify in a statute that has required that discipline for associations since 1967. Until such a confirmation stage is supplied, designation will remain the least procedurally protected grave determination in the Act, and that position, rather than the existence of the power itself, is its principal vulnerability.

## Placing any UAPA case in the correct half

The practical payoff of the two-halves reading is a method, and the method is short. Take the document that starts the case and find its chapter. A prosecution that begins with a Section 3 notification and speaks of membership, meetings, contributions and notified places is a first-half case, whatever the rhetoric around it: the live questions are whether the notification was confirmed in time, whether the accused's conduct falls within Section 10 or Section 13, and whether sanction under Section 45 was taken from the Central Government. A prosecution that begins with a bombing, a fund transfer or a listing, and speaks of Sections 15 to 23 or Sections 38 to 40, is a second-half case: the live questions are the custody clock of Section 43D(2), the bail bar of Section 43D(5), the sanction of the Central or State Government, and the forfeiture machinery of Chapter V. A designation case is a second-half case of a third kind, in which no prosecution need exist at all and the entire contest runs through Section 36, the Review Committee and the constitutional courts.

Keeping the halves apart also disciplines the argument about the statute. A criticism aimed at the breadth of "unlawful activity" says nothing about the terrorist act, and a criticism aimed at the 180 day charge sheet clock says nothing about the Tribunal. Reform proposals that ignore the distinction tend to amend the wrong half. The record of amendment shows Parliament repeatedly strengthening the second half while leaving the first half's Tribunal architecture untouched since 1967, which is itself a fact about where legislative attention has gone and where it has not.

For a reader working through the statute itself, the bare Act rewards being read as a document with a visible seam. The chapters added in 2004 sit between the original Chapter III and the original miscellaneous provisions, and the text circulated by official and commercial publishers carries the amendment footnotes that date each insertion; working from a clean copy of the Act, opened in [an office file viewer suited to reading long statutory documents](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html), makes the seam between the 1967 text and its grafted chapters easy to follow section by section. The statute that results is not one law with a long history. It is two laws with a shared cover, and it is read correctly only when the reader keeps asking, of every power and every safeguard, which of the two laws it belongs to.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the UAPA and why is it India's principal anti-terror law?

The Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act 1967 is the central statute through which India bans associations, lists terrorist organisations and prosecutes terrorist acts. It is two laws in one binding. The 1967 half empowers the Central Government to declare an association unlawful, subject to confirmation by a Tribunal presided over by a High Court judge. The terrorism half, grafted on in 2004 after the repeal of the Prevention of Terrorism Act, defines the terrorist act, creates membership, support and funding offences, and lists terrorist organisations in a schedule. Because the earlier special statutes lapsed or were repealed while the UAPA endured and absorbed their functions, the UAPA carries the whole field: proscription, financing, investigation and prosecution of terrorism all run through its sections.

### Q: Why was the UAPA enacted in 1967?

The Act was Parliament's answer to secessionist advocacy. The Committee on National Integration and Regionalism, appointed by the National Integration Council, recommended that Article 19 of the Constitution be amended so that the sovereignty and integrity of India became an express ground for restricting the freedoms of speech, assembly and association, and the Constitution (Sixteenth Amendment) Act 1963 made that change and reformed the oaths of office. A constitutional ground alone, however, restricts nothing by itself. The Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act 1967, Act 37 of 1967, was enacted to occupy the new space: it defined unlawful activity by reference to secession and to the sovereignty and territorial integrity of India, and built the declaration and Tribunal machinery to act on that definition.

### Q: What does "unlawful activity" mean in Section 2 of the UAPA?

Section 2(1)(o) defines unlawful activity as an action by an individual or association, whether an act, words spoken or written, signs or visible representation, falling in one of three limbs. The first limb covers action intended, or supporting a claim, to bring about the cession or secession of part of India's territory, or inciting another to bring it about. The second covers action that disclaims, questions, disrupts or is intended to disrupt the sovereignty and territorial integrity of India. The third covers action that causes or is intended to cause disaffection against India. The definition reaches expression as well as conduct, which is why its wording, rather than any single prosecution, is the centre of most argument about the first half of the Act.

### Q: What conduct makes an association unlawful under the UAPA?

An association is an unlawful association under Section 2(1)(p) through its objects and its acts. The definition catches an association whose object is an unlawful activity as Section 2(1)(o) defines it, an association that encourages or aids persons to undertake an unlawful activity, and an association whose members undertake such an activity. It also catches an association that has as its object an activity punishable under Section 153A or Section 153B of the Indian Penal Code, the provisions on promoting enmity between groups and on imputations prejudicial to national integration. The definition describes the association in the abstract; it takes a notification under Section 3, declaring that the association is or has become unlawful, to set the machinery of prohibition, confirmation and offences in motion.

### Q: How does the UAPA Tribunal confirm a ban?

Confirmation follows a fixed clock. The Central Government must refer its Section 3 notification to the Tribunal within thirty days of publication. The Tribunal then calls upon the association, by notice in writing, to show cause within thirty days of service why it should not be declared unlawful. After holding an inquiry, the Tribunal must pass its order, confirming the notification or cancelling it, within six months from the date the notification was issued. The Tribunal is a single person who must be a Judge of a High Court, and it works with the powers of a civil court to summon witnesses, compel documents and receive evidence on affidavit. A notification that the Tribunal does not confirm cannot continue: without confirmation, the declaration falls.

### Q: How long does a declaration that an association is unlawful remain in force?

A confirmed declaration remains in force for five years from the date the notification takes effect. The period stood at two years for most of the Act's life; the amendment of 2013 substituted five years, so a single confirmation carries a ban across a full Parliament's term. The period is a ceiling rather than a guaranteed term, because the Central Government may cancel the notification at any time, on its own motion or on an application by the association. When the five years expire, the declaration lapses unless the government issues and obtains confirmation of a fresh notification. Associations facing repeated bans, as several have over the decades, are therefore re-declared and re-confirmed in successive five year cycles rather than banned once for good.

### Q: What happens if the UAPA Tribunal does not confirm a ban?

The declaration fails. Under Section 4 the Tribunal, after its inquiry, either confirms the notification on being satisfied that sufficient cause exists or cancels it, and a notification that is not confirmed has no continuing effect. Section 3(3) builds the whole scheme on that condition: the notification ordinarily takes effect only on confirmation and publication of the order. Where the government used the immediate-effect proviso, so that the ban operated from publication, a refusal to confirm brings the ban to an end from that point, and the association's five year subjection to the declaration never begins. Cancellation is therefore a complete answer for the association, distinct from the narrower executive cancellation under Section 6, which depends on the government changing its own mind.

### Q: What counts as a terrorist act under Section 15 of the UAPA?

Section 15 requires both a specified intent and specified means producing specified harms. The intent is to threaten, or be likely to threaten, the unity, integrity, security (including economic security) or sovereignty of India, or to strike terror in the people or any section of them. The means include bombs, explosives, firearms, lethal weapons, poisonous or noxious gases, chemicals, biological, radioactive, nuclear or other hazardous substances, or any other means of whatever nature. The harms include death or injury, loss of or damage to property, disruption of essential supplies or services, damage to monetary stability through high quality counterfeit currency, and damage to defence or government property. Overawing public functionaries by criminal force, and detaining or kidnapping a person to compel a government or an international organisation, are drawn in as well, as are acts within the treaties in the Second Schedule.

### Q: What is the punishment for a terrorist act under Section 16 of the UAPA?

Section 16 grades the punishment by outcome. Where the terrorist act has resulted in the death of any person, the punishment is death or imprisonment for life, with liability to fine. In every other case the punishment is imprisonment for a term that cannot be less than five years and may extend to imprisonment for life, again with liability to fine. The five year floor matters as much as the life ceiling: a court convicting under Section 16 has no discretion to impose a short sentence, and the same five-to-life band recurs across the surrounding offences, in Section 17 for raising funds for a terrorist act, Section 18 for conspiracy and facilitation, and Sections 18A and 18B for organising camps and recruiting.

### Q: How long can a person be held before a charge sheet under the UAPA?

Section 43D(2) rewrites the remand clock of the Code of Criminal Procedure for UAPA cases. Police custody may run to thirty days in place of the ordinary fifteen. The total period of detention before a charge sheet must be filed is ninety days. If the investigation cannot be completed within those ninety days, the court may extend detention up to one hundred and eighty days, but only on the report of the Public Prosecutor indicating the progress of the investigation and the specific reasons for detaining the accused beyond ninety days. If no charge sheet is filed within the period as extended, the accused becomes entitled to default bail. The extension is judicial, not automatic, and the Public Prosecutor's report is the document on which it turns.

### Q: Who investigates UAPA cases?

Investigation is rank-gated. Under Section 43, offences under Chapters IV and VI may be investigated only by an officer of the Delhi Special Police Establishment not below the rank of Deputy Superintendent of Police, by an Assistant Commissioner of Police in a metropolitan area, or by a Deputy Superintendent of Police or an officer of equivalent rank elsewhere. Since the 2019 amendment, officers of the National Investigation Agency of Inspector rank and above may also investigate. Where the Central Government directs an investigation under the National Investigation Agency Act 2008, the Agency takes the case over from the state police. State police forces therefore open most UAPA investigations, senior officers conduct them, and the Agency carries the cases the Centre assigns to it.

### Q: Is mere membership of a banned association an offence under the UAPA?

Under the position settled by the Supreme Court in March 2023, yes. Two-judge benches in 2011, in Arup Bhuyan v State of Assam and Indra Das v State of Assam, had read the membership provisions down, holding that mere membership of a banned association, without incitement to violence or an overt act, did not constitute an offence. A three-judge bench, deciding the review in Arup Bhuyan in March 2023, overruled that reading and upheld Section 10(a)(i): being a member of an association that has been declared unlawful is itself punishable, with imprisonment of up to two years and fine. The position differs for terrorist organisations, where Section 38 requires association with intent to further the organisation's activities and carries an express statutory defence.

### Q: Can property be seized or forfeited under the UAPA before a conviction?

Yes, at two distinct stages. Under Section 25, an investigating officer who has reason to believe that property represents proceeds of terrorism may seize or attach it with the prior approval of the Director General of Police, or of the Director General of the National Investigation Agency where the Agency investigates. Cash seized must be released within forty eight hours unless the Designated Authority orders its retention, and the Designated Authority's order can be appealed to the court within thirty days. Forfeiture itself is a court order under Section 26, and the section states expressly that the court may order it whether or not the person is prosecuted under Chapter IV or Chapter VI. A show cause notice and hearing precede the order, a bona fide transferee for value without knowledge is protected, and an appeal lies to the High Court within one month.

### Q: Is government sanction required before a court can take cognizance of a UAPA offence?

Yes. Section 45 forbids any court from taking cognizance without previous sanction. For offences under Chapter III, the sanction must come from the Central Government or an officer it authorises. For offences under Chapters IV and VI, sanction may come from the Central Government or the State Government, except that where the offence is committed against the government of a foreign country, only the Central Government can sanction. Since 2008, Section 45(2) has added a discipline to the grant: sanction is to be given within the prescribed time and only after considering the report of an authority appointed to make an independent review of the evidence gathered in the investigation and to recommend whether sanction should issue. A prosecution launched without a valid sanction fails at the threshold, before the merits are reached.

### Q: How can a terrorist organisation be removed from the First Schedule?

Section 36 supplies the route. The organisation itself, or any person affected by its inclusion in the First Schedule, may apply to the Central Government for removal. If the Central Government rejects the application, the applicant may, within one month of receiving the order of refusal, seek review by the Review Committee constituted under Section 37. The Committee, chaired by a person who is or has been a Judge of a High Court, tests the rejection on the principles that govern judicial review: it asks whether the decision was flawed in legality, not whether it would have listed the organisation itself. If the Committee allows the review, the Central Government is bound to make an order removing the organisation from the Schedule on receiving the Committee's order.

### Q: Can an individual be designated a terrorist under the UAPA?

Since the amendment of 2019, yes. Before that amendment, only organisations could be listed; the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Amendment Act 2019, Act 28 of 2019, extended the Section 35 power to individuals, whose names are entered in a Fourth Schedule created for the purpose. The Central Government may designate an individual where it believes the individual is involved in terrorism, involvement bearing the same statutory meaning as for organisations: committing or participating in acts of terrorism, preparing for terrorism, promoting or encouraging terrorism, or being otherwise involved in terrorism. The first designations, announced on 4 September 2019, were Maulana Masood Azhar, Hafiz Muhammad Saeed, Zaki-ur-Rehman Lakhvi and Dawood Ibrahim Kaskar. Designation is not a conviction and carries no sentence; it is an executive listing with administrative and reputational consequences, made without a prior hearing.

### Q: What can an individual designated as a terrorist do to get the designation removed?

The statutory route mirrors the route for organisations. The designated individual may apply to the Central Government under Section 36 for removal of his name from the Fourth Schedule. If the government rejects the application, he may within one month seek review by the Review Committee under Section 37, chaired by a sitting or former Judge of a High Court, which examines the rejection on judicial-review principles and, if it finds the decision flawed, orders removal that the government must implement. What the route does not contain is any hearing before designation: the individual's first opportunity to contest the listing comes after his name is already in the Schedule. Alongside the statutory route, a designation remains an executive act open to challenge before the High Courts and the Supreme Court under Articles 226 and 32 of the Constitution.

### Q: What is the difference between the UAPA Tribunal and the UAPA Review Committee?

The two bodies sit at opposite ends of the statute's two halves. The Tribunal belongs to the 1967 regime: it is a single sitting Judge of a High Court, it is seized of a ban automatically because the Central Government must refer every Section 3 notification to it within thirty days, and its confirmation is a condition of the ban having effect at all. The Review Committee belongs to the listing regime of Chapter VI: it is chaired by a person who is or has been a Judge of a High Court, with up to three other members, and it acts only when a listed organisation or designated individual, having first applied to the Central Government and been refused, seeks review within one month. The Tribunal decides on the merits whether sufficient cause exists; the Committee reviews the legality of a refusal. One is a prior judicial check, the other a posterior legality review.

### Q: What is the difference between the First and Fourth Schedules of the UAPA?

The two Schedules hold two different kinds of listing. The First Schedule lists terrorist organisations: bodies such as Lashkar-e-Taiba and Jaish-e-Mohammed, together with organisations appearing in the Schedule to the United Nations Prevention and Suppression of Terrorism Order 2007. Listing an organisation activates the Chapter VI offences of membership, support and fund raising in Sections 38 to 40. The Fourth Schedule, created by the amendment of 2019, lists individuals designated as terrorists in their own names. An organisation is a collective that others join, fund or support; an individual in the Fourth Schedule is a person the Central Government believes to be involved in terrorism personally. The distinction decided the shape of the 2019 debate, because designation reaches a named human being, with a reputation and a livelihood, rather than a body that can dissolve and re-form.

### Q: Does the UAPA apply to offences committed outside India?

Yes, within stated limits. Section 1(2) extends the Act to the whole of India and applies its provisions to citizens of India outside India, to persons in the service of the Government wherever they may be, and to persons on ships and aircraft registered in India wherever they may be. A person who commits an offence under the Act outside India is dealt with under the Act in the same manner as if the act had been committed within India. The extension is personal rather than universal: it follows Indian citizenship, Indian government service and Indian-registered vessels and aircraft. It does not make the UAPA a general world criminal code, and a foreign national acting wholly abroad, unconnected to those categories, stands outside the extension the section creates.
