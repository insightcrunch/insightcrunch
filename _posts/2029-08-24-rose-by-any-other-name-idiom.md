The rose sentence is the most quoted sentence in Romeo and Juliet and the least believed. "A rose by any other name would smell as sweet" circulates through boardrooms, branding meetings, horticultural catalogues and newspaper headlines as settled wisdom: the label is nothing, the thing itself is everything, and anyone troubled by a name is troubled by a shadow. Restored to its scene, the sentence is nothing of the kind. It is a plea spoken by a girl of thirteen at a window, in the dark, to a young man she met that evening, and the play that contains it spends its remaining three acts proving the plea false. The name she asks Romeo to shed is the name that kills him. The verdict of this article is therefore a divided one, and the division is the whole story of the idiom. As a claim about words, Juliet's sentence is exact, and philosophy's most careful account of naming ratifies it. As a claim about names among people, it fails wherever it is tested: in the play, where the feud is a war between two words and the words win; in the rose trade, which kept four names for one flower because each name did work the others could not; and in modern commerce, where two of the most expensive renamings on record collapsed because the public refused to believe that the thing had stayed the same under a fresh label. The idiom survives by amputation. It keeps Juliet's flower and forgets her window, keeps the scent and forgets the tomb, and it quotes as natural law a sentence its own speaker stops believing before the scene that contains it has ended.

## What the Rose Sentence Actually Claims in the Balcony Scene

Juliet's sentence is the conclusion of an argument, and the argument is more rigorous than its proverb afterlife suggests. Speaking alone, unaware that Romeo stands below in the orchard, she builds the case in steps. It opens with a definition of the enemy: "'Tis but thy name that is my enemy" (2.2.38). The enemy is not the man, not his family in the flesh, not anything he has done; the enemy is a word. She then tests the word against the body. "What's Montague? It is nor hand, nor foot, / Nor arm, nor face, nor any other part / Belonging to a man" (2.2.40-42). A name fails every test of physical membership. It cannot be touched, wounded, fed or buried. From the failed anatomy she draws the general law: "What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other word would smell as sweet" (2.2.43-44, in the Folger Shakespeare text, which prints "word" where the popular form prints "name"). The application follows at once: "So Romeo would, were he not Romeo called, / Retain that dear perfection which he owes / Without that title" (2.2.44-46). Then the argument becomes a bargain: "Romeo, doff thy name, / And for thy name, which is no part of thee, / Take all myself" (2.2.47-49). A fuller treatment of that reasoning in its scene is the subject of ['What's in a Name?': The Balcony's Big Question](/2023/08/04/whats-in-a-name-romeo-and-juliet/), which follows the name speech step by step; the concern here is what the world did to the sentence once the sentence left the scene.

Three features of the original argument matter for everything that follows, because the proverb drops all three. The first is the verb. Juliet's sentence is about an act of calling: "that which we call a rose" locates the name in human practice, in what a community of speakers does with its mouth. The claim is not that the flower carries its identity in secret; the claim is that the label is ours, pasted on from outside, and the scent is the flower's, arising from within. The second feature is the conditional mood. "Would smell as sweet" is a hypothesis about a counterfactual world in which the renaming has already happened. Juliet is not reporting an experiment; nobody in the play renames anything. She is reasoning about a world she wishes existed. The third feature is the direction of the proof. The rose is evidence about flowers conscripted to settle a question about persons. A flower's scent is a chemical property, indifferent to opinion. A young man's position in Verona is a social property, constituted by opinion. The argument borrows the certainty of the first kind of property to pay the debts of the second, and the borrowed certainty is exactly what the proverb spends, four centuries later, in disputes where the property at stake is social every time: reputation, allegiance, custom, trust.

Juliet herself marks the exchange as a transaction requiring consent, which a genuine law of nature would not need. She offers herself in payment for the discarded name: "for thy name... take all myself." A name that were really nothing would not need to be bought off with a bride. And the plea form betrays the doctrine within lines of its statement. "Deny thy father and refuse thy name" (2.2.34) is an imperative addressed to the same scene whose opening question, the subject of ['Wherefore Art Thou' and the Great Misreading](/2029/08/10/wherefore-art-thou-misreading/), asks why Romeo must be Romeo at all. One does not beg a man to deny and refuse a thing that is no part of him and weighs nothing. The asking concedes the weight.

### Can Romeo take a new name by declaring one?

No. The scene stages the failure in real time: Romeo vows a new baptism and, within moments, confesses that the old designation still governs him. A private renaming between two speakers in a garden has no registry, no witnesses with standing, and no force beyond the garden wall.

Romeo answers the plea with the vow itself: "I take thee at thy word. / Call me but love, and I'll be new baptized; / Henceforth I never will be Romeo" (2.2.50-51). The vow changes nothing Verona recognizes. Within moments he is confessing that the old designation still governs him: "By a name / I know not how to tell thee who I am. / My name, dear saint, is hateful to myself / Because it is an enemy to thee" (2.2.55-57). The play never shows the new designation used by a third person, not even by the Friar who marries them.

The scene's closing movement prices the supposedly weightless word in blood. Juliet, romantic doctrine forgotten, turns practical: "If they do see thee, they will murder thee" (2.2.70). They would not murder a scent or a perfection; they would murder the bearer of a name, in the orchard, on sight, because of what the name enrolls him in. Her terror is the scene's own peer review of her philosophy, delivered minutes after the philosophy. The proverb's career begins when later centuries promote the hypothesis and discard the review.

## The Text Beneath the Idiom: "Word", "Name", and the Editor's Amalgam

The familiar sentence is doubly detached from its scene: detached from the argument, and detached from any text Shakespeare's lifetime printed. The early texts divide at the crucial syllable. The Second Quarto of 1599, the fuller and more authoritative printing, reads "By any other word would smell as sweete", and the First Folio of 1623 follows it. The First Quarto of 1597 reads "By any other name would smell as sweet". Modern scholarly editions, built chiefly on the Second Quarto, print "word": the Folger Shakespeare, the Oxford text behind the Norton Shakespeare, and the school-slide texts that quote the passage for students all give "word" at 2.2.44. The world memorizes "name". The proverb's chosen word comes from the shorter, pirated, memorially reconstructed quarto, the text editors trust least; the word the study texts print is the word nobody quotes. That split is the working subject of ['A Rose by Any Other Name': Word or Name?](/2026/06/19/rose-by-any-other-name-textual-crux/), which adjudicates the reading as an editorial question; the idiom's history is the story of the losing variant winning the culture.

The amalgam goes deeper than one syllable. The standard modern form of the whole passage, the form in which the speech is anthologized and memorized, exists in no early printing at all. The Second Quarto dislocates the sentence about the body: "Nor arme, nor face. O be some other name / Belonging to a man", scattering the catalogue across a broken line. The First Quarto keeps the catalogue tidy but lacks other pieces. The familiar smooth text was assembled in the eighteenth century by Edmond Malone, who corrected the Second Quarto's dislocation from the First Quarto and stitched the witnesses into the speech posterity knows. The Complete Pelican Shakespeare, edited by Stephen Orgel and A. R. Braunmuller, uses precisely this passage in its essay on the play's texts to show students that the most famous speech in the tragedy is an editorial compilation. The idiom therefore quotes a sentence that no single early book contains, in a wording its copy-text rejects, shorn of the plea that gave it purpose. Quotation did to the line what Juliet asked Romeo to do to himself: it doffed the line's name and history and kept the scented part.

A second drift, grammatical rather than textual, did even more work. Juliet's sentence is subordinate: "that which we call a rose / By any other name would smell as sweet" hangs from her question "What's in a name?" and functions as evidence inside a larger plea. The proverb amputates the clause and reissues it as a free-standing assertion with the flower promoted to subject: "A rose by any other name would smell as sweet." Every element of the promotion matters. The question mark disappears, and with it the admission that a question was being asked. The agent disappears: Juliet's "we call" names the callers, the human community whose practice the argument examines, while the proverb's grammar has no callers at all, only a rose and an abstraction. And the verb "call" disappears, although calling is the entire subject. What remains is a claim about a flower's chemistry wearing the costume of a claim about human institutions, which is the precise equivocation Juliet's argument commits and the precise one her scene corrects. The drift is not carelessness. It is the culture selecting the version of the sentence that can be used without remembering the window, the plea, or the tomb: a motto about things, portable into any dispute where a label is under pressure.

Even the packaging of the sentence in reference books confesses the strain. Michael Macrone's Brush Up Your Shakespeare!, a popular quotation companion, glosses "What's in a name?" with Juliet's claim and then appends the play's rebuttal in the same breath: names, Juliet insists, ought to be separable from the things they name, but Romeo never does change his name, and it would not have done much good anyway. A phrase guide for general readers found it impossible to print the idiom without footnoting it against its own play. The dictionaries of the phrase preserve the flower; the fine print keeps surrendering to Verona.

## The Rose Itself: An Example That Argues Back

Juliet's choice of exhibit deserves its own hearing, because the rose is not a neutral laboratory specimen. A rose proves her point better than any other flower could, and worse. Better, because the rose's fame rests on a property that no naming ceremony confers and no renaming can revoke: scent is emitted by the flower's chemistry, indifferent to Latin binomials, trade designations and the opinions of gardeners. If any thing in the world is what it is regardless of its label, a scented flower is that thing, and Juliet, reaching in the dark for the strongest available instance, reached correctly. The proverb inherits that genuine strength every time it is applied to the properties of things: to chemistry, to weight, to the tensile strength of steel. A ton of iron by any other designation still anchors a ship.

Worse, because the rose arrived in the sentence trailing a story in which a rose-named woman failed the scent test completely. The medieval epitaph of Fair Rosamund, King Henry II's mistress, preserved in Higden's Polychronicon and repeated by Fabyan, Grafton, Stow, Holinshed and Camden, ran in Latin: "Hic jacet in tumba rosa mundi, non rosa munda" (here lies in the tomb the rose of the world, not a pure rose), and its sting was olfactory: she smells not sweet but rank, who was wont to smell full sweet. Editors since George Steevens and Malone have connected the passage to Samuel Daniel's Complaint of Rosamond of 1592, and the scholar Geoffrey G. Hiller has argued that the epitaph tradition stands behind Juliet's musings, alerting a reader to the wit of her example: the most famous rose-name in English legend belonged to a woman whose name, for posterity, lied about her smell. On Hiller's showing, Juliet's proof-flower carries a documented counterexample folded inside it, a rose whose designation promised sweetness and delivered its opposite, and whose story turns entirely on a pun between the world's rose and the pure rose. The example argues back.

The contamination runs through the play's own garden as well. Jonathan Goldberg, in "Romeo and Juliet's Open Rs", hears Rosaline inside the rose speech: the syllables of the discarded beloved, Rose-line, haunt the flower Juliet chooses to prove that names leave no residue. The point is not decorative. Romeo entered the orchard fresh from a passion whose object's name he had rhymed into sonnets, and the play's audience has watched a name operate as a drug on him for two acts. Juliet selects an emblem of purity for her experiment while standing in a plot where the previous exhibit, a woman never seen, has governed the hero's conduct entirely through her name and the verses it generated. The rose is the strongest proof available to Juliet and it is still not clean, and the dirt on it is not incidental to the proverb's later career: the phrase's users inherit an example whose legendary and dramatic contexts both insist that names and scents do not keep the tidy separation the sentence asserts.

There is also the quiet matter of what kind of fact a scent is. A scent is private evidence. Each nose verifies it alone, and no institution can overrule a nose. Juliet's argument takes the most privately verifiable property in the world and uses it to model the most publicly constituted property in Verona, membership in a house. The category slide is the argument's engine and its defect, and it explains the idiom's strange distribution across later disputes: triumphant wherever the property in question is privately or physically verifiable, and a failure wherever the property exists because a community agrees to treat it as existing. Gold is the first kind. Credit is the second. A feud, a brand, a reputation and a marriage are all the second, and it is in second-kind disputes that the proverb is most often invoked, usually by the side about to learn the difference.


## Mill's Unmeaning Marks: The Philosophy That Agrees With Juliet

Juliet's doctrine found its rigorous form two and a half centuries later, in the philosophy of language. John Stuart Mill, in A System of Logic (1843), divided names into those that carry information and those that merely point. General terms, he argued, both denote the things they apply to and connote the attributes in virtue of which they apply: "man" denotes each man and connotes humanity. Proper names are the pure opposite. "Proper names are not connotative: they denote the individuals who are called by them; but they do not indicate or imply any attributes as belonging to those individuals." A child called Paul, a dog called Caesar: the designations are "simply marks used to enable those individuals to be made subjects of discourse". Mill's sentence for the doctrine could be pasted into the orchard scene without disturbing its argument: "A proper name is but an unmeaning mark which we connect in our minds with the idea of the object, in order that whenever the mark meets our eyes or occurs to our thoughts, we may think of that individual object." His illustration even handles Juliet's counterfactual. A town may be named Dartmouth because it stands at the mouth of the Dart; if the river shifts its course, the town keeps its designation, because the designation was never a description. The reason for the choice falls away and the mark remains, pointing at the same place. That is "by any other name" translated into logic: for proper names, Mill rules the rose argument sound.

The ruling, however, comes with a boundary that Verona sits outside. Mill's marks are unmeaning because a community agrees to use them as pure pointers; the doctrine describes what a proper name does in the logic of discourse, not what a house name does in a walled city. "Romeo" approaches Mill's model: a baptismal label, arbitrary, transferable in principle. "Montague" does not. In the play's economy the house designation behaves exactly like one of Mill's connotative terms. It denotes the young man and it connotes a schedule of attributes in the minds of everyone who hears it: enemy, brawler, the party of the other side of the Prince's edict, fair game in the orchard. Tybalt does not hear a mark; he hears a meaning, and he acts on the meaning. Juliet's proof trades on the ambiguity. Her exhibit is a general term for a flower, her subject is a baptismal mark, and her target is a connotative house designation: three different instruments, one borrowed certainty. Mill, the language scholar the idiom most needs, thus splits the verdict that the proverb lumps together. The sentence is right about roses and about "Romeo". It is wrong about "Montague", and "Montague" is the only word in the speech that can kill.

### Does Mill's doctrine cover a house name like Montague?

No. Mill's unmeaning marks are individual labels whose whole function is to point at one bearer for purposes of discourse. A dynastic designation in the play does the work of his connotative general terms: it implies attributes, allegiance, enmity and a history of brawls, and every hearer reads the implication before the man.

The doctrine that vindicates Juliet's logic expressly excludes the class of word her argument has to move, which is why the play can leave her reasoning intact and still bury her conclusion in the same action. In Verona the implication is not read in a study; it is read in the square, by kinsmen with rapiers, and the reading is enforced before the bearer can introduce himself.

The older debate frames the same boundary. In Plato's Cratylus, Hermogenes argues that names rest on convention and agreement, that a thing keeps its nature under whatever designation a community settles on: Juliet's position, stated in Athens two thousand years before Verona. Cratylus holds the opposite, that names belong to things by nature. The dialogue refuses to crown either party outright, and Socrates ends by warning that knowledge of things is better sought from the things themselves than from their names, a caution that cuts against both camps' confidence. Juliet speaks as Hermogenes' heir without his opponent in the room, and the play supplies the opponent: not a philosopher but a city, whose practice answers that in human affairs the convention is the nature. A rose's sweetness survives a vote of gardeners. A Montague's standing does not survive a vote of Capulets, because the standing is the vote.

## Belsey's Counter-Reading: The Name Is Not a Coat

The strongest scholarly refusal of the proverb's reading comes from the criticism of the names theme itself. Catherine Belsey's essay "The Name of the Rose in Romeo and Juliet" (1993), extended in her Arden study Romeo and Juliet: Language and Writing (2014), treats the speech not as a motto about flowers but as an argument about language, and reads its conclusion against its performance. On Belsey's account, the speech demonstrates the opposite of what it asserts. Juliet can separate Romeo from "Montague" only in sentences, only at night, only while she believes herself unheard; the separation cannot be performed in the world the sentences describe, because identity in that world is made in language, assigned by fathers, registered by households, enforced by watchmen and kinsmen. The speaking subject does not stand behind her designations choosing them like garments. She is constituted by them, and her plea for a renaming is itself proof: if the designation were no part of him, she would not need to negotiate for its removal, and he would not need to be re-baptized to escape it. What escapes the naming system, in Belsey's reading, is desire, an unspeakable residue that language gestures toward and fails to capture. The rose speech is moving precisely because it fails: it states the liberal theory of the label with perfect clarity at the moment of its practical collapse.

The garment metaphor carries the refutation inside its own imagery. "Doff" is a clothing word: Romeo is invited to take off his designation as a man takes off a coat. But a coat is doffed by its wearer at will, and the scene's action shows a designation worn by everyone except its bearer. Capulet's servants, Tybalt, the Prince's edict, the Nurse's errands, Juliet's own first question at the feast ("Is she a Capulet?" is Romeo's; her matching discovery asks his house before his character). The designation circulates in other mouths and other ledgers, and none of those mouths consults Romeo. Belsey's reading adjudicates the speech's famous question with a negative the proverb never prints: there is a great deal in a name, and most of it is other people. The broader study of what designations do to persons in this play is taken up in [Names and Identity: Becoming and Unbecoming](/2024/04/12/names-and-identity-romeo-and-juliet/), which tracks the play's whole economy of becoming and unbecoming through renaming; the present argument borrows its conclusion for the idiom's trial.

Against Belsey, the Mill position retains one province intact. Nothing in the counter-reading touches the flower. The rose's scent is not constituted by the gardeners' agreement, and Belsey's case, like the play's, is a case about persons and their social designations, not about the properties of things. The disagreement therefore resolves into a partition rather than a winner. Where the named property is natural, Juliet and Mill are right and the proverb instructs. Where the named property is institutional, where it exists because people treat it as existing, Belsey and the tragedy are right and the proverb misleads. The idiom's four-century career is the history of that partition being ignored: a sentence licensed for the first province, spent in the second.

## Kripke's Baptism, and Why the Play's Own Word Gives the Game Away

Modern philosophy of language supplies a second test, and Romeo supplies its vocabulary unprompted. Saul Kripke's Naming and Necessity (1972) argues that a proper name is a rigid designator: it picks out the same individual in every possible world in which that individual exists, and its reference is fixed not by a description the name secretly carries but by an initial baptism and a chain of use passed from speaker to speaker. The doctrine grants Juliet her metaphysics and denies her remedy. Granted: the reference of "Romeo" is not a bundle of attributes; strip away every description, the feud, the house, the crimes, and the designation still lands on the same young man, who is himself throughout. Denied: that any speaker, even the bearer, can refound the chain by declaration. Reference, for Kripke, is historical and social all the way down; a baptism works because a community takes it up, transmits it, files it. Romeo's orchard ceremony has a congregation of two, one of whom is hidden in the dark, and its registry is a garden wall. "I'll be new baptized" is the play's own phrase for the operation, and the play lets the phrase stand as a vow while refusing it as an event: no later scene addresses him by the new designation, no record is amended, and Tybalt's chain of use for "Montague" runs unbroken through the same history that fixed it.

Set the three authorities in a row and the idiom's position becomes exact. Mill: a proper name is an unmeaning mark, so the scent survives relabeling. Kripke: the mark's reference is a historical chain, so relabeling is a public act or it is nothing. Belsey: for persons, the mark is constitutive in practice, so the unmeaning mark is a fiction speakers tell at windows. The three do not cancel one another; they describe three layers of one phenomenon, the chemistry of the thing, the mechanics of reference, and the sociology of the registry. Juliet's sentence asserts the first layer and quietly assumes the other two will obey it. The proverb, quoting her, assumes the same. Every documented failure of the idiom, from the orchard to the boardroom, is a case of the second or third layer declining to obey the first.

## The Play Votes Against Its Own Proverb

The tragedy's action is the controlled experiment Juliet's hypothesis requires, and the hypothesis fails it at every stage. Begin with the feud itself. The Prologue announces "Two households, both alike in dignity" and a quarrel descending "From ancient grudge break to new mutiny" (Prologue, 1-3), and in five acts no character, not the Prince, not the fathers, not the audience, ever learns what the grudge is about. The feud has no territory, no insult on record, no disputed inheritance, no founding murder that anyone cites. Its entire content is two designations. Remove "Montague" and "Capulet" from Verona and the war has no casus belli, no roster, no flag; the enmity is the names, worn smooth as coins. Juliet's philosophy holds that the designation is no part of the man. The play's premise is that the designations are the whole of the war. A city cannot doff a feud whose substance is vocabulary, and the experiment the lovers run, treating the designations as detachable, is terminated by the designations' owners.

The feast supplies the first measurement. Romeo, masked, asking about Juliet, learns the registry entry before anything else about her: "Is she a Capulet? / O dear account! My life is my foe's debt" (1.5.117-118). The metaphor is accounting, not botany: a designation creates a debt, an obligation enforceable against his life, instantly, before a word of courtship has been exchanged. Juliet's matching discovery closes the scene in the same ledger: "My only love sprung from my only hate! / Too early seen unknown, and known too late!" (1.5.137-138). Known: the catastrophe in both speeches is an act of identification, the attachment of a designation to a face. Nothing about the persons changes at the moment of naming; everything about their situation does. That is the social physics the rose sentence denies, demonstrated by the play before the sentence is even spoken.

Act 3 turns the demonstration lethal. Tybalt seeks Romeo in the square armed with nothing but the registry: "Romeo, the love I bear thee can afford / No better term than this: thou art a villain" (3.1.57-58). Villain is the designation the house system assigns, and Romeo's refusal to fight is itself a naming event misread by every witness: he will not say why the designation has lapsed, because the marriage that lapsed it is unregistered in public, a baptism with no congregation. Mercutio dies intervening in a quarrel whose terms he can see are false and cannot correct, cursing the two designations impartially. The scene is the orchard argument restaged at noon with an audience: the private renaming, valid in the garden, has no standing in the square, and the square is where designations do their work. Juliet hears the news in the matching register, reaching for the language of books and bindings, fair covers over vile matter, as if identity were a title page that had lied about its contents. Her imagery has already conceded Belsey's point: in Verona a person is read by the cover, and the cover is the designation, and the reading is enforced with rapiers.

The ending writes the verdict in gold. The lovers die as bearers of the designations they tried to shed, Romeo poisoned as a Montague exile, Juliet stabbed as a Capulet daughter in her family's vault, in a tomb whose population is a registry of house dead. The fathers, reconciling over the bodies, do not conclude that designations were nothing. They conclude the opposite, and they express it in the only currency Verona trusts: monuments. Montague vows a statue of Juliet "in pure gold", Capulet matches it for Romeo (5.3.298-303), and the designations are thereby fixed in metal at public expense, attached forever to the two corpses that tried to escape them. The Prince closes the case in the play's final couplet, and the couplet is a pair of names: "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3.309-310). The persons perish. The designations survive, get statues, get a story, get a title on a book. Juliet had argued that the designation is no part of the person and can be discarded while the person remains entire. The play answers that in the end the designation is the only part of the person that remains at all.

One more witness deserves a hearing, because it is Juliet herself, an hour later in story time, facing the Friar's potion. Her terror in 4.3 is not of death but of the vault: of waking among her ancestors' bones, among "the mangled Tybalt", in the place where her designation says she belongs. The girl who theorized that a designation is no part of a person cannot stop experiencing her Capulet registry as a physical address her body will be delivered to. The play does not mock her for the inconsistency. It records, with a dramatist's fairness, that the theory of the detachable label is a theory of the orchard at midnight, and that by daylight its author lives in the registry like everyone else.

## Stein's Counter-Proverb: A Rose Is a Rose Is a Rose

The twentieth century produced a rival rose sentence, and the two patrol the same flower from opposite sides. Gertrude Stein wrote "Rose is a rose is a rose is a rose" in the poem "Sacred Emily", composed in 1913 and published in Geography and Plays in 1922. In the poem the first Rose is a woman's designation, a person before a flower, and Stein's glosses on the sentence push against Juliet in an instructive direction. The sentence, she maintained, records that using a thing's designation summons the thing itself, its image and its emotional weather: in that line, she said, "the rose is red for the first time in English poetry for a hundred years", because the repeated designation had scrubbed the word back to presence. Where Juliet argues that the label can be peeled away leaving the thing untouched, Stein argues that the label, repeated with attention, is how the thing gets back into the mind at all. Juliet's sentence dissolves the bond between word and thing. Stein's welds it. Between them they stake out the two poles of the modern debate, and it is worth noticing that the proverb market kept both in circulation, deploying Juliet's to dissolve attachments and Stein's to insist on them, as the dispute requires.

### Is Stein's rose a reply to Juliet, or an independent invention?

Independent in origin, a reply in effect. Stein's sentence grew from her own experiments with repetition and from the old problem of universals, in which a rose served philosophers as the standard example of a kind; its first Rose is a woman in her poem, not the Capulet daughter.

No documented debt to the balcony line exists. Yet once both sentences were proverbial, English speakers set them against each other as thesis and antithesis on the same flower, and the pairing does argumentative work neither author assigned it: the culture keeps a matched pair of rose mottoes, one for loosening names and one for tightening them.

Stein treated the sentence as a signature rather than a stray line. She returned to it across her work, varying its count of roses, letting a character in The World Is Round dream of carving it around a tree until the words met themselves, and building it into the circular devices with which she marked her pages. The gesture matters for the comparison. Juliet's sentence escaped its author and its speaker, and its career was conducted by strangers who cut it to fit their needs. Stein's sentence was managed by its maker like a trademark, repeated under supervision, kept in circulation as the sign of a single sensibility. Between them, the two roses exhibit the only two ways a phrase about names can live in the culture: as common property, cut and recut by the registry of quotation, or as a kept name, defended by its bearer. The balcony sentence became the first kind because its bearer died at thirteen inside a fiction. Stein, alive and exacting about her meanings, kept hers the second kind for the rest of her career.

Stein's sentence matters to the idiom's trial for a second reason. It demonstrates that a designation's emptiness or fullness is not a property of the word but of the practice around it. "Rose" in Juliet's mouth is a label because Juliet treats labels as costumes. "Rose" in Stein's line is an incarnation because Stein's repetition treats the word as a bell. The same four letters serve both doctrines, which is itself the deepest available comment on Juliet's: whether a designation is detachable depends on the community's habits of attachment, and communities, not speakers at windows, set those habits. Stein, the great collector of other people's sentences, built the counter-proverb out of the very mechanism, repetition, by which proverbs become law. Juliet's sentence was repeated into wisdom by people who dropped its context. Stein's was repeated into wisdom by design, context and all, its meaning being precisely that repetition does something to a word. The two roses mark the difference between a sentence that became a proverb by being misheard and a sentence engineered to be one.

## Eco's Naked Names: What Survives the Flower

The most famous modern book to put the two words together is a novel about the failure of interpretation, and its title is a citation of the whole quarrel. Umberto Eco's The Name of the Rose (1980) closes with a Latin hexameter adapted from Bernard of Cluny's De contemptu mundi, a twelfth-century poem of contempt for a perishing world: "stat rosa pristina nomine, nomina nuda tenemus", the pristine rose stands only in its designation, and we hold naked names. Bernard's verse mourned Rome: when the thing itself has crumbled, what remains in human hands is the word for it. Eco, a professor of semiotics before he was a novelist, explained in his Postscript that the title came to him virtually by chance and pleased him because the rose is a symbolic figure so rich in meanings that it hardly has any meaning left. He wanted a title that disorients, one worn smooth by significance until it points everywhere and nowhere. The explanation is Juliet's doctrine stood on its head. For Juliet, the designation is empty and the thing is full. For Eco, the designation is so full, freighted with centuries of symbolism, heraldry, mysticism and romance, that it behaves as if empty, and the novel's detective, William of Baskerville, an heir of the nominalist William of Ockham, spends seven days learning that the names in the abbey's library organize murders as efficiently as any feud organized Verona.

The symmetry with the play is exact and worth dwelling on, because it is the play's ending translated into bibliography. In Verona the lovers perish and their designations survive as statues and a title. In Eco's abbey the monastery and its library burn, the monks scatter, and what the narrator carries away is a list of designations, the naked names of books and men. Both works end with the word outliving the thing. The idiom, quoted in a branding meeting, asserts that this survival is a triviality, that the residue is weightless. The two greatest rose texts in the culture both end by weighing the residue and finding it the heaviest thing on the page. A semiotics professor and a tragedian, divided by four centuries, return the same verdict against the sentence uttered between them: the designation is what history keeps.

There is a further turn. Eco's Postscript notes that a title like his could only work because the rose had been used so much, by Dante, by the Roman de la Rose, by mystics and heralds and lovers, that its meaning had been spent down to a shine. Juliet's sentence has undergone the same spending. The phrase is quoted so readily because its context has been worn off it, and what circulates is the shine: the rhythm, the flower, the authority of a famous play. Eco chose a title whose emptiness was a documented effect of overuse. The idiom's users rarely notice that their proverb exhibits the same condition. "A rose by any other name" functions in a dispute the way Eco's title functions on a cover: as a prestigious sound whose argumentative content the speaker has not inspected, and whose prestige is itself a product of the naming system the sentence claims to transcend.

## The Peace Rose: Botany Runs the Experiment

Horticulture, alone among the fields where the idiom is quoted, has run Juliet's experiment under laboratory conditions, and its result is the most instructive document in the phrase's career. In 1935 the French breeder Francis Meilland made a cross and selected a seedling he recorded by number alone, 3-35-40, the third cross of the year, the fortieth selection. Budwood traveled to growers in several countries just before the war cut communications. Because the recipients could not confer, the one flower entered commerce under four designations: in France as 'Madame A. Meilland', a memorial to the breeder's dead mother Claudia; in Italy as 'Gioia', joy; in Germany as 'Gloria Dei', the glory of God; and in the United States, Sweden and Norway as 'Peace', christened at Pasadena on 29 April 1945, the day Berlin fell, for the world's greatest desire at that hour. Under the International Code of Nomenclature for Cultivated Plants, 'Madame A. Meilland' stands as the formal cultivar designation and the rest rank as trade or selling designations, one plant with a registry entry and a wardrobe of market names. It became the most successful garden rose of the century, the ancestor of much of the modern hybrid tea stock, and it smelled, in all four languages, exactly as sweet.

The christening itself was staged as an argument about what a name can do. At the Pasadena ceremony the actress Jinx Falkenburg released two white doves as the designation 'Peace' was pronounced over the flower, a piece of theatre that understood exactly what was being added to the plant: not information about its petals, which the crowd could inspect for itself, but a dedication, a public act fastening the flower to the hour's hope. The dedication worked commercially for generations. The cultivar became the standard against which hybrid teas were judged, and its seedlings colonized the catalogues: 'Garden Party', 'Royal Highness', 'Double Delight', 'Princesse de Monaco' and a regiment of other named descendants carry its blood, each announced to the market under a designation chosen to promise a distinct pleasure. An industry that believed Juliet's sentence in its strong form would have sold the century's best rose under its seedling number and saved the ceremony. Instead it bought doves.

The case looks like a clean win for Juliet, and at the level of chemistry it is one. The petals did not read their labels. But the trade's own record refuses the proverb's stronger claim, that the designation is a nothing. If the designation were a nothing, one of the four would have done, and the industry would have saved itself the bookkeeping. Instead each name was retained because each performed a distinct piece of work: a son's memorial in the home market, a devotional claim in Germany, an emotion in Italy, and in America a name timed to a day in history so precisely that it turned a flower into a commemoration. Francis Meilland, reviewing the outcome in a 1971 account written for the American Rose Society, did not conclude that the names had been interchangeable decoration. He concluded the opposite: circumstances had given the rose different names in different countries, and each name reminded its public of a different good, praise of God, a smile at life, the wish for peace, a family's loving memory. The flower was one. The meanings were four, and the meanings were the product being sold alongside the plant. Botany's verdict thus replicates the partition the philosophers reached. For the properties of the thing, scent and form and vigor, the designation is a label and Juliet is right. For the life of the thing among people, the designation is a load-bearing structure, and the rose trade keeps four of them under one registry entry to prove it.

Roses make the point with unusual clarity because their naming system is honest about its own machinery. A cultivar has exactly one correct designation under the code, established by registration, while trade designations multiply freely on top of it, and everyone in the industry can tell the two layers apart. Human naming in Verona had the same two layers and no code to sort them: a baptismal entry underneath, and the house designations, nicknames and registry labels multiplying above. Juliet's argument requires the layers to be one, requires "Montague" to be as shallow as a selling name on a rose. The play's whole action is the discovery that it is not, that in the human registry the selling name and the soul have been filed in the same drawer. Gardeners, who rename roses for markets every season without a single duel, could have told her the conditions under which her sentence holds: it holds for things whose value is in their properties. It stops holding the moment the value is in the story, and a house name in a feuding city is nothing but story.

## Consignia and Monday: The Corporate Record Votes No

Modern commerce has funded the idiom's largest field trials, and the two best documented ones returned verdicts for the play. In January 2001 the British Post Office group, a five-century-old institution, renamed itself Consignia, an invented word produced by the consultancy Dragon Brands for a fee reported at half a million pounds, defended by its chief executive John Roberts as modern, meaningful and entirely appropriate, and intended to announce that the group did more than deliver mail. The deliveries did not change. The vans, the counters, the letters and the staff were the same the morning after the launch as the morning before: as pure an instance of Juliet's counterfactual as business affords, the same rose under another designation. The public, the workforce and the press refused the premise. The Communication Workers Union boycotted the word. A BBC business verdict ran under the headline "Consignia: nine letters that spelled fiasco", and when the reversal came in June 2002, sixteen months after the launch, The Independent described the scrapping as the abandonment of one of the most disastrous corporate rebrandings ever undertaken. The group became Royal Mail Group, which is to say the experiment ended by restoring the old designation, at further cost, because the old designation turned out to be a constituent part of the institution. The thing had not changed. The name had been changed, and then changed back, at a total cost in fees and ridicule that no one would have paid for a nothing.

The second trial ran the same summer. In June 2002, PwC Consulting, preparing to separate from PricewaterhouseCoopers, announced through the branding agency Wolff Olins that its new designation would be Monday, supported by a campaign budgeted at 110 million dollars. The consultants, their clients, their methods and their offices were unchanged; only the word on the door was new. The word became the story. Press coverage reached for the songbook of Monday misery, a CNN survey found seventy-four percent of respondents asking what the firm had been thinking, the Financial Times called the choice beyond parody, and a comedy website registered the British domain of the launch address to post a taunt about the firm having its name taken. Within weeks the experiment was overtaken by IBM's agreement to buy the practice outright, and Monday died with the transaction, remembered in branding literature as form: the standard cautionary exhibit of a designation chosen as pure label by people who believed the substance would speak for itself. The substance never got the chance. The label spoke first, and loudest, and the market's judgment was delivered on the word before a single engagement could be credited to it.

Both trials exhibit the same mechanics as the square in Verona, at lower stakes and higher budgets. A designation in commerce is not a mark pasted on a finished substance; it is the handle by which strangers, customers, employees and journalists pick the substance up, and a handle that cuts the hand gets the substance dropped. The defenders of both renamings argued in Juliet's exact grammar: the service is unchanged, the people are unchanged, only the word is new. The grammar was flawless and the argument failed, because a corporate designation, like a house designation, is connotative in Mill's sense and historical in Kripke's: it arrives trailing associations no launch budget can launder, from the week's most dreaded morning to a five-hundred-year-old red pillar box. The branding industry, to its credit, keeps the receipts. Its textbooks and post-mortems treat Consignia and Monday the way the Prince treats the feud, as the standing evidence in the case, and its practitioners reach for Juliet's opening question, "What's in a name?", as the headline under which the evidence is filed, knowing the answer their archive gives: in commerce, everything the accountants cannot book.

### Why do branding consultants keep selling new names if names carry no weight?

Because the market for renamings is itself the refutation of the doctrine the renamings announce. Firms pay consultancies sums in the millions for designations precisely because boards believe, on evidence, that the word on the door moves customers, recruits and share prices; a designation that carried no weight could not command a fee.

The consultant's invoice is the commercial form of the Prince's edict, a public pricing of the power of a word, and it is issued by the same profession that then writes Juliet's sentence into the launch speech to soothe the staff.

## Two Careers in One Sentence: The Question and the Rose

Juliet's sentence entered the language as a pair, and the pair has since divorced, each half prospering separately. The first half, "What's in a name?", detached from its flower and became English's standing headline for any dispute in which a designation is contested. It requires no context, promises a verdict of nothing, and flatters the writer who uses it: the question performs worldly detachment while the article beneath it documents, line by line, the money, labor and fury the contested word commanded. Phrase guides gave it an entry of its own, separate from the rose, and the gloss they supply is tellingly double: the question insists that designations ought to be separable from the things they designate, and the glossator, Macrone among them, cannot resist appending that in the play the separation fails. A reference book's entry for a four-word question thus contains a miniature of the whole career: the motto on the surface, the refutation in the apparatus, and a readership that consumes the motto and skips the apparatus.

The second half, the rose clause, prospered in softer habitats: the greeting card, the wedding reading, the florist's advertisement, the valentine. Stripped of its question, it no longer argues; it consoles. A consolation requires no defense, and the rose clause in its proverb form has accordingly drifted further from the text than any other famous line of the play, acquiring its free-standing grammar, its promoted subject, its air of botanical law. The two halves serve opposing emotional functions in the culture. The question is the skeptic's instrument, deployed to dissolve a dispute. The clause is the romantic's instrument, deployed to bless a union across a boundary of names. They were spoken eleven seconds apart by one girl making one argument, and the argument's two halves cannot both be right in the uses to which they are put: the skeptic's question presupposes that the clause's comfort is an illusion, since a designation that can be questioned into nothingness at a headline writer's convenience is a designation whose bearers live at the mercy of other people's registries, which is exactly what the tragedy shows and exactly what the valentine denies.

The divorce has a lesson for quotation generally. A sentence's career is not governed by its author's design or its original force but by the shapes into which it can be cut without bleeding visibly. Juliet's argument was cut twice, once by Malone's generation into a smooth text and once by posterity into two mottoes, and each cut selected for portability against truth. What survives of a famous sentence is what fits through the needle's eye of a headline, a card, a launch speech. The rose got through because a flower fits anywhere. The feud, the statues, the Latin, the four names of one cultivar and the two failed renamings of one summer did not fit, and so the sentence travels the world unaccompanied by its evidence, the most successful refugee in English and the least examined. The stations catalogued in the note that follows are an attempt to reattach the evidence to the refugee.

In the classroom the line survives as a comprehension exercise, generations of students asked to translate it into plain English, in the process absorbing the amputated form as the text itself. In the language-teaching press it stands as a model idiom of English, a C1 exhibit, the flower doing duty for the whole doctrine of labels. Notice, finally, what the career does to the sentence's truth-value. In the orchard the sentence is a hypothesis, and a falsifiable one: rename Romeo socially, and observe. History ran the observation repeatedly, in registries, in rose catalogues, in rebranding launches, and returned a partitioned answer: true of properties, false of standings. A proverb cannot easily carry a partitioned answer, because a proverb's form is a verdict, not a table. So the culture did to the partitioned answer what it does to every unwieldy truth: it kept the portable half. The rose-idiom note exists to reattach the other half, the half that smells of the tomb.

## The Rose in the Schools: Philosophy's Oldest Exhibit

Juliet's flower was a philosophical exhibit long before it was a stage property, and the schools had been arguing over it for five hundred years when she picked it up. The medieval problem of universals asked what a general word stands for: whether "rose" names a real universal thing, a concept in the mind, or only the individual flowers. Roscelin of Compiègne, in the eleventh century, was reported by his opponents to have reduced universals to a flatus vocis, a breath of voice, a puff of air shaped by the mouth. Peter Abelard, a generation later, refined the attack and chose the rose as his test case: suppose every rose in the world had perished; the word would still function in sentences, still be understood, still pick out what it had picked out, which suggests that what a general term contributes survives the things themselves. William of Ockham, in the fourteenth century, built the nominalist settlement that Eco's detective inherits: universals are signs, not things, and the world contains only particulars and the names we impose on them. When Juliet reasons that the flower keeps its scent under any designation, she is reciting, in a garden, at night, the nominalist side of the schools' longest quarrel, with the schools' own flower as her witness.

The pedigree cuts both ways, and the article's verdict needs the complication registered. On one side, the lineage dignifies the sentence: this is not a schoolgirl's fancy but a position with arguments, held by formidable logicians, and it survives in Mill because the logicians were right about the logic of terms. On the other side, the lineage exposes the sentence's original sin of category. The universals debate is about general terms, about what "rose" or "man" stands for. Romeo's case is about a singular designation chained to a house, a feud and a registry of the dead. The schools never claimed that a person's civic identity dissolves when the breath of voice changes; Abelard's rose survives in sentences precisely because sentences are where names live, and the play is not a sentence. It is a city, where designations are cashed in conduct. The philosophical tradition, read whole rather than in its quotable half, therefore testifies for the partition this article has argued: the nominalists win the word, and the word is not the world.

## The Inverse Experiment: When the Name Stays and the Thing Changes

Commerce has also run the experiment backwards, and the backwards result is the cleaner measurement of what a designation carries. In April 1985 the Coca-Cola Company, armed with taste tests in which drinkers preferred a sweeter formula, replaced the drink itself while keeping the designation untouched. If Juliet's doctrine governed the market, the change should have passed unnoticed or been welcomed: the word on the can was identical, and by the company's own blind evidence the contents were improved, the scent sweeter under the same name. Instead the public revolted in defense of the vanished thing, deluging the company with complaints, organizing protest groups, and forcing, after seventy-nine days, the restoration of the original formula under the augmented designation Coca-Cola Classic. The episode is filed in business memory as New Coke, and its lesson is the mirror of Consignia's. There, the thing stayed and the designation changed, and the public treated the change as a loss. Here, the designation stayed and the thing changed, and the public treated the change as a theft. In both directions the market refused to price the two components separately. What a consumer holds is never a scent plus a label; it is the fused sign, and surgery on either half is felt in the whole.

The British confectionery shelf offers the same measurement at smaller scale. In 1990 the Mars company aligned its British bar Marathon with its international designation Snickers: the same chocolate, peanuts and nougat, a new word on the wrapper, adopted for the administrative convenience of a single global brand. The change was mourned in the press out of all proportion to its stakes, and the discarded designation retained enough affection to be revived decades later as a nostalgia edition, sold precisely on the value of the old word. A designation that were a nothing could not be sold back to the public as a premium. The wrapper word had accrued decades of lunchboxes, pocket money and habit, and the accrual was an asset the company's own accountants could measure in the sales of the revival. Verona's fathers, raising statues to two designations over two graves, understood the same accounting: a designation that a community has fed with memory becomes capital, and capital is not doffed by declaration.

Set the three commercial cases in a row and the idiom's commercial career comes into focus. Consignia says a designation cannot be treated as weightless by its owner. Monday says a designation speaks before the substance can. New Coke says the fusion runs both ways, that the public polices changes to the thing with the same jealousy it polices changes to the word. The sentence from the orchard survives in business speech as an incantation spoken over launches, and the business archive answers it with a trilogy of refutations, each purchased at a price Juliet's contemporaries would have recognized: the price of learning that a designation, once a community has invested in it, is part of the thing in the only court that sits on such questions, the court of other people's conduct.

## The Performer's Registry: Stage Names and the Symbol

No profession lives closer to Juliet's question than the one that renames people for a living, and its record is a long practical commentary on the speech. The entertainment industry renames as a matter of routine craft: Marion Morrison became John Wayne under studio direction, Frances Gumm became Judy Garland, Reginald Dwight rebuilt himself as Elton John. The practice presupposes the falsity of the proverb in its strong form. Studios and managers change designations because they believe, and their ledgers confirm, that the word on the marquee alters what audiences expect, forgive and pay for, that the same voice issues differently from behind a different name. Yet the industry's second law qualifies the first, and it is Kripke's law wearing greasepaint: once the new designation takes, it fuses. The manufactured name becomes the person for all public purposes, so that the discarded registry entry survives only as trivia, a pub-quiz answer trailing the star. A stage name succeeds by ceasing to be a label and becoming an identity, which is to say it succeeds by disproving, in its own case, the doctrine that justified its adoption.

The limit case arrived in 1993, when the performer Prince, in dispute with his record company, abandoned his designation altogether and adopted an unpronounceable symbol, part design, part sigil, no word at all. The maneuver was Juliet's argument staged as industrial action: the music unchanged, the man unchanged, the designation declared void, on the theory that what mattered was the work and not the mark. The public registry declined the baptism. Newspapers, broadcasters and fans, unable to print or pronounce the symbol, constructed a designation out of the refusal itself and called him the Artist Formerly Known as Prince, a name that is itself a citation of the discarded one. Reference, as Kripke's chain predicts, is not the bearer's to rewrite by decree; the community transmits the designation it can use, and the community's version carried the old name inside it like a watermark. When the contract expired in 2000, the performer resumed the designation Prince, the chain reasserting itself over the sigil. Verona could have supplied the moral without a single guitar: the orchard ceremony failed for want of a congregation, and the symbol failed in front of the largest congregation on earth, for the same reason. A renaming is an act of the registry or it is a wish.

The episode also clarifies why the idiom retains its glamour in the face of its record. The sentence flatters the renamed and the renamer alike; it promises that substance precedes designation and will outargue it. Every industry that traffics in persons knows the promise is half the transaction: the talent is real, and no designation manufactures a voice. But the other half is the registry, and the registry keeps voting the other way, in marquee fees, in trademark ledgers, in a symbol that the world's presses unanimously answered with the old name spelled out in the new one's definition. Juliet's question, asked in earnest by an industry for a century, keeps receiving Verona's answer.

## Civic Names: Cities Are Not Roses

The political registry returns the same answer at the largest scale. States rename cities because designations are understood, by governments and governed alike, to carry allegiance, memory and claim: the Turkish republic required Istanbul in place of Constantinople from 1930 and made the world's postal services comply; Bombay became Mumbai in 1995, the change argued for in the language of whose city it was; Peking yielded to Beijing as the pinyin romanization was adopted internationally, the same city respelled into a different allegiance of systems. Nobody in any of these transactions behaved as if the designation were a coat the place could be indifferent to wearing. The campaigns for and against each change were fought with the seriousness appropriate to property, because a civic designation is property: it is the title deed under which a population's past is stored. The rose sentence, quoted in such disputes by the side urging that nothing essential turns on the word, is always answered by the expenditure of the side that wins: if nothing turned on it, the treasury would not pay to change the signs at the border.

The parallel with the play is structural rather than decorative. Verona's houses are small states, and their designations function as civic names do, as compressed charters. To be called Montague in the square is to be read under a charter that predates the bearer and will outlast him, allocating friends, enemies, marriage prospects and the odds of surviving an orchard wall. Juliet's proposal asks for something no citizen of any renamed city has ever obtained by wishing: a private exemption from a public charter. The play's Prince, the only character with registry powers, uses them at the end not to abolish the designations but to publish the final account under them. Political history since has been one long footnote agreeing with the Prince. Designations among people are institutions, and institutions are changed, when they are changed at all, the way Istanbul was changed, by authority, enforcement and time, never by a lover's declaration in a garden that the word is no part of the man.

## The Property That Cannot Be Registered: Scent Against the Ledger

Juliet's proof deserves a precision it rarely gets, because the property she chooses is the least registrable property a thing can have. A scent cannot be filed. It cannot be witnessed by proxy, inherited, transferred, taxed, or entered in a ledger; it exists only at the nose of the person present, only while the flower exhales it, and it perishes with the flower. Every other term in her argument belongs to the opposite order of fact. A house designation is nothing but registry: it is witnessed by proxy constantly, inherited automatically, transferred by marriage, taxed in honor, and it survives its bearer by centuries, as the gold statues of the final scene attest. The argument's deepest structure is therefore a comparison between the most private property in nature and the most public artifact in society, with the conclusion smuggled across the gap. She might as well have argued that because a dream cannot be arrested, a citizen cannot be. The categories are not merely different; they are the two poles between which the whole play is stretched, the orchard of private sensation at one end and the square of public record at the other, and her sentence is the sound of the first pole claiming jurisdiction over the second.

The choice of scent has a further consequence for the idiom's afterlife, because it determines where the sentence can be honestly used. A scent is verified by presence. The proverb is therefore sound wherever verification is by presence: in gardens, kitchens, laboratories of the nose, anywhere the thing itself can be brought before the judge. It fails wherever verification is by record, which is most of adult life: credit, qualification, reputation, allegiance, precedent, brand. In record-worlds the designation is not a report on the thing; it is the access point to the thing, the handle by which the record is pulled from the shelf. A wine merchant who renamed his stock at random would learn in a season that the scent in the bottle, unchanged and excellent, had become unsellable, because buyers drink from the record first and the bottle second. Verona is a record-world with rapiers. Juliet, reasoning from her window above a garden, chose the one exhibit that record-worlds cannot use, and the proverb's users have been repeating the choice ever since, quoting the garden's evidence in the square's disputes.

There is a poignancy in the selection that criticism should not hurry past. Scent is also the property of the beloved that the play's lovers trade in from their first meeting: the feast, the orchard, the aubade, the tomb strewn with flowers by Paris, who brings "sweet flower" to a bridal bed that is a grave (5.3.12). Juliet thinks in scent because she is in the first hours of desire, when the beloved is apprehended by presence, in the dark, by voice and nearness, before the registry has been consulted in daylight. Her philosophy is the philosophy of the senses at midnight, exact for everything the senses can reach. The tragedy is what happens when daylight returns the lovers to the jurisdiction of the ledger, and the idiom is what happens when a culture takes midnight's philosophy for a daylight law.

## The Stage Keeps "Name": Performance Against the Edition

One more registry deserves inspection, because it shows the proverb defeating the edition in the edition's own house: the theatre. The American Shakespeare Center's rehearsal guidance for cutting and negotiating the text prints the three early versions of the passage side by side and introduces the modern one with a revealing label: the standard version, the form in which the speech runs in performance and memory, reads "By any other name", assembled from the witnesses by editorial compilation. Actors say "name". Audiences hear "name". The school texts that print "word" footnote a variant that the stage ignores, and the stage's choice is not ignorance but professional knowledge: a line whose entire argumentative point is the word name cannot be delivered with a synonym substituted at its climax without the audience feeling the seam, and feeling it at the exact syllable where four centuries of quotation have taught them what comes next. Performance is a registry too, kept in the memory of audiences, and its chain of transmission, to borrow Kripke's model, was fixed by the proverb's baptism long ago. An actor who sang "word" would be correct against the copy-text and false against the room.

The standoff is the idiom's whole career in miniature. The edition represents the authority of the document; the proverb represents the authority of use. Between them stands the actor, who must satisfy the room, and the room was taught the line by the culture, which took it from Malone's amalgam of a pirated quarto, which took it from a memory of a performance. Every link in that chain is a small act of the registry overriding the record, and the chain lands, by a pleasing recursion, on the very doctrine the line is used to deny: the form of the sentence that survives is the form the community transmitted, not the form the authorial document prescribes. "What's in a name?" asks the girl in the text that prints "word". "Everything the audience brought with it", answers the theatre that prints "name".

## The Weapon That Fires Backward: Both Sides of Every Renaming

A sentence that can be quoted by both parties to a dispute has usually stopped asserting anything, and the rose idiom's service record in renaming controversies shows the symptom in its purest form. When an institution renames, its defenders reach for Juliet: the substance is unchanged, the work continues, only the word is new, and a rose by any other name smells as sweet. When the renaming is resisted, the resisters reach for the same girl: if the designation is a nothing, why is the institution spending millions to change it, and why does the new word need a hundred-million-dollar campaign to teach the public to smell the sweetness? The Consignia affair ran both quotations in the same news cycle. The board's case was Juliet's premise wearing a suit: the group did more than deliver mail, the old designations misdescribed it, the service would be identical under the new word. The union's boycott and the press's ridicule ran on the reversed premise: the fury spent on the word proved the word was the substance's public body, and a management that could not see it had confessed its own diagnosis wrong. A proverb that arms both trenches is not a contribution to the argument; it is a decoration the argument wears, and its presence in a renaming debate is best read as a signal that the real stakes, registry stakes, are being politely unmentioned.

The double service explains the sentence's peculiar prestige. It allows a speaker to borrow Shakespeare's authority for a position while committing to nothing falsifiable, since the opposing speaker can borrow the same authority back at no cost. In that condition a quotation stops functioning as evidence and starts functioning as a crest, and the rose line's career in boardrooms and council chambers is largely a career of crests. The play anticipated even this. Verona's houses fight under designations that both sides treat as sacred and neither side can justify, empty of stated cause and full of enforced consequence: crests, precisely, whose emptiness is the source of their power to command. Juliet looked at the two crests and concluded that what is empty cannot bind. The play, the rose trade, the Post Office, the consultants and the symbol all return the same correction: emptiness is not the limit of a designation's power. It is, in registry after registry, the very form the power takes.

## Meeting the Motto First: The Idiom as the Play's Advance Guard

The strangest turn in the sentence's career is the one it performs on its own source. For a large share of its audience, the idiom is met before the play, and the play is then experienced as the idiom's illustration rather than its trial. Language-teaching materials build advanced lessons around the sentence as a specimen of English idiom, presenting the two lines, glossing them as the doctrine that changing a word does not change the thing, and moving on to exercises; the lesson's specimen is the amputated form, and the scene from which it was cut forms no part of the syllabus. School materials run the same order in reverse dress: the famous lines are excerpted into slide sets and quotation lists for translation into contemporary English, so that the student's first labor on the passage is to restate the motto in plainer words, a paraphrase exercise whose effect is to certify the motto as the passage's content. By the time such readers reach the orchard, in a classroom or a theatre, they arrive pre-persuaded. The plea sounds like a truth they have already learned elsewhere, and the play's three acts of refutation must argue uphill against a proverb the audience absorbed as settled fact before the curtain rose.

This advance-guard effect is not a complaint about teaching; it is a measurement of the sentence's power, and it explains a feature of the play's modern reputation that any rehearsal room discovers: the balcony scene is the one scene the audience can quote back, and quotation, once installed, resists reinterpretation. A line known by heart is heard as a monument, not as a move in an argument. The actor playing Juliet must therefore fight the audience's memory of her own sentence, delivering a desperate hypothesis to listeners who receive it as a framed motto, and the fight is structurally unfair because the motto had a four-century head start and the run of the world's classrooms. The textual record mirrors the imbalance with embarrassing fidelity. The classroom texts print "word", the reading the documents support; the classroom memory retains "name", the reading the motto installed; and the American Shakespeare Center's performance materials, compiled by practitioners rather than editors, simply concede the point and print the standard version the audience knows. Pedagogy, performance and proverb form a single registry here, transmitting the sentence in the shape the culture fixed, while the edition, correct and unread on this point, keeps the other shape in the apparatus.

The feedback loop completes itself in the way the play is marketed and mourned. The tragedy of the star-crossed lovers is sold to new audiences through its quotable surfaces, the balcony, the parting, the rose, and the surfaces are precisely the places where the play's characters assert the doctrines the play refutes. A culture that meets the motto first will reliably misremember the tragedy as a romance the feud interrupted from outside, rather than as an argument the lovers lost to the registry from inside. Restoring the sentence to its scene is therefore not antiquarian fuss. It is the single most consequential act of reading the play asks for, because the sentence is the door through which most readers enter, and the door was hung backwards by four centuries of quotation: what was written as a question the play answers no is encountered as an answer the play merely illustrates. The verdict that follows is offered as a correction of the door's hanging.

## The Verdict: Quote the Rose With Its Window Restored

The adjudication can be stated in full. Catherine Belsey, reading the speech as an argument about language, holds that identity is made in the designation and that the speech refutes itself in the uttering. John Stuart Mill, the language scholar, holds that a proper name is an unmeaning mark and that Juliet's logic is sound for the class of words he defines. Saul Kripke holds that reference is a historical chain descending from a baptism, which grants the mark its innocence and denies the speaker its remedy. The three positions conflict only where the proverb forces them onto one layer. Sorted by layer, they compose a single consistent account: a designation neither creates nor alters the properties of a thing; a designation's reference is public property, transferable only by public act; and a person's standing in a community is a property of exactly the public kind. Juliet's sentence is true in the first clause of that account, silent in the second, and false in the third, and her tragedy is the biography of the third clause. The editor's dispute resolves in parallel: Malone's amalgam, which put the First Quarto's "name" into the line the world memorizes, is indefensible as textual scholarship and unbeatable as cultural history, since the proverb is his construction's most successful descendant. The scholarly editions are right to print "word". The culture is unkillable in quoting "name". The idiom lives in the gap, and the gap is its native country.

The record assembled in this article amounts to three witnesses who were never in the same room and returned the same testimony. The gardener testifies that one flower can carry four designations across four nations and lose nothing of its scent, and that the four designations were nevertheless kept, paid for and ceremonially conferred, because each carried a meaning the flower could not carry itself. The registrar, in Eco's abbey and in Bernard's Latin, testifies that when the thing perishes the designation is the residue, the only part of the beloved that other hands can still hold. The market testifies, at the price of millions, that a designation changed in a launch speech can bankrupt the goodwill of a five-hundred-year institution and that a weekday's name can swallow a firm of thirty thousand consultants before its first engagement. Against these three witnesses the idiom offers one girl at one window reasoning from a flower at midnight. Her reasoning was flawless about the flower. The witnesses are unanimous about everything else, and everything else is where names do their work and their damage. A proverb that survives three unanimous witnesses against it is not being believed. It is being used, and the uses, in dispute after dispute, are the subject the sentence's career finally teaches.

### What does the proverb leave behind at the window?

Everything that makes the sentence tragic: a speaker who is thirteen, a listener she cannot see, a plea disguised as a proof, a bargain offering a person in exchange for a word, and a scene that answers the proof with terror of the watch within thirty lines.

The amputated sentence keeps the flower and the rhythm. Restoring the window restores the knowledge that the sentence was spoken by someone who needed it to be true, which is the least reliable credential a proposition can carry and the one quotation most reliably strips away.

The practical rule for quotation follows. Used of things, of cultivars, chemicals, cargoes and codes, the sentence states a modest truth with a great poet's music, and no harm follows. Used of persons, houses, firms, nations and reputations, it should be quoted the way Macrone's phrase book quotes it, with the play's rebuttal attached: Romeo never did change his name, and it would not have done much good anyway. The cliche quotes the rose emptily; restoring its context shows a girl arguing, beautifully and wrongly, that the word on her lover is a coat he can hang up, while the city outside the garden sharpens the word into the instrument of his death. The sentence deserves its fame. It does not deserve its authority. Quote it as the play's most seductive error, and it becomes, at last, what it was at the window: the sound of intelligence discovering, too late and in the dark, how much of a person other people keep in the name.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the idiom "a rose by any other name" mean when people use it outside the play?

In everyday use it means that a thing's nature matters more than its label: rename the thing and the thing itself, its qualities and value, stays exactly as it was. Speakers reach for it when a designation is under pressure, in debates about rebranding, stage names, renamed institutions and disputed titles, to argue that the argument about the word is a distraction from the substance underneath. The idiom is normally quoted as a complete sentence about a rose, with no reference to Juliet, the balcony or the feud, and it carries the serene authority of a proverb, as if it recorded a law of nature rather than one side of an argument that its own play goes on to refute.

### Q: What words does the popular proverb drop from Juliet's sentence, and what changes when they go?

The proverb drops the question that governs the sentence and the verb that is its subject. Juliet's lines hang from her question "What's in a name?" and read "That which we call a rose / By any other name would smell as sweet": a claim about calling, with the callers, "we", inside it. The free-standing proverb deletes the question, the callers and the verb "call", promotes the rose to subject, and issues a verdict about a flower's chemistry. What changes is the topic. Juliet argues about a human practice, the naming of a man; the proverb reports on a plant. The dropped words are precisely the ones that made the sentence an argument about names, and their disappearance is what lets the remainder pose as a law of things.

### Q: Did Juliet invent the idea that names are arbitrary labels?

No. The idea is the conventionalist side of philosophy's oldest argument about language. In Plato's dialogue Cratylus, Hermogenes maintains that names rest on convention and agreement, so a thing keeps its nature under whatever designation speakers settle on, while Cratylus holds that names belong to things by nature. The medieval schools then debated for centuries what general words stand for, with Roscelin reducing universals to a breath of voice and Abelard testing the question on the rose itself: the word would still signify if every rose had perished. Juliet speaks Hermogenes' position in a garden, with the schools' own flower as her example, apparently unaware that the opposing case in her city will be made not by a philosopher but by events.

### Q: What did John Stuart Mill claim about proper names, and where does his doctrine leave Juliet's argument?

Mill claimed, in A System of Logic (1843), that proper names denote their bearers without connoting any attributes: they are, in his phrase, unmeaning marks, attached to objects so that the objects can be made subjects of discourse, independent of whatever reason first suggested them. His doctrine leaves Juliet half vindicated and wholly exposed. Vindicated, because for a baptismal label like "Romeo" her logic is exactly Mill's: the mark carries no description, and the man survives its removal in every property that is his. Exposed, because "Montague" behaves in Verona like one of Mill's connotative terms, implying enmity, allegiance and history to every hearer. The doctrine licenses the rose sentence for proper names and refuses it to house names, and the speech needs it for a house name.

### Q: Why do quotation books so often print the rose line without the play's ending?

Because a quotation book's trade is portability, and the play's ending is the least portable part of the line. The two lines detach cleanly, scan beautifully and appear to state a complete thought, while the context, a plea that fails, a feud that wins, two statues and a closing couplet of names, would convert the entry from a motto into an argument. The strain shows even in the friendliest companions: Michael Macrone's Brush Up Your Shakespeare! glosses the line with Juliet's claim and immediately adds that Romeo never did change his name and it would not have done much good anyway. The fine print keeps surrendering to Verona, but fine print is not what gets quoted, framed or printed on cards.

### Q: What is Gertrude Stein's "a rose is a rose is a rose" asserting?

Stein's sentence asserts the identity of a thing with itself, and the power of its name to summon it. Written in the poem "Sacred Emily" in 1913 and published in Geography and Plays in 1922, it runs in full "Rose is a rose is a rose is a rose", opening on a woman's name before it reaches the flower. Stein glossed it as a restoration: by repetition the word is scrubbed of stale association until, as she put it, the rose is red for the first time in English poetry for a hundred years. Where Juliet argues that the label can be peeled off the thing, Stein argues that the label, attended to, is how the thing gets back into the mind. English keeps both sentences because naming disputes need both a solvent and a weld.

### Q: Why did Umberto Eco call his novel The Name of the Rose?

Eco explained in his Postscript that the title came to him virtually by chance and that he kept it because the rose is a symbolic figure so rich in meanings that it hardly has any meaning left: a title worn smooth by centuries of symbolism would disorient the reader and refuse to prescribe an interpretation. The choice is Juliet's doctrine inverted. For Juliet the designation is empty and the thing is full of itself; for Eco the designation is so overfilled, with Dante, the Roman de la Rose, mysticism, heraldry and romance, that it behaves like an empty one. His detective, William of Baskerville, heir of the nominalist William of Ockham, then spends the novel learning how much organizing power a library of names can exert over the men who live among them.

### Q: What does the Latin line at the end of Eco's novel claim survives a thing?

It claims that the name survives, and that names are all that survive. The hexameter, adapted from Bernard of Cluny's twelfth-century De contemptu mundi, reads "stat rosa pristina nomine, nomina nuda tenemus": the pristine rose stands only in its name; we hold naked names. Bernard's verse mourned a perished Rome; Eco transfers the sentence to the rose and closes his novel with it after the abbey and its library have burned. Read against Juliet, the line is the tomb's verdict in Latin. She had argued that the designation is no part of the person and can be discarded while the person remains. Bernard and Eco answer that when the person is gone, the designation is the only part remaining in human hands.

### Q: How many names did the Peace rose carry, and what did each one do?

One cultivar carried four commercial designations, and each did a distinct piece of work. Francis Meilland's seedling of 1935, registered by number as 3-35-40, was sold in France as 'Madame A. Meilland', a memorial to his dead mother; in Italy as 'Gioia', joy; in Germany as 'Gloria Dei', the glory of God; and in the United States, Sweden and Norway as 'Peace', christened at Pasadena on 29 April 1945, the day Berlin fell. The flower's scent and form were identical under all four. What differed was the meaning each market was offered: family piety, an emotion, a devotion, a commemoration. Meilland himself, writing in 1971, treated the four names not as interchangeable labels but as four goods the one rose could do, which is why the trade kept all of them.

### Q: What is the difference between a rose's cultivar name and its trade names?

The difference is between the registry and the shop window. Under the International Code of Nomenclature for Cultivated Plants, a cultivar has one formal designation, established for the variety and used to identify it exactly: for the famous case, 'Madame A. Meilland'. Trade or selling designations, such as 'Peace', 'Gioia' and 'Gloria Dei', are additional names under which the same plant may be marketed in different places, and they carry no standing in the registry. The rose trade thus operates openly with two layers of naming, a single registered identity beneath a wardrobe of commercial labels. Juliet's argument assumes the layers are one. The play's Verona had the same two layers, a baptismal entry beneath the house designations, and its tragedy is the discovery that the layers cannot be separated at will.

### Q: What happened when the Post Office renamed itself Consignia?

The name was rejected and reversed within sixteen months. In January 2001 the British Post Office group became Consignia, an invented word from the consultancy Dragon Brands, defended by its chief executive as modern, meaningful and entirely appropriate, while the service itself, the vans, counters and deliveries, continued unchanged. The public and the workforce refused the word: the postal union boycotted it, a BBC verdict called the nine letters a fiasco, and losses and ridicule mounted together. In June 2002 the group abandoned the name and became Royal Mail Group, restoring the old designation at further cost. The institution had run Juliet's counterfactual exactly, same thing under another name, and the market's answer was that the old name had been part of the institution all along.

### Q: Why did PwC Consulting's name Monday become a byword for a failed renaming?

Because the word consumed the firm it was meant to introduce. Announced in June 2002 through the agency Wolff Olins with a campaign budgeted at 110 million dollars, Monday named a consulting practice whose people, clients and methods were entirely unchanged. Coverage ignored the practice and feasted on the word: its dread-of-the-working-week associations, song lyrics about Mondays, a CNN survey in which seventy-four percent of respondents asked what the firm had been thinking, a Financial Times verdict of beyond parody, even a comedy website occupying the launch address's British domain. Within weeks IBM agreed to buy the practice, and the name died with the transaction. Branding literature keeps the case as the standard proof that a designation speaks before substance can, which is the play's doctrine at corporate scale.

### Q: When business writers reach for "What's in a name?", what story are they usually telling?

They are usually telling a story in which the name turned out to be the story. The question has become the headline formula for reporting on renamings, rebranding exercises and naming disputes, deployed with a confidence that the case at hand will show the word mattering more than the launch speech promised. One branding post-mortem opens its dissection of the Consignia affair with exactly the formula: "What's in a name? Well, in the case of the infamous Post Office/Consignia fiasco... about £2 million." The headline's irony is structural. Juliet's question expects the answer "nothing", and the journalist borrows it for articles whose entire content is the price, ridicule and reversal that the supposedly empty word commanded. The formula survives because its implied answer keeps being refuted by the news beneath it.

### Q: What do the Consignia and Monday cases together show about Juliet's claim?

Together they show its claim failing at the exact point where the play says it will. In both cases the substance was held constant and only the designation changed, which is Juliet's experimental design, and in both cases the designation determined the outcome: rejection, boycott, ridicule and reversal in one, absorption and oblivion in the other. Her claim requires a designation to be received as a neutral label. The corporate record shows designations being received as arguments, as insults, as jokes, as anything except neutral, because a public name in a community of strangers is connotative and historical, trailing associations its owners cannot launder. The cases also show her claim's defenders using her grammar, substance unchanged, word merely new, and losing with it, in boardrooms as in orchards.

### Q: Why does the idiom survive being wrong about people?

It survives because it is right about things, beautiful in form, and useful as a courtesy. About properties of things it states a truth no one disputes: the scent, the weight and the chemistry ignore the label. Its rhythm and its flower make it the most portable sentence Shakespeare ever wrote. And it lets a speaker end an awkward dispute gracefully, waving the label away without conceding the substance. Proverbs are not retained for their accuracy across all cases; they are retained for their fit to the cases where they are quoted and for the prestige of their source. The rose idiom is quoted in disputes about standings, where it is false, by speakers who need it to be true, which is precisely the situation of its first speaker at her window. The resemblance is not a coincidence; it is the sentence's natural habitat.

### Q: What happened when the performer Prince tried to do without a name?

The public registry refused the experiment and named him out of the refusal. In 1993, in dispute with his record company, Prince abandoned his designation for an unpronounceable symbol, on the theory that the music and the man were unchanged and the mark was void. Newspapers and broadcasters, unable to print or say the symbol, called him the Artist Formerly Known as Prince, a designation that carries the discarded name inside itself. The episode is the orchard scene at industrial scale: a private re-baptism, however sincere, has no force until a community takes it up, and this community voted, unanimously, for the old chain of reference. In 2000, when the contract expired, he resumed the name Prince, and the registry closed over the sigil as if settling an account.

### Q: What older rose-and-name story stands behind Juliet's example?

The epitaph of Fair Rosamund, Henry II's mistress, stands behind it, a rose whose name lied about her smell. Preserved in Higden's Polychronicon and repeated by Fabyan, Grafton, Stow, Holinshed and Camden, the Latin ran "Hic jacet in tumba rosa mundi, non rosa munda": here lies the rose of the world, not a pure rose, and its sting was that she smells not sweet but rank, who was wont to smell full sweet. Editors since Steevens and Malone connected the tradition to Samuel Daniel's Complaint of Rosamond (1592), and Geoffrey G. Hiller has argued that it informs Juliet's choice of example. The wit is cutting: the most famous rose-name in English legend belonged to a woman whose posterity is precisely a failure of scent to match designation, the counterexample folded inside Juliet's proof.

### Q: When is the rose idiom sound advice, and when is it a trap?

It is sound advice wherever the property at stake can be verified by presence, and a trap wherever the property exists by record. Quote it freely of flowers, chemicals, cargoes and code, of anything whose worth sits in its measurable nature: there the label is a label, and Juliet and Mill are right. Withhold it of persons, houses, firms, cities and reputations, of anything whose worth is a standing in other people's conduct: there the designation is an institution, connotative, historical and enforced, and the play, the rose trade's four names, the Post Office's reversal and the consultants' lost weekday all vote the other way. The test to apply before quoting is Juliet's own scene: if a watchman would act on the name, the name is part of the thing.
