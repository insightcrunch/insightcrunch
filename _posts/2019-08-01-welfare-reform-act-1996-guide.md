The operative question in welfare reform was not how generous cash assistance to poor families ought to be, but whether a family that met eligibility rules held a legal right to receive assistance at all. Before August 1996, federal law answered that question in the affirmative for families covered by Aid to Families with Dependent Children, and federal money followed eligibility without a fixed ceiling. Congress legislated against a structure in which enrollment, expenditure, and federal obligation rose together by operation of law, in which receipt of benefits carried no federal lifetime limit, and in which work conditions operated inside an entitlement that a state lacked authority to end for a family that met the governing eligibility tests.

![Welfare Reform Act signing and the end of the AFDC entitlement - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-87.webp)

The statute that answered the question bears the formal name Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996. Congress enacted the measure as H.R. 3734 in the 104th Congress, and the measure became Public Law 104-193 upon signature by President William J. Clinton on August 22, 1996, with publication at 110 Stat. 2105. The long title described a reconciliation bill, a procedural vehicle that limited Senate debate and required only a simple majority for passage. Title I carried the cash assistance provisions, and other titles addressed immigrant eligibility, child support, childhood disability, food assistance, child care, and child protection, so the statute reached far beyond the single program named in most public debate over enactment.

The namable claim of the statute can be stated plainly: one sentence did it. The provision at 42 U.S.C. 601(b), placed in section 401 of the Social Security Act under the heading No individual entitlement, provides that this part shall not be interpreted to entitle any individual or family to assistance under any State program funded under this part. That sentence, rather than the work rules, forms the structural core of the statute for two reasons. Work rules regulate the conduct of persons inside a program, while the sentence determines whether a program confers a right at all. Time limits, sanctions, and participation rates all presuppose a population whose presence on the rolls a state controls, and control of that kind exists only after entitlement ends. With entitlement intact, each of those devices would have operated as a condition attached to a claim that courts could enforce. With entitlement removed, a state could define eligibility, set benefit levels, and close a category of spending without breaching a federal promise to any family.

Finance changed in the same direction. Section 603 of the Social Security Act replaced open ended federal matching with a state family assistance grant of 16.489 billion dollars, an amount derived from federal spending in base years in the early and middle 1990s and left unadjusted for inflation, population change, or caseload movement. Section 608 barred the use of federal grant funds for a family that included an adult who had received sixty months of federally funded assistance, subject to hardship exemptions capped at 20 percent of the caseload. Section 607 imposed participation rates on states as institutions, softened by a caseload reduction credit measured from fiscal year 1995. Each substitution transferred risk away from the federal Treasury. Caseload risk, inflation risk, and recession risk passed to state budgets and, where a state declined to absorb a risk with state funds, to families whose assistance a state plan defined, reduced, or ended.

The route of the article follows that structure. The opening section traces the program that the statute replaced, from Aid to Dependent Children in the Social Security Act of 1935 through the Family Support Act of 1988 and the state waiver demonstrations of the early 1990s. A passage section then recounts two vetoes, a revised third bill, the removal of Medicaid restructuring, and the resignations of three officials. The operative core receives direct treatment through the entitlement sentence, the block grant, the sixty month clock, and the participation rates, followed by a table that sets the former and successor programs side by side. Later sections cover the remaining titles, amendment and partial restoration and litigation, and the measured effects of the statute, together with the limits of the evidence that those effects rest upon.

## Origins and the Program That Was Replaced

The Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996 did not create a federal cash assistance system for families with children. The Act dismantled one. For sixty one years before enactment, federal law had guaranteed a different arrangement, under which a family that met state and federal eligibility rules held a legal claim to aid, and the federal government paid a share of whatever a state spent in honoring that claim, without a fixed ceiling on total federal outlays. The central conflict in the decades before 1996 therefore concerned structure more than generosity. Benefit levels varied widely among states and fell in real terms across long stretches, and political attack on the program was sustained. None of those developments altered the underlying architecture. Eligibility generated entitlement, entitlement generated expenditure, and expenditure generated a federal matching payment. Reform proposals that left that sequence intact changed conditions at the margins. Proposals that broke the sequence changed the nature of the program itself.

### A Small Title Inside a Larger Settlement

Congress enacted the Social Security Act on August 14, 1935, as Public Law 74-271, during the Depression, when state and local relief systems financed largely through property taxes had been overwhelmed by mass unemployment. Title I of the Act provided federal grants for old age assistance, Title II created the contributory old age insurance system, and Title X addressed aid to the blind. Title IV, occupying only a few pages of the statute, created Aid to Dependent Children, known by the initials ADC. The title authorized federal grants to states for the support of needy dependent children, defined in the statute as children deprived of parental support or care because a parent had died, had become incapacitated, or was absent from the home, and who lived with a parent or another specified relative.

The program federalized an older state practice rather than inventing a new category of relief. By 1931, according to the historical summary prepared by the Office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation in the Department of Health and Human Services, mothers pension laws operated in every state except Georgia and South Carolina, and approximately 200,000 children lived in homes supported in part by such pensions. Those state laws directed local governments to pay small allowances, usually to widows, so that children could remain at home rather than enter an institution, and local officials applied moral tests concerning the suitability of the home. Title IV preserved state administration and state discretion over need standards and payment levels, while adding a federal financial contribution and federal conditions, including submission of a state plan for federal approval and a right to a fair hearing for an applicant denied aid.

The original federal share was modest and bounded at the level of the individual payment. In the 1935 Act, Congress set the federal contribution at one third of state expenditure, applied to maximum countable payments of 18 dollars per month for a first child and 12 dollars per month for each additional child, so that the federal government paid no more than 6 dollars for the first child in a family under the original formula. No federal payment for the caretaker relative formed part of the original design, because the statutory beneficiary was the child rather than the parent. Amendments in 1950 extended federal sharing to the needs of a caretaker relative living with the child. Legislation enacted in 1961 permitted states, at state option, to aid families in which a parent was unemployed, and the Public Welfare Amendments of 1962, Public Law 87-543, renamed the program Aid to Families with Dependent Children, known as AFDC. The Congressional Research Service records the 1962 renaming as a reflection of the broader family focus that the new coverage expressed.

One development in 1939 reshaped the caseload without changing the text of Title IV. Amendments in that year added survivors insurance to the contributory system, and widows with children who qualified through a deceased wage earner moved in large numbers into the insurance program, which paid benefits as a matter of earned right and carried no means test. The historical literature summarized by the Social Welfare History Project at Virginia Commonwealth University describes the consequence plainly: the insurance title absorbed the category of recipient that public debate in the 1930s had treated as the core case for ADC, and Title IV served a growing proportion of families formed through divorce, separation, desertion, and birth outside marriage.

### Entitlement and Matching as the Operating System

Two features of the postwar AFDC program mattered more than any benefit schedule. The first feature was individual entitlement. Federal law required a participating state to furnish aid promptly to all eligible persons under the approved state plan, and eligibility turned on categorical and financial tests rather than on the availability of an appropriation or a place in a capped enrollment. A family that met the tests held a claim that federal courts enforced as a statutory right. In Goldberg v. Kelly, decided in 1970, the Supreme Court held that the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment required an evidentiary hearing before a state terminated AFDC benefits. In King v. Smith, decided in 1968, the Court invalidated an Alabama substitute father rule under which the state denied aid to children whose mother cohabited with a man, holding that federal law defined parental absence by legal duty of support. The decisions did not set benefit levels. The decisions nonetheless confirmed that AFDC operated as law binding on administrators, not as charity distributed at administrative pleasure.

The second feature was open ended matching. Congress replaced the original per child maximums through a series of postwar amendments, and after the enactment of Medicaid in 1965, states that operated Medicaid programs claimed AFDC reimbursement under the Federal Medical Assistance Percentage, the matching formula inversely related to state per capita income. The Congressional Research Service, in its primer on Temporary Assistance for Needy Families financing, gives the statutory range for the fifty states and the District of Columbia as a federal share between a floor of 50 percent for the states with the highest per capita incomes and a ceiling of 83 percent for the states with the lowest per capita incomes, and describes the AFDC grant as open ended, meaning that no limit restricted total federal funding for those jurisdictions. A state that spent an additional dollar on assistance drew down an additional federal contribution of between fifty and eighty three cents. A state whose caseload rose during a recession received additional federal money in proportion to the rise, without exhausting a fixed annual allotment.

The combination of the two features produced automatic expansion. Enrollment responded to economic conditions, demographic change, court decisions, and amendments that widened covered categories, and expenditure followed enrollment as a matter of arithmetic. Congress retained full legal authority to amend the statute at any time. Between amendments, however, Congress exercised no annual control over the total, because appropriations for AFDC functioned as estimates of an obligation created in the eligibility rules themselves. As the Congressional Research Service summarized the predecessor system, funding was provided on an open ended, unlimited basis, and the federal government reimbursed states for a share of expenditures without limit.

### Growth in the Caseload and the Politics of Dependency

For the first twenty five years of operation, ADC remained a small program relative to the insurance titles of the same Act. State payment standards stayed low, and restrictive local administration kept many eligible families outside the rolls. The caseload transformation arrived in the 1960s. Tables compiled by the Office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation in the Department of Health and Human Services record an average monthly caseload of 924,000 families and 3,593,000 recipients in fiscal year 1962, rising to 1,909,000 families and 7,429,000 recipients in fiscal year 1970, and reaching 3,124,000 families and 11,038,000 recipients in fiscal year 1973. In eleven fiscal years, the number of recipient families more than tripled. Federal amendments widened coverage, federal courts struck down state rules that had excluded families through residency requirements and substitute father rules, and the National Welfare Rights Organization, founded in 1966, organized recipients to claim benefits as legal rights. Underlying family structure changed as well, as divorce rates and births outside marriage rose.

Political framing changed with the numbers. During the 1930s and 1940s, debate over ADC had centered on adequacy: whether grants sufficed to keep children in decent homes. During the late 1960s and the 1970s, debate increasingly centered on dependency. President Ronald Reagan presented long term receipt of aid as evidence that federal programs rewarded nonwork and family breakup, and the Reagan administration secured amendments in the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1981 that tightened eligibility and limited earnings disregards. Charles Murray, in Losing Ground, published in 1984, argued that the expansion of means tested benefits during the 1960s had raised the relative return to nonwork and childbearing outside marriage. Lawrence M. Mead, in Beyond Entitlement, published in 1986, argued that permissive administration rather than benefit levels constituted the central defect, and that government held an obligation to require work as a condition of aid. Researchers at the Institute for Research on Poverty at the University of Wisconsin, among others, disputed those accounts, attributing the caseload rise of the 1960s principally to increased participation among families already eligible under law. Writers in the Murray and Mead traditions treated the entitlement itself as the engine of the problem, since an entitlement paid benefits without requiring conduct and expanded without an annual legislative decision. Defenders of the program in the tradition of the National Welfare Rights Organization and the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities treated entitlement as the protection that prevented states from denying aid to eligible children. Both readings agreed on the mechanics. Aid followed eligibility automatically.

Caseload movement after the early 1970s reinforced the sense among legislators that enrollment answered to forces outside annual legislative control. The tables compiled by the Office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation record 3,974,000 families and 11,460,000 recipients in fiscal year 1990, followed by 5,046,000 families and 14,226,000 recipients in fiscal year 1994, the highest average monthly caseload in the history of the program. The Congressional Research Service identifies the record caseload of 1994 as a condition that helped set the stage for the legislative debates of 1995 and 1996. Expenditure followed the same curve, because the matching structure converted each additional case into additional spending without any separate decision to expand the program.

### A Guaranteed Income Proposed and Defeated

The first attempt to replace the structure, rather than adjust the structure, came from a Republican administration. On August 8, 1969, President Richard Nixon addressed the nation on television and proposed the Family Assistance Plan, drafted within the administration with the participation of Daniel Patrick Moynihan, the Assistant to the President for Urban Affairs. The plan would have repealed AFDC and substituted a federally administered income floor for all families with children, including two parent families and families with an employed head, categories that AFDC covered only partially and unevenly among states. Families with no other income would have received a basic federal payment, and earnings would have reduced the payment gradually rather than dollar for dollar. Adult recipients classified as employable faced a requirement to accept work or training as a condition of the family payment.

The plan passed the House of Representatives in 1970 and again in 1971, embedded in broader Social Security legislation, and failed in the Senate. The Senate Finance Committee, chaired by Senator Russell Long of Louisiana, declined to report a version acceptable to the administration, and in 1972 the family assistance provisions were dropped in conference from the legislation that created Supplemental Security Income. Opposition came from opposed directions that could not be reconciled within a single bill. The National Welfare Rights Organization campaigned against the plan on the ground that the proposed floor was inadequate, and demanded a substantially higher guaranteed income without a work condition. Conservative members objected that a federal floor would extend payments to the working poor on a large scale and raise federal expenditure. No coalition existed in the Senate of 1970 to 1972 willing to set a national benefit policy and absorb the fiscal exposure that a guarantee created, and AFDC survived the first serious repeal effort with the entitlement and the matching grant intact.

Congress instead pursued conduct requirements inside the existing structure. The Social Security Amendments of 1967, Public Law 90-248, had already created the Work Incentive Program, known as WIN, introduced earnings disregards designed to make employment pay, and enacted the first federal work requirements directed at AFDC mothers. Exemptions for mothers of young children kept the obligated population limited, and appropriations for services ran below the level that full implementation required. Across two decades, Congress layered expectations concerning work and child support enforcement onto a payment system whose core transaction remained governed by entitlement rules. The amendments changed what administrators asked of recipients. The amendments did not change what happened when a recession, a court order, or a demographic shift produced additional eligible families.

### Mutual Obligation Inside the Entitlement

The Family Support Act of 1988, Public Law 100-485, enacted on October 13, 1988, represented the most complete expression of the inside the structure strategy, and the Congressional Research Service and the Department of Health and Human Services both treat the Act as the direct precursor of the 1996 statute. The Act amended Title IV to emphasize work, child support, and family benefits, and replaced WIN with the Job Opportunities and Basic Skills Training program, known as JOBS. Every state was required to operate a JOBS program by October 1, 1990, with statewide operation required by October 1, 1992. States assessed the needs of recipients, entered into employability plans with individual recipients, and provided activities ranging from basic education and vocational training to job search and community work experience. Federal law for the first time required participation from parents whose youngest child had reached age three, subject to exemptions and to the availability of child care, and directed states to concentrate resources on groups facing a high risk of long term receipt.

The conceptual language of the Act supplied vocabulary that later legislation retained. The National Evaluation of Welfare to Work Strategies, conducted for the Department of Health and Human Services, describes the Family Support Act as establishing a system of mutual obligation within the AFDC benefit entitlement structure, under which government was to provide education, employment, and support services, and recipients were in turn required to participate in JOBS. Child support provisions required automatic wage withholding for support orders enforced through the state child support agency and set paternity establishment performance standards for states. The Act guaranteed child care for recipients participating in approved activities and provided transitional child care and Medicaid coverage for twelve months after a family left the rolls because of earnings, addressing two mechanisms through which employment could leave a family worse off than receipt of aid had left the family.

Implementation exposed the limit that the entitlement structure imposed on an obligation based strategy. JOBS received capped appropriations distinct from the open ended assistance account, and federal JOBS funding required state matching contributions that fiscally strained states did not draw down in full during the recession of the early 1990s. The share of the caseload engaged in a JOBS activity in any given month remained a minority of adult recipients, a result that administrators attributed in state reports to funding levels, child care supply, and the size of exemption categories. A recipient who declined to participate faced a sanction that reduced the family grant rather than ending eligibility altogether, because the underlying statute conferred eligibility on the family unit and protected the child portion of the grant. Reciprocity written into an entitlement left the entitlement as the operative fact.

### Waiver Laboratories and a Campaign Promise

Section 1115 of the Social Security Act, enacted in 1962, authorized the Secretary of Health and Human Services to waive specified state plan requirements of AFDC for experimental, pilot, or demonstration projects likely to promote the objectives of the program. For three decades the authority supported small research demonstrations. Under Presidents Reagan and George H. W. Bush, governors began to seek waivers for policy changes of wider scope. The practice expanded sharply after January 1993. The Congressional Research Service records that the Clinton administration granted waivers of AFDC law to forty three states before enactment of the 1996 law, allowing demonstrations that ranged from small county projects to statewide programs.

Wisconsin under Governor Tommy Thompson, who took office in January 1987, became the most cited laboratory in congressional debate. The Thompson administration obtained a sequence of waivers covering earnings disregards, asset rules, and benefit conditions, and Wisconsin reported a caseload decline of 21 percent between January 1987 and January 1994, a figure that the Institute for Research on Poverty at the University of Wisconsin recorded with the caution that most of the decline accumulated before the waiver initiatives were operating. The Work Not Welfare demonstration, approved by the Department of Health and Human Services in November 1993 and begun in January 1995 in Fond du Lac County and Pierce County, tested the element that federal law otherwise forbade: a time limit. Participants signed agreements to work for benefits, entered employment, education, or training within a short period after enrollment, and faced termination of cash benefits after two years of receipt, with eligibility barred for three years after termination. Enrollment remained under one thousand cases. Wisconsin Works, the statewide replacement plan developed under Thompson during 1995, translated the demonstration logic into a full program design built on immediate work attachment and a lifetime limit. Other states tested family caps, benefit conditions tied to school attendance, and earnings disregards larger than federal rules permitted. Each demonstration required a waiver precisely because the entitlement statute did not allow a state to impose the condition under test.

National politics supplied the demand that the demonstrations answered. In the presidential campaign of 1992, Governor Bill Clinton of Arkansas promised to end welfare as it was known, a formulation paired with a specific design: two years of education, training, and support services, followed by a requirement that recipients able to work take employment in the private sector or in a community service position. The campaign proposal extended the logic of the Family Support Act by attaching a time horizon to receipt that the 1988 Act had not contained. After the inauguration in January 1993, the administration developed a legislative proposal along those lines while approving state waivers at an accelerating pace, and the proposal reached Congress in 1994 without receiving consideration on the floor of either chamber. When voters in November 1994 returned a Republican majority in the House of Representatives for the first time in forty years, the incoming majority arrived with a written platform, the Contract with America, that contained a welfare proposal requiring work after two years, imposing a five year lifetime limit, and converting federal funding into a capped form.

The analytic thread that runs from Title IV in 1935 through the waiver demonstrations of 1994 is structural. A program built on individual entitlement and open ended matching expands automatically with need and finances whatever volume of eligibility the statute and the courts define, at whatever benefit level each state selects. Conditions attached to receipt inside such a program operate downstream of the financing decision, because the financing decision has already been made by the eligibility rules. Work requirements in 1967, earnings rules in 1981, and mutual obligation in 1988 each altered the experience of families on the rolls and each left the rolls themselves, and the federal obligation that followed the rolls, to be determined by forces the requirements did not govern. A reform intended to control the size, duration, and federal cost of cash assistance therefore had to legislate about entitlement and financing directly: either to end the individual entitlement, or to cap the federal contribution, or to limit the duration of federally financed receipt, or to adopt some combination of those measures. The bills introduced in the 104th Congress in 1995, and the presidential vetoes and negotiations that followed the bills, belong to the account of enactment that a companion section of this pillar presents.

## Passage: Two Vetoes, a Third Bill, and the Resignations

Public Law 104-193 reached the statute book only after two earlier measures had been returned without presidential approval, and the third measure differed from the vetoed pair in one respect that proved decisive for the administration of President William J. Clinton. The vehicle was a budget reconciliation bill, H.R. 3734 in the 104th Congress, and the welfare provisions inside the bill had a lineage that ran through the Personal Responsibility Act, the welfare title of the Contract with America program of the House Republican majority elected in 1994, and through a freestanding bill, H.R. 4, vetoed in January 1996. Between that veto and the signing of August 22, 1996, sponsors revised the package in response to a bipartisan proposal from the National Governors Association, removed a proposed restructuring of Medicaid, and assembled wide cross party majorities in both chambers. Three senior officials of the Department of Health and Human Services then resigned in protest, in a sequence that began on the morning after the President declared an intention to sign.

### The Reconciliation Vehicle

Reconciliation is a procedure created by the Congressional Budget Act of 1974 for legislation that changes spending, revenue, or the debt limit in conformity with a budget resolution adopted by both chambers. A budget resolution may direct committees to report legislation meeting specified budgetary targets, and the resulting reconciliation bill receives expedited treatment, most consequentially in the Senate. Debate there is limited, a filibuster cannot block a final vote, and passage requires a simple majority rather than the sixty votes ordinarily needed to close debate on contested legislation. The procedure carries limits: provisions without a budgetary effect, and provisions outside the budget resolution instructions, are exposed to challenge under the Senate rule named for Senator Robert C. Byrd of West Virginia, which treats such matter as extraneous. For welfare legislation the attraction was plain: cash assistance, food stamps, child care, and Supplemental Security Income were mandatory spending programs with direct effects on federal outlays, so a bill rewriting them could travel in a reconciliation measure, and a determined minority could delay the bill only within the expedited timetable rather than prevent a vote.

Reconciliation conferred no protection against a presidential veto, however. The official title of H.R. 3734 stated the procedural basis without describing the substance: a bill to provide for reconciliation pursuant to section 201(a)(1) of the concurrent resolution on the budget for fiscal year 1997. The House Budget Committee, chaired by Representative John Kasich of Ohio, reported the measure as an original bill on June 27, 1996. Title I carried the welfare provisions under the name Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Act, and other titles addressed child care, food stamps, child support, Supplemental Security Income, and Medicaid. The Medicaid title, proposed under the name Medicaid Restructuring Act, placed a second contested program inside the same vehicle, and the fate of that title became a turning point in the passage story.

### A First Veto Inside a Budget Bill

The first of the two vetoes fell on a predecessor reconciliation bill, and precision about the bill number matters to the record. On December 6, 1995, President Clinton returned H.R. 2491, the Seven Year Balanced Budget Reconciliation Act of 1995, without approval. H.R. 2491 was the reconciliation bill for fiscal year 1996, and welfare provisions derived from H.R. 4 travelled inside it alongside provisions on Medicare, Medicaid, taxation, student loans, and nutrition programs. The December veto was therefore a veto of a comprehensive budget bill in which welfare reform formed one part, and congressional debate in January 1996, including remarks by Senator Trent Lott of Mississippi, treated the action in exactly those terms, observing that the first veto of welfare reform had attracted limited attention because the measure had been part of a larger balanced budget act. The veto message addressed the bill as a whole. President Clinton described H.R. 2491 as making extreme cuts in Medicare and Medicaid and as raising taxes on working Americans through reductions in the Earned Income Tax Credit, while conferring a large tax cut whose benefits flowed disproportionately to the most well off. Against the bill, the message set an administration plan that would protect Medicare, retain the Medicaid guarantee of coverage, and reform welfare to reward work. No override attempt followed.

Because the welfare provisions had been submerged in a budget confrontation that also produced two partial shutdowns of the federal government in the winter of 1995 to 1996, the December veto settled little about a welfare bill standing alone. The conference version of H.R. 4, titled the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Act of 1995, passed the House on December 21, 1995 and the Senate on December 22, 1995, separating the welfare question from Medicare and from the broader tax and spending dispute. H.R. 4 proposed to end the federal guarantee of cash assistance, convert Aid to Families with Dependent Children and related programs into block grants to the states, impose a five year limit on federally funded assistance, tighten eligibility for Supplemental Security Income for disabled children, restrict benefits for noncitizens, and consolidate child care programs.

### A Second Veto Standing Alone

President Clinton vetoed H.R. 4 on January 9, 1996. The veto message, printed as House Document 104-164, returned the bill as the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Act of 1995 and opened with a declaration that the administration remained determined to work with Congress to enact bipartisan welfare reform, coupled with the judgment that the existing system was broken and required replacement. The objections that followed defined the negotiating ground for the remainder of 1996. The message held that H.R. 4 did too little to move people from welfare to work, and that the bill was burdened with deep budget cuts and structural changes that fell short of genuine reform. In the summary of the veto later prepared by the Congressional Research Service, the President specified the elements that final legislation would need to contain: sufficient child care to enable recipients to leave welfare for work, rewards for states that placed people in jobs, restoration of the guarantee of health coverage for poor families, a requirement that states maintain a financial stake in moving people from welfare to work, and protection for states and families in the event of an economic downturn or population growth. The administration objected in addition to provisions beyond the cash assistance title, including reductions affecting food stamps, benefits for disabled children, benefits for noncitizens, school lunch programs, and foster care and adoption assistance.

The message also recorded agreement on much of the congressional design, framing the dispute as one over degree and safeguards. President Clinton stated support for time limits, work requirements, the strongest possible child support enforcement, and a requirement that minor mothers live at home as a condition of assistance, and the message noted with approval that those elements of the administration approach appeared in H.R. 4. Senator Lauch Faircloth of North Carolina, speaking on the Senate floor in January 1996, answered from the sponsor side that the vetoed bill had taken the first step in sixty years toward requiring recipients to work for benefits, had placed a five year limit on receipt, had returned authority to the states, had toughened child support enforcement, and had prevented noncitizens from receiving benefits. No override attempt followed the second veto either, and the practical question for the spring of 1996 was whether a third bill could move enough on the administration list of conditions to obtain a signature without losing the congressional majorities that had passed H.R. 4 twice in substance.

### Governors, Revision, and the Removal of Medicaid

The National Governors Association supplied the framework for the revision. In February 1996 the association adopted a bipartisan policy position that accepted much of the structure of the vetoed bill, including block grants and a five year limit, while asking for additional funds for child care, a larger contingency fund that states could draw upon during periods of recession, and bonus payments for states meeting employment outcomes. State governments would administer the new program and would bear the fiscal exposure if caseloads rose during a downturn while federal funding remained fixed. In May 1996, House and Senate Republicans introduced revised welfare measures that reflected the policies of the vetoed H.R. 4 and incorporated the governors requests in part, providing additional funding for child care, an enlarged contingency fund, and performance bonuses. By the end of June 1996, the revised welfare provisions had been incorporated into H.R. 3734. In the form passed by the House on July 18, 1996, by a vote of 256 to 170, H.R. 3734 also contained a proposal to revise Medicaid, a restructuring that administration officials associated with the loss of the federal guarantee of coverage for eligible individuals.

The Senate removed the Medicaid proposal. The Congressional Research Service legislative history of the Temporary Assistance for Needy Families block grant describes the Senate action as the key modification to the bill: the Senate dropped the Medicaid provisions of H.R. 3734 while the welfare provisions remained, and the Senate passed the bill in that form on July 23, 1996, by a vote of 74 to 24, acting in lieu of the Senate companion bill, S. 1956. Contemporary reporting recorded Chairman Kasich predicting a presidential signature after the Medicaid changes had been cut. The delinkage operated at two levels. In packaging, Medicaid restructuring ceased to be a condition of welfare reform, so the President was no longer asked to accept a block grant conversion of health coverage as the price of a block grant conversion of cash assistance. In eligibility, the conference agreement preserved Medicaid as an individual entitlement and retained coverage for families meeting the standards of the former Aid to Families with Dependent Children program, with section 114 of the enacted act, titled Assuring Medicaid Coverage for Low Income Families, addressing the connection between the two programs; receipt of cash assistance and eligibility for Medicaid, which the old program structure had joined automatically, were separated without ending the coverage guarantee. The White House fact sheet issued at the signing listed guaranteed medical coverage among the improvements over the vetoed bill, since H.R. 4 would have ended the guarantee of Medicaid coverage for cash assistance recipients.

With Medicaid removed, the conference committee settled the remaining differences in the final week of July 1996. The conference report, House Report 104-725, was filed on July 30, 1996. The Congressional Research Service short history of the law records that the agreement dropped the optional food stamp block grant that had appeared in earlier versions, loosened the new food stamp work rule for able bodied adults without dependents in comparison with the House position, and retained the restrictions on aid to noncitizens. Supporters in the Senate enumerated further changes from the vetoed bill: child care funding increased by four billion dollars, the contingency fund increased from one billion dollars to two billion dollars, child protection programs retained as entitlements rather than converted into a block grant, and child care health and safety protections maintained. President Clinton announced the decision on July 31, 1996, before the House voted on the conference report. In remarks at the White House on that date, the President gave three reasons for signing: the existing system was broken, Congress had made many of the changes the administration had sought, and the bill represented the best available chance for a long time to end welfare as the country had known the system, notwithstanding serious problems in provisions unrelated to welfare reform. The President described the legislation as far from perfect, identified the provisions restricting benefits for legal immigrants and the food stamp provisions as wrong, and committed the administration to repair efforts after enactment.

### Final Votes and the Signing

The House agreed to the conference report on July 31, 1996, by 328 to 101, Roll Number 383, and the Senate agreed on August 1, 1996, by 78 to 21, Record Vote Number 262, both tallies appearing in the official action record for H.R. 3734. The bill was presented to the President on August 19, 1996. Enactment also completed the reconciliation instruction in the budget resolution for fiscal year 1997, the purpose stated in the long title of the act, closing a welfare negotiation that had occupied the 104th Congress since the introduction of H.R. 4 in January 1995.

President Clinton signed H.R. 3734 at the White House on August 22, 1996. The signing ceremony took place in the Rose Garden at 11:15 in the morning, Eastern Daylight Time, according to the White House transcript of the remarks. Lillie Harden of Arkansas, a former recipient of Aid to Families with Dependent Children who had left the program for employment and whom the President had met a decade earlier at a meeting of governors, spoke at the ceremony. In the signing remarks, President Clinton stated that the legislation provided an opportunity to end welfare as the country had known the system and to transform a broken system by promoting the values of work, responsibility, and family. The remarks presented the act as the fulfillment of the pledge made in the presidential campaign of 1992 and repeated the commitment to address provisions the President regarded as harmful. A statement issued by the Press Secretary on August 23, 1996 identified legal immigrant families as a particular concern and recorded a presidential vow to repair the provisions affecting such families. Upon signature the act became Public Law 104-193, published at 110 Statutes at Large 2105, with the approval date of August 22, 1996 printed on the opening page of the statute.

### Three Resignations from the Department of Health and Human Services

The first resignation followed the announcement of the signing decision rather than the signing itself. Wendell E. Primus held the post of Deputy Assistant Secretary for Human Services Policy in the Department of Health and Human Services, serving within the office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation, and Primus had worked on welfare policy as a congressional staff member before joining the department. On the morning after the announcement of July 31, 1996, Primus told members of the staff that continued work for the administration was not possible, according to reports published in August 1996 in National Journal and summarized by Government Executive. Department officials asked Primus to reconsider during a week of vacation in Maine. After the vacation, Primus submitted a letter of resignation to Secretary Donna E. Shalala, and reports of August 19, 1996 stated that Primus had resigned on the preceding Saturday. Primus had been associated within the department with analyses of the effects of the congressional bills on poverty, analyses that figured in the Senate record of January 1996, in which Senator Daniel Patrick Moynihan of New York referred to a departmental analysis submitted to the White House during consideration of H.R. 4.

Mary Jo Bane and Peter B. Edelman resigned in September 1996, in announcements reported on September 12, 1996. Bane served from 1993 to 1996 as Assistant Secretary for Children and Families, heading the Administration for Children and Families, which administered Aid to Families with Dependent Children, child support enforcement, Head Start, and child welfare programs; Bane had previously served as Commissioner of the New York State Department of Social Services. Edelman served as Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation, and Edelman and Marian Wright Edelman, president of the Children's Defense Fund, were described in the reporting of September 1996 as longtime friends of the President and the First Lady. In a letter of resignation as reported at the time, Edelman stated a devotion of more than thirty years to work intended to reduce poverty in America and a belief that the enacted welfare bill moved in the opposite direction. Bane, in the statement reported with the resignations, attributed the decision to deep concerns about the bill and a conclusion that continued service was not possible. Department officials stated in September 1996 that the departures would not disrupt implementation of the new law.

Edelman extended the protest in print after leaving office. The Atlantic Monthly published an account by Edelman in the issue of March 1997 under the title The Worst Thing Bill Clinton Has Done. In that account, Edelman wrote that the signed bill did not constitute welfare reform, did not promote work effectively, and would harm millions of poor children by full implementation; the account also objected to the exclusion of legal immigrants from disability and old age assistance and food stamps. The resignations thus remained attached to the passage record rather than to later evaluations of the law in operation, since the grounds stated in 1996 and in March 1997 were predictions and objections of principle concerning the text as enacted.

### Positions Stated at the Time

Speaker Newt Gingrich of Georgia had placed the Personal Responsibility Act among the ten bills of the Contract with America, and Representative E. Clay Shaw of Florida, chairman of the Subcommittee on Human Resources of the Committee on Ways and Means, directed passage of the House welfare proposal and served as a chief drafter of the enacted law. At the signing, Shaw described the legislation in terms reported on August 23, 1996 as the most important piece of legislation of the half century, a characterization belonging to Shaw rather than to the statute. Senate sponsors, including Senator William V. Roth of Delaware, chairman of the Committee on Finance, presented the conference agreement in the debate of August 1, 1996 as the product of compromise that ended a destructive welfare cycle, in the words used on the floor that day, and Senator Don Nickles of Oklahoma credited Roth and others while noting that the President had reached the correct decision after two vetoes. Across those remarks, the sponsor case rested on work requirements, time limits, state flexibility, child support enforcement, and the proposition that the federal guarantee of cash assistance had itself produced dependency.

The administration case for the signature, as stated on July 31 and August 22, 1996, accepted much of the sponsor description of the former system while disputing parts of the remedy. The President described the former system as undermining the values of work, responsibility, and family and as harming most the people the system was designed to help, and presented the act as making welfare a second chance rather than a way of life. The same remarks left the objections of January 1996 substantially intact: the food stamp work rule for able bodied adults without dependents and the restrictions on legal immigrants were identified as provisions causing harm and requiring repair. The decision presented to the public in 1996 was therefore not a claim that the third bill had satisfied every administration condition, but a judgment that the removal of the Medicaid restructuring, the preservation of the coverage guarantee, increased child care funding, and an enlarged contingency fund had moved the bill across a line that H.R. 2491 and H.R. 4 had not crossed, and that the opportunity to enact the agreed architecture justified accepting provisions the administration continued to oppose.

Senator Daniel Patrick Moynihan of New York occupied the most prominent opposition position within the President's party. Moynihan, the principal Senate figure associated with the Family Support Act of 1988, voted against the conference agreement. On the Senate floor in 1996, Moynihan repeated a prediction first stated on the floor in September 1995: that enactment would produce children sleeping on grates, picked up in the morning frozen, in the phrasing by which the prediction circulated in accounts of the debate, and Moynihan stated a hope of having been proved wrong. The floor statement objected to the sharp cutoff of assistance associated with the two year work requirement and the five year lifetime limit, and warned that conservative social analysts had counseled caution about the state of knowledge underlying the policy. The record of passage closes with that division preserved: a President signing with stated reservations and a pledge of repair, sponsors claiming a transformation of the relationship between assistance and work, a senior senator of the President's party predicting catastrophe in the Congressional Record, and three officials of the department charged with administering the new law leaving office rather than implementing the statute in the form enacted.

## The Operative Core: Entitlement, Block Grant, Time Limit, and Participation

### The Sentence That Ended the Entitlement

Title I of the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996, Public Law 104-193, enacted on August 22, 1996, struck Part A of Title IV of the Social Security Act and substituted a new Part A in its place. The former Part A had contained Aid to Families with Dependent Children, together with the Emergency Assistance program and the Job Opportunities and Basic Skills Training program. The new Part A contained Temporary Assistance for Needy Families. In section 401 of the Social Security Act, codified at 42 U.S.C. 601, Congress placed a purpose statement in subsection (a) and a single operative sentence in subsection (b), under the heading No individual entitlement. Subsection (b) provides: "This part shall not be interpreted to entitle any individual or family to assistance under any State program funded under this part."

Section 601 stands first in the new part, before state plans, grants, uses of funds, participation rates, prohibitions, and penalties, so the denial of entitlement precedes any description of money, eligibility, or work rules. The sentence also speaks in terms of interpretation. The text does not merely omit a grant of rights; the text instructs courts, agencies, and states against reading the part as a source of an individual or family right to assistance. Assistance therefore rested on the state plan and state law, within the federal conditions attached to the grant, rather than on a federal statutory promise running to the family.

An entitlement under the repealed program had operated on two levels at once, one fiscal and one legal. On the fiscal level, the federal government reimbursed a share of what each state spent on assistance, and the reimbursement had no dollar ceiling for the fifty states and the District of Columbia. The Congressional Research Service, in its primer on the Temporary Assistance for Needy Families block grant, described the Aid to Families with Dependent Children matching grant as open ended and recorded that the federal share was paid at the Medicaid matching rate, which ranged from a statutory minimum of 50 percent for states with the highest per capita incomes to a statutory maximum of 83 percent for states with the lowest per capita incomes. A state that paid benefits to more eligible families incurred more spending, and the federal Treasury matched the additional spending at the applicable rate, without a fixed pot from which one family drew at the expense of another. On the legal level, a family that met the eligibility conditions in the approved state plan held a claim to payment that a court could enforce. The Congressional Research Service account of the former program described Aid to Families with Dependent Children as an entitlement to individuals who were eligible for benefits under state determined rules, and recorded that eligible persons had a legal right to benefits, with procedural due process required to terminate them under the holding of the Supreme Court of the United States in Goldberg v. Kelly in 1970.

Section 601(b) removed the legal level, and the block grant in section 603 removed the fiscal level. After the substitution, a family that satisfied every condition in a state plan possessed no federal statutory entitlement to assistance under a program funded under Part A. The state could define eligibility more narrowly than the population described in the federal purpose statement, could set a benefit below the level of measured need, and could exhaust a category of spending without violating a federal promise to the family, because the federal text disclaimed the promise in advance. The entitlement that remained in the structure ran in the opposite direction. Federal law entitled each state to a specified allocation of the state family assistance grant, payable in quarterly installments, so the holder of the enforceable funding claim after 1996 was the state government rather than the family. The sentence also reversed state fiscal incentives. Under an open ended match, broader eligibility drew additional federal dollars and narrower eligibility surrendered federal dollars in proportion. Under a fixed grant, narrower eligibility conserved funds inside a grant received in any event, for redirection to other allowable purposes, reservation for a later year, or transfer within statutory limits.

### A Fixed Grant in Place of an Open Ended Match

Section 403 of the Social Security Act, codified at 42 U.S.C. 603, replaced reimbursement with a grant. The central component, in section 603(a)(1), is the state family assistance grant. The Congressional Research Service, in its funding tables for Temporary Assistance for Needy Families updated on July 1, 2019, listed State Family Assistance Grants at 16,489 million dollars for each fiscal year, a figure equivalent to 16.489 billion dollars. The Congressional Research Service commonly expressed the component in rounded form as a basic block grant of 16.5 billion dollars per year. With territories included, the total basic block grant was commonly stated at 16.6 billion dollars.

Each state share was a product of history rather than of measured need in the year of payment. Under the original formula in Public Law 104-193, as summarized by the Congressional Research Service, each state received the greatest of three amounts drawn from the federal share of expenditures in the three former programs: the average federal share for fiscal years 1992 through 1994, the federal share for fiscal year 1994 with an adjustment for states that had amended an Emergency Assistance plan in fiscal year 1994 or fiscal year 1995, or the federal share for fiscal year 1995. A state with generous benefits or high caseloads in the base period locked in a large grant; a state with low base period benefits locked in a small grant per poor child, and no mechanism equalized grants according to later child poverty.

The defining fiscal feature of the grant was the absence of an adjustment mechanism. The amount established in 1996 was not indexed to inflation, was not recalculated for population change, and did not rise or fall with the assistance caseload, the unemployment rate, or the number of children in poverty. Later extensions preserved the nominal amount, apart from a reduction of 0.33 percent in each allocation beginning in fiscal year 2017. The 1996 figure therefore remained the figure in the fiscal year that ended on September 30, 2019. A fixed nominal amount in an economy with positive inflation is a declining real amount, and two named sources quantified the decline against the vantage of August 1, 2019. The Congressional Research Service, in responses to frequently asked questions updated on July 1, 2019, calculated that the real value of the basic block grant in fiscal year 2018 stood 36.2 percent below the real value in fiscal year 1997, using the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers. The Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, in an analysis dated April 2, 2018, stated that the block grant had lost more than a third of the inflation adjusted value recorded in 1996 and projected a loss of 39 percent of inflation adjusted value by 2019.

The block grant did not make the program exclusively federal in financing. Section 409 of the Social Security Act, codified at 42 U.S.C. 609, imposed a penalty for failure to maintain a specified level of state effort, and section 609(a)(7) supplied the operative definitions of qualified state expenditures and historic state expenditures. Maintenance of effort obliged each state to spend state funds on eligible families and allowable activities in an amount tied to state spending in fiscal year 1994. The threshold was 80 percent of historic state spending for a state that failed to meet the applicable participation rates, and 75 percent of historic state spending for a state that met both the all families rate and the two parent rate described in section 607. Required nonfederal spending across the states totaled about 10.3 billion dollars per year. The threshold froze alongside the federal grant, because a percentage of a 1994 amount does not grow with inflation, population, or caseload. The five point difference converted the participation rates into a spending question, since a state that missed a rate faced the higher threshold in addition to direct penalty exposure under section 609(a)(3), and a maintenance of effort shortfall reduced the grant dollar for dollar by the amount of the shortfall.

### The Sixty Month Clock in Federal Funds

Section 408 of the Social Security Act, codified at 42 U.S.C. 608, collected the prohibitions that applied to a state use of the grant, and section 608(a)(7), under a heading that denied assistance for more than five years, contained the time limit. Subparagraph (A) barred a state from using any part of a grant made under section 603 to provide assistance to a family that included an adult who had received assistance under a state program funded under Part A, attributable to federal funds, for sixty months, whether or not the months were consecutive, after the date on which the state program commenced. The provision regulated the use of federal money rather than eligibility in the abstract, and months accumulated across moves between states because the text counted assistance under any state program funded under the part. Months of services outside the regulatory definition of assistance did not advance the count, which made the boundary of the term assistance consequential.

Three qualifications inside paragraph (7) narrowed the count or softened the bar. Subparagraph (B) supplied a minor child exception, directing the state to disregard any month in which assistance was provided with respect to an individual who was a minor child and was not the head of a household or married to the head of a household. Subparagraph (D) directed a state or tribe to disregard any month during which the adult lived in Indian country or in an Alaskan Native village, where the most reliable data available for the month, or for a period including the month, indicated that at least 50 percent of the adults living in Indian country or in the village were not employed. Subparagraph (C) supplied the hardship exception. A state could exempt a family from subparagraph (A) by reason of hardship, or where the family included an individual who had been battered or subjected to extreme cruelty within the statutory definition. The exemption carried a ceiling: the average monthly number of families with an exemption in effect for a fiscal year could not exceed 20 percent of the average monthly number of families receiving assistance under the state program during that fiscal year or the immediately preceding fiscal year, at the election of the state.

Two consequences followed. Because subparagraph (A) was triggered by a family that included an adult who had received the counted assistance, a child only case, in which assistance was paid on behalf of a child living with a caretaker who was not included in the assistance unit, did not present an adult whose months could exhaust the limit, and assistance for the child was not barred by paragraph (7) on that basis alone. Child only cases nevertheless counted in the caseload against which the 20 percent ceiling was measured, which increased the practical room for hardship exemptions among adult headed cases, as an Administration for Children and Families analysis of state time limit policies observed. The second consequence concerned the source of funds. Paragraph (7) barred use of the federal grant; paragraph (7) did not bar a state from assisting the same family with state funds segregated from federal funds, including funds counted toward maintenance of effort in a separate state program. A state could therefore continue assistance beyond sixty months at state expense, could stop assistance at sixty months, or could impose a shorter limit of state design. Territorial and tribal administration fit inside the same paragraph. Puerto Rico, Guam, and the United States Virgin Islands received grants on the same historic formula, subject to the separate overall ceiling on federal public assistance funding for the territories that Public Law 104-193 raised, and Puerto Rico applied a sixty month limit, with the first families reaching the limit in July 2002. Tribal programs operated under plans approved under 42 U.S.C. 612, with the tribal grant deducted from the grant of the state containing the service area, and subparagraph (D) addressed a state or tribe in terms.

### Participation Rates Written Into the Statute

Section 407 of the Social Security Act, codified at 42 U.S.C. 607, imposed mandatory work requirements in the form of participation rates that a state was required to achieve. The rates were obligations of the state, measured across the caseload, rather than duties of any single family. For all families receiving assistance, the minimum rate rose by fiscal year in a statutory table: 25 percent for fiscal year 1997, 30 percent for fiscal year 1998, 35 percent for fiscal year 1999, 40 percent for fiscal year 2000, 45 percent for fiscal year 2001, and 50 percent for fiscal year 2002 and each fiscal year thereafter. For two parent families receiving assistance, the table set 75 percent for fiscal year 1997, 75 percent for fiscal year 1998, and 90 percent for fiscal year 1999 and each fiscal year thereafter. A family counted toward the numerator through engagement in work activities listed in section 607(d) for the weekly hours specified in section 607(c), generally thirty hours per week, or twenty hours for a single custodial parent caring for a child under six years of age, with higher combined hours for two parent families.

The statutory rates of 50 percent and 90 percent described the ceiling of the obligation rather than the obligation that a given state faced in a given year, because section 607(b) supplied the caseload reduction credit. The credit reduced the minimum rate by a number of percentage points equal to the percentage by which the average monthly caseload of the state in the preceding fiscal year had fallen below the average monthly caseload of the state in the base year, which the statute as enacted in 1996 fixed at fiscal year 1995. The Secretary of Health and Human Services was directed to disregard caseload decline that resulted from federal law or from changes in state eligibility criteria, so the credit rewarded decline that occurred for other reasons rather than decline manufactured by narrowing the door. The arithmetic was direct subtraction. A state with an all families caseload that stood 40 percent below the base year caseload received a credit of forty percentage points, which reduced a nominal 50 percent standard to an effective standard of 10 percent. A caseload reduction of 50 percent or more reduced the effective all families standard to zero, a result recorded for New York by the Independent Budget Office of New York City. Because caseloads fell sharply between fiscal year 1995 and the early 2000s, the credit determined whether the rates bound in practice. Any caseload reduction, through employment, sanction, diversion, or closure, improved the state position under section 607, subject to the eligibility exclusion. The Deficit Reduction Act of 2005, Public Law 109-171, later reset the base year to fiscal year 2005; the rates as enacted in 1996 were measured from the 1995 base.

Section 609 converted the participation standard into a fiscal risk. Under section 609(a)(3), a state that failed to achieve a required rate faced a reduction of the state family assistance grant by a maximum of 5 percent for the first year of failure, with the applicable percentage rising by two percentage points for each subsequent year of failure, subject to a ceiling of 21 percent, and with the Secretary directed to impose the reduction on the basis of the degree of noncompliance. Where a state failed only the two parent standard, regulation prorated the maximum penalty according to the share of two parent cases in the total caseload. Relief was available through reasonable cause exceptions and corrective compliance plans under section 609(b) and section 609(c).

Two funding components of the original enactment pointed in the opposite direction from the penalty. The contingency fund in section 603(b) received a total of 2 billion dollars under Public Law 104-193, available to states that qualified as needy states, through an unemployment test or a food stamp caseload above the fiscal year 1994 or fiscal year 1995 level, and that spent state funds above 100 percent of the fiscal year 1994 historic level, with spending above that threshold matched at the applicable rate. The original 2 billion dollars was depleted in early fiscal year 2010, and later appropriations continued the fund, at 608 million dollars per year beginning in fiscal year 2017, according to the Congressional Research Service. A pair of bonuses completed the incentive structure. The bonus to reward high performance states, in section 603(a)(4) as enacted, appropriated a total of 1 billion dollars across fiscal years 1999 through 2003, averaging 200 million dollars per year, for grants to states that the Secretary ranked as successful in meeting the goals of the program, with awards for fiscal years 1999 and 2000 based on job entry and workforce success measures. The bonus to reward a decrease in illegitimacy, in section 603(a)(5) as enacted, appropriated 100 million dollars for each of fiscal years 1999 through 2002, divided equally among as many as five states with the greatest decrease in the ratio of out of wedlock births to total births over a two year period, conditional on an abortion rate below the fiscal year 1995 level for the state, with a qualifying state receiving 20 million dollars, or 25 million dollars where fewer than five states qualified. Both bonuses were time limited authorizations rather than permanent features of the grant.

### The Authority States Received in Exchange

Measured against the former program, the exchange that Title I offered a state was a fixed and declining federal sum in return for control over design questions that federal law had previously answered or constrained. Within the four purposes stated in section 601(a), the broad use authority in section 604(a), and the prohibitions in section 608, a state set the income and resource tests that defined a needy family, because the federal statute supplied no national definition of need and no federal benefit standard. A state set the benefit level for a family of a given size. A state designed sanctions for refusal to engage in work without good cause, subject to the requirement in section 608 that assistance be reduced by at least a pro rata amount for a refusal and to the protection for a single parent of a child under six years of age who demonstrated an inability to obtain needed child care. A state set time limits shorter than sixty months, set earnings disregards that determined how much income a family could retain before assistance fell, and decided the mix among cash assistance, child care, work services, and nonrecurrent short term benefits, which regulation excluded from the definition of assistance and therefore from the federal clock and the participation denominator.

Transfer authority completed the design discretion. Section 604(d) permitted a state to transfer a portion of the state family assistance grant to the Child Care and Development Block Grant program and to the Social Services Block Grant, subject to an overall ceiling of 30 percent of the grant and a separate ceiling of 10 percent of the grant for transfers to the Social Services Block Grant. Funds transferred became subject to the rules of the receiving program, with amounts transferred to the Social Services Block Grant required to serve families with incomes below 200 percent of the official poverty line. A state could also reserve unobligated balances without a fiscal year limitation, for use in a later year for assistance and, after amendment by the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2009, for any allowable benefit or service.

The operative core of Public Law 104-193 therefore consisted of four interlocking substitutions, each traceable to a single statutory location. Section 601(b) substituted state definition for an individual federal right. Section 603(a)(1) substituted a fixed sum derived from spending in fiscal years 1992 through 1995 for an open ended match on spending in the year of payment. Section 608(a)(7) substituted a sixty month limit on the use of federal funds, qualified by hardship exemptions capped at 20 percent of the caseload, for a program in which duration of receipt had carried no federal lifetime ceiling. Section 607 substituted a state level participation standard, softened by a caseload reduction credit measured from fiscal year 1995, for case level work rules alone. Caseload risk, inflation risk, and recession risk, which the open ended match had placed on the federal Treasury, rested after August 22, 1996 on state budgets and, where a state declined to absorb a risk with state funds, on families whose assistance a state plan defined, reduced, or ended.

The substitutions described in the preceding section can be compressed into a single comparison. The table below sets the former program and the successor program side by side across the five elements that defined each design: entitlement status, funding mechanism, eligibility authority, time limits, and work rules.

## The Other Titles: Immigrants, Child Support, Disability, Food Assistance, and Charitable Choice

The cash assistance title occupied most of the public argument over the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996, Public Law 104-193, signed on August 22, 1996. The remaining titles reached a far wider set of households. Title IV rewrote the eligibility of noncitizens for federal, state, and local benefits. Title III rebuilt the machinery for locating parents and collecting child support across state lines. Title II narrowed the childhood disability standard in Supplemental Security Income. Title VIII imposed a time limit on food stamp receipt by a defined class of adults and reduced food assistance spending on several fronts at once. Section 104, placed inside Title I, opened government contracting to religious organizations on stated conditions.

### Immigrant Eligibility Under Title IV

Title IV, captioned as restricting welfare and public benefits for aliens, created a framework codified at 8 U.S.C. 1601 to 1641. The framework began with a classification. Under 8 U.S.C. 1641, a qualified alien included a person lawfully admitted for permanent residence, a refugee admitted under section 207 of the Immigration and Nationality Act, a person granted asylum under section 208 of that act, a person whose deportation or removal was being withheld, a person paroled into the United States for at least one year, a conditional entrant, and a Cuban and Haitian entrant, among other listed categories. Under 8 U.S.C. 1611, a person who was not a qualified alien was ineligible for a federal public benefit, subject to exceptions that included emergency medical assistance, short term disaster relief, immunizations, and services the Attorney General specified as necessary for the protection of life or safety. Classification therefore determined the starting point for every later question, and lawful presence alone did not settle eligibility.

For qualified aliens, two provisions did separate work. Section 402 of the act, codified at 8 U.S.C. 1612, limited the eligibility of qualified aliens for specified federal programs, including Supplemental Security Income and food stamps, and it preserved eligibility for defined groups, including persons credited with forty qualifying quarters of work and refugees and asylees within the periods the section set. Section 403, codified at 8 U.S.C. 1613 rather than at section 1612, imposed the five year bar. A qualified alien who entered the United States on or after August 22, 1996, was not eligible for any federal means tested public benefit for a period of five years beginning on the date of entry with a qualified status. Federal means tested benefits, as agencies applied the term, covered cash assistance under the new block grant, Supplemental Security Income, food stamps, and nonemergency Medicaid.

The bar carried exceptions written into the same section. Refugees, asylees, persons whose deportation was being withheld, Cuban and Haitian entrants, and Amerasian immigrants were excepted from the limitation. A separate exception covered a lawfully residing veteran with an honorable discharge who met minimum active duty service requirements, a person on active duty in the Armed Forces other than active duty for training, and the spouse and unmarried dependent children of persons in those military categories. An alien who entered before August 22, 1996, fell outside the bar, as the Administration for Children and Families explained in guidance on section 403, because the bar attached to entry on or after the date of enactment. Eligibility therefore turned on entry date and immigration category as well as on financial need.

Title IV also attributed a sponsor's resources to a sponsored immigrant. Under 8 U.S.C. 1631, in determining eligibility for a federal means tested benefit and the amount of that benefit, the sponsored alien's income and resources were deemed to include the income and resources of the person who had executed an affidavit of support under section 213A of the Immigration and Nationality Act, and of that sponsor's spouse. Deeming applied until the alien became a citizen through naturalization or accumulated, or was credited with, forty qualifying quarters of coverage. The statute included an indigence exception, under which the amount attributed was limited to the amount actually provided, for a twelve month period, where a determination established that the alien would, without the benefit, be unable to obtain food and shelter. Deeming therefore operated after the five year bar expired as well as during it.

States received a defined role. Under 8 U.S.C. 1622, a state was authorized to determine the eligibility of qualified aliens for state public benefits, subject to exceptions that protected refugees, asylees, and certain other groups for the periods the section specified. Under 8 U.S.C. 1621, a person who was not a qualified alien was ineligible for a state or local public benefit, but a state could extend eligibility to a person not lawfully present only by enacting a state law after August 22, 1996, that affirmatively provided for that eligibility. Nothing in Title IV prevented a state from spending state funds on coverage for immigrants whom federal law excluded, without federal matching funds for the excluded benefit. Partial restorations of immigrant eligibility enacted in 1997, 1998, and 2002 are treated in the section of this article on the statute's later evolution.

### Child Support Enforcement Under Title III

Title III amended the child support enforcement program in Title IV, Part D of the Social Security Act, a program that had operated since 1975 through state agencies supervised by a federal office in the Department of Health and Human Services. The title did not create a benefit. It expanded the information systems, the procedures for establishing paternity and support orders, and the remedies available when a parent did not pay. During the 1996 debate the child support title drew support across party lines, and its provisions were framed around collecting support owed to children rather than around the eligibility disputes that dominated the cash assistance and immigrant titles.

The center of the title was information. Each state was required to operate a state directory of new hires, to which employers reported the name, address, and Social Security number of each newly hired employee and the employer's name, address, and identification number. Within three business days after receiving new hire information from an employer, the state directory was required to furnish the information to the National Directory of New Hires, maintained as part of the Federal Parent Locator Service. When information in the directory matched a person who owed support, the state directory reported the match to the state child support agency, and the agency, within two business days, instructed the appropriate employer to withhold the obligation from the employee's wages unless the income was not subject to withholding. Title III also required a federal case registry of child support orders and state case registries, so that orders, and the persons named in them, could be tracked when a parent, an employer, or a child moved between states.

Paternity establishment received a parallel expansion, because a support order required an identified father for a child born outside marriage. States were required to maintain hospital based programs under which unmarried parents could voluntarily acknowledge paternity at or shortly after a child's birth, and a voluntary acknowledgment, once filed, carried the legal effect the statute assigned to it, subject to a limited period for rescission and later challenge on grounds the statute listed, including fraud and duress. Title III simplified procedures for genetic testing, permitted administrative establishment of paternity and support orders without a full court proceeding in defined circumstances, and set performance expectations for the share of cases in which paternity was established. Interstate practice was standardized as well. As a condition of federal funding, each state was required to adopt the Uniform Interstate Family Support Act in the form the act specified, so that one state could enforce an order issued by another state and an employer in one state was required to honor an income withholding order issued in another.

The enforcement remedies reached income, assets, and privileges. States were required to have procedures for withholding support from wages and other income, for intercepting tax refunds and other payments under the offset programs, for imposing liens against real and personal property, and for reporting arrears to consumer reporting agencies. Title III required state procedures under which a driver's license, a professional or occupational license, and a recreational license could be withheld, suspended, or restricted for a person who owed overdue support or who failed to comply with a subpoena or warrant relating to paternity or support. The federal office responsible for the program, the Office of Child Support Enforcement, administered federal matching funds for state systems, set deadlines for the automated data systems the title required, and operated the federal components of the locator service.

### Childhood Disability Under Title II

Children's Supplemental Security Income was addressed in Title II of the act, captioned Supplemental Security Income, and specifically in Subtitle B of that title. Sections 211 and 212 of the act amended the definition of disability for a person under age eighteen, codified at 42 U.S.C. 1382c(a)(3)(C). Before August 22, 1996, the Social Security Act granted benefits to a child with an impairment of comparable severity to an impairment that would disable an adult, as the Social Security Administration described the earlier standard in a 2004 analysis of the program. Following the Supreme Court decision in Sullivan v. Zebley, 493 U.S. 521, decided in 1990, the agency had applied that standard through an individualized functional assessment, under which a child whose impairment did not meet or equal a listed impairment could qualify on the basis of an assessment of the child's ability to perform age appropriate activities.

Section 211 replaced that test. A child was considered disabled only if the child had a medically determinable physical or mental impairment that resulted in marked and severe functional limitations and that could be expected to result in death or had lasted, or could be expected to last, for a continuous period of not less than twelve months. Comparable severity disappeared from the definition, and the individualized functional assessment disappeared with it, so a claim that failed to meet or equal a listing could no longer be carried by a separate functional inquiry of the kind the Zebley framework had produced. The act also removed the reference to maladaptive behavior that had appeared in the regulatory listings for childhood mental impairments. The same amendment governed new applicants and, through redetermination, children already on the rolls.

Redetermination and review provisions gave the new definition its reach into existing cases. Children who were receiving benefits on the date of enactment and who would not qualify under the new definition were subject to redetermination of eligibility against the statutory deadline the act set. Continuing disability reviews were required on a periodic cycle for children whose impairments were expected to improve, with reviews at least once every three years for a child whose impairment was not permanent, and with a review by age one for a child whose eligibility had rested on low birth weight. A recipient who had received benefits as a child was also subject to a redetermination at age eighteen under the adult disability standard, treated as an initial determination rather than as a continuing disability review, so the agency was not required to show medical improvement before finding the recipient not disabled as an adult.

The numbers projected at enactment were disputed in range rather than in direction. Education Week reported in September 1997 that the Congressional Budget Office had estimated that as many as 200,000 children could be affected by the narrowed definition, and that John Trollinger, a spokesman for the Social Security Administration, expected the number losing benefits to fall at the low side of the Congressional Budget Office figures. The same reporting placed approximately one million children on the children's Supplemental Security Income rolls before the redeterminations, with average monthly payments of 436 dollars.

### Food Assistance Under Title VIII

Title VIII, captioned Food Stamps and Commodity Distribution, amended the Food Stamp Act of 1977. Its most precisely drawn provision created the category of able bodied adults without dependents and attached a time limit to it, codified at 7 U.S.C. 2015(o). A person aged eighteen to forty nine who was not exempt was required to work, or participate in a qualifying work program, for at least eighty hours in a month, or to participate in and comply with a workfare assignment, in order to receive food stamps for more than three months in a thirty six month period. The three months were months in which the person received the full monthly benefit while not meeting the requirement. Exemptions covered a person medically certified as physically or mentally unfit for employment, a person residing in a household with a child under eighteen, a pregnant person, and a person otherwise exempt from the general food stamp work requirements or complying with a related work program. A person who lost eligibility under the time limit could regain it by meeting the work requirement during a thirty day period, as the statute and the regulations at 7 C.F.R. 273.24 provided.

The time limit did not apply uniformly across the map, because the statute allowed the Secretary of Agriculture to waive it for a group of individuals in an area on a state's request. The Department of Agriculture stated in guidance issued in May 2018 that a state agency could request a waiver where an area had an unemployment rate over ten percent or where the state met one of the regulatory options for showing that an area lacked a sufficient number of jobs. A waiver removed the obligation to meet the eighty hour requirement as a condition of receiving benefits beyond three months, although persons in a waived area remained subject to the general work registration requirements, which could not be waived. States also received a limited pool of individual exemptions, set in Department of Agriculture guidance at fifteen percent of the individuals who would otherwise be subject to the time limit, with each exemption extending eligibility for one person for one month.

The rest of Title VIII reduced spending through eligibility and benefit rules that applied to households of every composition. The Congressional Research Service summarized the title as reducing maximum benefits, reducing the deductions from income that had increased benefits for households with countable income, and denying benefits to fugitive felons, and Title IV of the same act separately barred most noncitizens from the program. Title VIII also tightened penalties for program violations and trafficking in benefits and, in Subtitle C, required states to move toward electronic benefit transfer systems for delivering food stamp benefits. The Congressional Research Service, in an issue overview of the 1996 law, reported that at passage net federal food stamp outlay savings over five years were estimated at 23.3 billion dollars. That estimate combined the time limit, the benefit and deduction changes, and the noncitizen restrictions, so no single provision accounted for the total.

### Charitable Choice

Section 104 of the act, enacted as part of Title I and codified at 42 U.S.C. 604a, stated the provision that became known as charitable choice. The Administration for Children and Families described the provision in guidance issued in 2007 as allowing states to contract with religious organizations, or to accept certificates or other forms of disbursement from them, on the same basis as any other nongovernmental provider, without impairing the religious character of the organization and without diminishing the religious freedom of beneficiaries. A state could administer and provide services under the covered programs through contracts with charitable, religious, or private organizations, and it could give beneficiaries certificates, vouchers, or other forms of disbursement redeemable with such organizations. The covered programs included the state program funded under the cash assistance part and other programs established or modified by Titles I and II of the act that permitted contracts or vouchers.

The statute protected the provider's religious identity in specific terms. A religious organization that participated retained its religious character, could display religious symbols in the facilities where services were offered, and retained the exemption available to religious employers under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 to make employment decisions on the basis of religion, an exemption the Supreme Court had upheld in Corporation of the Presiding Bishop v. Amos in 1987. At the same time, the organization was subject to the same financial accountability as other nongovernmental providers, was required to account for government funds separately, and could not expend funds provided under a direct contract for sectarian worship, instruction, or proselytization. The section stated that its provisions were to be implemented in a manner consistent with the Establishment Clause of the Constitution.

Beneficiaries received protections of a different kind. A provider could not discriminate against a beneficiary on the basis of religion, and a beneficiary could not be required to participate in religious activity as a condition of receiving a publicly funded service. Where a person objected to the religious character of the organization providing a service, the statute required the state to provide an alternative provider that was accessible and that offered services of equal value, within a reasonable period after the objection.

The provision's influence after 1996 was principally as a template. Testimony presented to the Senate Judiciary Committee in 2001 by the Department of Justice recorded that charitable choice first appeared in the 1996 act, that Congress incorporated the provision into the Community Services Block Grant Act in 1998, and that Congress made it part of the reauthorization of funding for the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration in 2000. Each enactment applied the same structure to a different program: eligibility of religious providers on the same basis as other nongovernmental providers, preservation of religious character, and protections for beneficiaries. On January 29, 2001, President George W. Bush identified the participation of faith based and community groups in federally funded services as an administration priority, in terms the Administration for Children and Families reproduced in its charitable choice guidance.

### Child Care and Child Protection

Two shorter titles completed the act's treatment of children. Title VI, captioned Child Care, reauthorized and substantially amended the Child Care and Development Block Grant and replaced the three child care programs that had been attached to Aid to Families with Dependent Children with a single mandatory block grant, the Child Care Entitlement to States, created under section 418 of the Social Security Act. The Congressional Research Service explained that states were required to integrate the new mandatory funds with Child Care and Development Block Grant funds and to spend the mandatory funds generally under the block grant's rules, and that the combination of the two streams was called the Child Care and Development Fund in regulation. Title VI provided annual increases in mandatory appropriations for the period the act authorized and ended the guarantee of child care that the repealed programs had extended to families receiving, or moving off, cash assistance.

Title V, captioned Child Protection, made targeted adjustments to the foster care and adoption assistance programs in Title IV, Part E of the Social Security Act rather than restructuring them. Section 505 of the act required a state plan to provide that the state consider giving preference to an adult relative over a nonrelated caregiver when determining a placement for a child, provided the relative caregiver met all relevant state child protection standards. Because the act had repealed the cash assistance program on which foster care and adoption assistance eligibility had depended, Title V and related provisions tied that eligibility to the Aid to Families with Dependent Children standards in effect on July 16, 1996, a fixed look back date that separated a child's eligibility for federal foster care payments from the rules of the new cash assistance block grant. Title V also established a longitudinal study of the outcomes of children affected by the child welfare system, as the Congressional Research Service summary of the act recorded.

## Amendment, Partial Restoration, and the Courts

The Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996, Public Law 104-193, was altered within a year of enactment, and the pattern of alteration repeated for more than two decades. Congress restored eligibility to defined groups of immigrants in three statutes between 1997 and 2002, left the central cash assistance program on temporary extensions for more than three years before reauthorizing the program inside a budget law, added a temporary emergency fund during a severe recession and allowed the fund to expire on schedule, and declined to adjust the nominal size of the basic block grant. The courts played a smaller part. Litigation produced one Supreme Court decision of constitutional significance, Saenz v. Roe, 526 U.S. 489 (1999), which invalidated a state benefit limit that the 1996 Act had expressly authorized. No comparable Supreme Court decision tested the five year limit on federally funded assistance, the work participation standards, or the block grant structure itself before the vantage of August 1, 2019. Amendment therefore proceeded almost entirely through legislation that repaired the edges of the 1996 settlement while preserving the settlement itself.

### The First Corrections to the Immigrant Restrictions

The earliest corrections addressed Supplemental Security Income and Medicaid. As enacted in 1996, the Act terminated Supplemental Security Income for most noncitizens, including many persons who had entered lawfully and who had been receiving benefits on the basis of age, blindness, or disability, and loss of Supplemental Security Income carried loss of associated Medicaid coverage in most states. The Balanced Budget Act of 1997, Public Law 105-33, signed on August 5, 1997, reversed the termination for defined categories. The Congressional Research Service summarized the 1997 Act as continuing Supplemental Security Income and related Medicaid for qualified aliens who were receiving benefits on August 22, 1996, permitting benefits for aliens who were present on that date and who later became disabled, and extending the exemption for refugees and asylees from five years to seven years after entry. The Congressional Budget Office estimated the cost of the immigration related provisions of the 1997 Act at 11.5 billion dollars over the five year period from 1998 to 2002, with 9.5 billion dollars attributed to Supplemental Security Income and 2 billion dollars attributed to Medicaid. A related protection concerned children. The tightened childhood disability standard of the 1996 Act had been enacted in Title II, Subtitle B, sections 211 and 212, amending 42 U.S.C. 1382c(a)(3)(C), and section 4913 of the 1997 Act preserved Medicaid eligibility for children who had received Supplemental Security Income on August 22, 1996 and who lost payment under that new standard.

The structure of the 1997 restoration mattered as much as its size. Eligibility turned on presence on a single date, August 22, 1996, the date of enactment of the 1996 Act. Persons who entered after that date remained subject to two distinct restrictions of the 1996 Act: the five year bar on federal means tested benefits codified at 8 U.S.C. 1613, enacted as section 403, and the separate limited eligibility rule for specified federal programs codified at 8 U.S.C. 1612, enacted as section 402, under which Supplemental Security Income was restricted on terms that in most cases lasted until naturalization or the accumulation of forty qualifying quarters of work. The restorations that followed operated principally through exceptions to the section 402 rule in 8 U.S.C. 1612, and the section 403 bar in 8 U.S.C. 1613 remained a separate provision. Congress thus drew a line between a settled population, for which the 1996 termination was corrected, and a future population, for which the restrictions were retained. The same distinction governed the food stamp restorations of 1998 and 2002, although the later statutes loosened the entry date condition for particular groups.

The Balanced Budget Act of 1997 also added a program that had no counterpart in the 1996 Act. Acting on a proposal from President Bill Clinton, Congress provided 3 billion dollars over two years, fiscal 1998 and fiscal 1999, for welfare to work grants intended to move recipients described in program guidance as the hardest to employ into jobs, and to serve noncustodial parents of children receiving assistance. The Congressional Research Service recorded that the grants were administered by the Department of Labor rather than by the Department of Health and Human Services, which administered Temporary Assistance for Needy Families. No new welfare to work funding was provided after fiscal 1999. The episode illustrated a recurring preference: when Congress concluded that the original statute had underprovided for a purpose, Congress supplied a separate and time limited appropriation alongside the block grant rather than enlarging the block grant itself.

### Food Assistance Restorations in Two Steps

Food stamp eligibility followed a slower path. The Balanced Budget Act of 1997 left the food stamp cutoff for most legal immigrants in place, so that the first food stamp restoration arrived in a separate vehicle, the Agricultural Research, Extension, and Education Reform Act of 1998, Public Law 105-185, signed on June 23, 1998. That Act restored food stamp eligibility to three groups defined by presence before the 1996 enactment: legal noncitizen children under eighteen years of age who were lawfully residing in the United States on August 22, 1996, legal noncitizens who were sixty five years of age or older and lawfully residing in the United States on that date, and legal noncitizen persons with disabilities who had entered the United States before August 22, 1996. The Act also restored eligibility for certain Hmong and Highland Laotian persons whose tribes had assisted United States personnel during the Vietnam era. The restoration took effect on November 1, 1998, for the principal categories.

The Farm Security and Rural Investment Act of 2002, Public Law 107-171, completed most of the food stamp restoration in three staggered steps. The Congressional Research Service recorded that the 2002 Act extended food stamp eligibility to qualified aliens receiving disability related benefits regardless of date of entry, effective October 1, 2002, to qualified aliens who had resided in the United States in qualified status for five years or more, effective April 1, 2003, and to all qualified alien children regardless of date of entry, effective October 1, 2003. The five year residence rule aligned food stamp eligibility for adult qualified aliens, a specified program governed by 8 U.S.C. 1612, with the separate five year bar on federal means tested benefits codified at 8 U.S.C. 1613, while the treatment of children removed the waiting period for a category that the 1998 Act had covered only for children present in 1996. A study prepared by the Urban Institute for the Economic Research Service of the Department of Agriculture estimated that by 2004 the 2002 restorations had extended eligibility to roughly one million legal immigrants. Eligibility for Supplemental Security Income and for Temporary Assistance for Needy Families was not restored on comparable terms, so that the immigrant restrictions of 1996 survived in different form across programs: largely dismantled for food assistance, preserved in modified form for cash assistance and for Supplemental Security Income.

### Reauthorization Delayed and Then Folded Into a Budget Law

The authorization for Temporary Assistance for Needy Families ran through fiscal 2002, and reauthorization politics consumed the following three years without producing a freestanding statute. The Congressional Research Service recorded that, in the absence of reauthorization legislation, program and funding authority was extended on a temporary basis more than a dozen times between 2002 and 2006. Proposals considered between 2002 and 2005 differed over child care funding and over proposed increases in work requirements, and none completed passage as a freestanding welfare bill. The resolution arrived through the budget reconciliation process. The Deficit Reduction Act of 2005, Public Law 109-171, was signed on February 8, 2006, and extended Temporary Assistance for Needy Families funding through the end of fiscal 2010. Placement inside a deficit reduction law shaped the content: the Congressional Research Service summarized the Act as eliminating the performance bonus funds, establishing a 150 million dollar annual fund for healthy marriage and responsible fatherhood initiatives, and increasing mandatory funding from 2.717 billion dollars per year to 2.917 billion dollars per year for the child care programs that Title VI of the 1996 Act had consolidated.

The most consequential change in the Deficit Reduction Act of 2005 operated through a baseline rather than through a stated rate. Federal law required states to engage 50 percent of families with a parent in work activities and 90 percent of two parent families, subject to a caseload reduction credit that reduced the applicable standard by one percentage point for each percentage point decline in the caseload from a base year. Under the 1996 Act the base year was fiscal 1995, so that the large caseload declines of the late 1990s had reduced the effective standard for many states to a small fraction of the stated rate, and for some states to zero. The Deficit Reduction Act of 2005 recalibrated the credit to a fiscal 2005 base year, effective in fiscal 2007, so that only caseload declines after 2005 counted toward the credit. The stated rates of 50 percent and 90 percent were not altered. The practical effect, as the Congressional Research Service explained in summaries of the Act, was a substantial tightening of the standard that states actually faced, because a decade of caseload decline ceased to generate credit. The Act also broadened the population counted in the participation calculation to include families receiving assistance in separate state programs funded with state maintenance of effort dollars.

A second tightening arrived through regulation. The Deficit Reduction Act of 2005 directed the Secretary of Health and Human Services to define the specific activities that could count under each of the twelve work activities listed in the statute, to define the term work eligible individual, and to require states to establish procedures for verifying participation. The Department of Health and Human Services issued an interim final rule on June 29, 2006, and published a final rule on February 5, 2008, at volume 73 of the Federal Register, page 6772, with an effective date of October 1, 2008. The Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, in a February 2008 summary of the final rule prepared by Liz Schott, described the rule as supplying federal definitions for activities that states had previously defined with considerable variation, together with federal rules for counting and verifying hours. State discretion under the 1996 Act had included discretion over the content of countable activity; after October 1, 2008, whether an activity and the hours devoted to the activity counted toward the federal standard was governed by a federal definition. Reauthorization in 2006 therefore preserved the outward architecture of 1996 while narrowing state flexibility at the two points where flexibility had most diluted federal standards: the credit baseline and the definition of work.

### A Recession Test and a Temporary Emergency Fund

The recession that began in December 2007 supplied the first severe economic test of the block grant structure, and Congress answered with a temporary fund rather than with any change to the structure. The American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2009, Public Law 111-5, signed on February 17, 2009, created a 5 billion dollar Emergency Contingency Fund within Temporary Assistance for Needy Families for fiscal 2009 and fiscal 2010. The Congressional Research Service recorded that a state could receive federal payment for 80 percent of increased spending, measured against a base year of fiscal 2007 or fiscal 2008, in three categories: basic assistance, nonrecurrent short term benefits limited to no more than four months, and subsidized employment. Combined receipts from the new fund and the preexisting contingency fund were capped for each state at 50 percent of one year of the state family assistance grant. The same Act permitted states to freeze caseload reduction credits at prerecession levels for the work participation standards applicable through fiscal 2011.

Uptake was nearly universal. The Congressional Research Service recorded that all of the available 5 billion dollars was awarded by the expiration date of September 30, 2010. The Center on Budget and Policy Priorities recorded that every state except Wyoming drew on the Emergency Fund and that states used the funds for emergency payments, including payments to avert utility shutoffs and eviction, and for subsidized jobs that placed more than 250,000 low income parents and youth in employment. The House of Representatives twice passed legislation in 2010 to extend the fund, and the Senate did not approve either measure, according to the Congressional Research Service. The Claims Resolution Act of 2010, Public Law 111-291, extended the basic program through the end of fiscal 2011 without the Emergency Contingency Fund, so that the emergency instrument ended on the date written into the 2009 Act, September 30, 2010, while unemployment remained elevated by the standards of the preceding decade.

The caseload record of the recession supplied the contrast that shaped later assessment of the block grant design. Legal Momentum, analyzing data reported by the Department of Health and Human Services and the Department of Agriculture, calculated that the number of families receiving Temporary Assistance for Needy Families rose by 12 percent between December 2007 and March 2010, from 1,703,656 families to 1,913,210 families, while the number of households receiving food stamps rose by 48 percent over a comparable interval, from 12,313,466 households in December 2007 to 18,273,141 households in February 2010. The two programs differed in financing in a way that explained part of the divergence: food assistance benefits, renamed the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program in 2008, were funded as an entitlement with federal benefit costs paid for each eligible household that applied, whereas basic assistance was paid from a fixed grant that did not increase when applications increased, supplemented during the recession only by the temporary fund. The full treatment of caseload evidence belongs to the assessment of program impact; for the amendment history, the salient point is institutional. Congress recognized the limitation in 2009, addressed the limitation with an appropriation that expired in twenty months, and enacted no permanent countercyclical mechanism.

### Limited Doctrine From the Supreme Court

The judicial history of the 1996 Act is brief at the level of the Supreme Court. Challenges to immigrant eligibility classifications and to benefit administration proceeded in the lower federal courts and in state courts, and produced no Supreme Court decision invalidating a principal provision of the Act apart from the durational residency question decided in 1999. Litigation over the charitable choice provision, codified at 42 U.S.C. 604a, which permitted religious organizations to deliver services under specified programs without relinquishing religious character and subject to protections for beneficiaries, likewise produced no Supreme Court merits decision on the provision itself before August 1, 2019. The closest Supreme Court treatment of adjacent faith based funding, Hein v. Freedom from Religion Foundation, 551 U.S. 587 (2007), resolved a challenge to executive branch faith based initiatives on taxpayer standing grounds and did not reach the merits of any statutory charitable choice enactment.

Saenz v. Roe arose from a California statute enacted four years before the federal Act. In 1992 California amended the predecessor Aid to Families with Dependent Children program through section 11450.03 of the California Welfare and Institutions Code, limiting families in the first year of California residence to the benefit amount that the family would have received in the state of prior residence. Implementation was enjoined in litigation grounded in Shapiro v. Thompson, 394 U.S. 618 (1969), and approval by the Secretary of Health and Human Services was invalidated in a separate proceeding, so that the provision remained inoperative when Congress enacted the 1996 Act. Section 604(c) of the Social Security Act, codified at 42 U.S.C. 604(c), then supplied the connection to the federal statute: the provision expressly authorized a state receiving a Temporary Assistance for Needy Families grant to apply the program rules, including benefit amounts, of another state to a family that had resided in the state for less than twelve months. Because federal authorization replaced the waiver approval that California had previously required, the state announced that enforcement would begin on April 1, 1997. Plaintiffs filed a class action on that date challenging both the California statute and the federal authorization, and the District Court for the Eastern District of California issued a preliminary injunction, which the Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit affirmed without reaching the merits.

The Supreme Court decided the case on May 17, 1999, by a vote of seven to two. Justice John Paul Stevens wrote the majority opinion, joined by Justices Sandra Day O'Connor, Antonin Scalia, Anthony Kennedy, David Souter, Ruth Bader Ginsburg, and Stephen Breyer; Chief Justice William Rehnquist and Justice Clarence Thomas dissented. The majority described the right to travel as embracing three components: the right of a citizen of one state to enter and to leave another state, the right to be treated as a welcome visitor while temporarily present in another state, and, for travelers who elect to become residents, the right to be treated like other citizens of the adopted state. The third component governed the case. The majority held that the right of a newly arrived citizen to the same privileges and immunities enjoyed by other citizens of the new state is protected by the Privileges or Immunities Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment, a ground that the opinion located in the discussion of national citizenship in the Slaughter-House Cases, 83 U.S. 36 (1873). On that ground the classification in section 11450.03 penalized the exercise of the right to travel by assigning a lower benefit on the basis of duration of residence, and fiscal savings did not sustain the classification. Congressional authorization in 42 U.S.C. 604(c) did not save the California statute, because Congress possesses no authority to license a state to abridge a privilege protected by the Fourteenth Amendment; the federal provision was accordingly inoperative to the extent that the provision purported to authorize the durational limit. The decision left the remainder of the 1996 Act untouched.

### Permanence Through Extension Without Revision

After the Deficit Reduction Act of 2005 extension expired at the end of fiscal 2010, Congress did not enact a further multi year reauthorization before the vantage of August 1, 2019. The Congressional Research Service recorded that Congress acted on a recurring basis to extend funding, and that the Consolidated Appropriations Act for fiscal 2017 extended funding through September 30, 2018, at the established levels. Further extensions carried the program into 2019. Funding for supplemental grants was discontinued after June 30, 2011, according to the Congressional Research Service, so that the extension era involved contraction at the margins alongside preservation at the center. Each extension preserved the statutory purposes, the work participation framework as recalibrated in 2006, the sixty month federal time limit, and the state maintenance of effort requirement, without reopening the allocation of the grant among states.

The frozen grant is therefore an amendment decision repeated by omission. The Congressional Research Service recorded that the basic block grant amount established in the 1996 law totaled 16.6 billion dollars for the fifty states, the District of Columbia, the territories, and tribal programs, with 16.5 billion dollars for the fifty states and the District of Columbia, and that the amount remained unchanged from fiscal 1997 through fiscal 2016, without adjustment for inflation, for changes in the assistance caseload, or for changes in the population in poverty. The Congressional Research Service calculated that the real value of the grant declined by one third, measured at 33.1 percent, over the period from fiscal 1997 through fiscal 2016, and that the grant in fiscal 2018 stood 36 percent below the fiscal 1997 value in inflation adjusted terms. Beginning in fiscal 2017, appropriations law reduced the state family assistance grant by 0.33 percent to finance research, evaluation, and technical assistance. A grant fixed in nominal dollars transferred the consequences of inflation to state budgets automatically, without any vote on a reduction. By August 1, 2019, the period after fiscal 2010 had been governed entirely by extensions, and the defining fiscal fact of the statute remained the figure chosen in 1996.

## Measured Effects, Contested Readings, and Honest Limits

### The Caseload Record in the Administrative Series

The clearest measured effect of the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996 (Public Law 104-193) was a contraction in cash assistance receipt that began before enactment and accelerated after enactment. The Congressional Research Service, in its recurring report on the Temporary Assistance for Needy Families block grant, compiled a calendar year series from data supplied by the Department of Health and Human Services that placed Aid to Families with Dependent Children recipients at 14.161 million in 1994, across 5.033 million families, at 12.321 million in 1996, and, after enactment, at 10.376 million in 1997, at 8.347 million in 1998, at 6.924 million in 1999, and at 6.143 million in 2000. A portion of the decline therefore preceded the federal statute and coincided with state waivers and a strengthening labor market. In four calendar years after enactment, receipt had fallen by roughly one half from the 1996 level and by well over one half from the 1994 peak. Family counts moved in parallel, from 5.033 million families in 1994 to 2.303 million families in 2000 in the same table.

The contraction did not reverse in the following decade. The Congressional Research Service series placed recipients at 5.717 million in 2001, at 5.028 million in 2005, at 4.598 million in 2010, at 3.957 million in 2014, and at 3.516 million in 2017, a markedly smaller population than the population served in the mid 1990s, even in years when poverty among children rose. Analysts who treated caseload decline as the central success measure, including Ron Haskins, a principal House staff architect of the statute who later assessed its record at the Brookings Institution, pointed to that trajectory as evidence that the statute had changed expectations, office practice, and recipient behavior on a national scale. Analysts who treated caseload decline as an ambiguous measure, including Peter Edelman, who resigned from the Department of Health and Human Services in 1996 in protest against the signing of the statute, answered that a smaller caseload proved only that fewer families received cash assistance, and proved nothing by itself about whether departing families had earnings, other support, or neither. Both readings used the same administrative counts. The disagreement concerned what a count of receipt could and could not establish.

A second measure deepened that disagreement, because the destination of block grant money changed as receipt fell. The Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, drawing on federal and state financial data, reported that states spent 70 percent of combined federal and state Temporary Assistance for Needy Families funds on basic assistance in fiscal year 1997, and that the share had fallen to 28 percent in fiscal year 2009 and to 29 percent in fiscal year 2011, with the balance directed toward child care, work activities, transfers, administration, and other allowable purposes. Supporters of state flexibility, including Robert Rector of the Heritage Foundation in his published assessments, treated the redirection as a legitimate use of a block grant, on the ground that child care, transportation, and work supports could serve low income families in ways that a monthly cash payment did not. Analysts at the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities treated the same redirection as a dilution of the cash safety net, on the ground that a program spending less than one third of its resources on basic assistance performed a different function from the function described at enactment. The expenditure shares were not in dispute. The dispute concerned whether flexibility was the design working as intended or the design drifting away from cash assistance.

### Employment, Earnings, and the Problem of Attribution

Employment among single mothers rose sharply in the second half of the 1990s, and the rise was largest in the groups most exposed to the statute. Robert A. Moffitt of Johns Hopkins University, summarizing Bureau of Labor Statistics based tabulations in a 2002 Brookings Institution policy brief, reported that employment among single mothers rose from 60 percent in 1994 to 72 percent in 1999, and that employment among single mothers who had never married, a group with comparatively low average education and high rates of welfare receipt, rose from 47 percent in 1994 to 65 percent in 1999. Ron Haskins, in congressional testimony summarizing Census Bureau income data, reported that employment among single mothers reached nearly 75 percent by 2000, the highest level recorded to that date, and that a majority of mothers who left the rolls worked at some point after exit, while acknowledging that the jobs obtained in 2000 paid low wages, at around eight dollars per hour in the studies he cited. The direction of the employment change and its concentration among never married mothers were common ground across most assessments published between 2000 and 2019. The causal shares assigned to the statute were not common ground.

Rebecca M. Blank of the University of Michigan, in reviews published in 2002 and in later syntheses in 2005 and 2007, concluded that welfare reform had a significant effect on caseloads and on employment, while also concluding that reform did not explain the majority of the changes observed in the 1990s. Blank emphasized a methodological constraint: the statute, the state waivers that preceded it, an expansion of the Earned Income Tax Credit enacted in 1993, increases in child care funding, and an exceptionally tight labor market arrived in overlapping sequence and interacted with one another, so that a precise allocation among them exceeded what the research designs of the period could deliver. The Council of Economic Advisers, in reports issued during the Clinton administration, likewise treated employment gains as the product of combined forces. Bruce D. Meyer and Dan T. Rosenbaum, in a study of single mothers between 1984 and 1996, attributed about 60 percent of a twelve percentage point increase in employment to changes in taxes, principally the Earned Income Tax Credit, and about 15 percent to welfare reform, with the weight of welfare reform rising in the 1992 to 1996 subperiod, when Meyer and Rosenbaum attributed about 35 percent of the increase to the Earned Income Tax Credit and about 20 percent to welfare reform. The distinction mattered, because a policy can reduce receipt through diversion, sanction, and time limits without producing an equivalent gain in employment, and a tax credit can raise employment among mothers who never received cash assistance at all.

Earnings evidence produced a similar pattern. Haskins, summarizing Census Bureau data for female headed families in the lower part of the income distribution, reported that earnings rose in the late 1990s while income from cash, food, and housing assistance fell, and that total income for the group ended the decade about 25 percent higher in inflation adjusted terms than at its start in the series he presented. On that evidence, Haskins concluded that increased earnings more than offset lost transfer income for the group as a whole. Blank, in her 2007 analysis for The Future of Children, reported a different distribution underneath the averages. Blank estimated that disconnected single mothers, defined as mothers who were neither working nor receiving welfare, made up 20 percent to 25 percent of low income single mothers by the mid 2000s, and that about 2.2 million women heading families were unable to find jobs or to keep jobs they found, with almost 4 million children in their care. Edelman, writing in 2001 and in later work, argued in related terms that a substantial fraction of the more than 2.5 million women who left the rolls after 1996 had neither employment nor cash assistance at the points measured. The success reading asked whether the typical trajectory after reform involved more work and higher total income than the typical trajectory before reform, and answered affirmatively on the late 1990s evidence. The failure reading asked what happened to mothers at the bottom of the distribution, in particular mothers with low education, health limitations, caregiving burdens, or unstable housing, and answered that the statute offered time limited aid and work requirements to a population part of which could not sustain employment, with no entitlement remaining when employment failed.

### Poverty, Deep Poverty, and a Dispute Over Measurement

Official poverty among children fell during the late 1990s expansion and rose again when labor markets weakened. The Census Bureau, in its Current Population Survey Annual Social and Economic Supplements, recorded the poverty rate for persons under age 18 at 16.2 percent in 2000, after a decline through the second half of the 1990s. The Census Bureau recorded the same rate at 22.0 percent in 2010, a level that the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, in its statement on the 2010 release, described as the highest child poverty rate in seventeen years. Haskins treated the late 1990s decline as central evidence for the success reading, noting in testimony that poverty among Black children and poverty among children in female headed families reached the lowest levels recorded to that point in 2000 or 2001 in the Census Bureau series, and attributing the decline primarily to increased maternal employment rather than to increased transfer payments. Blank offered a more qualified assessment, crediting the combination of employment growth, the Earned Income Tax Credit, and transfer programs other than cash assistance for the late 1990s improvement, while warning that gains concentrated in a period of very low unemployment provided a weak test of how the reformed system would perform under adverse conditions. Between 2000 and 2010, child poverty rose as employment fell, and the reformed cash program expanded only modestly in response, a pattern examined in the recession evidence below.

Deep poverty produced the sharpest factual dispute in the literature, because the dispute joined a claim about hardship to a technical claim about survey measurement. Kathryn J. Edin of Johns Hopkins University and H. Luke Shaefer of the University of Michigan, in $2.00 a Day: Living on Almost Nothing in America, published in 2015, reported an analysis of the Census Bureau Survey of Income and Program Participation indicating that in early 2011 about 1.5 million households with about 3 million children were surviving on cash incomes of no more than two dollars per person per day in a given month, and that the number of households in that condition had more than doubled since 1996. Edin and Shaefer combined the survey analysis with fieldwork in Chicago, Cleveland, Appalachian communities, and communities in the Mississippi Delta, documenting households that sold plasma, collected recyclable material, and relied on private charity in the absence of cash assistance and steady earnings. Edin and Shaefer presented a supplementary calculation that included the value of nutrition assistance and tax credits, while maintaining that cash held a distinct function, because rent, transportation, and household necessities required cash payment. On the authorship account, the growth of near zero cash income households was a direct consequence of replacing an entitlement with a block grant under which states could deny, divert, or time limit aid, leaving households without earnings and without a cash benefit of last resort.

Published rebuttals challenged the survey inference, the trend, and the causal attribution. Scott Winship of the Manhattan Institute, in a 2016 report reexamining the evidence on extreme poverty, argued that the Survey of Income and Program Participation understated transfer income, that reporting error could manufacture an apparent rise in near zero income households, and that alternative measures corrected for inflation and for noncash benefits showed improvement rather than deterioration. Winship reported adjusted estimates placing child poverty at 13.1 percent in 1996 and at 7.8 percent in 2014 under his preferred corrections, and argued that children living on less than two dollars per day were so rare in the corrected data that a reliable trend could not be established. Bruce D. Meyer of the University of Chicago and James X. Sullivan of the University of Notre Dame, in consumption based research, argued that consumption provided a more reliable measure of material well being than reported income, because consumption drew on savings, assistance from others, and benefits that income surveys captured poorly. Meyer and Sullivan reported consumption based poverty rates substantially below official income based rates, and material conditions that did not match a picture of widespread destitution on the scale implied by the two dollar threshold. Edin and Shaefer replied that underreporting corrections relied on assumptions of their own, that consumption data had measurement limits of a different kind, and that field observation corroborated the existence of households with almost no cash regardless of disputes about the exact national count. By the vantage of August 1, 2019, the literature had not converged. One body of work, grounded in cash income surveys and fieldwork, reported a large and growing population at the very bottom. Another, grounded in consumption data and administrative corrections, reported that population as small, mismeasured, and not demonstrably growing.

### A Changed Safety Net Rather Than an Ended Safety Net

A counter reading, advanced by researchers across otherwise opposed positions, held that describing the statute as the end of the American safety net misdescribed the system that existed after 1996. The statute repealed one entitlement, Aid to Families with Dependent Children, and replaced that entitlement with a fixed block grant. The statute did not repeal the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, which operated under the former name of food stamps until a statutory renaming in 2008, and did not repeal Medicaid, Supplemental Security Income, federal housing assistance, or the Earned Income Tax Credit. Several of those programs expanded substantially after 1996. Robert A. Moffitt, in research on the composition of the safety net presented in 2014 and published in Demography in 2015, examined fifteen large means tested programs and reported that total inflation adjusted spending on those programs was 74 percent higher in 2007 than in 1975. Moffitt reported that the growth concealed a redistribution. For single parent families with the lowest earnings, aid fell by 35 percent between 1983 and 2004, while aid rose by 74 percent over the same period for families with somewhat higher earnings, and assistance shifted toward married parent families, toward the elderly, and toward persons with disabilities, and away from nonelderly, nondisabled single parent families with very low earnings.

That compositional framing reconciled facts that the simpler accounts each used selectively. Total means tested spending rose after 1996 rather than falling, which undercut any description of a general retrenchment of public support for low income households. Cash assistance for nonworking parents fell sharply and reached a shrinking share of poor families, which undercut any description of the post 1996 system as a preserved safety net for parents without earnings. Support conditioned on work, delivered through the Earned Income Tax Credit, child care subsidies tied to employment, and nutrition assistance that supplemented low wages, grew as a share of the whole, while support available without work contracted as a share of the whole. Haskins incorporated the compositional point into the success reading, arguing that Congress had deliberately expanded work supports alongside the statute, so that a working mother in a low wage job in 2000 could assemble earnings, tax credits, nutrition assistance, Medicaid coverage for her children, and child care help into a package exceeding the value of the cash grant she had left. Edelman incorporated the same point into the failure reading, arguing that a system generous to workers and restrictive toward nonworkers abandoned the function of a floor, because the households in greatest need were households in which work had failed, had not been found, or could not be sustained. Blank positioned her assessment between those poles, treating the reorientation toward work conditioned aid as an achievement for mothers able to work substantial hours, and as an unresolved failure for mothers facing multiple barriers to employment, for whom the reformed system provided neither sustained cash support nor services intensive enough to produce stable employment.

### The Recession Test and the Limits of the Record

The recession that began in December 2007 supplied the test that Blank and other analysts had identified in advance, a period in which employment among low wage workers fell sharply and demand for assistance rose. The cash program responded weakly in comparison with nutrition assistance. Legal Momentum, analyzing data reported by the Department of Health and Human Services in 2010, reported that families receiving Temporary Assistance for Needy Families rose by 12 percent between December 2007 and March 2010, from 1,703,656 families to 1,913,210 families, while households receiving food stamp benefits, under the program renamed the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program in 2008, rose by 48 percent over a closely comparable period, from 12,313,466 households in December 2007 to 18,273,141 households in February 2010. Department of Agriculture administrative data told the same story in individual terms, with nutrition assistance receipt rising from 26.3 million persons in 2007 to 40.2 million persons in 2010. The Congressional Research Service noted that Congress responded with temporary measures layered on top of the reformed structure, including a temporary five billion dollar fund for the cash program, rather than through any automatic expansion of the block grant itself. Haskins, in testimony during the recession, acknowledged that employment among female headed families fell as jobs disappeared, while maintaining that temporary federal action was an appropriate response to an exceptional downturn. Analysts at the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities answered that a safety net requiring emergency legislation to respond to a recession had failed a basic design test, because need rose automatically while the fixed grant did not. The figures supported the narrower proposition on which both sides relied: nutrition assistance, operating as an entitlement, expanded with need, and cash assistance, operating as a fixed block grant, expanded only modestly while unemployment approached ten percent.

The honest limits of the record followed from the structure Congress enacted in 1996. The statute did not adjust its own funding for inflation, for population change, or for changes in the number of families in poverty. The Congressional Research Service reported that the basic block grant totaled 16.6 billion dollars, that the amount remained unchanged from fiscal year 1997 through fiscal year 2016, and that its inflation adjusted value fell by about one third over that period, before a reduction of 0.33 percent from fiscal year 2017 left a total of 16.5 billion dollars. Congress enacted no general increase in the basic grant during the period ending on August 1, 2019. The reach of cash assistance contracted accordingly on the measure constructed by the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities from Current Population Survey poverty data and Department of Health and Human Services caseload data. The Center on Budget and Policy Priorities calculated that for every 100 families with children in poverty, 68 families received cash assistance in 1996, and that the same ratio, named the TANF to poverty ratio, stood at 23 families for every 100 families in poverty in 2015, after reaching that level in 2014. State ratios varied widely, so that access to cash assistance by 2015 depended heavily on the state in which a poor family lived.

What the statute demonstrably did not do can be stated without resolving the larger dispute. The statute did not preserve an entitlement to cash assistance for families meeting a federal definition of need, because repeal of that entitlement was its central legal act. The statute did not index its principal grant, and therefore did not maintain the real value of federal cash assistance funding across the business cycles and price changes of 1997 to 2019. The statute did not, on the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities ratio, reach most families in poverty with cash assistance by the middle of the 2010s. The statute did not eliminate the broader safety net, because nutrition assistance, health coverage, disability assistance, housing assistance, and work conditioned tax credits continued after 1996 and, in aggregate spending terms documented by Moffitt, expanded. The statute did not, on the evidence available by August 1, 2019, settle whether mothers who left cash assistance in the late 1990s achieved durable economic security, because follow up studies tracked employment for limited periods, because earnings gains were concentrated in an exceptional expansion, and because the population that left divided into groups with sharply different outcomes that aggregate statistics concealed. The record through 2019 supported narrower findings with confidence: receipt of cash assistance fell by more than half within four years of enactment and continued falling thereafter; employment among single mothers, and especially among never married mothers, rose to recorded highs in 1999 and 2000 before retreating in weaker labor markets; official child poverty fell to 2000 and rose thereafter with the cycle; total means tested support grew while its composition shifted toward work, toward disability, and toward in kind benefits; and cash assistance responded only modestly to the deepest recession of the period. Whether those findings amounted to success depended on the weight assigned to employment and self support relative to the weight assigned to a guaranteed cash floor for families without earnings. The statute itself assigned the first set of weights in its stated purposes. Its critics assigned the second. The administrative and survey record, read in full, supplied evidence for the consequences of each choice without supplying the choice itself.

## What the Statute Settled and What It Left Open

### Did One Sentence Really End the Entitlement?

Yes. Section 601(b) of Title IV provided that the part shall not be interpreted to entitle any individual or family to assistance. That language removed the federal legal claim that eligible families held under the former program, leaving assistance dependent on state plan choices, state eligibility rules, and the funds available under a fixed federal grant.

### What Did States Gain and Lose Under the Block Grant?

States gained control over eligibility definitions, benefit levels, sanctions, earnings disregards, shorter time limits, and the mix among cash assistance, child care, and work services. States lost open ended federal matching, so additional cases drew no additional federal dollars. Caseload, inflation, and recession risk therefore rested on state budgets rather than on the federal Treasury.

### Did the Act Abolish the Wider Safety Net?

No. The act repealed one entitlement, Aid to Families with Dependent Children, and replaced that entitlement with a block grant. Medicaid coverage was preserved and separated from cash receipt, food assistance continued as an entitlement subject to new restrictions, Supplemental Security Income continued under a narrowed childhood standard, and work conditioned tax credits continued alongside those programs.

### Has the Core Settlement Survived Later Amendment?

In substance, yes. Congress restored immigrant eligibility for defined groups between 1997 and 2002, recalibrated the caseload reduction credit in the Deficit Reduction Act of 2005, and created a temporary emergency fund during the recession that began in 2007. The entitlement sentence, the fixed grant amount, the sixty month limit, and the participation framework survived each of those changes.

The evidence question deserves a separate sitting as well. The companion account of [welfare reform outcomes](/2019/10/15/welfare-reform-1996-outcomes/) assembles the employment, earnings, poverty, and deep poverty literature in sequence, so that the claims made for the statute and the claims made against the statute can each be tested against the studies that proponents and critics actually cited, rather than against summaries of summaries.

Comparison sharpens both accounts. The direct comparison of [the AFDC and TANF welfare models](/2019/11/15/afdc-vs-tanf-welfare-models/) places entitlement, matching finance, and state discretion beside block grant finance and state definition, element by element. Misreadings of the statute are common enough to merit separate treatment, and the catalogue of [welfare reform myths](/2019/12/15/welfare-reform-myths/) separates propositions that the text supports from propositions that entered debate without textual support, including claims about benefit levels, family formation, and the scope of the repeal. Procedure explains part of the outcome, because the reconciliation vehicle determined which provisions could travel together and which majority sufficed for passage, and the explanation of [the reconciliation process](/2020/03/01/reconciliation-process-explained/) supplies the budget law background that the passage section of this article compressed.

Taken together, those five routes return to the same statutory text from five directions: finance, evidence, comparison, misconception, and procedure. A durable reading of Public Law 104-193 depends on holding those directions apart, because a finding about caseload decline is not a finding about family income, a finding about state flexibility is not a finding about adequacy, and a finding about legislative procedure is not a finding about merit. The statute rewards that discipline, since the central act of the statute was itself an act of legal precision, a single sentence that changed the status of every family that might otherwise have claimed assistance. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did welfare reform actually change in 1996?

Welfare reform replaced Aid to Families with Dependent Children with Temporary Assistance for Needy Families. The act ended the individual entitlement to cash assistance, replaced open ended federal matching with a fixed block grant of 16.489 billion dollars, imposed a sixty month limit on federally funded assistance, and set state participation rates for work activities. The act also restricted benefits for immigrants, rebuilt child support enforcement, narrowed childhood disability in Supplemental Security Income, imposed a work based time limit on food assistance for a defined class of adults, and authorized charitable choice contracting. Medicaid coverage for low income families was preserved and separated from receipt of cash assistance.

### Q: Which president signed welfare reform?

President William J. Clinton signed welfare reform on August 22, 1996, in a Rose Garden ceremony at the White House. Clinton had promised during the presidential campaign of 1992 to end welfare as the country had known the system, and the signing remarks presented the act as fulfillment of that pledge, as a transformation built on work, responsibility, and family. The same remarks repeated reservations about provisions restricting benefits for legal immigrants and about food stamp provisions, together with a commitment to repair efforts after enactment. Lillie Harden of Arkansas, a former recipient who had left assistance for employment, spoke at the ceremony.

### Q: Did the president veto welfare reform before signing it?

Yes, twice. President Clinton vetoed H.R. 2491, the Seven Year Balanced Budget Reconciliation Act of 1995, on December 6, 1995, a comprehensive budget bill in which welfare provisions derived from H.R. 4 travelled alongside Medicare, Medicaid, tax, and nutrition provisions. Clinton then vetoed H.R. 4 itself, the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Act of 1995, on January 9, 1996, objecting that the bill did too little to move people from welfare to work and carried deep budget cuts. No override attempt followed either veto, and the third bill, H.R. 3734, obtained a signature after revisions addressed part of the administration list of conditions.

### Q: Did welfare reform end the entitlement to assistance?

Yes. Section 401 of the Social Security Act, codified at 42 U.S.C. 601, placed a purpose statement in subsection (a) and a single operative sentence in subsection (b), under the heading No individual entitlement. The sentence provides that the part shall not be interpreted to entitle any individual or family to assistance under any State program funded under the part. Under the former program, a family meeting the rules of an approved state plan held a claim that courts could enforce, with procedural due process required for termination under Goldberg v. Kelly. After enactment, assistance rested on state plan choices rather than on a federal promise to the family.

### Q: What is the five year limit in welfare reform?

The five year limit is a sixty month limit on the use of federal funds, set at 42 U.S.C. 608(a)(7). A state may not use federal grant funds to provide assistance to a family that includes an adult who has received sixty months of federally funded assistance, whether or not the months were consecutive. Months as a minor child who was not a head of household are disregarded, as are certain months lived in Indian country or an Alaskan Native village with very high joblessness. A hardship exemption covers at most 20 percent of the caseload. A state may assist a family past sixty months with segregated state funds.

### Q: Did officials resign over welfare reform?

Yes. Three senior officials of the Department of Health and Human Services resigned in protest. Wendell E. Primus, Deputy Assistant Secretary for Human Services Policy, resigned in August 1996, in a sequence that began on the morning after President Clinton announced an intention to sign on July 31, 1996. Mary Jo Bane, Assistant Secretary for Children and Families, and Peter B. Edelman, Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation, resigned in September 1996, in announcements reported on September 12, 1996. Edelman later extended the protest in print, in an account published in March 1997 under the title The Worst Thing Bill Clinton Has Done.

### Q: Did welfare reform restrict benefits for immigrants?

Yes. Title IV created a framework codified at 8 U.S.C. 1601 to 1641, built on the category of qualified alien. A person who was not a qualified alien became ineligible for a federal public benefit, subject to exceptions including emergency medical assistance and immunizations. Section 402 limited qualified alien eligibility for specified programs including Supplemental Security Income and food stamps, and section 403 imposed a five year bar on federal means tested benefits for qualified aliens entering on or after August 22, 1996. Sponsor deeming attributed a sponsor's resources to a sponsored immigrant. Congress restored eligibility for defined groups in 1997, 1998, and 2002, with the broadest restorations in food assistance.

### Q: What is the public law number of welfare reform?

The public law number is Public Law 104-193. The measure travelled as H.R. 3734 in the 104th Congress, a budget reconciliation bill reported by the House Budget Committee on June 27, 1996. The House agreed to the conference report on July 31, 1996, by 328 to 101, and the Senate agreed on August 1, 1996, by 78 to 21. President Clinton signed the bill on August 22, 1996, and the statute was published at 110 Stat. 2105, with the approval date printed on the opening page. The formal name is the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996.

### Q: How large was the TANF block grant and did the amount keep pace with inflation?

The state family assistance grant, the central component of the block grant, was 16.489 billion dollars per year, commonly rounded to 16.5 billion dollars, with the total basic block grant commonly stated at 16.6 billion dollars including territories. Each state share derived from federal spending in base years between fiscal 1992 and fiscal 1995, rather than from measured need in the year of payment. The amount carried no adjustment for inflation, population change, caseload, unemployment, or child poverty. The Congressional Research Service calculated that the real value of the basic block grant in fiscal 2018 stood 36.2 percent below the real value in fiscal 1997, a decline of about 36 percent in real terms.

### Q: What is the caseload reduction credit and how does the credit change the work rates a state faces?

The caseload reduction credit, at 42 U.S.C. 607(b), reduces the participation rate a state must achieve by the percentage decline in the state average monthly caseload against a base year, which the act as enacted fixed at fiscal 1995. The Secretary disregards decline caused by federal law or by changes in state eligibility criteria. The arithmetic is direct subtraction: a caseload 40 percent below the base year yields a credit of forty percentage points, reducing a nominal 50 percent all families standard to an effective standard of 10 percent. Because caseloads fell sharply after 1995, the credit determined whether the stated rates bound in practice for many states.

### Q: What did the act change in child support enforcement?

Title III expanded the child support enforcement program in Part D of Title IV of the Social Security Act. Each state was required to operate a directory of new hires, feeding a National Directory of New Hires inside the Federal Parent Locator Service, with matches triggering instructions to employers to withhold support from wages. The title required federal and state case registries of support orders, hospital based voluntary paternity acknowledgment, simplified genetic testing procedures, and adoption of the Uniform Interstate Family Support Act by every state. Enforcement remedies expanded to cover tax refund intercepts, liens on real and personal property, reporting of arrears to consumer reporting agencies, and suspension of driver, professional, and recreational licenses.

### Q: How did the act change Supplemental Security Income for children?

Title II, Subtitle B, sections 211 and 212, amended the definition of disability for a person under age eighteen at 42 U.S.C. 1382c(a)(3)(C). The former standard asked whether a child impairment was of comparable severity to an impairment that would disable an adult, applied after Sullivan v. Zebley through an individualized functional assessment. The act substituted a requirement of marked and severe functional limitations from a medically determinable impairment expected to last at least twelve months or to result in death, and removed the individualized functional assessment and the reference to maladaptive behavior. Children on the rolls faced redetermination under the new standard, and the Congressional Budget Office estimated that as many as 200,000 children could be affected.

### Q: What limit did the act place on food assistance for able bodied adults without dependents?

Title VIII created the category of able bodied adults without dependents, codified at 7 U.S.C. 2015(o). A person aged eighteen to forty nine who was not exempt had to work, or take part in a qualifying work program, for at least eighty hours in a month, or comply with a workfare assignment, in order to receive food stamps for more than three months in a thirty six month period. Exemptions covered persons medically certified as unfit for employment, persons in a household with a child under eighteen, pregnant persons, and persons meeting related work rules. The Secretary could waive the limit for areas with unemployment above ten percent or a demonstrated lack of jobs, and eligibility could be regained through thirty days of compliance.

### Q: What is charitable choice and which organizations did the provision cover?

Charitable choice is the name given to section 104 of the act, codified at 42 U.S.C. 604a and placed inside Title I. The provision allowed a state to contract with religious organizations, and to use certificates or vouchers redeemable with such organizations, on the same basis as any other nongovernmental provider. A participating religious organization retained religious character, could display religious symbols, and retained the Title VII exemption for religious employers, while accounting separately for government funds and refraining from expenditure of direct contract funds on sectarian worship, instruction, or proselytization. A provider could not discriminate against a beneficiary on the basis of religion, and a beneficiary who objected to the religious character of a provider was entitled to an accessible alternative of equal value.

### Q: How far did the cash assistance caseload fall after enactment?

The fall was large and sustained. A Congressional Research Service calendar year series placed recipients at 14.161 million in 1994 and 12.321 million in 1996, then at 10.376 million in 1997, 8.347 million in 1998, 6.924 million in 1999, and 6.143 million in 2000. Family counts fell from 5.033 million in 1994 to 2.303 million in 2000 in the same table. The decline began before enactment, during the waiver period and a strengthening labor market, and accelerated after enactment. The series placed recipients at 5.028 million in 2005, 4.598 million in 2010, and 3.516 million in 2017, so the contraction did not reverse in the following decade.

### Q: How much of TANF spending reaches families as basic assistance?

The share fell sharply as caseloads declined. The Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, drawing on federal and state financial data, reported that states spent 70 percent of combined federal and state funds on basic assistance in fiscal 1997. The share had fallen to 28 percent in fiscal 2009 and stood at 29 percent in fiscal 2011, with the balance directed toward child care, work activities, transfers, administration, and other allowable purposes. Supporters of state flexibility treated the redirection as a legitimate use of a block grant, because child care and work supports serve low income families. Critics at the Center treated the same redirection as dilution of the cash safety net.

### Q: What changed in the reauthorization completed under the Deficit Reduction Act?

The Deficit Reduction Act of 2005, Public Law 109-171, signed on February 8, 2006, extended funding through fiscal 2010 inside a budget law, after more than a dozen temporary extensions. The act left the stated participation rates of 50 percent and 90 percent untouched, but reset the caseload reduction credit base year from fiscal 1995 to fiscal 2005, effective in fiscal 2007, so a decade of caseload decline ceased to generate credit and the effective standard tightened substantially. The act eliminated performance bonus funds, created a 150 million dollar annual fund for healthy marriage and responsible fatherhood initiatives, raised mandatory child care funding, and directed the Secretary to define countable work activities and verification procedures by regulation.

### Q: Can a state use state funds to assist a family after sixty months of federally funded assistance?

Yes. The sixty month limit at 42 U.S.C. 608(a)(7) bars the use of a federal grant to provide assistance to a family that includes an adult who has received sixty months of federally funded assistance. The paragraph does not bar a state from assisting the same family with state funds segregated from federal funds, including funds counted toward maintenance of effort in a separate state program. A state may therefore continue assistance beyond sixty months at state expense, end assistance at sixty months, or impose a shorter limit of state design. Separately, hardship exemptions allow federally funded assistance past sixty months for at most 20 percent of the caseload.

### Q: Did the act end the wider safety net?

No. The act repealed the entitlement to Aid to Families with Dependent Children and replaced that entitlement with a fixed block grant, and the act left the other principal programs in place. Food assistance, renamed the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program in 2008, expanded as an entitlement during the recession that began in 2007, while cash assistance rose only modestly. Medicaid, Supplemental Security Income, federal housing assistance, and the Earned Income Tax Credit also continued. Robert A. Moffitt, examining fifteen large means tested programs, reported that total inflation adjusted spending on those programs was 74 percent higher in 2007 than in 1975, alongside a redistribution away from nonworking single parent families toward families with earnings.

### Q: How is the entitlement sentence cited and where does the sentence appear in the Code?

The citation is 42 U.S.C. 601(b). The sentence appears in section 401 of the Social Security Act, the opening section of the new Part A of Title IV that the act substituted for the former Part A, ahead of provisions on state plans, grants, uses of funds, participation rates, prohibitions, and penalties. Subsection (a) states the purposes of the part, and subsection (b), under the heading No individual entitlement, provides that this part shall not be interpreted to entitle any individual or family to assistance under any State program funded under this part. The placement matters, because the denial of entitlement precedes every description of money, eligibility, or work rules in the part.
