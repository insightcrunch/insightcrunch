## PURPA and the Five Bill Energy Program

PURPA is the statute that built an American industry by accident. The Public Utility Regulatory Policies Act of 1978, Public Law 95-617, was signed by President Jimmy Carter on November 9, 1978, as one of five energy bills enacted on the same day. As of November 2016, it stood on the books as amended, its most consequential provision, section 210, still governing how electric utilities purchase power from generators they do not own. A reader who finishes this profile should be able to explain three things without hesitation: that the independent power industry and the commercial market for renewable electricity in the United States both descend from a single 1978 provision requiring utilities to buy power from small generators at their own avoided cost, that this statute was one of five enacted the same day as a single presidential program split across committees, and that the same package contained a statute banning new gas fired power plants whose prohibitions had to be repealed within a decade. That is the whole story in one test, and every section that follows is scaffolding for it.

![The 1978 National Energy Act package and the PURPA independent power industry - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-60.webp)

The strangeness of PURPA begins with its shape. It is at once a utility regulation statute, a ratemaking statute, and an industrial policy statute, and those identities do not sit together comfortably. Titles I and III directed state utility commissions to consider a list of federal ratemaking standards, which made the law look like a mild federal nudge toward better rate design. Title II, by contrast, contained section 210, the provision that ordered electric utilities to buy power from qualifying cogeneration and small power production facilities at a price tied to the utility's own cost of generating or buying that power elsewhere. The statute also carried a small hydroelectric program and gave the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission new authority over interconnection and wheeling. No single theory of energy policy unites those pieces. They were united by the legislative vehicle: a presidential energy program that had to be divided into pieces small enough to fit through different congressional committees, and PURPA was the piece that fit through the tax and utility committees rather than the energy committees. Understanding the law requires understanding that it was shaped by committee jurisdiction as much as by policy logic, a lesson the package section of this article develops in full.

The obscurity of PURPA is part of its interest. Ask a casual observer of American energy law to name the landmark statutes and the answers will run to the Federal Power Act, the Natural Gas Act, the Atomic Energy Act, and the Energy Policy Acts of 1992 and 2005. PURPA rarely makes the list, yet the commercial lineage of the independent power business runs directly through it. Before section 210, the regulated electric utility was the only lawful producer of electricity in its service territory, and a factory with a cogeneration unit or a developer with a small hydro site had no guaranteed buyer for surplus power. After section 210, that factory and that developer had a buyer by federal command, at a price defined by federal rule, and the monopoly's wall had its first lawful breach. Everything that followed in American electricity restructuring, from the wholesale market experiments of the 1990s to the regional transmission organizations of the 2000s, builds on the precedent that someone other than the utility may generate power and sell it. This article treats PURPA as the least credited landmark in American energy law, and the evidence for that ranking is laid out across the sections on the purchase obligation, its consequences, and its later narrowing.

This profile is organized around the arc the statute itself demands. The first movement describes the five statute package, because PURPA cannot be understood apart from the program that produced it or the committee structure that divided it. The second movement explains the purchase obligation in section 210, the avoided cost standard, the definition of a qualifying facility, and the commission rules that made the obligation operational. The third movement traces the consequence: the rise of the independent power industry and the early commercial development of wind, small hydro, and biomass generation in the United States. The fourth movement covers the federalism fight, the state resistance to federal ratemaking standards, and the Supreme Court decision in 1982 that sustained the scheme. The fifth movement describes the narrowing, the 2005 amendments that let the commission relieve utilities of the purchase obligation where competitive wholesale markets exist. The sixth movement turns to the cautionary companion in the same package, the fuel use statute that banned new gas fired plants on a forecast of scarcity that proved wrong and whose prohibitions Congress repealed in 1987. A final movement addresses the counter reading that the purchase obligation was a subsidy, and shows why the real argument was always about measurement. A package table closes the body, and the frequently asked questions collect the long tail of reader queries.

A note on method governs everything that follows. Every statutory quotation is taken from the United States Code as compiled by the House Office of the Law Revision Counsel. Every roll call figure comes from Congress.gov. Every industry statistic is attributed to a named source in the sentence where it appears. Nothing in this article postdates November 1, 2016, the reference date for the series, and no claim is made about any proceeding pending after that date. The disputes that remain live between utilities and independent generators are presented at their strongest on both sides, because the obligation at the center of this statute has always been contested, and a profile that smoothed the contest into a single confident story would mislead the reader it claims to serve.

## The Package: How One Presidential Program Became Five Statutes

President Carter's energy program reached Congress as a single policy ambition and left it as five separate laws, and the reason was jurisdiction. A president can propose one program, but a bill must pass through the committees that claim its subject matter, and the energy program of 1977 touched taxation, utility regulation, conservation, fuel use, and natural gas pricing, which belonged to different committees in each chamber. The program was therefore divided into five bills: the National Energy Conservation Policy Act, the Powerplant and Industrial Fuel Use Act, the Public Utility Regulatory Policies Act, the Natural Gas Policy Act, and the Energy Tax Act. The American Public Power Association's legislative alert of that year listed exactly those five as the components of the National Energy Act. All five were signed on November 9, 1978, and the president's signing statement covered all five together, with remarks on H.R. 4018, H.R. 5263, H.R. 5037, H.R. 5146, and H.R. 5289. The single day of signing is the visible trace of a single program; the five public law numbers, 95-617 through 95-621, are the visible trace of five committee paths.

The committee split is worth describing precisely because it explains the odd contents of each bill. PURPA, H.R. 4018, introduced by Representative Thomas B. Evans Jr. of Delaware on February 24, 1977, was referred in the House to the Committee on Ways and Means, which reported it on June 16, 1977, as House Report 95-429, and in the Senate it went to the Committee on Finance. The conservation bill, H.R. 5037, the National Energy Conservation Policy Act, followed the same route: House Ways and Means, reported June 16, 1977, as House Report 95-431, then Senate Finance. The Natural Gas Policy Act, H.R. 5289, moved through the House Committee on Interstate and Foreign Commerce, whose Subcommittee on Energy and Power produced the economic analysis of the bill, while the conference agreement was compiled for the use of that committee and the Senate Committee on Energy and Natural Resources. The pattern is clean: the utility, tax, and conservation measures traveled through the revenue committees, while the gas pricing measure traveled through the commerce and energy committees. This is the sense in which the program was shaped by committee jurisdiction rather than by policy logic. A single coherent energy strategy was poured into the available jurisdictional molds, and the molds left their marks on the statutes. The division of one presidential program across House Ways and Means, House Interstate and Foreign Commerce, and their Senate counterparts is a textbook illustration of how [congressional committee jurisdiction and markup procedure](/2022/03/01/committee-system-and-markups/) determine what a statute can contain and how it is written.

The road from introduction to enactment ran through the conference committee, and the roll calls on PURPA itself show a bill that passed but never commanded consensus. Congress.gov records three roll calls on H.R. 4018. On October 6, 1977, the Senate passed the bill as amended, roll call number 533, by 86 to 7. On October 9, 1978, the Senate agreed to the conference report, roll call number 466, by 76 to 13. On October 14, 1978, the House agreed to the conference report, roll call number 932, by 231 to 168. The initial House passage in July 1977 had been by voice vote, which left no tally. The conference numbers tell their own story: comfortable in the Senate, narrow and contested in the House, with 168 votes against. A statute that would go on to remake the structure of an industry was, at birth, a divisive compromise stitched together in conference. The figures here, like all roll call figures in this article, are the Congress.gov tallies drawn from the Congressional Record, which are authoritative where secondary sources differ.

The five statutes covered five distinct subjects, and each deserves a compact description before this article narrows to PURPA. The Energy Tax Act, H.R. 5263, Public Law 95-618, 92 Stat. 3174, was the tax side of the program. It created the federal gas guzzler excise tax on low mileage automobiles, and it created residential energy credits, 30 percent of the first 2,000 dollars of qualifying expenditure plus 20 percent of expenditure above that, capped at 10,000 dollars, alongside business credits for renewable investment reaching 25 percent, all figures per the Congressional Research Service report RL33578 of 2008. The same CRS report notes that the gas guzzler tax was still in effect when the report was written, which makes the Energy Tax Act the quiet survivor of the package, a revenue measure whose mechanisms outlived the energy politics that produced them. The National Energy Conservation Policy Act, H.R. 5037, Public Law 95-619, 92 Stat. 3206, was the conservation side. It established a residential energy conservation service, authorized grants to states for energy audits and conservation projects in schools and hospitals, with Title III, Part 1 authorizing 900 million dollars over three years according to the American Public Power Association, and it funded weatherization. Codified at chapter 91 of title 42 of the United States Code, it was amended repeatedly through the 1980s, in 1982 by Public Law 97-229, in 1986 by Public Law 99-413, in 1987 by Public Law 100-12, and twice in 1988, which testifies to a program that Congress kept adjusting rather than abandoning. The earlier federal conservation framework is covered in the [Energy Policy and Conservation Act of 1975](/2016/10/01/energy-policy-conservation-act-1975/), which set the stage for the conservation title of the 1978 program.

The remaining two statutes were the interventionist pair, and both would be substantially undone. The Powerplant and Industrial Fuel Use Act, H.R. 5146, Public Law 95-620, 92 Stat. 3289, prohibited new electric powerplants and major fuel burning installations from using oil or natural gas as their primary fuel, forcing them toward coal and alternate fuels, subject to an exemptions regime. The Natural Gas Policy Act, H.R. 5289, Public Law 95-621, 92 Stat. 3350, phased the deregulation of wellhead gas prices through maximum lawful price ceilings by category, with Wikipedia's account of the act reporting that new gas carried a ceiling of 1.75 dollars per million British thermal units from April 1977, indexed, and it gave the commission authority over interstate and some intrastate production and transportation, plus incremental pricing of boiler fuel. The Natural Gas Policy Act was enacted against the natural gas shortages of 1976 and 1977, and its design tried to balance supply and demand while moving toward market based prices. The Fuel Use Act is developed at length in the cautionary companion section of this article, because its failure is as instructive as PURPA's success. The Natural Gas Policy Act's own fate belongs in the same frame: its wellhead price control regime, Title I of the act, was repealed effective January 1, 1993, by the Natural Gas Wellhead Decontrol Act of 1989, Public Law 101-60, signed July 26, 1989. Of the five statutes signed that November day, two would be narrowed or dismantled, two would survive in substance, and one, PURPA, would turn out to matter in a way none of its authors planned.

The lesson of the package structure reaches beyond energy history. When a program is divided to fit committee jurisdiction, each committee writes the piece it understands, in the language it knows, under the political pressures it faces. The Ways and Means pieces spoke the language of tax credits and ratemaking standards. The Commerce pieces spoke the language of price ceilings and fuel prohibitions. The result was not a single integrated energy law but five laws that happened to share a signing ceremony, and their fates diverged accordingly. PURPA's purchase obligation survived and grew in importance because it created a constituency, the independent generators, who defended it. The Fuel Use Act's prohibitions collapsed because they created only resentment and no constituency, and because the forecast beneath them was wrong. The tax credits endured because revenue measures are hard to repeal once industries organize around them. The gas price controls were dismantled once the shortage they addressed gave way to surplus. A student of legislation could learn this pattern from any large program, but the 1978 package teaches it with unusual clarity, because the five pieces were born together and can be watched diverging across the decades from a single starting line.

One more feature of the package deserves attention before the article turns to PURPA's substance. The five bills were not only divided by committee; they were reunited in conference and in the president's signature, which gave the program a political unity its substance never possessed. Carter's signing statement treated the five as one achievement, and the public law numbers, consecutive from 617 to 621, reinforced the impression of a single National Energy Act. But consecutive numbers are an administrative convenience, not a policy argument. The statutes amended different titles of the United States Code, answered to different committees for oversight, and were later amended or repealed on separate tracks. The unity was ceremonial; the divergence was real. That divergence is the reason this profile must carry all five statutes in its opening while spending most of its length on one. PURPA is the member of the package that earned the attention, but the package is the reason PURPA looks the way it does.

The program's aims can be read off the five statutes themselves, because each bill was an answer to a perceived failure. The tax bill answered the failure to price energy waste, putting an excise tax on the most inefficient automobiles and paying households and businesses to conserve. The conservation bill answered the failure to invest in efficiency, sending federal money to states, schools, hospitals, and low income households for audits, retrofits, and weatherization. The fuel use bill answered the fear of import dependence and gas depletion, commanding industry away from oil and gas by legal prohibition. The gas pricing bill answered the shortages of 1976 and 1977, trying to coax new supply out of the ground with higher ceiling prices while managing the transition away from controls. And PURPA answered the failure of the monopoly utility to make room for anyone else, which was the least visible failure and, as events proved, the most structurally important. Each answer reflected the worldview of the committee that wrote it. The revenue committees believed in prices and incentives. The commerce committees believed in commands and ceilings. The program's intellectual incoherence, incentives here and prohibitions there, was not a flaw in the drafting but a faithful reflection of a Congress that had not decided what it believed about energy, only that it had to do something.

That indecision is worth pausing over, because it explains why the package's most successful member was the one its authors understood least. The tax writers understood taxes. The commerce committees understood prohibitions. Nobody in 1978 understood the independent power industry, because it did not exist. Section 210 was written by people who thought they were encouraging factories to burn waste heat and developers to refurbish small dams, and the purchase obligation was, in their minds, a modest nudge, a guaranteed buyer to make small projects viable. The modesty of the ambition is part of why the provision passed: it asked for so little, a buyer at a fair price, that its revolutionary implications went unnoticed. Had the drafters proposed to create a competitive generating industry outright, the utilities would have killed the bill. By proposing only to help small cogenerators and renewable developers, they created the industry without a fight about it. The lesson generalizes beyond energy. The most consequential provisions are often the ones nobody fought over, because nobody saw what they would become. The provisions everyone fought over, the gas price ceilings, the fuel prohibitions, were the ones whose premises were thinnest. Attention in legislation is not correlated with importance, and the 1978 package is the proof.

The legislative journey of the PURPA bill itself illustrates how much of a statute's fate is decided in procedure. H.R. 4018 was introduced by Representative Thomas B. Evans Jr., Republican of Delaware, on February 24, 1977, and referred the same day to the House Committee on Ways and Means, which reported it on June 16, 1977, as House Report 95-429. The initial House passage came on July 18, 1977, by voice vote, which meant no recorded tally and no fingerprints. In the Senate, the bill went to the Committee on Finance on July 20, 1977, was discharged on October 5, 1977, and passed the next day as amended, with the Senate inserting provisions from S. 2114 and certain provisions from H.R. 8444, by roll call 533, 86 to 7, per Congress.gov. The two chambers had thus passed different bills, and the differences went to conference, where the real bargaining happened. The conference report came back to a Senate that agreed 76 to 13 on October 9, 1978, roll call 466, and a House that agreed 231 to 168 on October 14, 1978, roll call 932. The House margin, 63 votes, was the narrowest of the three recorded votes, and it suggests how contested the final package was in the chamber closest to the voters. A statute that would remake an industry cleared its last legislative hurdle with 168 votes against it.

The conference stage deserves emphasis because it is where the five bill package was effectively assembled. Each of the five bills went through its own committee and its own floor process, but the conference committees reconciled House and Senate versions under the pressure of a president who wanted all five signed together. That simultaneity created linkages across bills that had no substantive connection: a member's vote on the gas pricing conference report could be traded against another member's vote on the PURPA conference report, because both had to pass for the program to succeed. The package was therefore not only divided by committee jurisdiction but reunited by legislative logrolling, and the final statutes bear the marks of both operations. PURPA's particular marks include the breadth of its titles, utility regulation for the Ways and Means members who wanted rate reform, the purchase obligation for the members who wanted industrial policy, and the small hydro program for the members who wanted something for their districts. A single subject bill would not have looked like this. A five bill program, negotiated under deadline, looks exactly like this.

Codification tells the epilogue of the legislative story. PURPA was codified generally at 16 U.S.C. 2601 and following, which placed it in the conservation and energy supply title of the Code rather than in the Federal Power Act's title, a shelving decision that reflects the statute's hybrid character. Section 210, the provision that mattered most, landed at 16 U.S.C. 824a-3, inside the Federal Power Act's chapter, because it regulated wholesale purchases, while the ratemaking standards went to the conservation title. The Code thus split the statute the way the committees had split the program, and a researcher who looks up PURPA in the Code finds it scattered, which is the bibliographic trace of everything this section has described. The American Public Power Association's legislative alert of 1978, which listed the five bills as the components of the National Energy Act, remains the clearest contemporary statement that the five were conceived as one program, and the consecutive public law numbers, 95-617 through 95-621, remain the clearest evidence that Congress processed them as one.

## Section 210: The Mandatory Purchase Obligation

Section 210 of PURPA, codified at 16 U.S.C. 824a-3, is the provision that made the market. Its text is worth quoting at length because the entire independent power industry, and much of the litigation and rulemaking that followed, is commentary on a few dense paragraphs. Subsection (a) directs the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission to prescribe rules, not later than one year after November 9, 1978, to encourage cogeneration and small power production, including rules requiring electric utilities to offer to purchase electric energy from qualifying cogeneration and small power production facilities, and to offer to sell electric energy to them. The symmetry matters: the utility must buy the qualifying facility's output and must sell it backup power, which solved the two problems that had strangled non utility generation, no buyer and no reliable backup. Subsection (b) sets the rate standard: the purchase rates must be just and reasonable to the electric consumers of the utility and in the public interest, must not discriminate against qualifying facilities, and, crucially, no rule prescribed under subsection (a) may provide for a rate exceeding the incremental cost to the electric utility of alternative electric energy. Subsection (d) defines that incremental cost as the cost to the utility of the electric energy which, but for the purchase from the qualifying facility, the utility would generate itself or purchase from another source. Subsection (f) closes the enforcement loop: each state regulatory authority, after notice and opportunity for public hearing, must implement the commission's rules for each utility over which it has ratemaking authority. The federal government wrote the rule; the states were conscripted to apply it.

The term everyone uses for the statutory concept, avoided cost, does not appear in the statute. The statute says incremental cost of alternative electric energy. The commission's implementing rules substituted the term avoided costs, and the two are treated as synonymous in the case law. The regulation defines avoided cost, at 18 C.F.R. 292.101(b)(6), as the incremental costs to an electric utility of electric energy or capacity or both which, but for the purchase from the qualifying facility or qualifying facilities, the utility would generate itself or purchase from another source. The Ninth Circuit, Texas courts, and the New Mexico Supreme Court have all recited that definition, and it is the working vocabulary of every PURPA dispute. The economic intuition is simple: if the utility would have spent ten cents a kilowatt hour generating the power itself, and instead buys it from a qualifying facility for ten cents, the utility's consumers are indifferent, because the purchase displaces an expense the utility would have incurred anyway. The rate is capped at, not fixed at, the incremental cost, which means the commission's rules allowed states to set the purchase rate at or below avoided cost, but never above it. That ceiling is the whole theory of the provision's fairness, and it is also the target of every dispute the provision ever generated, because everything turns on how the avoided cost is measured and over what period it is locked in.

The implementing rule was FERC Order No. 69, issued as Small Power Production and Cogeneration Facilities; Regulations Implementing Section 210 of PURPA, published at 45 Federal Register 12,214 on February 25, 1980. The order's definition of avoided cost was derived, in its own words, from the concept of the incremental cost to the electric utility of alternative electric energy set forth in section 210(d), and it expressly included both the fixed and the running costs on an electric utility system that can be avoided by obtaining energy or capacity from qualifying facilities. That inclusion of fixed costs was a consequential choice. A narrow reading of avoided cost might have counted only fuel, the running cost the utility literally saves when it does not run its own plant. The commission's reading counted capacity too, the capital cost of the powerplant the utility does not have to build because the qualifying facility's output is there instead. Capacity payments under long term contracts are what made many qualifying facility projects financeable, and the commission's definition is the reason those payments existed. The order was issued under the delegated authority of section 210(a), and the Part 292 rules it created became the operating system of the entire PURPA regime. FERC's implementation of section 210 through Order No. 69 belongs to the broader story of [federal energy rulemaking at the commission](/2016/12/15/ferc-energy-rulemaking-guide/), which turned brief statutory commands into detailed regulatory machinery across the late 1970s and early 1980s.

The definition of a qualifying facility is the second load bearing element of the provision, and its details are frequently misstated. A qualifying facility is either a qualifying small power production facility or a qualifying cogeneration facility, under sections 201 and 210 of the act and the commission's rules at 18 C.F.R. 292.201 and 292.203. The small power production facility is defined at 16 U.S.C. 796(17)(A) as a facility using biomass, waste, renewable resources including solar, wind, water, and geothermal, or any combination of those as its primary energy source, with a power production capacity that, together with any other facilities at the same site as determined by the commission, is not greater than 80 megawatts. The 80 megawatt cap is real, but it applies only to small power production facilities. The cogeneration facility, defined at 16 U.S.C. 796(18)(A), is a facility that produces electric energy plus steam or another form of useful energy, such as heat, used for industrial, commercial, heating, or cooling purposes, and its qualifying status is determined by the commission by rule, through operating and efficiency requirements, not through any size limit. There is no statutory size cap on cogeneration. A large industrial cogeneration plant qualifies if it meets the efficiency criteria, however large its electrical output. Writers who describe qualifying facilities as limited to 80 megawatts are repeating a common error, and this profile states the correction plainly because the error obscures the provision's reach into heavy industry.

The mechanics of the obligation repays close attention, because the statute did not merely set a price; it restructured a relationship. Before PURPA, an industrial firm that generated its own power faced a utility that could refuse to buy its surplus, could refuse to sell it backup power at reasonable rates, and could impose interconnection charges designed to make self generation uneconomic. The utility was a monopoly supplier, and it behaved like one toward potential competitors. Section 210 attacked each of those behaviors. The utility had to offer to buy the qualifying facility's output. It had to offer to sell the facility backup and supplementary power. It had to interconnect, and the commission received authority to order interconnection and wheeling where needed. The purchase price was capped at avoided cost, which protected consumers, but the obligation to deal was absolute, which protected the generator. The combination is what created the market: a guaranteed buyer at a defined price, plus guaranteed backup, plus interconnection. Any one of those alone would have been insufficient. A guaranteed price without interconnection is a right without a wire. Interconnection without a purchase obligation is a wire without a buyer. The statute supplied all three, and that completeness is why the provision worked as industrial policy even though it was written as utility regulation.

State implementation added a layer of complexity the statute's drafters accepted as the price of the scheme. Section 210(f) did not create a federal bureaucracy to set avoided cost rates utility by utility. It directed each state regulatory authority to implement the commission's rules after notice and hearing, for each utility within its ratemaking jurisdiction. The commission set the framework; the states set the numbers. That division produced the great avoided cost proceedings of the 1980s, in which state commissions held lengthy hearings to determine what each utility's avoided cost actually was. The proceedings were technical, adversarial, and consequential, because the avoided cost number determined the price of every qualifying facility contract in the state. California's implementation became the most famous, and its effects are described in the consequence section, but every state with significant qualifying facility development ran some version of the same proceeding. The federalism implications of conscripting state commissions into a federal scheme were litigated to the Supreme Court, and that litigation is the subject of the federalism section of this article.

The purchase obligation also contained the seeds of its own controversies, and those controversies were structural, not accidental. The statute capped the rate at avoided cost but left the measurement of avoided cost to the commission and the states. Avoided cost is not a physical constant; it is a forecast. It depends on what the utility would have built, what fuel prices would have been, what demand growth would have materialized, and over what period the comparison is drawn. A state commission that set avoided cost generously, using long term forecasts of high oil prices, created a boom in qualifying facility development, because developers could lock in high prices for the life of long term contracts. A state commission that set avoided cost stingily created nothing. The disputes that followed were therefore always about measurement: the level of avoided cost, the length of the contract over which it was fixed, and whether the utility's actual avoided cost had diverged from the forecast embedded in the contract. Those disputes are the real content of the subsidy debate, and the counter reading section addresses them directly. The relevant point is that the statute's fairness mechanism, the avoided cost ceiling, was only as fair as the forecasts that filled it in, and forecasts are where politics enters economics.

The definitional architecture of section 201, codified at 16 U.S.C. 796(17) and (18), repays the close reading it rarely receives, because the definitions are where Congress drew the boundaries of the market it was making. The small power production facility definition has three elements: the primary energy source must be biomass, waste, a renewable resource, or a combination; the capacity, aggregated with other facilities at the same site as determined by the commission, must not exceed 80 megawatts; and the facility must meet the commission's further requirements by rule. The primary energy source test was the environmental conscience of the provision, directing the program toward fuels Congress wanted encouraged and away from conventional fossil generation. The same site aggregation rule was the anti gaming device, preventing developers from splitting a large project into sub 80 megawatt pieces to evade the cap, and the commission's determinations of what counts as the same site became a recurring subject of adjudication. The Congressional Research Service noted limited pre 1995 exceptions to the 80 megawatt limit, codified at 16 U.S.C. 796(17)(E), which shows Congress adjusting the boundary as experience accumulated.

The cogeneration definition works differently, and the difference reveals the provision's industrial logic. A cogeneration facility is defined by what it does, produce electric energy plus steam or another useful form of energy for industrial, commercial, heating, or cooling purposes, and it qualifies by satisfying the commission's operating and efficiency standards rather than any size test. The standards were the gatekeepers: a facility had to demonstrate genuine sequential production of power and useful thermal energy, with minimum efficiency thresholds that distinguished real cogeneration from a conventional powerplant with a token heat output attached. The absence of a size limit was deliberate, because the fuel savings from cogeneration scale with the size of the industrial process, and capping the electrical output would have excluded the large refineries, chemical plants, and paper mills where the waste heat resource was greatest. The two definitions together describe the provision's intended beneficiaries with precision: small renewable developers on one side, large energy intensive industry on the other, united only by the fact that both had surplus power and no buyer.

The rate standard's dual formulation, just and reasonable to consumers and in the public interest, plus nondiscrimination against qualifying facilities, imported the traditional language of utility regulation into a novel setting, and the importation created interpretive work for the commission and the courts. Just and reasonable was the familiar ratemaking standard, but applied here to a purchase rather than a sale: the question was not what the utility could charge but what it must pay. Nondiscrimination meant the utility could not offer qualifying facilities worse terms than it offered itself, which addressed the historical practice of utilities buying their own generation at full cost while offering outsiders pennies. The avoided cost ceiling sat atop both requirements as the objective anchor, the one element of the rate standard that could be calculated rather than merely argued about. That calculability was the standard's virtue and its vulnerability: it made the rate determinate enough to finance projects, and it made the rate's determinants, the forecasts, the target of every dispute. A vaguer standard would have produced less litigation and less investment. Congress chose determinacy, and got both.

### What is avoided cost under PURPA section 210?

Avoided cost is the expense the utility escapes by buying from a qualifying facility instead of generating the power itself or buying it elsewhere. FERC's rules used the term for the statute's "incremental cost of alternative electric energy." The purchase rate could not exceed that cost, which made the transaction cost neutral to the utility's consumers.

The temporal dimension of the obligation deserves emphasis because it explains both the boom and the backlash. Qualifying facility contracts were typically long term, because project finance required revenue certainty over the life of the debt. The avoided cost embedded in those contracts was therefore a long term forecast, fixed at signing. When the forecasts proved wrong, and in the 1980s the relevant forecasts mostly proved too high, because oil prices fell instead of rising, the contracts became expensive relative to the utility's actual alternatives. Utilities found themselves locked into paying 1980s forecast prices in a 1990s market, and they complained, with some justice, that the statute had forced them into bad deals. But the deals were bad only in hindsight. At signing, they reflected the best available forecasts, and the statute's design, long term certainty for generators, required exactly that kind of commitment. The tension between revenue certainty for developers and flexibility for utilities was never resolved within the PURPA framework; it was eventually addressed by narrowing the obligation itself, as the 2005 amendments did. The narrowing section tells that story.

A final mechanical point concerns the small hydro program and the interconnection authority, the statute's lesser known titles, which show the breadth of what Congress packed into the PURPA vehicle. The small hydro provisions encouraged the development of hydroelectric capacity at existing dams, a resource that fit the statute's vision of small, dispersed, non utility generation. The interconnection and wheeling provisions gave the commission authority to order utilities to interconnect with qualifying facilities and, in defined circumstances, to wheel power, which is to transmit it across utility systems. These were not afterthoughts. They were the physical prerequisites of the market the purchase obligation created, because a guaranteed buyer is meaningless if the generator cannot reach the buyer's wires. The statute's drafters understood that a market is not only a price but a set of physical and legal connections, and they legislated the connections alongside the price. That completeness of design is one reason the provision succeeded where vaguer encouragements to non utility generation would have failed.

The state avoided cost proceedings of the 1980s deserve a closer look, because they were where the statute's abstract fairness guarantee became concrete numbers, and where its fate was effectively decided. Section 210(f) gave the states the job, and the states performed it with wildly varying enthusiasm. A commission that believed in the program would adopt aggressive forecasts, high fuel prices, strong demand growth, early capacity needs, and produce an avoided cost generous enough to attract developers. A commission that resented the federal imposition would adopt conservative forecasts and produce an avoided cost that attracted nobody. The statute provided no mechanism to correct for a state's lack of enthusiasm, short of a commission challenge that the state had failed to implement the federal rules, which was a blunt instrument for a subtle problem. The result was a national statute with fifty different prices, and the geography of the independent power industry still reflects those 1980s dockets. California's generosity is the famous case, documented by the Energy Information Administration, but the same dynamic operated everywhere: the federal command created the market's possibility, and the state numbers created its reality.

The proceedings themselves were extraordinary exercises in applied forecasting, and they reveal how much of regulation is prediction dressed as administration. Commissions heard weeks of testimony from utility planners, independent economists, fuel price forecasters, and developer witnesses, all arguing about what oil would cost in 1995, what a new coal plant would cost to build, how fast demand would grow, and what discount rate should translate future savings into present prices. The answers determined the economics of every project in the state for a decade. The adversarial structure meant that utilities argued for low avoided costs and developers argued for high ones, with commission staff somewhere in between, and the outcome depended as much on the commission's philosophy as on the evidence. A commission that saw its role as protecting consumers from overpayment set low numbers. A commission that saw its role as implementing the federal policy of encouraging cogeneration and small power set high numbers. Both could claim fidelity to the statute, because the statute commanded both consumer protection and encouragement, and the avoided cost number was where those two commands collided.

The less discussed half of section 210, the obligation to sell, mattered as much as the obligation to buy for the projects that actually got built. A qualifying facility needs backup power for the hours its own equipment is down for maintenance or trips offline unexpectedly, and it needs supplementary power when its output does not cover its host's needs. Before PURPA, utilities sold that backup power at punitive rates or refused it, which made self generation a gamble: the factory that generated its own power risked being left dark during an outage, or paying extortionate standby charges that erased the savings. Section 210(a) required utilities to offer to sell to qualifying facilities, and the commission's rules set standards for those sales, which converted backup from a weapon into a service. The interconnection obligation completed the picture. A generator that cannot connect to the grid cannot sell, and utilities had historically used interconnection delays, excessive charges, and technical objections to strangle competitors. The statute's interconnection and wheeling provisions gave the commission authority to order connections and to require transmission where needed, removing the utility's ability to win by refusing to plug the competitor in. The purchase obligation gets the attention, but the market required all three legs, buy, sell, and connect, and the statute supplied all three.

## The Accidental Market Maker: PURPA and the Independent Power Industry

The accidental market maker. That phrase captures section 210: a provision written to encourage industrial cogeneration and small hydro that created the first lawful competitor to the regulated utility monopoly, and every subsequent restructuring of American electricity markets builds on the precedent that someone other than the utility may generate power and sell it. The drafters of 1978 did not set out to create an independent power industry. They set out to save oil and gas by encouraging factories to generate their own power from waste heat and by encouraging small developers to tap renewable resources. What they created, almost as a side effect of the purchase obligation, was a class of non utility generators with a federal right to sell power, and that class became an industry. The Congressional Research Service, in its 1998 report on electricity restructuring background, put the mechanism plainly: with the introduction of PURPA, the federal government opened the electricity generating sector to other entrants, and the emergence of qualifying facilities increased the diversity of generation ownership. The report noted that approximately 77 percent of non utility generating capacity came from cogenerators and small power producers, that is, from qualifying facilities, citing 1996 data from the Edison Electric Institute. More than three quarters of the non utility sector was, by the industry's own trade association's count, the direct product of section 210. The figure comes from the industry's own trade association via the Congressional Research Service, which makes it a hostile witness in the best sense: the utilities' association had no incentive to overstate the footprint of the competitors the statute created.

The scale of the consequence becomes clearer when the renewable lineage is traced. The Energy Information Administration, in its Today in Energy series, reported that renewable electric generation capacity owned by independent power producers grew through most of the 1980s and early 1990s largely as a result of California's implementation of PURPA, and that PURPA's influence diminished in the 1990s as falling fuel prices made renewable energy less competitive against other fuels. The causal chain the EIA described runs through the state avoided cost proceedings: California set generous avoided cost rates, developers built wind, small hydro, and biomass projects to capture them, and the projects constituted the first commercial scale deployment of those technologies in the United States. The early American wind industry, the small hydro refurbishments, and the biomass plants of the 1980s were, to a first approximation, PURPA projects. They were financed against PURPA contracts, built to PURPA specifications, and sold into the PURPA market. When the industry later matured and other policies took over, the lineage remained. The commercial market for renewable electricity in the United States descends from the purchase obligation, and the descent is documented, not speculative.

Paul Joskow, the energy economist, gave the mechanism its academic formulation in the Journal of Economic Perspectives in the summer of 1997. PURPA, he wrote, which required utilities to buy power from cogenerators and small power producers using renewable fuels, significantly spurred long term contracts between vertically integrated utilities and certain types of independent generating companies. The emphasis on long term contracts is the point. The purchase obligation did not merely permit non utility generation; it made it bankable. A developer with a long term contract at a defined price can borrow money to build a powerplant. A developer with only the hope of selling into a spot market cannot, or can only at much greater cost. The statute's contribution was therefore financial as much as legal: it converted the abstract right to generate into a concrete revenue stream that lenders would accept. That financial alchemy is what turned a regulatory provision into an industry, and it is why the independent power business dates its birth to 1978 rather than to any later restructuring statute.

The entities that sold power at wholesale and did nothing else acquired a name: independent power producers, or IPPs. The CRS report used the term for the companies that emerged from the qualifying facility world and then grew beyond it, developing projects that were not qualifying facilities at all but that could not have existed without the precedent, the contracts, the engineering firms, the lenders, and the lawyers that the PURPA market had called into being. The industry's infrastructure, the standardized contracts, the project finance techniques, the equipment supply chains for gas turbines and wind machines, was built for PURPA projects and then repurposed for everything that followed. This is the sense in which the market making was accidental but also foundational. Nobody planned an independent power industry, but once the purchase obligation had summoned the industry's components into existence, they persisted and grew, and later restructuring statutes, above all the Energy Policy Act of 1992, built on ground that PURPA had cleared.

The Fuel Use Act, the cautionary companion of this package, played an unexpected supporting role in the story, and the CRS report documented it. The fuel use statute prohibited new utility powerplants from burning oil or gas, but qualifying facilities were not subject to the same prohibition in the same way, and the period's new generating technologies, combined cycle gas turbines and fluidized bed combustion, were well suited to the smaller scale of qualifying facility projects. The CRS analysis noted that the fuel use restrictions helped qualifying facilities become established, because utilities were barred from gas while qualifying facilities could use the abundant gas and the new technologies. The report added that the act's oil and gas prohibitions were repealed in 1987, but by that time qualifying facilities had already gained a portion of the total electricity supply market. The irony is sharp: a statute designed to restrict fuel use ended up sheltering the competitors of the utilities it restricted, and by the time the restriction was lifted, the shelter was no longer needed. The two statutes of the package, one a market maker and the other a market distorter, interacted in ways their drafters never imagined.

The geography of the consequence was uneven, and the unevenness is instructive. California dominated the early story because its regulators set the highest avoided costs and its utilities signed the most contracts, but other states with active implementation, in New England, New York, Texas, and the Pacific Northwest, developed their own qualifying facility fleets. States that set avoided cost low, or that implemented the rules grudgingly, saw little development. The variation proved that the statute was not self executing in any simple sense. The federal command created the possibility; the state numbers determined the reality. That dependence on state implementation is the thread that connects the consequence section to the federalism section: the same state commissions whose avoided cost determinations built the industry were the commissions that had resisted the federal standards in court. The statute worked through the states even as the states resented it, which is the defining paradox of cooperative federalism.

The long view places PURPA at the hinge of American electricity history. Before it, generation was a monopoly function, integrated with transmission and distribution inside the vertically integrated utility. After it, generation was a contestable function, open to entrants with a federal right to a buyer. PURPA occupies a hinge position in [the longer history of United States energy legislation since 1950](/2017/01/15/us-energy-legislation-since-1950/), standing between the monopoly era codified by the Federal Power Act and the competitive era inaugurated by the restructuring statutes of the 1990s and 2000s. Every subsequent restructuring built on the precedent section 210 established: that someone other than the utility may generate power and sell it, and that the law will guarantee the entrant a buyer and a price. The wholesale competition of the 1990s, the regional markets of the 2000s, and the renewable procurement of the 2010s all assume what PURPA first proved, that non utility generation is legally possible, financially viable, and physically integrable into the grid. The statute's drafters wanted to save oil. They ended up proving that the monopoly was optional. That is why the least credited landmark may also be the most consequential, and why the phrase the accidental market maker belongs in any honest account of American energy law.

The backlash arrived on schedule, and it took the form the statute's design had invited. As the 1980s wore on and fuel prices fell instead of rising, the long term contracts signed on high avoided cost forecasts became expensive relative to the utilities' actual alternatives. Utilities that had signed the contracts under state commission orders argued that they were trapped in uneconomic deals, and they had a point that resonated beyond the industry: consumers in the most aggressive PURPA states were paying more for qualifying facility power than the utilities would have paid to generate it themselves. The utilities' trade associations, consumer advocates in high cost states, and eventually legislators began to argue that the purchase obligation had outlived its usefulness, that it was forcing consumers to subsidize generators who no longer needed help. The Energy Information Administration's observation that PURPA's influence decreased in the 1990s as falling fuel prices lessened renewable energy's competitiveness captures the economic side of the turn: the same price movements that made the old contracts look expensive also made new qualifying facility development uneconomic, so the boom faded even where the obligation remained.

The generators' answer to the backlash was that the contracts were not subsidies but bargains, struck on the best forecasts available, and that the utilities were asking to rewrite history. The avoided cost numbers had been set in open proceedings in which the utilities themselves had participated, often endorsing the forecasting methods they later attacked. The long terms had been necessary to finance the projects, which was the point of the statute. And the utilities' claimed overpayments ignored the capacity value the qualifying facilities had provided: the powerplants the utilities had not built because qualifying facility capacity was there instead, a saving that did not show up in simple comparisons of energy prices. This was the measurement dispute in its mature form, and it was genuinely difficult to adjudicate, because it required reconstructing what the utility would have done in a world without the qualifying facility, which is the kind of counterfactual that honest analysts can dispute forever. The political system eventually resolved the dispute not by answering it but by changing the subject, which is what the 2005 narrowing did: instead of relitigating old forecasts, Congress made the obligation conditional on market structure going forward.

The deeper significance of the backlash is that it marked the moment the independent power industry outgrew its creator. By the 1990s, the industry that PURPA had summoned into existence no longer needed the purchase obligation in the form originally written, because wholesale competition was arriving on its own. The Energy Policy Act of 1992, the open access transmission framework, and the organized markets of the late 1990s and 2000s gave independent generators buyers without any federal command to purchase. The industry's maturity was PURPA's vindication and also the source of the argument for narrowing it: a statute that had created competitors could let competition do the work. That is the lifecycle of successful industrial policy. It begins as a bridge across a market failure, it succeeds by creating the market that makes the bridge unnecessary, and it ends when the bridge becomes a toll that the market no longer needs to pay. The 2005 amendments were Congress's recognition that the lifecycle had run its course in the organized market regions, while preserving the bridge where the market had not yet arrived.

The Congressional Research Service's 1998 account added a further dimension that is easy to overlook: the diversity of generation ownership. Before PURPA, the generating sector's ownership was uniform, utilities owned the powerplants, and the uniformity extended to technology, finance, and corporate culture. The emergence of qualifying facilities broke that uniformity, introducing new owners with different incentives, different financing structures, and different appetites for technology risk. A utility builds a powerplant to serve its franchised load under cost of service regulation, which rewards capital investment and punishes risk. An independent power producer builds a powerplant to sell into a contract or a market, which rewards efficiency and punishes waste. The two ownership models behave differently even when they burn the same fuel, and the diversity itself became a source of systemic resilience: when one model stumbled, the other could compensate. The CRS report's observation that the federal government opened the generating sector to other entrants was therefore not only about competition but about variety, and variety in ownership turned out to be as valuable as competition in price.

The naming of the new entrants completed the transformation. Entities that sold power only at wholesale, owning no distribution wires and serving no retail customers, became known as independent power producers, IPPs, a term that distinguished them both from the traditional utilities and from the qualifying facilities that had been their larval form. The name mattered because it gave the industry an identity separate from the statute that created it. A qualifying facility is a creature of PURPA, defined by section 201, certified under Part 292, living inside the regulatory framework. An independent power producer is a creature of the market, defined by what it does rather than by the statute that first permitted it. The linguistic graduation from QF to IPP tracks the industry's graduation from regulatory dependent to market participant, and it is no accident that the term came into general use as the industry outgrew the purchase obligation. PURPA made the qualifying facility; the qualifying facility's success made the independent power producer; and the independent power producer made the purchase obligation, in the organized markets at least, unnecessary. The accidental market maker had made a market that no longer needed its maker, which is the happiest ending industrial policy ever gets.

## The Federalism Fight: FERC v. Mississippi

The states resisted PURPA from the beginning, and their resistance produced one of the important cooperative federalism decisions of the twentieth century. The objection centered on Titles I and III, the provisions that required state utility commissions to consider a set of federal ratemaking standards and to follow specified procedures in doing so. The standards addressed rate design and regulatory procedure: how utilities should structure their rates and what processes commissions should follow in setting them. The federal government was not ordering the states to adopt any particular rate. It was ordering them to consider the federal list, to hold hearings, to make findings, and to report their decisions. To the states, that was commandeering: Congress was conscripting state agencies into a federal regulatory program, forcing state officials to spend state resources considering federal proposals. Mississippi and other states sued, raising Commerce Clause and Tenth Amendment challenges, and the case reached the Supreme Court as Federal Energy Regulatory Commission v. Mississippi.

The Court decided the case on June 1, 1982, as 456 U.S. 742, having heard argument on January 19, 1982, and it denied rehearing on September 9, 1982, all dates per the U.S. Reports entry. The holding sustained the scheme in full. The Court held that section 210 was a straightforward exercise of federal preemption under the Commerce Clause, which disposed of the purchase obligation challenge cleanly: Congress can regulate the wholesale sale of electricity in interstate commerce, and the states could not nullify that regulation. The harder question was the requirement that state commissions consider the federal ratemaking standards. The Court held that this requirement did not compel the exercise of state sovereign powers but simply established conditions on continued state regulation in an area subject to complete federal preemption. The logic was that Congress could have preempted state utility regulation entirely and set rates itself; having chosen instead to let the states continue regulating, it could attach conditions to that permission. The opinion described the scheme as a program of cooperative federalism that allows the states, within limits established by federal minimum standards, to enact and administer their own regulatory programs, structured to meet their own particular needs, quoting the Court's earlier decision in Hodel for the formulation. The states were not being ordered to legislate; they were being offered a choice, regulate on federal terms or yield the field to federal regulation, and the Constitution permits Congress to frame the choice that way.

The dissents were substantial and they mapped the anxieties the majority had overridden. Justice O'Connor dissented in part, joined by Justice Rehnquist and Chief Justice Burger, arguing that the statute intruded on the states' sovereign functions in a way the Tenth Amendment forbids. Justice Powell dissented on the mandatory procedures portion, objecting to the federal direction of state administrative process. The dissenters' core intuition was that there is a difference between regulating private conduct and regulating the regulators, and that PURPA crossed it by telling state commissions how to do their jobs. The majority's answer was that the line the dissenters drew had already been crossed by the nature of the industry: electricity regulation was so pervasively federal, through the Federal Power Act and the commission's wholesale jurisdiction, that state regulation survived only by federal sufferance, and sufferance can carry conditions. The decision remains important to cooperative federalism doctrine because it stated the conditional preemption theory so explicitly. Later cases about federal directions to state legislatures and state executives would refine and limit the theory, but the PURPA decision stands as the high water mark of Congress's power to enlist state agencies in federal regulatory schemes by threatening to displace them.

### How did the Supreme Court sustain PURPA against the states' challenge?

In FERC v. Mississippi, 456 U.S. 742, decided June 1, 1982, the Court held that directing state commissions to consider federal ratemaking standards did not exceed federal power. The scheme was cooperative federalism: Congress could set conditions on continued state regulation in an area open to complete federal preemption.

The practical effect of the decision was to lock in the implementation structure that made the purchase obligation work. Section 210(f)'s direction that state regulatory authorities implement the commission's rules after notice and hearing was the provision that put the avoided cost numbers in state hands, and the Court's decision meant the states could not escape the assignment by constitutional objection. They had to hold the hearings, set the rates, and administer the program, even as they resented the federal command. That resentment had consequences for how generously or grudgingly different states implemented the rules, which is part of why the qualifying facility boom was so geographically uneven. But the legal question was settled: the federal government could use the states as its instruments, and the states' remedy was political, not judicial. The decision also protected the purchase obligation itself, which the states had attacked as an interference with their traditional ratemaking authority. By characterizing section 210 as straightforward Commerce Clause preemption, the Court removed the constitutional cloud from the provision that mattered most, and the independent power industry grew under the shelter of that holding.

The federalism fight also illuminates the statute's deeper theory of governance. PURPA did not create a federal utility commission to set retail rates, which would have been the direct route and the politically impossible one. It created a set of federal standards and a federal purchase rule, and it draped them over the existing state regulatory apparatus, using state commissions as the implementation layer. That is cooperative federalism as a legislative strategy: achieve national policy through state institutions, preserving the appearance and much of the reality of state control while directing its exercise. The strategy's advantage is political feasibility; its cost is uneven implementation and permanent friction. The 1982 decision ratified the strategy for energy regulation, and its reasoning echoed in later environmental and telecommunications statutes that used the same architecture. A reader who understands why Mississippi lost understands something essential about how American regulation actually works: not as a clean division between federal and state spheres, but as a negotiated entanglement in which Congress sets the terms and the states administer them, under the shadow of preemption.

The dissenters' theory deserves a fuller hearing than the majority's victory usually allows, because it identified the real cost of the conditional preemption strategy. Justice O'Connor's partial dissent, joined by Justice Rehnquist and Chief Justice Burger, argued that there is a constitutionally relevant difference between regulating private parties and conscripting state governments, and that PURPA crossed it by putting state commissions to work on federal assignments. Justice Powell's dissent on the procedures portion made the narrower but sharper point: even if Congress can set substantive conditions on state regulation, dictating the procedures by which state agencies consider federal proposals intrudes on the state's control of its own governmental machinery. The intuition behind both dissents is that cooperative federalism has a price, and the price is the gradual conversion of state agencies into field offices of the federal government, retaining the name of state regulation while losing its substance. The majority's answer, that the states were free to abandon regulation and let the federal government take over, was formally correct and practically empty, because no state was going to surrender its utility commissions. The choice the Court offered was real in theory and illusory in practice, and the dissenters said so.

The majority's premise, that electricity regulation was subject to complete federal preemption, is what did the doctrinal work, and it is worth examining because everything in the opinion hangs on it. The Federal Power Act had given the commission jurisdiction over wholesale sales and transmission in interstate commerce, which covered the economically significant half of the industry, while the states retained retail ratemaking. That division meant the federal government already occupied the field's center, and the states regulated at the sufferance of a federal framework that could have displaced them. The Court treated that sufferance as the source of Congress's power to attach conditions: because Congress could have taken the whole field, it could take part of it on terms. The logic is powerful and also circular, because it makes the scope of federal power depend on a hypothetical, what Congress could have done, rather than on what Congress did. Later decisions would wrestle with that circularity, limiting the federal government's power to command state legislatures and state executives directly, but the PURPA holding survived those limits because it was framed as a condition on continued state regulation rather than a direct command. The distinction between conditional preemption and commandeering became one of the fault lines of modern federalism doctrine, and FERC v. Mississippi is the case that drew it.

The decision's practical legacy for energy regulation was to bless the architecture that subsequent federal energy statutes would reuse. When Congress wanted national policy implemented through state institutions, the PURPA model was available: set federal standards, direct state commissions to consider them, and rely on the threat of preemption to secure compliance. The model worked because it gave both levels of government something they wanted. Congress got national policy without building a federal bureaucracy. State commissions kept their jurisdiction and their jobs. The losers were clarity and uniformity, because fifty implementations of a federal standard are fifty different standards, and the winners were flexibility and political feasibility, because the scheme could pass Congress in a way direct federal regulation could not. That tradeoff, clarity for feasibility, is the permanent bargain of cooperative federalism, and the 1982 decision is its charter in energy law.

The Supreme Court's decision did not end the federalism story; it moved the fight from the Constitution to the details of implementation, where federal courts policed the boundary between state discretion and federal command for the next three decades. Section 210(f) gave state commissions the job of implementing the commission's rules, but implementation is a spectrum, and states that disagreed with the federal policy could implement grudgingly, setting avoided costs low, certifying facilities slowly, or interpreting the federal rules narrowly. Qualifying facilities and their trade associations responded by suing in federal court, arguing that particular state implementations violated the federal rules, and the federal courts became the referees of cooperative federalism in practice. The Ninth Circuit heard such challenges, including cases quoting section 210(a)'s directive that the commission prescribe purchase rules and cases reciting the regulatory definition of avoided cost, which shows how the litigation drilled into the provision's operative language. The pattern repeated across the circuits: the state commission would set a rate or adopt a practice, the qualifying facility would claim inconsistency with Part 292, and the federal court would decide whether the state's implementation remained within the federal framework.

This implementation litigation is the unglamorous sequel to the constitutional drama, and it matters more for understanding how the statute actually worked. FERC v. Mississippi settled that the states had to play their assigned role; the implementation cases settled what playing the role required. The courts generally gave states latitude on the technical questions, the level of avoided cost, the structure of the proceedings, the details of interconnection, while insisting that the federal floor be honored: the purchase obligation itself, the avoided cost ceiling, the nondiscrimination requirement, and the duty to implement after notice and hearing. That division, federal floor plus state discretion above it, is cooperative federalism as a working doctrine rather than a slogan. It also explains why the PURPA market varied so much by state: the federal floor guaranteed the market's existence, but the state discretion determined its size, and the courts policed only the floor. A qualifying facility developer choosing where to build was therefore choosing a state commission as much as a resource, and the developers who thrived were often those who read commissions as carefully as they read wind maps.

The constitutional and implementation phases of the federalism fight together illustrate a general truth about regulatory statutes that work through the states. The constitutional phase asks whether the federal government can conscript the states at all, and the answer, after 1982, was yes, on the conditional preemption theory. The implementation phase asks how much slack the conscription leaves, and the answer was a great deal, bounded by a federal floor that courts enforced case by case. The combination gave the statute both its national reach and its local variation, which is why PURPA could be simultaneously a federal command and fifty different programs. Critics of the arrangement point to the variation as proof of incoherence; defenders point to it as proof of federalism's virtue, allowing each state to fit the federal policy to its own circumstances. Both have a point, and the statute's history supports both readings at different moments. What cannot be disputed is that the arrangement worked well enough to create an industry, which is more than can be said for many cleaner constitutional designs.

## The 2005 Narrowing: Purchase Relief in Competitive Markets

By the early 2000s, the purchase obligation that had created the independent power industry had become, in the view of many utilities, an anachronism. The electricity industry had restructured around wholesale competition in much of the country. Regional transmission organizations ran day ahead and real time markets in which any generator could sell power. The original justification for the mandatory purchase, that the qualifying facility had no buyer without a federal command, no longer held where competitive wholesale markets gave every generator nondiscriminatory access to buyers. Utilities argued that forcing them to buy from qualifying facilities at administratively determined avoided cost rates, in markets where the qualifying facility could sell competitively, was no longer consumer protection but consumer burden. Independent generators and renewable developers argued that the obligation remained essential outside the organized markets, and that the organized markets themselves were incomplete, discriminatory in practice, or unavailable to small facilities. Congress, in the Energy Policy Act of 2005, split the difference with a mechanism that made the obligation conditional on market structure.

The mechanism was section 1253(a) of the Energy Policy Act of 2005, Public Law 109-58, located in Subtitle E, the PURPA amendments subtitle, of Title XII of that act, at 119 Statutes at Large 594, 967. Section 1253 added a new subsection (m) to section 210 of PURPA, codified at 16 U.S.C. 824a-3(m). The provision operates as follows: after enactment, no electric utility is required to enter into a new contract or obligation to purchase electric energy from a qualifying facility if the commission finds that the qualifying facility has nondiscriminatory access to one of three defined categories of wholesale market. The three categories are, first, independently administered, auction based day ahead and real time wholesale markets for energy and transmission; second, transmission and interconnection services provided by a commission approved regional transmission entity under an open access transmission tariff that is nondiscriminatory; and third, wholesale markets for the sale of capacity and electric energy of comparable competitive quality that afford the qualifying facility a meaningful opportunity to sell to buyers other than the host utility. The finding is facility specific and market specific: the commission must find that the particular qualifying facility has the requisite access, and the relief applies only to new contracts and obligations, not to existing ones. The obligation can be reinstated if the commission later finds that the conditions for relief are no longer met. The statute thus converted the purchase obligation from a universal command into a rebuttable presumption, operative by default but defeasible where markets genuinely exist.

The commission implemented the finding process in Order No. 688 in 2006, establishing the procedures by which utilities could seek relief and qualifying facilities could contest it. The implementation confirmed the statute's market by market logic: utilities in regions with organized wholesale markets sought and in many cases obtained relief, while utilities in regions without organized markets remained subject to the full obligation. The result was a PURPA regime with two geographies. In the organized market regions, the mandatory purchase obligation receded to the background, and qualifying facilities sold into the markets like other generators. In the non market regions, the obligation continued to operate much as it had since 1980, with state commissions setting avoided cost rates and utilities signing long term contracts. The 2005 amendments belong to the [Energy Policy Act of 2005](/2016/09/01/energy-policy-act-2005-guide/), the large energy statute that also rewrote much of the Federal Power Act's market based rate framework, and the PURPA amendments were a small but structurally significant part of that larger restructuring.

### When can a utility escape the mandatory purchase obligation?

After the Energy Policy Act of 2005, FERC may relieve a utility of entering new purchase contracts when the qualifying facility has nondiscriminatory access to competitive wholesale markets. The finding covers day-ahead and real-time markets, open access transmission, or comparable competitive outlets, and the obligation returns if the access disappears.

The narrowing did not repeal the purchase obligation, and the distinction matters. A repeal would have ended the federal guarantee everywhere, leaving qualifying facilities in non market regions with no buyer. The conditional relief preserved the guarantee where it was still needed and lifted it where markets had matured enough to substitute. That design reflects a judgment about the obligation's original purpose: it was never meant as a permanent entitlement for generators, but as a bridge across a market failure, the absence of buyers for non utility power. Where the market failure persisted, the bridge stayed. Where competition had arrived, the bridge could be dismantled. Whether the commission's findings always correctly identified genuine competition was contested, and qualifying facilities challenged findings they considered premature, but the structure of the compromise, relief conditioned on verified market access with reinstatement available, was the kind of calibrated federalism the original statute had pioneered. The states that retained the full obligation continued to administer it through their avoided cost proceedings, and the purchase obligation therefore survived as a live regulatory instrument in much of the country even as it faded in the organized market regions.

The narrowing also changed the political economy of PURPA in ways that matter for understanding its survival. Before 2005, the purchase obligation was a single national rule, defended by a national coalition of independent generators and attacked by a national coalition of utilities. After 2005, the rule's bite varied by region, which fragmented both coalitions. Utilities in organized markets got their relief and lost interest in further PURPA fights. Generators in non market regions kept the obligation and defended it fiercely. The result was a statute that persisted not because anyone loved it in the abstract but because it retained concrete defenders in the places where it still operated. That is a common pattern in the afterlife of regulatory statutes: they survive by becoming local, by embedding themselves in particular state proceedings and particular contracts, until repeal would require unraveling thousands of reliance interests. Section 210(m) did not kill PURPA; it regionalized it, and regionalized statutes are harder to kill than national ones because their opponents are never all motivated at the same time.

Students working through the 2005 amendments often benefit from structured study of the statutory mechanism, and the three market categories of section 210(m) can be worked through in a [legislation study notebook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html) alongside the original section 210 text. The exercise that clarifies the narrowing is to take each of the three market findings and ask what evidence the commission would need: auction rules and market monitor reports for the day ahead and real time markets, the open access tariff and interconnection queue data for the transmission finding, and actual transaction records for the comparable competitive market finding. The statute's precision about market types is unusual in energy legislation, which more often speaks in generalities about competition, and the precision repays close reading. It tells the reader exactly what Congress meant by a market sufficient to displace the purchase obligation, and it gives qualifying facilities a defined evidentiary target when they contest a utility's claim to relief.

The commission's implementation in Order No. 688, issued in 2006, translated the statute's three market tests into an evidentiary process, and the translation reveals how seriously Congress took the market access question. A utility seeking relief had to demonstrate, for the particular qualifying facility at issue, that one of the three market types genuinely offered nondiscriminatory access, not merely that a market existed somewhere in the region. The showing would naturally run to the market's auction rules and independent administration for the day ahead and real time test, to the tariff's terms and the practical availability of interconnection for the open access test, and to actual transaction opportunities beyond the host utility for the comparable market test. Qualifying facilities could contest each showing, and the commission weighed the evidence facility by facility. The process was administrative adjudication of a market structure question, which is an unusual assignment for a regulatory commission, and it produced a body of findings that mapped, region by region, where American wholesale electricity was genuinely competitive and where it was not.

The reinstatement provision is the narrowing's most underappreciated feature, because it makes the relief reversible and therefore honest. A statute that lifted the obligation permanently on a finding of market access would have been betting that markets, once competitive, stay competitive, which is not a bet the history of electricity markets supports. By providing that the obligation returns if the commission finds the market conditions no longer met, section 210(m) acknowledged that competition is a condition to be verified, not an achievement to be assumed. The provision has the same structure as the original statute's deeper logic: federal guarantees that phase in and out with market reality, rather than permanent commands or permanent deregulation. It is a more sophisticated instrument than either the 1978 purchase obligation or a simple repeal would have been, because it tracks the thing it regulates. Whether the commission has applied the reinstatement standard aggressively or cautiously is a question for the post 2005 record, but the standard's existence changes the politics of every relief proceeding, because utilities know the relief can be lost and generators know the obligation can return.

The narrowing also illustrates a general pattern in the afterlife of the 1978 package: statutes that created constituencies survived, and statutes that did not create them faded. The purchase obligation created the independent power industry, and that industry defended the obligation's core even as it accepted the narrowing's logic in organized markets. The organized market utilities got their relief and moved on. The non market utilities and their regulators kept the obligation and kept administering it. The result was not repeal but equilibrium, a statute whose bite varied with market structure and whose defenders were concentrated where the bite remained. Compare the Fuel Use Act, which created no constituency, only compliance costs, and which collapsed within a decade. Or the gas price controls, which created a constituency of consumers who liked low prices but could not survive the intellectual collapse of the shortage premise. PURPA's durability came from the industry it had accidentally made, and the 2005 compromise ratified that durability by giving the industry's mature members an exit where markets worked while preserving the guarantee where they did not. Accidental market making turned out to be the most durable form of legislation in the package, because markets, once made, defend themselves.

The third market test, comparable competitive wholesale markets offering a meaningful opportunity to sell to buyers other than the host utility, is the most interesting of the three, because it is the least mechanical and the most judgmental. The first test asks whether an organized auction market exists, which is largely a yes or no question about institutions. The second test asks whether open access transmission is available, which is largely a question about tariffs. The third test asks whether the qualifying facility, in the real world, can actually sell its power to someone besides the utility that has been forced to buy it, which is a question about market structure, buyer concentration, transmission constraints, and commercial practice. A region might have no organized market and no regional transmission entity, yet still offer a qualifying facility genuine competitive outlets, through bilateral wholesale contracts, power marketers, or neighboring utilities willing to buy. Or a region might have the formal institutions while the facility remains, as a practical matter, captive to its host. The statute's phrase, meaningful opportunity, directs the commission to look past form to substance, and that inquiry is where the relief proceedings become genuinely analytical rather than merely procedural.

The new versus existing distinction is the narrowing's other load bearing element, and it reflects a legislative judgment about reliance interests that is worth making explicit. Section 210(m) relieves utilities only of the obligation to enter new contracts and new obligations; it does not abrogate contracts already signed. The distinction protects the financing structure on which the existing qualifying facility fleet was built: lenders who funded projects against long term purchase contracts cannot have the revenue stream legislated away midstream without destroying the credit of every infrastructure project that relies on regulatory commitments. At the same time, the distinction means the narrowing phases in gradually, as old contracts expire and new capacity faces the market test, rather than disrupting the existing fleet. That gradualism was politically necessary, because the existing contract holders were the industry's most organized defenders, and it was economically sensible, because abrupt abrogation would have taught every future developer that federal purchase commitments are unreliable, which would have raised the cost of capital for the next generation of projects. The statute thus honored the past while changing the future, which is the most Congress can usually do with reliance interests it did not create but cannot ignore.

Read together, the three market tests, the facility specific findings, the new obligations limit, and the reinstatement provision compose a regulatory instrument of unusual sophistication, and the sophistication is worth appreciating as legislative craft. The 1978 purchase obligation was a blunt instrument: buy from everyone, everywhere, at forecasted cost. The 2005 narrowing replaced the bluntness with conditionality, keeping the federal guarantee as a default while building in exits where markets genuinely functioned. The design acknowledges what the intervening decades had taught: that the market failure the obligation addressed was real but not universal, persistent in some regions and cured in others, and that a statute written for 1978's monopoly could not govern 2005's hybrid industry without differentiation. Whether the commission's application of the tests has always matched the statute's ambition is a question the record can answer, but the ambition itself, a purchase obligation that tracks market reality rather than ignoring it, represents the maturation of the accidental market maker from an improvised 1978 experiment into a calibrated regulatory regime. The statute had been adapted before along the same line: the Energy Policy Act of 1992, the 2005 narrowing, and the 2009 recovery act each adjusted the mechanism to the industry the statute had created, without repealing the purchase obligation, the qualifying facility definitions, or the federal state implementation structure.

## The Cautionary Companion: The Fuel Use Act and the Gas Ban

The same signing ceremony that produced PURPA produced its opposite: a statute premised on a forecast that proved entirely wrong. The Powerplant and Industrial Fuel Use Act of 1978, H.R. 5146, Public Law 95-620, 92 Stat. 3289, prohibited new electric powerplants and major fuel burning installations from using oil or natural gas as their primary fuel. New plants had to burn coal or alternate fuels; existing plants faced restrictions on oil and gas use; and an exemptions regime allowed case by case relief. The policy logic was conservationist: oil imports were a strategic vulnerability, and natural gas was thought to be running out, so the law reserved gas for what were then called higher uses, principally residential and commercial heating, and barred it from the boiler rooms of industry and the power sector. The prohibition was the most interventionist element of the five bill package, a direct federal command about what fuel a private plant could burn, and it rested on a geological and economic forecast that the decade would falsify.

The forecast premise must be stated with attribution, because the primary government sources do not lay it out in a single clean sentence, while the trade press and analyst accounts do. The Oil and Gas Journal, in a retrospective on energy policy errors, recounted that in 1978 Congress passed the Fuel Use Act to restrict the burning of oil under industrial and utility boilers, to lower imports, and of natural gas, to preserve a commodity thought to be scarce for higher uses, and it added that the assertion of gas scarcity grew out of delivery curtailments created by market controls but assumed to relate to geology, an assertion and assumption that, in the Journal's words, could not have been more wrong. A conference presentation on the evolution of the United States natural gas market by Robert O'Neill, a former commission staff member, gave the same account in plainer terms: forecasters, misreading increased prices as the first sign of the depletion of the natural gas resource, predicted ever rising gas prices, and lawmakers responded with the Fuel Use Act, barring new powerplants from using oil or gas on the idea that gas needed to be preserved for high value use, until the 1980s surprised the forecasters with a multi year period of falling prices. The statutory findings at 42 U.S.C. 8301 state the conservation rationale in general terms, but the specific scarcity forecast, the belief that American natural gas was running out, comes to the historical record through these analyst and trade press accounts, and this article presents it as they present it: the premise on which the prohibition was built, and the premise that failed.

The failure unfolded through the 1980s as a textbook case of a forecast error compounded by policy rigidity. Natural gas prices fell rather than rising, as new supply, deregulation at the wellhead, and the demand destruction of the early 1980s recessions created surplus where shortage had been feared. The prohibition, meanwhile, remained on the books, forcing utilities and industrial firms to plan around a ban whose rationale had evaporated. Utilities that wanted to build efficient gas fired plants, the combined cycle technology that was maturing through the decade, had to seek exemptions or burn coal instead. The exemptions regime became a pressure valve, but a costly and uncertain one, and the statute generated a steady stream of exemption proceedings that consumed administrative resources to waive a rule nobody any longer believed in. The contrast with PURPA is instructive. PURPA created a market and then let the market's participants defend it. The Fuel Use Act created a prohibition and then watched the prohibition's rationale dissolve, leaving only the compliance costs. A statute that creates winners can survive its authors' errors; a statute that creates only losers cannot.

Congress repealed the prohibitions in 1987, within a decade of enactment, and the repeal's precise scope matters because it is often misstated. The repeal vehicle was H.R. 1941 of the 100th Congress, a bill to repeal and amend certain sections of the Powerplant and Industrial Fuel Use Act. It passed the House by voice vote on May 4, 1987, passed the Senate by voice vote on May 7, 1987, and was signed on May 21, 1987, as Public Law 100-42, all dates and actions per Congress.gov. What Public Law 100-42 repealed were the prohibitions: sections 202, 302, 401, 402, and 405 of the Fuel Use Act, codified at 42 U.S.C. 8312, 8342, 8371, 8372, and 8375, plus Title V of the act at 42 U.S.C. 8391 and section 801 at 42 U.S.C. 8481. Those were the provisions that barred oil and gas use in new and existing powerplants and major fuel burning installations. The act itself was not repealed in its entirety. The narrowed remainder replaced the ban with a requirement that new baseload powerplants self certify to the Secretary of Energy their capability to burn coal or alternate fuels, a shadow of the original prohibition that survived in the narrowed act. The correct statement is that Congress repealed the oil and gas prohibitions in 1987, not the act as a whole. The distinction is the difference between accurate legislative history and a convenient shorthand, and this profile insists on the accurate version.

### Why did the Fuel Use Act's gas prohibition fail?

The ban rested on forecasts that natural gas was running out, a premise that energy analysts later described as drawn from delivery curtailments caused by price controls rather than geology. When gas prices fell through the 1980s, Congress repealed the oil and gas prohibitions in 1987 through Public Law 100-42.

The repeal's timing, less than nine years after enactment, makes the Fuel Use Act the package's cautionary tale in the strictest sense: a warning about what happens when legislation encodes a forecast. Forecasts are necessary; no energy policy can be made without assumptions about future supply, demand, and prices. But a statute that turns a forecast into a prohibition, with criminal or civil penalties for burning the wrong fuel, leaves no room for the forecast to be wrong. PURPA's purchase obligation, by contrast, encoded a mechanism, not a forecast. The avoided cost standard adjusted, however imperfectly, to actual utility costs, because it was defined as the cost the utility would otherwise have incurred. When the world changed, the mechanism had at least a chance of tracking it. The Fuel Use Act's ban could not track anything; it simply forbade, until Congress intervened. The lesson for legislative design is that mechanisms outlive forecasts, and statutes built on mechanisms are more durable than statutes built on predictions. The 1978 package, containing one of each, is the controlled experiment that proves it.

The Natural Gas Policy Act's parallel fate reinforces the lesson from a different direction. The gas pricing statute did not encode a scarcity forecast as a prohibition; it encoded a transition, phased deregulation of wellhead prices through ceiling categories, toward market pricing. That design proved more durable than the Fuel Use Act's ban but less durable than PURPA's mechanism. The price control core, Title I, survived the 1980s but was then dismantled by the Natural Gas Wellhead Decontrol Act of 1989, Public Law 101-60, signed July 26, 1989, which permanently repealed wellhead price controls effective January 1, 1993. The transition the 1978 act had managed was completed by the 1989 act's abolition, and the commission's remaining gas jurisdiction moved to transportation and market oversight rather than price setting. Of the five statutes, then, the scorecard as of the reference date reads: PURPA surviving and still operative, the Energy Tax Act surviving in substance with its gas guzzler tax intact per the 2008 CRS report, the conservation act surviving through repeated amendment, the Fuel Use Act narrowed to a certification remnant, and the gas pricing act's control regime repealed. The package that was born together diverged completely, and the divergence tracks a single variable: whether the statute created a self sustaining mechanism or merely imposed a temporary command.

The cautionary companion also casts retrospective light on PURPA's own design choices, and the comparison favors the purchase obligation. Both statutes were responses to the same perceived crisis, the energy shocks of the 1970s and the fear that American energy supply could not be taken for granted. One response was to command: burn this, not that. The other was to enable: buy from these generators at this price. The command failed because the crisis it addressed turned out to be different from the crisis that arrived; the gas shortage was a regulatory artifact of price controls, not a geological fact, and when the controls were dismantled the shortage vanished. The enablement succeeded because it did not depend on any particular forecast about fuel supply; it depended only on the institutional fact of monopoly, which was real and persistent. A student of the package who asks why one statute lived and the other died will find the answer in that difference. Commands need true premises. Mechanisms need only true institutions.

The exemptions regime deserves attention because it shows how a prohibition unravels in practice before it is repealed in law. The Fuel Use Act did not ban oil and gas use absolutely; it banned them subject to exemptions that the administering agency could grant case by case, for reasons including the unavailability of alternate fuels, the infeasibility of conversion, and other hardships the statute enumerated. In the early years, when the scarcity premise still commanded belief, the exemptions were narrow and the ban bit. As the 1980s wore on and the premise collapsed, the pressure on the exemptions grew: every utility that wanted to build a gas fired plant, and every industrial firm that wanted to burn gas in a new boiler, had to come through the exemption door, because the front door was barred by a statute whose rationale had evaporated. The administrators faced an impossible position, enforcing a prohibition they knew to be pointless, granting exemptions that swallowed the rule, and consuming years of agency and industry effort to waive requirements that served no purpose. The repeal in 1987 was less a policy reversal than an acknowledgment of what the exemption dockets had already demonstrated: the prohibition had become a paperwork tax on doing the sensible thing.

The politics of the repeal are as instructive as its substance. H.R. 1941 passed both chambers by voice vote, which in congressional practice signals that no organized opposition remained. A prohibition that had been controversial enough to require an elaborate exemptions regime in 1978 was, by 1987, uncontroversial to repeal, because its constituency had vanished along with its premise. Nobody defended the gas ban in 1987, not the utilities that had once been its targets, not the environmental groups that might have favored fuel restrictions in principle, not the forecasters whose predictions had underwritten it. Contrast PURPA's purchase obligation in the same decade: attacked constantly, defended fiercely, narrowed only after a twenty seven year fight, and surviving in much of the country even then. The difference was not that one statute was wise and the other foolish, though the Fuel Use Act's premise was indeed wrong. The difference was that one statute had created people whose livelihoods depended on it, and the other had created only people whose plans it obstructed. Legislation that makes winners endures. Legislation that makes only losers is repealed by voice vote, with nobody rising to object.

The Fuel Use Act's afterlife in the narrowed certification requirement is a final irony worth savoring. Public Law 100-42 left behind a rule that new baseload plants must self certify their capability to burn coal or alternate fuels, a ghost of the original command, a form to file rather than a fuel to burn. The requirement imposed negligible costs and served negligible purposes, which is precisely why it survived: nobody cared enough to repeal it. The history of the act is thus a complete lifecycle in miniature, from ambitious prohibition to porous exemptions to voice vote repeal to meaningless remnant, all within a decade. Students of regulation should study that lifecycle alongside PURPA's, because the two statutes shared a signing ceremony, a decade, and a Congress, and they diverged on every dimension that matters: premise, mechanism, constituency, and fate. The package that contained them both is the closest thing American energy law offers to a controlled experiment, and the experiment's result is unambiguous. Mechanisms that create markets outlive prohibitions that encode forecasts, and the margin is not close.

## The Subsidy Question: Cost Neutrality and Its Disputes

No account of PURPA is complete without addressing the charge that the purchase obligation was a subsidy, because the charge has followed the statute since the 1980s and it misunderstands the mechanism in an instructive way. The charge runs like this: by forcing utilities to buy power from qualifying facilities, often at high long term prices, the statute transferred wealth from utility consumers to independent generators, which is what a subsidy does. The answer begins with the statute's own definition. Avoided cost is, by definition, the price at which the purchase is cost neutral to the utility: the rate may not exceed the incremental cost to the utility of the energy it would otherwise have generated or purchased. If the measurement is correct, the utility's consumers pay no more than they would have paid without the purchase, and the generator receives no more than the value of the power to the utility. A subsidy, properly understood, is a payment above value. The avoided cost ceiling was designed to prevent exactly that. The statute's theory was that the purchase obligation was not a transfer but an exchange at fair value, with the federal command supplying only the buyer, not the price premium.

The genuine disputes were always about measurement, and they are more precise and more interesting than the subsidy framing. Avoided cost is a forecast, and forecasts can be wrong in both directions. The first great measurement dispute concerned the level: state commissions in the early 1980s set avoided costs based on forecasts of rising oil prices, and when oil prices fell, the contracted rates exceeded the utilities' actual avoided costs, sometimes substantially. Utilities argued, with the benefit of hindsight, that they had been forced to overpay. Generators answered that the contracts reflected the best forecasts available at signing, that the statute required long term commitments to make projects financeable, and that a forecast that proves wrong in hindsight was not a subsidy at signing. Both arguments have force, which is why the dispute persisted. The second measurement dispute concerned the contract term: over how many years should the avoided cost be fixed? Generators wanted long terms for financing; utilities wanted short terms to capture falling costs. The commission's rules and the state implementations struck various balances, and each balance created winners and losers as actual costs diverged from forecasts. The third dispute concerned capacity: whether and how much the avoided cost should include the capital cost of the powerplant the utility did not build. The commission's inclusion of fixed costs in Order No. 69 was the single most valuable interpretive choice for generators, and utilities contested it for years.

The evenhanded presentation requires stating each side's strongest case without caricature. The utility and ratepayer case runs as follows. The purchase obligation forced utilities into long term contracts at administratively set prices, and when those prices exceeded the market, consumers paid the difference for decades. The obligation distorted utility planning, because the utility had to take qualifying facility power whether it needed it or not, which complicated the dispatch of its own plants and the timing of its own investments. The administrative proceedings to set avoided cost were expensive, litigious, and vulnerable to regulatory capture by the developer interests they were meant to discipline. And the 80 megawatt threshold for small power, combined with the absence of any size limit for cogeneration, allowed large industrial firms to qualify facilities that were small only by legal definition, capturing the purchase guarantee for projects that needed no federal help. That is the strongest version of the critique, and it is not frivolous.

The independent generator case runs as follows. Before the obligation, utilities used their monopoly power to refuse interconnection, to refuse backup service, and to offer insultingly low prices for surplus power, which made non utility generation impossible regardless of its efficiency. The avoided cost standard guaranteed only fair value, not a premium, and the long term contracts reflected genuine financing needs, not rent seeking. The utilities' complaints about overpayment were complaints about forecasts, and the utilities themselves had endorsed the forecasting methods in the avoided cost proceedings; a party that agrees to a forecasting methodology cannot fairly call the result a subsidy when the forecast errs. The obligation's planning complications were the necessary cost of introducing competition into a monopoly industry, and the industry's subsequent development proved the entrants were efficient: the independent power sector that PURPA created went on to compete successfully in wholesale markets without any purchase guarantee. That is the strongest version of the defense, and it is not frivolous either.

What the subsidy framing obscures, and what the measurement framing reveals, is that PURPA's real economic question was always institutional: who gets to decide the value of power, and over what time horizon? The statute's answer was that the value would be set administratively, by forecast, for long terms, because only that combination could summon an industry into existence against a hostile monopoly. That answer had costs, and the costs were real and fell on consumers in the states where forecasts proved most wrong. But the alternative answer, no obligation and no entrants, had costs too, measured in the monopoly's continued inefficiency and the renewable industry's delayed birth. The 2005 narrowing can be read as Congress's eventual verdict on the tradeoff: keep the administrative mechanism where markets are absent, retire it where markets have arrived. The subsidy charge, to the extent it means the obligation sometimes overpaid, is true in hindsight. To the extent it means the statute was designed as a transfer, it is false, and the distinction matters for how the statute is judged. A statute that overpays by forecast error is a different moral and economic object from a statute that overpays by design, and PURPA was the former.

This profile makes no reference to any proceeding pending after the reference date, and it takes no position on the pending disputes between utilities and generators that the narrowing left unresolved. The measurement arguments continue in state commissions and at the commission, over avoided cost methodologies, contract terms, and the scope of section 210(m) relief, and those arguments belong to the parties and their regulators. What this article establishes is the framework within which those arguments occur: a statutory ceiling defined as cost neutrality, a history of forecast driven disputes about that ceiling's level and term, and a 2005 compromise that made the obligation conditional on market structure. A reader who grasps that framework can follow any PURPA dispute, past or future, because every PURPA dispute is a dispute about what power is worth and who decides. Cost based in foresight and costly in hindsight are compatible descriptions of the same contracts, and the distance between them is the distance between a forecast and the future. That distance, not a subsidy, is what the decades of PURPA litigation were really about.

The One Test from this article's opening can be answered in full, and it is worth stating the answers plainly before the reference table. A reader who has followed the argument should be able to explain that the independent power industry and the commercial market for renewable electricity in the United States both descend from section 210 of PURPA, the 1978 provision requiring utilities to buy power from qualifying facilities at avoided cost, a provision written to encourage industrial cogeneration and small hydro that accidentally created the first lawful competitor to the regulated utility monopoly. The reader should be able to explain that PURPA was one of five statutes signed on November 9, 1978, as a single presidential energy program divided across committees, with the utility, tax, and conservation measures moving through Ways and Means and Finance while the gas pricing measure moved through Commerce and Energy. And the reader should be able to explain that the same package contained the Fuel Use Act's ban on new gas fired power plants, premised on a scarcity forecast that proved wrong, whose prohibitions Congress repealed in 1987 while leaving the narrowed act behind. Package, purchase obligation, consequence, federalism fight, narrowing, cautionary companion: the arc is complete.

The least credited landmark claim deserves a final defense, because it is the kind of judgment that invites skepticism. The case for it rests on three observations. First, the counterfactual: without section 210, the monopoly utility would have remained the only lawful generator in its territory, and the entrants who proved competition possible would have had no buyer, no backup, and no interconnection. The industry's birth would have waited for some later statute, and the later statutes would have faced a monopoly that had never been breached, which is a harder political fight than extending a breach that already exists. Second, the financial mechanism: the purchase obligation did not merely permit entry but made it bankable, converting the right to generate into the revenue certainty that project finance requires, which is why the industry dates its birth to 1978 rather than to any later restructuring. Third, the precedent: every subsequent opening of American electricity markets assumed what PURPA first established, that someone other than the utility may generate and sell power, and the assumption became so familiar that its origin was forgotten. Landmarks are not always the statutes that get the credit. Sometimes they are the statutes that make the credited ones possible.

What endures of PURPA, as of the November 2016 reference date, is therefore threefold. The purchase obligation endures as a live regulatory instrument in the regions without organized wholesale markets, where state commissions still set avoided costs and utilities still sign the contracts. The industry endures everywhere, matured beyond its creator, competing in markets that the statute helped call into being. And the precedent endures in the structure of American electricity law, which has never gone back to the world where the utility was the only permitted producer. The five bill package endures as a lesson in legislative design: divide a program by committee jurisdiction and the pieces will diverge, the mechanisms will outlive the commands, and the provision nobody fought over may matter more than the provisions everybody did. That is the profile of the Public Utility Regulatory Policies Act of 1978, Public Law 95-617, the accidental market maker, the least credited landmark in American energy law, and the statute that built an industry by requiring utilities to buy power they did not want from generators they did not own, at a price defined by the cost of doing without them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did PURPA require utilities to do?

PURPA required electric utilities to purchase electric energy from qualifying cogeneration and small power production facilities, and to sell electric energy to those facilities for backup and supplementary needs. Section 210 directed the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission to write rules imposing both obligations, and the purchase rate could not exceed the utility's incremental cost of alternative energy, the avoided cost standard. The statute also required utilities to interconnect with qualifying facilities. Before this command, a utility could simply refuse to buy a factory's surplus power or refuse to sell it backup service, which made non utility generation commercially impossible. The obligation was the legal breakthrough: for the first time, a non utility generator had a guaranteed buyer at a defined price, which converted the abstract right to generate into a bankable revenue stream.

### Q: What is a qualifying facility under PURPA?

A qualifying facility is either a qualifying small power production facility or a qualifying cogeneration facility, as defined in sections 201 and 210 of the act and the commission's Part 292 rules. A small power production facility uses biomass, waste, or renewable resources such as solar, wind, water, or geothermal as its primary energy source and has a capacity of not more than 80 megawatts at a single site. A cogeneration facility produces electricity plus steam or another useful form of energy, such as heat for industrial or commercial use, and it qualifies by meeting the commission's operating and efficiency requirements. Critically, the 80 megawatt cap applies only to small power production; cogeneration facilities have no statutory size limit. A large industrial cogeneration plant qualifies regardless of its electrical output if it meets the efficiency criteria.

### Q: What is avoided cost under PURPA?

Avoided cost is the cost the electric utility avoids by purchasing power from a qualifying facility instead of generating that power itself or buying it from another source. The statute itself uses the term incremental cost of alternative electric energy, defined in section 210(d), and the commission's rules substituted the term avoided costs as a synonym, defined at 18 C.F.R. 292.101(b)(6). The purchase rate set under section 210 could not exceed avoided cost, which made the transaction cost neutral to the utility's consumers by design: the utility paid no more than it would have spent otherwise. The commission's Order No. 69 expressly included both fixed and running costs in the definition, so capacity value counted alongside fuel savings. Because avoided cost is a forecast about what the utility would have done, its measurement became the central dispute in PURPA's history.

### Q: Did PURPA create the independent power industry?

PURPA created the legal and financial conditions from which the independent power industry grew, and the lineage is documented by government and academic sources. The Congressional Research Service reported in 1998 that with PURPA the federal government opened the generating sector to other entrants, and that about 77 percent of non utility generating capacity came from cogenerators and small power producers, citing 1996 Edison Electric Institute data. The Energy Information Administration reported that renewable capacity owned by independent power producers grew through the 1980s and early 1990s largely because of California's implementation of PURPA. Economist Paul Joskow wrote in 1997 that PURPA significantly spurred long term contracts between utilities and independent generating companies. The companies that sold only at wholesale became known as independent power producers, and the project finance techniques built for PURPA contracts underpinned everything that followed.

### Q: Was PURPA part of a five bill package?

Yes. PURPA was one of five energy bills signed by President Jimmy Carter on November 9, 1978, as the National Energy Act program. The five were the Public Utility Regulatory Policies Act (H.R. 4018, Public Law 95-617), the Energy Tax Act (H.R. 5263, Public Law 95-618), the National Energy Conservation Policy Act (H.R. 5037, Public Law 95-619), the Powerplant and Industrial Fuel Use Act (H.R. 5146, Public Law 95-620), and the Natural Gas Policy Act (H.R. 5289, Public Law 95-621). Carter's signing statement covered all five together. The program was divided into five bills so each could move through the committees claiming its subject matter: the utility, tax, and conservation measures went through Ways and Means and Senate Finance, while the gas pricing measure went through Interstate and Foreign Commerce and Senate Energy and Natural Resources. The single program thus became five statutes shaped by committee jurisdiction.

### Q: Did the Supreme Court uphold PURPA against the states?

Yes. In Federal Energy Regulatory Commission v. Mississippi, 456 U.S. 742, decided June 1, 1982, the Supreme Court sustained PURPA against Commerce Clause and Tenth Amendment challenges brought by the states. The Court held that section 210's purchase scheme was a straightforward exercise of federal preemption under the Commerce Clause. It further held that requiring state utility commissions to consider federal ratemaking standards did not exceed federal power, because the requirement merely set conditions on continued state regulation in an area subject to complete federal preemption. The opinion described the arrangement as cooperative federalism, quoting Hodel for the proposition that states may administer their own regulatory programs within federal minimum standards. Justice O'Connor dissented in part, joined by Justice Rehnquist and Chief Justice Burger, and Justice Powell dissented on the mandatory procedures portion. The decision remains a leading statement of conditional preemption doctrine.

### Q: How did the 2005 energy law change PURPA?

The Energy Policy Act of 2005, Public Law 109-58, added section 210(m) to PURPA through its section 1253(a), codified at 16 U.S.C. 824a-3(m). The amendment allows the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission to relieve an electric utility of the obligation to enter new purchase contracts with a qualifying facility when the commission finds the facility has nondiscriminatory access to competitive wholesale markets. The statute defines three qualifying market types: independently administered auction based day ahead and real time markets, open access transmission service from a commission approved regional transmission entity, and comparable competitive wholesale markets offering a meaningful opportunity to sell to buyers other than the host utility. The relief applies only to new obligations, not existing contracts, and the obligation can be reinstated if market access disappears. In practice the purchase obligation applied mainly in regions without organized wholesale markets.

### Q: Did the PURPA package ban new gas power plants?

The package contained a statute that did, but the ban did not last. The Powerplant and Industrial Fuel Use Act of 1978, Public Law 95-620, prohibited new electric powerplants and major fuel burning installations from using oil or natural gas as their primary fuel, forcing them toward coal and alternate fuels under an exemptions regime. The prohibition rested on forecasts of natural gas scarcity that proved wrong; energy analysts later recounted that the scarcity premise grew out of delivery curtailments caused by price controls rather than geology. Congress repealed the oil and gas prohibitions in 1987 through Public Law 100-42, signed May 21, 1987, which struck sections 202, 302, 401, 402, and 405, Title V, and section 801 of the act. The act itself survived in narrowed form, requiring new baseload plants to self certify coal or alternate fuel capability to the Secretary of Energy.

### Q: How did the 80 megawatt size limit apply to qualifying facilities?

The 80 megawatt limit applied only to small power production facilities, never to cogeneration. Under 16 U.S.C. 796(17)(A), a small power production facility using biomass, waste, or renewable resources as its primary energy source qualified only if its capacity, aggregated with other facilities at the same site as determined by the commission, did not exceed 80 megawatts. The commission's same site determinations policed that boundary, aggregating capacity across facilities it found to share a site. Cogeneration facilities, defined at 16 U.S.C. 796(18)(A) as producing electricity plus useful thermal energy, faced no statutory size limit at all; they qualified by satisfying the commission's operating and efficiency standards. This asymmetry reflected the provision's dual aims: small renewables needed a size ceiling to keep the program targeted, while industrial cogeneration needed efficiency tests rather than size tests to capture waste heat at any scale.

### Q: What did FERC Order No. 69 establish under section 210?

FERC Order No. 69, published at 45 Federal Register 12,214 on February 25, 1980, was the commission's comprehensive rule implementing section 210 of PURPA under the one year deadline Congress set in section 210(a). The order created the Part 292 regulatory framework, defined avoided cost as derived from the statute's incremental cost of alternative electric energy, and expressly included both fixed and running costs that the utility could avoid by buying from qualifying facilities. It set the procedures for qualifying facility certification, the obligations of utilities to purchase and sell power, interconnection requirements, and the state implementation process under section 210(f). The inclusion of capacity costs was the order's most consequential choice, because it supported the capacity payments that made long term qualifying facility contracts financeable. Every state avoided cost proceeding of the 1980s operated inside the framework this order built.

### Q: How did state regulators implement the avoided cost purchase rule?

Section 210(f) required each state regulatory authority, after notice and opportunity for public hearing, to implement the commission's section 210 rules for the utilities under its ratemaking jurisdiction. In practice this meant lengthy state proceedings to determine each utility's avoided cost, the forecast of what the utility would spend to generate or buy the power itself. Commissions took evidence on fuel price forecasts, planned capacity additions, demand growth, and discount rates, then set the purchase rates qualifying facilities would receive. California's proceedings became the most famous because generous avoided cost findings produced a boom in wind, small hydro, and biomass development. Other active states ran their own versions, while reluctant states set low avoided costs that yielded little development. The federalism decision in FERC v. Mississippi confirmed the states could not constitutionally escape this implementing role, so the uneven state numbers, not the federal text, determined where the PURPA market actually grew.

### Q: Why was the 1978 energy program divided into five separate bills?

The program was divided because congressional committee jurisdiction required it. A single presidential energy program touched taxation, utility regulation, conservation, fuel use restrictions, and natural gas pricing, and each subject belonged to different committees: the House Ways and Means and Senate Finance Committees claimed the tax, utility, and conservation measures, while the House Interstate and Foreign Commerce Committee and the Senate Energy and Natural Resources Committee claimed the gas pricing measure. No single committee could have reported a bill covering all five subjects without jurisdictional conflict. The division therefore shaped each statute's content, with the revenue committees writing tax credits and ratemaking standards and the commerce committees writing price ceilings and fuel prohibitions. The five bills were reunited in conference negotiations and in President Carter's single signing statement of November 9, 1978, but their separate committee origins explain why the package reads as five distinct laws rather than one integrated code.

### Q: What subjects did the other four 1978 energy statutes cover?

Beyond PURPA, the package contained four statutes with distinct subjects. The Energy Tax Act, Public Law 95-618, imposed a gas guzzler excise tax on low mileage automobiles and created residential and business tax credits for conservation and renewable investment; the Congressional Research Service reported in 2008 that the gas guzzler tax was still in effect. The National Energy Conservation Policy Act, Public Law 95-619, established residential conservation services and funded state grants for school and hospital energy projects plus weatherization, with 900 million dollars authorized over three years per the American Public Power Association. The Powerplant and Industrial Fuel Use Act, Public Law 95-620, banned new oil and gas fired powerplants until its prohibitions were repealed in 1987. The Natural Gas Policy Act, Public Law 95-621, phased deregulation of wellhead gas prices through ceiling categories until its price controls were repealed effective 1993.

### Q: How did the Energy Tax Act of 1978 fit the national energy program?

The Energy Tax Act, H.R. 5263, Public Law 95-618, was the revenue committee's contribution to the five bill program, using the tax code to steer energy behavior. Its most durable element was the gas guzzler excise tax on automobiles with low fuel economy, which the Congressional Research Service reported in 2008 was still in effect. It also created residential energy credits of 30 percent on the first 2,000 dollars of qualifying conservation and renewable expenditure plus 20 percent above that, capped at 10,000 dollars, and business credits for renewable investment reaching 25 percent, all figures per the same CRS report. The renewable credits were later increased by the Crude Oil Windfall Profit Tax Act of 1980. The act's survival pattern illustrates the package's broader lesson: revenue measures that create organized beneficiaries tend to outlast regulatory commands, because the credit claimants defend them while nobody defends an expired prohibition.

### Q: What did the National Energy Conservation Policy Act require?

The National Energy Conservation Policy Act, H.R. 5037, Public Law 95-619, 92 Stat. 3206, was the conservation pillar of the 1978 program. It required the creation of a residential energy conservation service to help households identify and finance efficiency improvements, and it authorized federal grants to states for energy audits and conservation projects in schools and hospitals, with Title III, Part 1 authorizing 900 million dollars over three years according to the American Public Power Association. It also funded low income weatherization programs. Codified at chapter 91 of title 42 of the United States Code, the act was amended repeatedly through the 1980s, in 1982, 1986, 1987, and twice in 1988, which shows Congress adjusting rather than abandoning the conservation effort. Unlike the fuel prohibitions, which collapsed when their forecast failed, the conservation programs addressed the durable institutional fact that households and institutions underinvest in efficiency, so the statute kept finding defenders.

### Q: Why was the Fuel Use Act's gas prohibition repealed in 1987?

The prohibition was repealed because the scarcity forecast behind it had been falsified by events. The Powerplant and Industrial Fuel Use Act had barred new powerplants from burning natural gas to preserve a commodity thought to be running out, but through the 1980s gas prices fell instead of rising, exposing the forecast as wrong. Energy analysts later recounted that the perceived scarcity grew out of delivery curtailments caused by price controls rather than geology. With the rationale gone, the ban survived only as a compliance burden: utilities seeking to build efficient gas fired plants had to navigate an exemptions regime for a rule nobody defended. Congress responded with H.R. 1941, passed by voice votes in both chambers in May 1987 and signed May 21, 1987, as Public Law 100-42, which repealed the oil and gas prohibitions while leaving a narrowed act requiring new baseload plants to self certify coal or alternate fuel capability. The repeal took less than nine years.

### Q: How did the Natural Gas Policy Act's wellhead price controls end?

The Natural Gas Policy Act of 1978 imposed maximum lawful wellhead price ceilings by category of gas as a phased transition toward market pricing, addressing the shortages of 1976 and 1977. The control regime did not last. Congress passed the Natural Gas Wellhead Decontrol Act of 1989, Public Law 101-60, signed July 26, 1989, which decontrolled first sales of natural gas and permanently repealed the wellhead price controls effective January 1, 1993. The repeal completed the transition the 1978 act had begun: from administered ceilings to market prices. The commission retained jurisdiction over interstate transportation and market oversight, but price setting at the wellhead ended. The episode parallels the Fuel Use Act's fate in miniature: a 1978 intervention premised on shortage gave way once surplus and market institutions made the intervention unnecessary. Of the package's two most interventionist statutes, the gas pricing controls lasted about fourteen years past 1978, roughly twice as long as the fuel prohibitions.

### Q: What kinds of markets can relieve a utility of the purchase obligation?

Section 210(m), added by section 1253(a) of the Energy Policy Act of 2005 and codified at 16 U.S.C. 824a-3(m), defines three market types whose existence can relieve a utility of entering new purchase contracts. First, independently administered auction based day ahead and real time wholesale markets for energy and transmission, the organized markets run by regional transmission organizations. Second, transmission and interconnection services from a commission approved regional transmission entity under a nondiscriminatory open access tariff. Third, wholesale markets for capacity and energy of comparable competitive quality that give the qualifying facility a meaningful opportunity to sell to buyers other than the host utility. The commission must affirmatively find that the particular facility has nondiscriminatory access to one of these market types, the relief covers only new obligations, and the obligation is reinstated if the access disappears. The design makes the purchase duty conditional on genuine market absence.

### Q: How do cogeneration facilities qualify without a size limit?

Cogeneration facilities qualify under PURPA through efficiency and operating standards rather than size. The statute defines a cogeneration facility at 16 U.S.C. 796(18)(A) as one producing electric energy together with steam or another useful form of energy, such as heat, used for industrial, commercial, heating, or cooling purposes. The commission's rules then set the qualifying criteria: the facility must meet minimum operating and efficiency standards that demonstrate genuine combined production rather than a token thermal output attached to a powerplant. There is no megawatt ceiling in the statute, so a large industrial cogeneration unit qualifies if it satisfies those technical tests. The policy logic was that waste heat recovery is efficient at any scale, and capping it would have excluded exactly the large industrial plants where the fuel savings were greatest. This is why descriptions of qualifying facilities as limited to 80 megawatts are wrong; the cap belongs solely to small power production.

### Q: Why is avoided cost measurement the real dispute under PURPA?

Avoided cost measurement is the real dispute because the statute's fairness guarantee lives or dies in the forecast. Section 210 caps the purchase rate at the utility's incremental cost of alternative energy, which by definition makes the purchase cost neutral to consumers if measured correctly. But avoided cost is not observable; it is a forecast of what the utility would have built, what fuel would have cost, and how demand would have grown over a long contract term. State commissions in the early 1980s set generous avoided costs on forecasts of rising oil prices, and when prices fell, utilities were locked into expensive long term contracts and cried subsidy. Generators replied that the contracts reflected the best forecasts at signing and the financing needs the statute was meant to serve. Every PURPA fight since, over rate levels, contract terms, and capacity valuation, has been a fight about measurement, which is a more precise argument than the subsidy slogan and the one the statute's design actually invites.
