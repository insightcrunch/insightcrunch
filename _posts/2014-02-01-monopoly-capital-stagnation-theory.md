Monopoly capital theory begins with an act of replacement, and everything distinctive about the school flows from that single decision. Where the classical Marxist account of capitalist crisis locates the system's central difficulty in a tendency for the rate of profit to fall, Paul Baran and Paul Sweezy argued that under the conditions of advanced monopoly capitalism the opposite tendency governs: the economic surplus tends to rise, and the system's central difficulty is finding ways to absorb what it generates. A tradition organised around scarcity of surplus was turned, deliberately and explicitly, into a tradition organised around excess of it. The school that resulted, associated with the journal Monthly Review and often called the Monthly Review school, predicted stagnation rather than breakdown, treated advertising and military spending as structural necessities rather than incidental waste, and built its analysis on a category, the economic surplus, that it openly substituted for Marx's surplus value. Whether that substitution counts as creative development or as quiet abandonment of the theory it claims to extend is the question around which the school's reputation has always turned.

![Monopoly capital and stagnation theory, the rising surplus and the problem of absorption - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-12.webp)

The claim at the centre of this article can be given a name at once, because it organises every section that follows. The absorption inversion is the move by which monopoly capital theory replaces a problem of generating surplus with a problem of disposing of it, and then derives the system's characteristic features from the disposal problem: the enormous apparatus of selling, the tolerance for waste, the reliance on state expenditure, the periodic rescue performed by finance. Read the school through that inversion and its otherwise miscellaneous positions fall into a pattern. Its account of why giant firms behave as they do, its explanation of why growth falters even when profitability is strong, its attention to institutions that other Marxist traditions treat as secondary, all become consequences of one premise about which side of the ledger generates the pressure. Read it without the inversion and the school looks like a loose collection of observations about bigness. The inversion is its identity.

Scope should be fixed before the argument begins. This article treats the school itself: its founding texts, its central categories, its absorption mechanisms, its later development into the theory of monopoly-finance capital, and the criticisms directed at it from inside and outside the Marxist tradition. The doctrine it replaced, the tendency of the rate of profit to fall, has its own article in this series, the [tendency rate of profit to fall](/2013/10/15/tendency-rate-of-profit-to-fall/), and that mechanism is summarised here only to the extent needed to show what the inversion inverted. The wider family of crisis theories, within which the school occupies the demand-side wing, is mapped in the [Marxist crisis theory guide](/2013/10/01/marxist-crisis-theory-guide/), and the demand tradition it descends from is examined in the article on [overproduction and underconsumption](/2013/11/01/overproduction-and-underconsumption/). What follows keeps its eyes on the one school, because the school rewards that focus: it is the most internally unified branch of Marxist crisis theory, and its unity comes precisely from the founding replacement that this article examines.

## A school defined by a single move

Most traditions within Marxism are defined by a text, a party, or a country. The monopoly capital school is defined by a theoretical operation performed at a specific moment in a specific book. The book, Monopoly Capital, appeared in 1966 under the names of Baran and Sweezy, although Baran had died in 1964 and the published text was completed by Sweezy from their joint work and drafts. Its authors announced, with unusual candour for a work of theory, that the competitive model underlying classical Marxist economics no longer described the economy they were analysing, and that the laws derived from that model had to be reformulated for an economy dominated by the giant corporation. The reformulation they proposed was not a refinement of the falling rate of profit. It was a substitution: the tendency of the surplus to rise, they argued, is the counterpart under monopoly conditions of the tendency of the rate of profit to fall under competitive conditions, and the analyst who keeps the old law in the new setting will misread nearly everything that follows.

The candour matters because it distinguishes the school from traditions that present their revisions as mere restatements. Baran and Sweezy did not claim that Marx, properly read, had secretly taught a rising surplus all along. They claimed that the economy's structure had changed in a way that changed which tendency dominates, and that fidelity to Marx's method required altering Marx's conclusions. That posture, respectful toward the method and revisionist toward the results, explains both the school's durability and the hostility it attracted. Admirers saw in it the only honest way to keep a research programme alive: hold the questions, update the mechanisms. Detractors saw a theory that kept the vocabulary of the tradition, crisis, exploitation, imperialism, while quietly discarding the analytical engine, the labour theory of value and its profit rate, that made the vocabulary cohere. Both reactions were present from the book's first reviews, and neither has exhausted itself since.

The operation also fixed the school's characteristic method. Because the substituted category, the economic surplus, is defined in terms of output and costs rather than in terms of value embodied in labour, the school's arguments could be conducted largely in the language of observable magnitudes: shares of national income, margins over cost, capacity utilisation, expenditure flows. That made its claims unusually exposed to empirical checking, a virtue its defenders celebrate and a vulnerability its critics exploit. A theory built on the falling rate of profit can retreat, when pressed, into disputes about how value magnitudes ought to be measured. A theory built on rising surplus shares and unabsorbed capacity invites the statistician into the room. The school accepted that invitation from the beginning, and much of its subsequent history, including the remarkable convergence with mainstream research on market concentration examined later in this article, consists of statisticians arriving.

### Why did Baran and Sweezy replace the falling rate of profit?

Because they argued that price competition no longer governs the core of the economy. With large firms administering prices, they held that the surplus tends to rise rather than the profit rate to fall, making absorption of that surplus, rather than its generation, the system's central problem.

The structure of the replacement deserves a slower look, because its logic is easily caricatured. The falling rate of profit, in Marx's construction, arises under conditions where firms compete by mechanising: each capitalist introduces labour-saving equipment to cut costs, the economy-wide ratio of capital to labour climbs, and since labour is the source of surplus value, the rate of profit on the total capital tends downward even as the mass of profit grows. Prices in that story are disciplined by competition; no firm can simply decree its margin. Baran and Sweezy's point was that the disciplining mechanism had weakened at the commanding heights of the economy. Where a handful of corporations dominate an industry, price cutting becomes a weapon none of them wishes to use, because a price war damages all and displaces none. Competition migrates to other terrains: product differentiation, advertising, distribution, credit terms. Prices become administered, in the phrase Gardiner Means had developed for the American economy of the nineteen thirties, set by firms with an eye to target returns and defended rather than discovered. Under such conditions the cost-cutting drive continues, indeed intensifies, but its fruits are no longer competed away into lower prices. They accumulate as widening margins. The surplus rises.

Notice what the argument does and does not claim. It does not deny that competitive sectors persist, in agriculture, in small-scale services, in the supply chains beneath the corporate core. It claims that the core sets the system's dynamic, and that the core's pricing behaviour differs in kind from the textbook mechanism. Nor does it deny that the rate of profit can fall in particular periods for particular reasons: wars, wage pressure, raw material shocks. It denies that the falling tendency is the system's gravitational centre, the law around which long-run explanation should be organised. The gravitational centre, on this account, is the widening gap between what monopoly capitalism can produce and what it can profitably use, a gap that must be filled by absorption channels or else express itself as idle capacity and unemployed labour. Stagnation, in short, is what an unabsorbed rising surplus looks like from the outside.

## From competition to monopoly: the economy the theory describes

The theory's portrait of the economy begins with scale. The giant corporation, controlling assets comparable to the budgets of states, operating across continents, and planning its investments over horizons that dwarf electoral cycles, is treated not as an enlarged version of the competitive firm but as a different kind of actor. It does not take prices as given. It makes prices, and it makes them with a strong preference for stability, because its planning requires predictable margins and because price instability in oligopolistic markets threatens a mutually destructive spiral. Baran and Sweezy drew here on a body of observation that extended well beyond Marxist circles: Berle and Means on the separation of ownership and control in the modern corporation, Means on inflexible administered prices, and the institutionalist tradition that had long insisted the large firm obeys a logic of administration rather than of auction markets.

Cost behaviour under monopoly completes the picture. The giant firm is a formidable cost cutter. It rationalises production, automates, exploits economies of scale, and presses its suppliers, and it does all this with an intensity the competitive firm would envy. But where the competitive firm's cost reductions are transmitted to buyers through falling prices, the monopoly firm's reductions are retained behind the defended price. Unit costs fall; prices hold or rise; the margin between them widens. Multiplied across the corporate core, this asymmetry is the microeconomic engine of the rising surplus. The engine does not require conspiracy or even explicit collusion. It requires only that each large firm, acting rationally in its own interest, declines to compete on price while competing ferociously on everything else.

Rationalisation has a second face that the school emphasised and its critics sometimes missed. The same firm that guards its margin also plans its capacity conservatively, because excess capacity in the hands of a rival is a threat and excess capacity in its own hands is a cost. Whole industries settle into patterns where utilisation runs persistently below what the installed equipment could deliver, not because demand failed in some accidental year but because the structure of the industry rewards restraint. The economy's problem is thereby inverted at the level of the firm as well: not how to produce enough, but how to avoid producing too much for the market the administered price can clear. Surplus capacity and surplus product are twins born of the same pricing regime.

### How does a giant corporation actually set its prices?

A giant corporation sets prices by administration rather than by auction: it calculates unit costs, adds a margin set to deliver a target rate of return on its capital, and holds the resulting price steady across fluctuations in demand. Rivals in such industries, recognising that price cuts invite retaliation without gain, compete through advertising, styling, and credit terms, not price.

Administration, described this way, is a practice before it is a theory, and the school's account gains strength from how much ordinary business behaviour it explains without strain. List prices are published and revised at intervals. Discounting is channelled into rebates, trade-ins, and financing offers that leave the headline price intact. Product lines are tiered so that effective competition occurs between models rather than between firms. None of this requires a smoke-filled room. It requires firms large enough to see the whole market, patient enough to value the market's stability over a quarter's advantage, and similar enough in cost structure to converge on similar prices without instruction. The administered price is the institutional form that competition takes when the competitors have grown too large to fight in the old way.

Kalecki supplied the school with its sharpest analytical instrument for this world. The Polish economist Michał Kalecki, working in the nineteen thirties, had argued that the prices of manufactured goods are fixed by firms as a mark-up over prime costs, and that the size of the mark-up expresses what he called the degree of monopoly: a composite of industrial concentration, the strength of trade unions, the importance of overheads, and the power relations within the industry. Where concentration is high and unions weak, the mark-up is fat; where sellers crowd and buyers organise, it thins. Kalecki's framework did two things for Baran and Sweezy. It gave the administered price a formal structure instead of a journalistic description, and it connected the margin directly to distribution, since a rising degree of monopoly shifts income from wages toward profits across the whole economy. The mark-up, in this line of thought, is not a detail of price theory. It is the valve through which the surplus expands.

Pricing theory of this kind also reframes what competition means, and the reframing carries the school's argument into territories far from price lists. If firms compete by persuading rather than by undercutting, then the persuasive apparatus, advertising agencies, marketing departments, media channels sustained by advertising revenue, becomes part of the economy's core machinery rather than decoration on its surface. If firms compete by differentiating products whose underlying costs converge, then planned variation, annual model changes, and the cultivation of dissatisfaction with last year's purchase become production activities in their own right, absorbing labour and resources. The sales effort, treated at length later in this article, is not an afterthought bolted onto the pricing story. It is what competition looks like after price has been taken off the table.

## Inside the giant corporation: managers, motives, and the firm's soul

A theory that makes the giant corporation its central actor owes an account of what the actor wants, and the school's answer positioned it against the managerial theories fashionable in its formative decades. The thesis of the managerial revolution, in its several versions, held that the separation of ownership from control had softened capitalism's driving motive: salaried managers, secure in their positions, pursued growth, stability, prestige, and quiet lives rather than the maximal profit that owners would have demanded, and the economy's dynamics softened accordingly. Berle and Means had documented the separation; James Burnham had prophesied a managerial society; John Kenneth Galbraith would give the tendency its most elegant form in the technostructure, the collective intelligence of the large organisation, planning its environment rather than obeying its market. Baran and Sweezy read the same evidence and drew the opposite conclusion. The manager of the giant corporation, they argued, is not an emancipated technocrat but a functionary of capital whose career, income, and standing are bound to the firm's accumulation; the profit motive does not weaken as ownership disperses, it becomes institutional, embedded in promotion ladders, performance measures, and the takeover market that disciplines laggard managements. Where the managerial theorists saw the corporation's soul changing, the school saw its incentives perfected: the firm no longer needed a hungry proprietor, because hunger had been built into its constitution.

The disagreement is not antiquarian, because the two accounts predict different corporate behaviours under the absorption problem. A technostructure seeking a quiet life would let margins rest where they are comfortable, invest enough to grow respectably, and distribute the remainder in organisational slack. The school's corporation, driven by institutionalised accumulation, behaves as the theory requires: it cuts costs relentlessly because every saving widens the surplus it exists to widen, defends prices because the margin is its measure, and confronts the resulting disposal problem as an engineering challenge to be solved with sales effort, acquisitions, political influence, and financial placement. The modern landscape of corporate behaviour, in which managements announce their devotion to shareholder value while behaving with a uniformity no shareholder instructed, has generally been kinder to the school's reading than to Galbraith's, and historians of the firm have noted the convergence with interest. The school's own gloss is characteristically structural: it never mattered what managers felt. A system that selects its executives from among those who widen margins, and discards those who do not, will behave as if profit were its soul whether or not any individual manager believes in souls.

Growth of the firm, as distinct from growth of its profit, supplied the school's one concession to the managerial literature, and the concession is woven into the stagnation argument. Giant firms do pursue size for its own strategic sake, since size brings market power, political weight, and survival; but size pursued through merger and acquisition, the dominant route, absorbs surplus without adding proportionate capacity, buying existing plant rather than building new. The distinction matters for the absorption accounts: an acquisition transfers ownership and employs bankers, lawyers, and financiers in the transfer, absorbing surplus in fees and in the premium paid to the acquired firm's shareholders, while the economy's stock of workplaces stands unchanged. The merger wave, that recurring fever of the corporate world, is therefore legible in the school's terms as an absorption festival, a bonfire of surplus in which the fuel is consumed by the ceremony of rearrangement itself. Few spectacles confirm the absorption inversion more neatly than an economy whose most dynamic firms compete to grow by purchasing one another.

## Price rigidity and the kink: Sweezy's earlier oligopoly theory

The administered price had a formal ancestor in Sweezy's own work, and the genealogy is worth recovering because it shows the school's pricing argument developing inside professional economics before it was restated as Marxist doctrine. In 1939, in a paper that became one of the most cited in the theory of oligopoly, Sweezy proposed the kinked demand curve as an explanation of price rigidity in concentrated industries. Imagine the firm contemplating a price change. If it raises its price, rivals will not follow, since each gains by holding and harvesting the defectors' customers; demand therefore falls away sharply. If it cuts its price, rivals must follow or lose their markets, so the cutter gains little additional volume; demand responds sluggishly. The demand curve facing the firm is kinked at the going price, elastic above it and inelastic below, and within a range of cost movements the profit-maximising price does not move at all. Prices in oligopoly are sticky, on this account, not because of any agreement but because each firm, reasoning about its rivals' reactions, finds stability individually rational.

The kink was a modest device with an immodest implication. Professional theory had treated price as the economy's great adjusting variable, the signal through which scarcity speaks. If the prices of the industrial core are rigid by structure, the signal is muffled exactly where the economy's weight is concentrated, and adjustment must occur somewhere else: in output, in employment, in the utilisation of capacity. The young Sweezy's device thus contained, in embryo, the mature school's macroeconomic conclusion, for an economy whose prices will not fall when demand falters is an economy that takes its recessions in idle plant and dismissed workers rather than in cheaper goods. Later critics of the kink objected that it explains why a price, once established, persists, but not how the price was established in the first place, and the objection is fair as far as it goes. The school's mature work answered by supplying the missing half from Kalecki: the level of price is set by the mark-up that the degree of monopoly sustains, and the kink then explains the level's defence. Level from power, rigidity from interdependence: the two halves joined produce the administered price in its complete form, a price chosen by firms and then frozen by the structure of their rivalry.

The genealogy matters for a further reason. It rebuts, in advance, the lazy version of the charge that the school's economics was amateur doctrine imposed on the profession from outside. Its pricing theory grew from the profession's own oligopoly literature, to which its founder had made a canonical contribution before he was a Marxist publicist, and its stagnation theory grew from Steindl's and Kalecki's work at the boundary of the Keynesian mainstream. The school's distinctiveness was never ignorance of orthodox tools. It was the decision to take those tools' implications seriously at the level of the whole economy, where orthodoxy preferred to file them as special cases. Administered prices in the core plus competitive prices at the fringe equals, in the textbook treatment, a detail for industrial organisation courses. In the school's treatment it equals a different central law, a different normal state, and a different politics. The distance between the two treatments is the distance between a friction and a foundation.

## The economic surplus: the category that replaced surplus value

Every theoretical substitution has a biography, and this one begins a decade before Monopoly Capital, in Baran's The Political Economy of Growth, published in 1957. Writing about underdeveloped economies and the legacies of colonialism, Baran needed a magnitude that could express how much a society produced beyond what it consumed in the course of producing it, because his argument about development turned on how that excess was used: hoarded, wasted on luxury and armaments, or invested in expanded capacity. He defined the economic surplus as, in its actual form, the difference between a society's actual output and its actual consumption, and he paired it with a potential form: the difference between the output a society could produce with its resources fully and rationally used and the consumption genuinely needed to sustain its population. The gap between potential and actual surplus, in his analysis of poor countries, measured the waste imposed by irrational social arrangements: idle land, unemployed labour, resources squandered on unproductive display.

When the same category was carried into the analysis of rich monopoly economies, its meaning sharpened. In Monopoly Capital the surplus appears as all that a society produces over and above the costs of producing it, gathered from wherever it accumulates: corporate profits, interest, rent, the incomes of unproductive workers, the tax take that funds state activity. The category is deliberately aggregative. It refuses to sort receipts by their moral or theoretical pedigree and instead asks a single quantitative question: how large is the excess of product over necessary cost, and where does it go? That question, Baran and Sweezy believed, was the question monopoly capitalism poses, because an economy whose surplus swells beyond its capacity to absorb it will stagnate no matter how efficiently its firms perform.

The substitution of surplus for surplus value was therefore principled, not careless, and its rationale should be stated as its authors stated it. Surplus value, in Marx's construction, is a value category: it measures unpaid labour time embodied in commodities, and its magnitude can only be read through the mediations of the value form. Under competitive capitalism, where prices hug values closely enough for the theory's purposes, the mediation is manageable. Under monopoly, prices are set systematically away from values by firms with market power, and the distribution of the social product among profit, rent, interest and salary is reshuffled by pricing power itself. A category that tries to track embodied labour through that reshuffling, the authors argued, will measure the analyst's assumptions rather than the economy. The surplus, defined on the surface of the economy in terms of output and cost, sacrifices depth for traction. Whether the sacrifice was necessary, and whether what was lost exceeds what was gained, is the deepest dispute the school provoked, and it receives its own section below. What matters at this stage is that the trade was conscious: depth of value analysis exchanged, knowingly, for a measurable aggregate matched to administered pricing.

### What separates the actual surplus from the potential surplus?

The actual surplus is the difference between what a society really produces and what it really consumes, a magnitude realised within existing arrangements. The potential surplus is the larger difference between what the same society could produce with its resources fully employed and rationally organised and the consumption its population genuinely requires, a magnitude existing arrangements suppress.

The distinction does explanatory work in both directions. Looking at poor economies, it allowed Baran to argue that poverty was not primarily a shortage of surplus but a misuse of it: landlords, foreign firms, and bloated state apparatuses consumed or exported what could have built industry. Looking at rich economies, it allowed the school to treat unemployment and idle plant not as temporary failures of demand but as visible fragments of the potential surplus, the portion of possible output that the structure declines to realise. An economy photographed at a moment of slack is, in this framework, showing the observer its unabsorbed surplus in physical form. The dole queue and the mothballed line are accounting entries written in people and steel.

Baran's category also carried a normative charge that its authors did not hide and that readers should register without exaggerating. To speak of potential surplus is to assert that a society's actual performance falls short of its own possibilities, and to measure the shortfall is to indict the arrangements that produce it. The school held this to be a strength: a political economy that cannot say how much is wasted cannot criticise waste. Critics replied, reasonably, that a magnitude defined against a counterfactual rational order imports the analyst's preferred order into the measurement, and that two analysts with different rational orders will report different potentials. The reply from the school was that the concept does not need a single agreed counterfactual to be useful; ranges and orders of magnitude suffice to show that the gap is large. The dispute is real and unresolved, and it foreshadows the measurement quarrels that dog the rising surplus claim itself.

Sweezy's own preparation for the substitution ran through his Theory of Capitalist Development of 1942, the book that introduced a generation of English-speaking readers to Marxist economics and to Kalecki. Already there, Sweezy treated the realisation problem, the difficulty of selling what has been produced at prices that validate the surplus it embodies, as central to capitalist crisis, and already there he was sceptical that the falling rate of profit could bear the full explanatory weight the tradition placed on it. Monopoly Capital can thus be read as the working out, over a quarter century, of doubts present in the school's founding generation from the start. The inversion was not an improvisation of the nineteen sixties. It was the mature form of a long unease with the tradition's central law, an unease that the structure of the postwar American economy, dominated by corporations of unprecedented scale, finally forced into the open.

## The law of rising surplus

The positive law of the system, in the school's construction, is disarmingly simple to state. Under monopoly capitalism the economic surplus tends to rise, both absolutely and as a share of total output. The mechanisms are the ones already described: unit costs fall under the pressure of rationalisation and technical progress, while prices, defended by administration and oligopolistic discipline, do not fall with them. What competition once transferred to consumers as cheaper goods, monopoly retains as margin. Profits, interest, rent, executive salaries, and the revenues flowing to the state all draw on the widening stream, and the stream widens because the two blades of the scissors, cost and price, move in opposite directions with structural regularity rather than by accident.

Three features of the law deserve notice, since confusion about each generates spurious refutations. First, the law concerns the surplus, not the rate of profit on capital. The school's central variable is a share of output, the portion of the social product exceeding production costs, and a rising share is entirely compatible with a falling, stable, or rising profit rate depending on how capital accumulates alongside. Critics who test the theory against profit-rate series are testing a variable the theory deliberately demoted, though the school is partly responsible for the confusion, since its rhetoric sometimes slides between surplus and profitability. Second, the law is a tendency, subject to counteracting influences the book itself names: intensified exploitation is not required for it, but new industries, wars, and booms in state spending can all change the surplus's destination without changing its trend of generation. Third, the law carries no automatic catastrophe. A rising surplus is a problem only relative to absorption, and the theory's predictive content lies in the race between generation and absorption rather than in the surplus alone.

The law's elegance is also its exposure. Formulated as a claim about shares of output, it can in principle be checked against national accounts, and everyone in the debate understood this from the outset. Baran and Sweezy themselves offered estimates suggesting that the surplus had risen from a modest share of American output at the start of the twentieth century to roughly half by the later nineteen fifties. Those figures, built by summing profits, rents, interest, advertising outlays, finance and insurance earnings, and government expenditure, were offered as orders of magnitude rather than precise measurements, and they were attacked as such: the category's boundaries are contestable, several components risk double counting, and the inclusion of all government spending builds a conclusion about state absorption into the very measure of what needs absorbing. Defenders answered that the direction of the movement is robust across reasonable boundary choices, a claim that later researchers in the tradition repeatedly tested. The quarrel over the surplus's measured size, pursued in detail in the criticisms section of this article, is best understood as the price the school paid for the traction it wanted. It got a theory that could be argued with in the language of data, and it got the arguments.

One further component of the rising surplus is easy to overlook because it operates quietly: the growth of unproductive employment within and around the corporation. As the margin widens, firms can carry, and rationally do carry, layers of activity that produce nothing saleable in themselves: larger legal departments, expanded supervisory hierarchies, marketing divisions, lobbying offices, financial engineers. The salaries of these layers are paid out of the surplus, and their growth is at once evidence of the surplus's size and a channel of its absorption, since the incomes support consumption demand. The school treated this not as bureaucratic accident but as a systematic feature: a surplus that cannot be invested productively will be spent, and one of the ways it is spent is on the salaries of the people who administer, defend, and celebrate the arrangements that generate it. The corporate headquarters, gleaming and fully staffed amid unused capacity on the factory floor, is the theory's emblematic building.

It is worth pausing on how completely this picture inverts the emotional register of the classical crisis story. In the falling-rate tradition, capitalism's problem is a kind of exhaustion: the golden goose of surplus value is progressively starved by the very mechanisation that enrichment requires, and crisis is the convulsion of a system running short of its fuel. In the monopoly capital picture the system is not starved but glutted. Its fuel accumulates faster than its engine can burn it. Crisis arrives not as famine but as indigestion, and the system's characteristic pathologies are those of disposal: waste cultivated as policy, demand manufactured as industry, swords beaten into the ploughshares of budgets that must find something, anything, to spend on. The absorption inversion names exactly this reversal of register, and it is why the school's work reads so differently from the rest of the tradition even when its conclusions about the system's irrationality sound similar.

## The absorption problem: outlets for a surplus that keeps growing

A rising surplus must go somewhere. If it is neither consumed nor invested nor otherwise spent, it does not vanish politely; it reappears as unsold output, then as cutbacks, then as the idle men and machines of a slump. Monopoly Capital organises its middle chapters around an audit of the available outlets, asking of each whether it can plausibly expand fast enough to keep pace with a surplus whose growth is built into the system's pricing structure. The audit's method is worth noticing: each channel is examined generously, credited with real absorptive power, and then measured against the trend it must match. The pessimism of the conclusions is meant to be earned channel by channel, not assumed.

Capitalists' consumption disposes of the first portion almost by definition, and its limits are arithmetical. The owners of the surplus could, in principle, spend their incomes on luxury goods, estates, yachts, and services, and the school never denied that conspicuous consumption on a Veblenian scale performs real absorptive work. But the consumption of a small class, however extravagant, grows slowly compared with the surplus generated by an entire corporate economy, and it is bounded by satiation in a way production is not. A magnate can eat only so many dinners. More fundamentally, capitalist consumption is not the system's purpose in its own accounting; the drive of the firm is accumulation, and income routed to the dining room is income the firm has failed to deploy. Luxury absorption is therefore treated as a minor and roughly constant drain, significant for the sociology of the rich, incapable of carrying the trend.

Investment is the outlet classical economics trusts, and its failure is the theoretical heart of the book. Investment does absorb surplus in the year it occurs: spending on plant and equipment employs workers, orders materials, and validates demand. But investment is also the one outlet that enlarges the problem it relieves, because new capacity comes on stream able to pour out still more output and generate still more surplus. The system must run faster each year to stand still, finding investment opportunities larger than last year's merely to absorb a surplus swollen by last year's success. And the opportunities themselves are not, under monopoly conditions, abundant: firms already carrying excess capacity have weak motives to build more, and an oligopolist contemplating a new plant must reckon that its output will press against the very administered prices its margins depend on. Investment under monopoly is thus self-limiting in a way investment under competition was not. The competitive firm invested or died; the monopoly firm can choose to wait, and waiting, multiplied across the corporate core, is stagnation.

### Why can investment not absorb the surplus it helps to create?

Investment absorbs surplus as spending in the present while enlarging productive capacity for the future, so every successful act of absorption increases the output, and therefore the surplus, that later periods must absorb. Under monopoly conditions, where firms already operate below capacity and guard their prices against added supply, the incentive to undertake such self-defeating expansion is chronically weak.

Kalecki's aphorism about profits belongs here, because it captures the same circularity from the side of income: capitalists, as a class, get what they spend. Their investment and consumption decisions determine their aggregate profits, which means the surplus is never simply a pre-existing pot waiting for uses; it is realised through the very expenditures meant to absorb it. Baran and Sweezy worked this insight into the structure of their argument. Absorption is not a matter of finding a hole into which a fixed surplus can be poured. It is a question of whether the spending flows of the economy grow on a trajectory steep enough to validate a surplus that the pricing system keeps enlarging. When the flows flatten, the surplus is not absorbed but unrealised, and the statistical signature of unrealisation is the slump.

Two outlets remain within the private economy before the state is reached, and the book gives each a full chapter's weight. The first is the sales effort, the entire apparatus of advertising, marketing, product differentiation, packaging, model change, and consumer credit promotion, which absorbs surplus twice over: directly, through the resources and salaries it consumes, and indirectly, by coaxing households to spend income they might otherwise save. The second is foreign expansion: investment abroad, the export of capital to regions where returns are higher and outlets wider, the economic dimension of what the tradition calls imperialism. Baran and Sweezy treated the foreign outlet as historically mighty but self-exhausting, since the development of the periphery creates new competitors and new centres of surplus generation, while the domination required to keep outlets open generates political resistance that raises its cost. Each of these channels receives extended treatment in the sections that follow, because each became a research programme in its own right within the school.

The state completes the audit, and its role is double. Civilian government spending on infrastructure, education, and welfare absorbs surplus through taxation and borrowing, and the school credited it with real effect; the postwar expansions of public provision across the advanced economies coincided with the long boom the theory had to explain. But civilian spending runs into political limits the book analysed carefully: programmes that compete with private provision, strengthen labour's bargaining position, or compress the margins of the corporate sector meet resistance from the very class whose taxes fund them. Military spending suffers none of these disabilities. It does not compete with private output, it does not strengthen workers, its products are consumed in use or in storage without flooding any market, and its budgets are protected by the politics of national security. The school's conclusion followed with uncomfortable logic: within the menu of state absorbers, the one least constrained is the one devoted to armaments, and a system that needs absorption on an ever-growing scale will drift toward the absorber that works. Later sections return to the military channel and to the financial channel, the absorber the school's successors placed at the centre.

## The inversion table: the two crisis stories side by side

Arguments conducted across a century accumulate misunderstandings, and the most persistent misunderstanding in this territory is the assumption that monopoly capital theory is a variant of the falling rate of profit account, perhaps with larger firms added. The inversion table is this article's instrument for preventing that error. It sets the two positions side by side on the questions where they diverge, so that a reader can see at a glance that the school did not adjust the classical story's parameters but reversed its sign. The classical column summarises the tradition organised around the falling rate of profit, treated fully in its own article; the monopoly column summarises Baran and Sweezy. Rows are questions; the answers are doctrines.

| Question | The classical falling-rate position | The monopoly capital position |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What happens to profitability over time? | The rate of profit tends to fall as capital accumulates and mechanisation raises the ratio of capital to labour | The economic surplus tends to rise as a share of output because costs fall while administered prices hold |
| What is the system's central problem? | Generating enough surplus value relative to the capital advanced | Absorbing a surplus that grows faster than the available outlets |
| Where does crisis originate? | In production, through the squeeze on profitability imposed by rising capital intensity | In realisation, through the shortfall of spending relative to productive capacity |
| What is the normal state of the system? | Expansion punctuated by crises that restore profitability | Stagnation, relieved only by exceptional stimuli or expanding waste absorption |
| What role does the state play? | Auxiliary: it manages conditions for accumulation and responds to crisis | Structural: state spending, above all military spending, is a principal absorber of surplus |
| What follows for policy and expectation? | Crisis disciplines capital and restores the conditions of a new expansion | Reform must address absorption and waste; no automatic recovery mechanism operates |

The table's first row carries the whole dispute in miniature, and it repays slow reading. A falling rate of profit and a rising share of surplus are not two estimates of one quantity; they are claims about different quantities that happen to share a vocabulary of profit. The rate of profit divides profit by the capital stock. The surplus share divides the surplus by output. A world in which both margins widen and capital accumulates sluggishly can deliver a rising surplus share alongside an undistinguished profit rate, which is roughly the world the school claimed to inhabit. Examiners of the theory who begin from the wrong row will refute claims the school never made and miss the ones it did.

Read down the third row and the geography of crisis moves. In the classical story the difficulty gestates in the factory, in the proportions between machinery and labour, and erupts into the market from there. In the monopoly story the factory is almost too successful; the difficulty gestates in the market, in the insufficiency of spending channels, and erupts back into the factory as short time and shutdowns. The movement matters for diagnosis. A physician who locates the disease in production prescribes differently from one who locates it in circulation, and the two schools' divergent attitudes toward demand management, public spending, and the welfare state trace back to this row more than to any difference of political temperament.

The final row states the practical upshot with the neutrality the subject requires: the two stories disagree about whether the system contains a self-correcting mechanism. The classical account, for all its severity, builds recovery into crisis, since bankruptcies devalue capital, unemployment disciplines wages, and profitability is restored for a new cycle. The monopoly account contains no equivalent restorative. Stagnation is not a purge from which the economy emerges leaner; it is a condition the economy remains in until absorption expands, and absorption expands through channels, arms budgets, advertising industries, credit systems, that the theory regards as wasteful or dangerous. That is why the school's tone differs from the classical tradition's almost tragic optimism about crisis. Its pessimism is administrative: the problem is permanent, and only its management varies.

## Stagnation as the normal state

From the rising surplus and the weakness of the outlets, the school's best known conclusion follows: stagnation is the normal state of monopoly capitalism, and growth is what requires explanation. The sentence deliberately reverses the presumption of both orthodox economics and the older Marxist tradition, for which expansion was the baseline and interruption the puzzle. Baran and Sweezy proposed instead that an economy generating surplus faster than it can absorb will operate chronically below capacity, with unemployment a standing feature rather than a malfunction, and that episodes of vigorous growth should be traced to specific countervailing forces: epoch-making innovations that open vast new investment fields, wars and the reconstructions that follow them, population surges, or the expansion of the absorption apparatus itself. The long postwar boom, unfolding as the book was written, was explained on exactly these terms: the automobile's transformation of the continent, suburbanisation, military budgets of unprecedented peacetime size, and the rebuilding of shattered economies abroad supplied, for a generation, the exceptional stimuli the normal state lacks.

The idea of maturity supplied the argument's bridge to an older debate. In the late nineteen thirties the American economist Alvin Hansen had warned that a mature economy, its frontier closed, its population growth slowing, its innovations turning capital-saving, would face secular stagnation: a chronic insufficiency of investment outlets. Hansen's thesis, Keynesian in inspiration, treated the condition as a matter of demography and technology. The monopoly school absorbed the maturity intuition and rebuilt it on structural foundations: what declines as capitalism ages is not investment opportunity in the abstract but the willingness of oligopolistic firms to invest against their own price structures, and what rises is not a shortage but a surplus. Maturity, in the school's vocabulary, names the stage at which the corporate form has generalised enough for administered pricing to govern the core. The economy is mature when its problem has become disposal.

### What did Josef Steindl contribute to the stagnation argument?

Josef Steindl, an Austrian economist whose Maturity and Stagnation in American Capitalism appeared in the early nineteen fifties, supplied the oligopoly mechanism: as industries concentrate, leading firms accumulate internal funds beyond their investment needs while their pricing power discourages expansion, so the economy's saving rises as its outlets narrow, generating stagnation from within the firm's own balance sheet.

Steindl's contribution deserves emphasis because it shows the school's central argument arising independently, from within mainstream-adjacent European economics, before Baran and Sweezy codified it. Working with Kaleckian tools, Steindl asked what happens when the typical firm becomes large enough to finance investment from retained earnings. Such a firm is free of the capital market's discipline, and its accumulation becomes a function of its profit margin rather than of its opportunities. High margins in concentrated industries therefore swell the flow of funds seeking placement at exactly the moment when concentration has thinned the field of attractive placements, since each dominant firm hesitates to expand capacity against its rivals' defended shares. The result is a structural excess of saving over investment inside the corporate sector itself, no failure of animal spirits required. Baran and Sweezy absorbed this mechanism wholesale, acknowledged the debt, and set it within their larger audit of outlets. Later economists in the tradition treated Steindl's book as the school's second founding text, and its rediscovery by historians of stagnation thought has been one of the quieter revivals in twentieth-century economics.

The stagnation conclusion must be stated with precision, because the school's most common misreading begins here. Stagnation in this theory is not permanent recession, not a prophecy that output will fall year after year or that living standards are frozen. It is a tendency claim about utilisation and growth rates: the economy settles into a regime of operation below its capacity, with growth that is positive but anaemic relative to its possibilities, interrupted by booms when exceptional absorbers or stimuli engage. The distinction matters empirically, since a theory predicting permanent depression would have been falsified by any decade of expansion, whereas a theory predicting chronic slack punctuated by stimulus-driven booms claims a subtler pattern that must be argued over trend lines, utilisation averages, and the composition of spending. It matters politically as well. A system in permanent collapse invites one kind of politics; a system that functions, but functions wastefully and far below its potential, invites another, centred on what the wasted potential could have built.

### Does stagnation mean permanent recession in this theory?

No. Stagnation names a tendency for the economy to operate below capacity, with growth slower than its productive potential and unemployment persistently above frictional levels, not an unbroken decline in output. Booms occur when exceptional stimuli or expanded absorption channels engage, and the theory explains those booms by the same apparatus that explains the slack surrounding them.

The exceptional stimuli repay a closer inventory, since the theory stands or falls by whether they are genuinely exceptional. Epoch-making innovations, in the book's usage, are technologies that reorganise the whole economy's investment map: steam power and the railways in the nineteenth century, electrification and the automobile in the twentieth. Such innovations absorb surplus on a heroic scale because they demand not only their own plant but the reconstruction of cities, supply chains, and habits around them. The school's sobering observation was that innovations of this rank are rare by definition, and that an economy cannot schedule them. Wars absorb doubly, by destruction and by the budgets they legitimise, and postwar reconstruction converts the destruction into an investment field. Population growth widens markets steadily but unglamorously. The catalogue amounts to a claim about the baseline: remove war, reconstruction, epochal technology, and demographic surge, and the system's own devices, consumption, investment, salesmanship, are not enough. Growth becomes a gift of circumstance rather than a property of the machine.

Critics have pressed hard on the falsifiability of this construction, and the pressure is legitimate. If every boom is credited to exceptional stimuli and every slump to the normal state, the theory risks explaining everything and predicting nothing; the exceptional becomes a residual category elastic enough to absorb any contrary decade. The school's defenders answer that the stimuli are not residual but specifiable in advance: one can ask, of any period, whether military budgets were swelling, whether a general-purpose technology was in its build-out phase, whether reconstruction or rapid population growth was under way, and the answers are factual, not interpretive. The dispute then becomes empirical and comparative, a matter of whether the growth record correlates with those specifiable conditions better than with rival explanations. That is a fair fight, and later sections of this article take up how it has gone. For the moment the point to hold is that stagnation, as the school framed it, is a claim about the unaided economy, and its defenders insist the unaided economy is a legitimate object of theory even though history rarely leaves it unaided for long.

## Between Keynes and Marx: the school's uneasy neighbours

The school's position between the Keynesian and Marxist traditions explains much of its reception in both. To Keynesians, Baran and Sweezy looked like allies who had supplied the missing sociology: Keynesian theory explained why demand fails, in terms of investment's volatility and the propensity to consume, but treated the failure as a malfunction of an otherwise serviceable machine, correctable by a competent state. The school took the same demand failure and grounded it in the economy's structure of ownership and pricing, concluding that the competent state would be prevented, by the interests it served, from applying the correction in the forms that would work best, and driven instead toward armaments and the subsidising of waste. Joan Robinson, who had done more than anyone to carry Kalecki's ideas into English economics and who knew both camps intimately, occupied the border between them, sympathetic to the school's stagnationism, insistent on the historical and institutional concreteness she found missing in its abstractions, and scathing about the mainstream Keynesians, whose diluted doctrine she named bastard Keynesianism. The border was fertile: the school's analysis of absorption is unthinkable without the Keynesian revolution it criticised, and its founders said so.

Hyman Minsky is the neighbour whose resemblance to the school grew most striking with time, though the two parties arrived by different roads. Minsky, a post-Keynesian economist of finance, argued that capitalist economies pass through stages distinguished by the structure of their financial commitments, and that success itself breeds the fragility that ends it, as prudent hedged finance gives way to speculative and then Ponzi positions during long expansions. His later stage, which he called money manager capitalism, describes an economy dominated by vast institutional funds, preoccupied with asset values, and prone to exactly the alternation of fever and collapse that the monopoly school's finance writers were charting in the same decades from their side of the fence. The two analyses converge on the configuration while differing on the engine: for Minsky the instability is endogenous to finance in any capitalism, requiring no rising surplus to drive it, while for the school the financial expansion is fuelled by the productive economy's unplaceable surplus and would subside if the surplus found productive use. The difference is testable in principle and instructive in practice, and the two traditions' mutual citations in the crisis literature after 2008 constitute one of the few genuine dialogues between Marxist and post-Keynesian economics. A reader mapping the school's intellectual neighbourhood should place Minsky on its border, facing both ways: proof that its central observations could be reached from within the Keynesian inheritance, and a standing challenge to its claim that monopoly, rather than finance itself, is the root.

The neighbourhood also explains the school's peculiar isolation. Orthodox Marxists saw the Keynesian furniture and doubted the house was Marx's; orthodox Keynesians saw the Marxist address and declined to visit. The school's writers cultivated the isolation as evidence of independence, sometimes past the point where independence shades into sectarian comfort, and their journal's pages can read, in the weaker contributions, as a conversation the tradition holds with itself. The stronger contributions break the pattern, and they share a method: an opponent's argument stated well enough to be worth defeating, a magnitude taken to the data, a concession entered where the data require it. That method, rather than any single doctrine, may be the school's most exportable product.

## Maturity and its discontents: the stagnation debate the school inherited

The school did not invent stagnation theory, and its claim to originality should be located precisely: not in noticing that mature capitalism might falter, but in explaining the faltering through monopoly and surplus rather than through demography and the exhaustion of invention. The debate it inherited had run through the nineteen thirties and forties with high stakes and distinguished combatants. Hansen's secular stagnation thesis, delivered as his presidential address to the American Economic Association in 1938, blamed the closing of the frontier, the slowing of population growth, and the capital-saving character of new technology for a permanent deficiency of investment outlets, and it implied that government spending had become a permanent necessity rather than a depression expedient. The thesis had the authority of Keynes's circle behind it and the evidence of a decade of incomplete recovery in front of it. Against it, the economist George Terborgh, in a widely read 1945 volume whose title called economic maturity a bogey, argued that investment opportunities renew themselves endlessly, that new industries arise from new wants, and that the maturity doctrine mistook a catastrophic depression for a law of development. The postwar boom seemed, for a generation, to settle the argument for Terborgh: population surged, suburbs sprawled, and investment ran ahead of every obituary written for it.

The school's intervention in this settled argument was to deny the settlement. The boom, on its account, did not refute the stagnation tendency; it displayed the countervailing forces, and several of those forces, war budgets above all, were visible in plain sight in the national accounts. More subtly, the school relocated the maturity mechanism. For Hansen, maturity was a condition of an economy's age: inventions thinned, frontiers closed. For Steindl and after him Baran and Sweezy, maturity was a condition of an economy's structure: firms grew, industries concentrated, and pricing power spread, so that the deficiency of outlets arose from the success of accumulation itself rather than from any failure of ingenuity or demography. The relocation changed the prognosis. A Hansen economy might be rejuvenated by a new demographic wave or a lucky technology; a Steindl economy reproduces its problem with every increment of concentration, and concentration is what its firms' competition for survival reliably produces. When, decades later, a prominent American economist revived Hansen's very phrase, secular stagnation, to describe the advanced economies' condition after the financial crisis, the school's writers noted the revival with restrained satisfaction: the phenomenon had been renamed by the mainstream twice in a century, each time as discovery, while the structural explanation they had offered between the two discoveries remained, in their view, the one that explained why the condition kept returning.

The Terborgh side of the old debate also survives inside the theory's critical literature, under new management. The modern form of the bogey argument holds that the economy's frontier renews itself through innovation ecosystems, that each platform technology opens investment fields its predecessors could not imagine, and that observed slack reflects policy errors, financial accidents, or measurement failures in an age of intangible output rather than any structural tendency. The school's response repeats its founding move: the question is never whether opportunities can be listed, but whether the corporate structure will fund them at the pace the surplus requires, against the drag of its own defended margins and existing capacity. Lists of possibilities are cheap; the theory is about the filter the possibilities must pass. A fair reader will conclude that the maturity debate never actually ended. It changed vocabularies, from frontiers to markups, and the school is the thread of continuity that connects its phases.

## The sales effort: absorption dressed as competition

Of all the absorption channels, none gave the school a more distinctive public voice than its treatment of the sales effort. The category gathers everything spent on moving goods whose production is not in question: advertising in all its media, the salaries of sales forces and marketing departments, packaging and branding, the cosmetic differentiation of functionally identical products, the annual restyling of durable goods, and the promotional credit apparatus that teaches households to buy ahead of income. Monopoly Capital argued that this apparatus is not a froth on the economy's surface but one of its load-bearing walls. It absorbs surplus directly, employing millions and consuming resources on a scale comparable to major industries, and it absorbs surplus indirectly by defending the realised demand on which the whole pricing structure rests. In an economy that cannot sell what it can make, selling becomes a branch of production.

The analysis had a distinguished ancestor. Thorstein Veblen, writing at the start of the twentieth century, had distinguished the machine process and its engineers, oriented to serviceable output, from the business enterprise oriented to vendibility, and had treated salesmanship as the purest expression of the second: effort directed not at making goods but at making goods marketable, including making rival goods unmarketable. Baran and Sweezy radicalised Veblen's distinction by quantifying its growth and assigning it a macroeconomic function. What for Veblen was a cultural pathology of the price system became, in their hands, a structural necessity of the surplus economy: remove the sales effort and the absorption accounts no longer balance, demand sags toward the level set by wages and replacement, and the stagnation the apparatus masks stands revealed. The advertising industry, on this telling, is not deceiving the system about its health. It is one of the treatments keeping the patient upright.

The school's treatment of product differentiation added a further twist that students of the theory often miss. Under price competition, a firm that restyles a product annually is wasting resources it cannot afford; rivals selling the plainer article cheaper will punish it. Under monopoly conditions, where price is defended and competition migrates to the showroom, restyling becomes rational and even necessary, because a firm that leaves its product unchanged surrenders the only battlefield available. The waste is therefore not a failure of managerial intelligence but an equilibrium of the structure, which is why the book's tone toward the sales effort is analytical rather than merely satirical. Individual advertisers may be cynical or sincere; the system requires their function regardless. Criticism aimed at the morality of persuaders misses the theory's point, which is that persuasion has become a macroeconomic department.

### What is the sales effort, and why does it count as absorption?

The sales effort is the sum of resources devoted to selling what the economy produces: advertising, marketing, packaging, branding, product differentiation, and the promotion of credit. It counts as absorption because it spends surplus through the labour and materials it employs, and defends realised demand indirectly, by sustaining the flow of household spending on which administered prices depend.

The sales effort links the school's economics to its cultural criticism, a connection the journal's wider work developed extensively. Advertising does not merely absorb surplus in the accounting sense; it shapes wants, normalises debt, and wraps the product system in a continuous festival of novelty, and the school's analysts of media and communications, Herbert Schiller prominent among them, treated the commercial media as institutions of the absorption economy: channels funded by the sales effort, carrying the sales effort, and therefore structured in their content by its needs. Readers who want that cultural wing of the argument can follow it in the article on [advertising, consumption and ideology](/2012/06/01/advertising-consumption-ideology/), and the school's broader account of media institutions in the article on [Marxist media theory](/2012/05/15/marxist-media-theory-explained/). The economic point stands alone, however, and should not be confused with the cultural one: even if every advertisement informed rather than persuaded, the spending would still figure in the absorption ledger, because the ledger records expenditure, not manipulation.

Measurement quarrels surround the sales effort as they surround the surplus itself. Advertising outlays are only the visible tip of the category; the salaries of internal marketing staffs, the cost of packaging redesign, the dealer networks and trade promotion budgets, and the finance arms that subsidise consumer credit all belong in a complete accounting, and reasonable accountants draw the boundaries differently. The school was content with orders of magnitude: the sales effort, broadly reckoned, absorbs a share of output large enough to matter for the stagnation accounts, and its growth across the twentieth century tracks the growth of the surplus it helps dispose of. Sceptics reply that much selling expenditure is a zero-sum contest among firms that would cancel out in aggregate demand terms, since one firm's campaign merely steals another's customers. The school's answer distinguishes the microeconomic contest from the macroeconomic function: the campaigns may cancel as market-share weapons while still constituting, in aggregate, a vast expenditure of the surplus on unproductive activity, which is precisely what absorption means. The energy is wasted from the standpoint of useful output; that is the point, not an objection to it.

## The sales effort's instruments: from broadcast to the audience commodity

The sales effort's history within the theory tracks the media system's own transformations, and the school's analysts supplied one of the tradition's most original concepts for its broadcast age. Dallas Smythe, a communications scholar working in the school's orbit, argued that the commercial media's economic product is misdescribed when it is called content. What a broadcaster sells to advertisers is the audience itself: blocks of human attention, assembled by programmes the way a factory assembles a product, and delivered to the sales effort as its raw material. The programmes are the free lunch that draws the crowd into the hall where the selling happens. Smythe's blindspot debate with the cultural studies tradition, over whether audiences are workers in the attention factory or interpreters making their own meanings, became a set piece of media studies; for the absorption theory its importance is simpler. The audience commodity completes the sales effort's circuit: households supply not only the wages that buy the goods and the credit that extends the buying, but the very attention through which the buying is solicited, so that leisure itself is drafted into the disposal of the surplus.

The digital transformation of advertising has been absorbed into the framework with less strain than most of the theory's extensions, because the framework's categories were functional from the start. Targeted advertising, in which the audience is sorted by data into ever finer lots and sold by auction in real time, is the audience commodity with improved machinery: the attention is measured more precisely, priced more exactly, and wasted, from the standpoint of useful output, with undiminished completeness. The school's writers have been careful to mark what genuinely changed: the new instruments lowered the cost of reaching a buyer, which competitive logic would transmit into lower selling costs across the economy, yet the aggregate resources devoted to selling did not shrink as the instruments cheapened, because the contest among firms expanded to fill the cheaper medium, each firm's targeting answered by its rivals' targeting in an arms race whose total expenditure is set by the surplus demanding disposal rather than by the technology of disposal. The pattern is the theory's signature: improve the absorber's efficiency and the system does not bank the saving, it enlarges the absorption. A reader who grasps that sentence grasps why the school's adherents are unimpressed by each new economy of the sales effort, and why they treat the attention industries' growth, across every technological generation, as a datum about the surplus rather than about the media.

## Military spending and the state as absorber

Government spending enters the school's theory not as a policy choice among others but as a structural requirement with a preferred form. The state can absorb surplus through any expenditure, yet the book argued that the composition of state spending is governed by the interests of the class the state serves, and that this governance sorts absorbers into the permitted and the resisted. Public housing competes with private landlords and builders. Public health provision competes with private insurers and providers. Generous unemployment support raises the wage floor beneath every employer. Each of these absorbers therefore encounters organised opposition proportional to its effectiveness, and the history of welfare state expansion, on the school's reading, is a history of that opposition being overcome only partially, in crises or under unusual political pressure. Military spending encounters no such sorting. Weapons do not compete with any private product. Garrisons do not underbid any employer. The armed forces consume steel, electronics, fuel, and labour without returning goods to any market where they might press on prices. As an absorber, armaments are perfect: unlimited in scale, immune to the charge of unfair competition, and wrapped in the legitimating mantle of national defence.

### Why does the theory single out military spending among government outlays?

Military spending is singled out because it absorbs surplus without colliding with the interests that constrain other expenditure. Weapons and forces return no competing goods to civilian markets, do not strengthen labour's bargaining position, know no satiation point, and enjoy the protection of security politics, making armaments the one absorber able to grow to the scale the rising surplus requires.

The argument was tested, in the school's own judgement, by the postwar decades it sought to explain. The United States emerged from the Second World War with military budgets that subsided from wartime peaks yet stabilised at levels no peacetime generation before it would have contemplated, and those budgets, together with the highway programme and suburban construction, underwrote the long boom. When civilian absorbers were proposed at comparable scale, in housing, health, or urban renewal, the proposals emerged from legislatures shrunken and fenced with exclusions. The school read this asymmetry not as a sequence of political accidents but as the system selecting its absorber according to the theory's rule. Arms spending also had a Keynesian productivity its advocates rarely advertised: it employed scientists and engineers, seeded technologies, and regionalised prosperity to districts built around bases and contractors, all without expanding the social wage that might have emboldened workers elsewhere. The absorber that asks nothing of the social order is the absorber the social order prefers.

A rival Marxist account of the same budgets should be registered, because the school debated it internally for years. The theory of the permanent arms economy, associated with Michael Kidron and the International Socialists tradition in Britain, held that arms spending stabilised postwar capitalism not by absorbing surplus demand but by draining surplus value out of the accumulation process, slowing the rise of the economy's capital intensity and thereby checking the fall in the rate of profit. The two accounts agree that military budgets were load-bearing for the long boom and disagree about which beam they carried: absorption of demand on one side, restraint of mechanisation on the other. The dispute is instructive precisely because it cannot be settled by pointing at the budgets themselves, which both sides honour. It turns on the prior question this article keeps returning to, which variable, surplus share or profit rate, organises the system's motion. Kidron's construction, notably, was offered from within the falling-rate framework as a repair of that framework, and its ingenuity is a reminder that the classical tradition did not lack resources for answering the monopoly school. It lacked, in the monopoly school's judgement, candour about how much repair the answer required.

The state's role in the theory extends beyond spending. The same state that absorbs surplus through budgets also guarantees the conditions of the surplus's generation: it enforces the property and contract law beneath administered pricing, rescues the financial system when its absorptions turn toxic, maintains the international order within which the giant corporation operates, and manages, through central banking, the credit cycles the system generates. Baran and Sweezy's account of the state was thinner than their account of the firm, a gap later writers in the tradition worked to fill, and critics have fairly noted that a theory which makes the state structurally indispensable owes a fuller theory of the state than the founding book supplied. That debt, acknowledged within the tradition, belongs on the ledger of the school's unfinished business rather than among refutations of its core claim. A theory of absorption can survive an underdeveloped theory of the absorber's politics, though it cannot indefinitely postpone one.

## The foreign outlet and its limits

Long before the sales effort or the arms budget, the surplus of the advanced economies sought room abroad, and the school inherited the classical Marxist account of imperialism while reshaping it to the absorption frame. Capital exports, railway building across continents, the scramble for colonial markets: in the older literature these figured as escapes from falling profitability or as hunts for cheap raw materials and labour. In the monopoly school's retelling, foreign expansion is one more answer to the disposal question. Surplus generated at home, unable to find domestic placement without undermining prices or wages, is placed where returns are unprotected by oligopolistic restraint and where new markets stand open. Baran's Political Economy of Growth had already applied the surplus category to the periphery, arguing that imperialism's deepest injury to poor countries was not the surplus it extracted, though it extracted plenty, but the surplus it prevented them from using: the potential product of their resources, diverted into luxury imports for local elites, repatriated profits, and the maintenance of archaic land systems whose rents fed consumption rather than industry.

The school's distinctive claim about the foreign outlet was its self-limiting character. Each successful placement of capital abroad develops the recipient: builds its industry, trains its workforce, creates its national bourgeoisie and its nationalist politics. The outlet thus manufactures its own obstacles, in the form of competitors for third markets and of movements determined to retain more of the locally generated surplus at home. Meanwhile the metropolitan corporations grow to a scale where foreign earnings, though vast, remain a fraction of what the domestic surplus requires them to place. Foreign investment can supplement domestic absorption; it cannot substitute for it, because the surplus grows with the whole corporate economy while the hospitable periphery shrinks as it develops. The school's analysts consequently expected the metropolitan economies to rely ever more heavily on the two absorbers that do not develop their hosts into rivals: the sales effort, which colonises the domestic consumer's imagination, and the state, which can spend without creating competitors. The trajectory of the later twentieth century, with its swelling advertising industries and its permanent military establishments, was read within the tradition as that expectation working itself out.

Empire also furnished the theory with a feedback mechanism that purely domestic accounts lack. The defence of foreign placements requires force, and force requires budgets, and budgets absorb surplus, so that the foreign outlet and the military absorber reinforce one another in a single circuit. A corporation's mines abroad justify a fleet; the fleet's budget absorbs the surplus the mines remit; the remittances swell the surplus the fleet must help absorb. Baran and Sweezy did not invent the observation that armaments and empire travel together, but they gave it a precise macroeconomic articulation: both are solutions to the same disposal problem, each generating demand for the other. Readers assessing the theory should note how much of its explanatory reach comes from such couplings, where an institution explained in one chapter reappears as a condition in another. The system's parts, in this account, are not a list but a web, and the web's strands are all spun from the one inversion.

## Finance: the absorber the founding book understated

Every long-lived theory has a chapter its authors would rewrite, and in Monopoly Capital that chapter concerns finance. The founding book treated the financial sector mainly as a recipient of surplus and a source of useful employment for it, a relatively minor drain beside the sales effort and the state. What followed in the advanced economies from the nineteen seventies onward was a financial expansion of a scale the book had not anticipated: the multiplication of credit instruments, the growth of trading in assets old and new, the rise of institutional funds commanding portfolios larger than national outputs, and the colonisation of household life by debt. The school's response to this development, worked out in the pages of Monthly Review over three decades and above all by Sweezy together with Harry Magdoff, is among the most creditable episodes in its history, because the response neither denied the novelty nor abandoned the framework. It asked what finance does in an economy defined by the disposal problem, and found that finance is absorption by other means.

The analysis runs as follows. A stagnating productive economy throws off surplus it cannot place in plant and equipment. The surplus seeks returns wherever they can be found, and financial markets exist to manufacture claims that promise them: securities, properties, currencies, and eventually claims on claims, layered into structures of stupefying complexity. The financial sector absorbs the surplus in several senses at once. Its fees, salaries, and profits consume a growing share of the surplus directly. Its credit creation sustains the household consumption on which realised demand depends, allowing wages that stagnate to support spending that does not. Its asset inflations generate paper wealth whose owners spend as if the wealth were real, converting unrealised claims into actual demand. And its booms puncture the stagnation for years at a time, supplying exactly the exceptional stimulus the normal state lacks, until the claims outrun any plausible stream of income and the structure collapses back into the stagnation it had papered over. Finance, on this account, is not a distraction from the real economy's failure to absorb. It is the form absorption takes when every other channel has been developed and found insufficient.

### How does financial expansion absorb surplus, and what price does it exact?

Financial expansion absorbs surplus by employing it in intermediation, by sustaining household spending through credit, and by inflating asset values whose owners spend against paper wealth. The price is instability: the claims finance creates must be validated by income from production, and as the productive base stagnates beneath a superstructure of claims, crises of validation become larger and frequent.

Sweezy gave the development its canonical statement late in his life, arguing that the stagnation of the productive economy and the explosion of finance were not rival tendencies but two faces of one process, since the surplus denied productive placement was the fuel of the financial expansion itself. Magdoff, who had watched the process from inside government and banking before joining Monthly Review, supplied the institutional detail: the growth of the great money-centre banks, the internationalisation of credit, the transformation of the corporation's own treasury operations into profit centres rivalling its factories. Their joint essays, gathered in the nineteen eighties under the title Stagnation and the Financial Explosion, stand to the founding book as a second storey stands to a first: the architecture is extended, the foundations are the same. The surplus still rises; the outlets still lag; only the outlet under examination had grown from a footnote into a tower.

The household side of the circuit deserves its own paragraph, because it quietly revised the school's picture of consumption. Baran and Sweezy had treated workers' consumption as bounded by wages, and the wage relation as the economy's demand floor. Financialisation loosened that bound: households in the advanced economies, above all in the United States, sustained and expanded consumption across decades in which median wages stagnated, by borrowing against houses, by revolving credit, by the conversion of future income into present spending. From the absorption standpoint this was a genuine innovation, a new channel of the first importance, since every borrowed dollar realised surplus that wages alone would have left unrealised. From the standpoint of stability it was a deferral, since the debts remained claims on the very wage incomes whose stagnation had made the borrowing necessary. The school's writers insisted on holding both truths together: consumer credit is absorption, and absorption purchased with claims on stagnant incomes is absorption with a fuse attached. The financial crisis of 2008, arriving after Sweezy's death but along lines the tradition had traced for decades, was received within the school less as a surprise than as a melancholy confirmation, and its aftermath, in which the stagnation the boom had masked resumed across the advanced world, fitted the framework's expectation that the underlying condition survives the bursting of its temporary remedy.

## Consumer credit: the household drafted into absorption

One absorber grew so gradually that the founding book registered it without grasping its future scale: the indebtedness of households. Instalment credit had entered American life with the automobile age, when manufacturers discovered that a car priced beyond a year's wages could be sold to a worker paid by the week if the payments were sliced thin enough, and the finance arms of the great manufacturers were built to do the slicing. Monopoly Capital noticed the phenomenon in its treatment of the sales effort, since credit promotion is selling by other means, and it recorded the steady growth of consumer debt as an absorption device: spending validated in the present against income expected in the future realises surplus that present wages would have left unrealised. What the book could not foresee was the device's generalisation, as credit escaped the durable-goods showroom and became a universal supplement to wages, carried on cards, secured on houses, and extended to households whose incomes had ceased to grow. The later writers of the school, studying that generalisation, treated it as one of the hinges of the monopoly-finance era: the household, in the mature doctrine, is no longer merely the source of labour and the terminus of consumption, but a balance sheet pressed into service as an absorber of the corporate surplus.

The mechanism deserves a precise statement, because it is easily confused with ordinary borrowing. In a growing economy with rising incomes, household debt is a bridge that rising income later redeems; the borrowing anticipates the borrower's own future. In the configuration the school describes, the bridge is built over a gap that does not close: median wages stagnate while the consumption required to realise the surplus keeps rising, so the debt must be rolled, enlarged, and serviced out of the same stagnant income whose deficiency made it necessary. Absorption is achieved, genuinely, in each period; the claims accumulate, genuinely, across periods. The school compared the arrangement, in its journal's essays, to the position of a firm that borrows to pay its dividends: the accounts balance each year and the structure rots across the years. The comparison is polemical but the arithmetic is not. A system that requires household spending to grow faster than household income, indefinitely, has built a contradiction into its absorption accounts, and the financial crisis that began in American housing in 2007 was, in the school's reading, that contradiction arriving at its term: the moment when the claims on stagnant incomes could no longer be refinanced and the absorber itself became the detonator.

Notice how the episode completes the absorption audit the founding book began. Each channel on the original list was found to carry a self-limiting flaw: investment enlarges capacity, foreign placement develops rivals, civilian state spending provokes resistance. Household credit's flaw is symmetrical with theirs: it borrows demand from a future whose income the system itself refuses to raise. The mature theory's pessimism is thus not a mood but a pattern, the repeated discovery that every absorber purchases time at the price of a larger eventual shortfall. Critics of the school are entitled to ask whether a flaw can always be found, in retrospect, for whatever channel expanded, and the falsifiability section of this article takes that charge seriously. The school's rejoinder is that the flaws were named in advance, in print, channel by channel, and that the household credit flaw in particular was being described in the tradition's journal while the arrangement it described was still being celebrated elsewhere as the democratisation of finance.

## Monopoly finance capital: the school's second synthesis

Out of the finance work came the concept under which the school's mature doctrine now travels: monopoly-finance capital. The term, developed in Monthly Review in the early twenty first century and associated above all with John Bellamy Foster, names a stage in which the two great absorptive facts of the system, corporate monopoly and financial expansion, are no longer separable. The giant corporation is itself financialised, holding portfolios, running captive credit operations, and managing its share price as a primary objective; the financial institution is itself monopolised, concentrated into firms whose failure the state cannot permit. Each sector supplies what the other lacks. Production generates the surplus and the stagnation; finance generates the demand and the instability; the hybrid system lurches between the long slack of its productive base and the fever of its asset markets, with the state standing behind both as absorber and rescuer of last resort.

Foster's route to the synthesis ran through a systematic rereading of the founding texts. His The Theory of Monopoly Capitalism, published in 1986, had already reworked Baran and Sweezy's doctrine into a comprehensive statement, tightening its logic and answering its early critics, and his editorship of Monthly Review from 2000 placed him to conduct the tradition's engagement with financialisation as a research programme rather than a commentary. The monopoly-finance formulation also reconnected the school to an older lineage it had always honoured: Rudolf Hilferding's Finance Capital of 1910, which had analysed the fusion of bank and industrial capital in the age of cartels, and Lenin's theory of imperialism built partly upon it. Where Hilferding's finance capital described banks dominating industry, the new concept describes a symbiosis in which the distinction between the industrial and the financial has itself blurred, and in which the system's surplus sloshes between the two poles of the hybrid according to where returns momentarily beckon.

Three commitments distinguish the mature doctrine from a mere relabelling. First, stagnation is treated as deepening rather than static, because the share of output the surplus claims has continued to grow while the classic absorbers approach their limits: military budgets are politically capped as shares of output in peacetime, the sales effort's growth cannot indefinitely outrun the economy it serves, and the periphery's hospitality to foreign placement is exhausted. Second, financial expansion is treated as necessary rather than accidental, the system's own discovery of the one absorber capable of growing faster than the surplus, with instability as its inescapable shadow. Third, the whole is treated as a phase of the same capitalism the founding book analysed, not a new mode of production: the categories of 1966, surplus, absorption, administered price, giant corporation, are held sufficient to comprehend the hybrid, which is why the tradition speaks of a stage within monopoly capitalism rather than of a successor system. Whether those commitments cohere, and whether the evidence sustains them, is the business of the assessment sections below. What can be said without controversy is that the school accomplished something rare among theoretical traditions: it survived its founder generation, revised its weakest chapter in public, and produced a second synthesis that its critics must engage rather than a catechism they can ignore.

## The view from the periphery: the school's global career and its hardest case

A theory born half in the study of underdevelopment was bound to have a global career, and the surplus category's travels through the developing world form a chapter of the school's history too important to leave as a footnote to its domestic doctrine. Baran's founding insight about the periphery was an inversion of its own, parallel to the one this article has traced at home: the poor countries' problem, he argued, was not that they generated too little surplus, for even desperately poor economies produce more than bare subsistence consumes, but that their surplus was systematically misused or removed: consumed in luxury by landed elites whose rents depended on agricultural backwardness, repatriated as profit by foreign firms, absorbed by bloated military and bureaucratic establishments, or frozen in speculation and hoarding. The potential surplus of the periphery, the margin by which its output could exceed its needs if its resources were rationally organised, was in Baran's estimates startlingly large, which converted underdevelopment from a condition of scarcity into a condition of blockage. Colonialism and its successor arrangements stood accused not of taking what the periphery lacked but of preventing the periphery from using what it had.

The generation of Latin American and other scholars who built dependency theory on this foundation generalised the claim into a law of the world system: development at the centre and underdevelopment at the periphery are products of one process, linked by the flows of surplus along the chains that bind satellite to metropolis. Harry Magdoff's studies of imperialism, from his essays of the nineteen sixties to his historical survey of the age of imperialism, gave the school's version its most careful form, attentive to the changing mechanics of domination from colonial possession through corporate investment to financial discipline. For a period the doctrine shaped policy as well as scholarship: import-substituting industrialisation, resource nationalism, and the demands for a new international economic order all drew on the surplus-drain analysis, and the school could claim, as few theoretical traditions can, to have been a premise in the deliberations of governments.

The hardest case arrived from East Asia, and intellectual honesty requires stating it as the school's standing difficulty abroad, matching the value dispute at home. Several economies of that region, beginning from poverty and subordination fully as deep as any the dependency analysis described, industrialised with extraordinary speed inside the very world economy whose structures were supposed to block such ascent, exporting through the metropolitan markets and disciplining their own elites into productive investment. If surplus drain and metropolitan domination made development structurally impossible, the tigers of East Asia should not exist; they do. The school's responses have been several and not fully harmonious: that the ascents occurred under exceptional geopolitical shelter, with the hegemon tolerating protection and land reform in frontline states for strategic reasons; that the successful cases confirm rather than refute the analysis, since their states did precisely what Baran prescribed, seizing the surplus from unproductive uses and directing it to industry; and that the category of blockage was always about the use of surplus rather than about a mechanical bar on growth, so that a state strong enough to revolutionise the use escapes the sentence. Each response has force, and the third in particular recovers the founder's actual argument from its vulgarisation. But the episode permanently complicates the school's global doctrine, and its best writers have treated the complication as instruction: the surplus framework illuminates the politics of development, what is produced, who captures it, what it is spent on, better than it predicts development's outcomes, and a theory of questions may be a lesser thing than a theory of laws while still being a considerable one.

## The convergence: what mainstream research found on its own

The strangest turn in the school's fortunes arrived from a direction no one in the tradition had predicted: the centre of the economics profession. Across the later twenty tens and into the following decade, a substantial body of mainstream research documented, with the profession's own data and methods, the two phenomena at the heart of the monopoly capital thesis: rising market concentration across advanced economies, and rising price-cost markups, the margins firms charge over the cost of producing an additional unit. The researchers involved owed the Monthly Review school nothing; many had never read it. They worked from firm-level accounts, production data, and antitrust case records, and their motives ranged from concern about innovation to concern about inequality. Yet the picture their findings compose, of economies in which a shrinking number of firms command widening margins over cost while investment lags behind profitability, is recognisably the landscape Baran and Sweezy described in 1966. The convergence is the strongest evidence the school has ever possessed, and this article will state it as convergence, carefully, because the difference between convergence and vindication is precisely where the interesting questions lie.

The markup literature is the convergence's centrepiece. Jan De Loecker and Jan Eeckhout, in work that became the reference point for the whole discussion, estimated markups for American publicly traded firms across the postwar era and reported a long period of rough stability followed by a sustained rise beginning around 1980, concentrated in the upper reaches of the distribution: it was the highest-markup firms whose margins widened most, pulling the average up while the median firm changed far less. Companion work extended the finding internationally and asked what followed from it: declining shares of income flowing to labour, weaker incentives to invest despite fat margins, and a reallocation of activity toward the firms with the greatest pricing power. Simha Barkai, approaching the accounts from the national income side, reported that the shares of output paid to labour and to the owners of capital equipment both declined across the same decades, leaving a rising residue of pure profit, income attributable neither to workers nor to the cost of the capital stock: a category with an unmistakable family resemblance to a rising surplus. Studies by the International Monetary Fund and the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, institutions not given to Marxist vocabulary, reported rising markups and concentration across many advanced economies and connected them to sluggish investment and wage growth. Thomas Philippon's The Great Reversal, published in 2019, argued to a general readership that American markets had become less competitive than European ones across sector after sector, with prices to match. Each of these contributions must be described as its authors framed it, and none was framed as a test of Baran and Sweezy. Their authors were measuring the economy they had. The school simply notes that the economy they measured is the one its founders described.

### What do rising markups actually measure?

A markup measures the ratio of a firm's price to its marginal cost, the cost of producing one more unit. A rising markup records widening distance between what buyers pay and what output costs to make, a distance that may reflect growing market power, rising overhead costs spread over volumes, or the weight of firms whose products command premium prices.

The rival interpretation of the same data was developed with equal distinction, and honesty requires its full statement. David Autor and co-authors, studying the rise of what they called superstar firms, proposed that concentration has risen because the most productive firms, often those best exploiting new information technologies, have won market share from less productive rivals. On this reading, high margins reward efficiency and innovation rather than entrenched power; the successful firm's markup is the prize in a contest that remains open, and its low labour share reflects the scalability of its technology rather than the suppression of wages. The interpretation changes the moral and the policy conclusion entirely: concentration as the footprint of competition working well, not of competition failing. Subsequent debate has not crowned either reading. Both camps work from the same firm-level data; they differ in what they take margins to signify, and in the weight they give to evidence about entry, persistence of profits, and the behaviour of prices when costs change. The monopoly school follows this debate as an interested party, not as a judge, and its members have been careful, in their better moments, to acknowledge that a markup is a measurement of distance between price and cost, not a photograph of power. What the school claims is narrower and stronger than a photograph: that whatever the microeconomic story firm by firm, the macroeconomic configuration the mainstream literature has assembled, wide margins, weak investment relative to profits, declining labour share, chronic demand shortfall, is the configuration its theory leads one to expect, and no rival macroeconomic framework predicted the configuration in advance.

Three qualifications keep the convergence honest. First, the mainstream findings concern markups and concentration, not the surplus as Baran defined it: the surplus includes rents, interest, and unproductive expenditure alongside profit, so the literatures overlap without coinciding, and neither can be substituted for the other in a strict test. Second, the mainstream time frame is dominated by the decades after 1980, whereas the school claims a tendency operating across the whole monopoly era; the convergence therefore testifies to a period, not yet to the full sweep of the law. Third, the mainstream explanations remain contested among themselves, and the school's interpretation is one contestant among several, distinguished by seniority rather than by exemption from proof. With those qualifications recorded, the fact remains remarkable. A heterodox tradition, marginal to the profession for half a century, announced that the core tendency of advanced capitalism was the widening of margins over cost and the consequent failure of demand, and the profession's own instruments, turned on the economy two generations later, registered widening margins and deficient demand. Traditions are not often granted such moments, and the school's literature has made the most of this one, in the main responsibly.

For students of the episode, the convergence also raises a question about the sociology of knowledge that the school itself has not been shy about posing. Why did the description of administered prices and rising margins, available in print since 1966 and in Kalecki's work since the nineteen thirties, wait half a century for mainstream confirmation? The tradition's answer points to the profession's theoretical commitments: a framework that treats competition as the economy's default state will process evidence of its absence slowly, reclassifying each observation as friction, transition, or measurement error, until the accumulation becomes impossible to file. Sceptics of the school will answer differently, that the early statements lacked the data and estimation technique the later literature commands, and that confirmation awaited method rather than courage. Both answers contain truth, and the reader weighing the school should hold them together: the founders saw the landscape early and described it in the vocabulary they had; the mainstream mapped it late and measured it in a vocabulary of its own; the landscape, on the evidence of the maps, resembles the early description more than the intervening orthodoxy allowed.

## Exploitation, class, and what the school kept from Marx

A school accused of abandoning Marx invites the question of what, in its own understanding, it retained, and the answer begins with exploitation. Baran and Sweezy never denied that the surplus originates in production or that its magnitude reflects the relation between the working class and the owners of capital. Their framework treats wages as the cost line against which the surplus is defined, and the pressure to widen the surplus operates in their account exactly where Marx located it: in the length and intensity of the working day, the pace of the line, the resistance and organisation of workers, the reserve of unemployed labour that disciplines wage demands. The surplus, one might say, is surplus value viewed from the spending side: the same social excess, followed at the point where it must find employment rather than at the point where it is pumped out. The school's focus on disposal never required indifference to extraction, and its journal's coverage of labour struggles across the decades is evidence that the indifference was not felt in practice.

Class analysis persists in the theory at a second level, in the account of who decides absorption. The channels through which surplus is spent are not chosen by an impersonal market; they are chosen, blocked, or subsidised by identifiable groups with identifiable interests. Executives decide the scale of the sales effort. Legislators responsive to donors decide the composition of budgets. Creditors and central bankers decide the terms on which household borrowing can expand. The theory's political sociology, sketched in Monopoly Capital and elaborated by the journal's contributors, portrays a ruling class that is not conspiring but selecting: from the menu of possible absorbers it funds those compatible with its position and starves those that threaten it, and the pattern of selection explains why the system prefers armaments to housing and advertising to leisure. A shorter working week, after all, would absorb surplus in the most direct way imaginable, by converting potential output into free time; the school noted that this absorber, alone among the candidates, is never seriously proposed by the system's managers, because it strengthens the class that sells labour and shrinks the domain of the class that buys it. The observation is among the book's most quoted in spirit, and it locates the theory's Marxism precisely: the constraint on absorption is not technical but social, a matter of whose interests the spending must spare.

The school's account of the working class under monopoly conditions also departed from inherited expectations in a way that deserves record. Where an older tradition expected immiseration to radicalise, Baran and Sweezy described a working class integrated, unevenly and insecurely, into the absorption economy itself: employed by it in the sales and service sectors, indebted to it through consumer credit, addressed by it as consumers more effectively than as producers. The famous difficulties of organising under such conditions are not the subject of this article, but the analytical point belongs to the theory's core: a system that must absorb surplus through household spending has reasons to cultivate the household as spender even while it squeezes the household as earner, and the tension between those two treatments is one of the places the school expects the system's contradictions to be felt. The later financialisation literature within the tradition simply followed that tension to its conclusion, as the spender role came to be financed by debt against the earner's stagnating wage.

Internationally, the school's class analysis produced its most influential offshoot in theories of underdevelopment. Baran's argument that imperialism blocks the productive use of the periphery's surplus was extended by Andre Gunder Frank and others into the claim that development and underdevelopment are two products of a single process, the metropolis developing by organising the satellite's economy around extraction. Dependency theory, as that offshoot became known across Latin America and beyond, carried the surplus category into regions and debates its inventor had only sketched, and its policy echoes, in import substitution and resource nationalism, shaped governments. The offshoot's fate also illustrates the category's portability and its risks: travelled far from the pricing theory that anchored it at home, the surplus became for some users a moral label for any income they disapproved of, and the discipline of the original construction, output minus the costs of producing it, was lost. The school's custodians have generally treated the dependency inheritance with pride tempered by this reservation, an attitude readers will meet again in the assessment below: the category travels, and travelling costs precision.

## Monopoly capital and innovation: the Schumpeterian challenge

No account of the school is complete without the rival it spent its life arguing against in the theory of the firm itself: Joseph Schumpeter's defence of monopoly as the engine of progress. Schumpeter, writing in the nineteen forties, had argued that the large firm with market power is not capitalism's pathology but its instrument of development: monopoly profits fund the research laboratories, bear the risks of innovation, and reward the entrepreneur who shatters the existing structure in the gale of creative destruction. On this account the margin that the monopoly school treats as the source of stagnation is the prize and the provision of technical progress, and the restless competition that matters occurs not within markets but between successive waves of innovation, each wave's monopolist destined to be dethroned by the next. The defence is formidable because it explains the same facts, giant firms, fat margins, planned research, by an opposite mechanism with an opposite prognosis: dynamism, not stagnation, as the large firm's signature.

Monopoly Capital's answer conceded the facts and disputed the prognosis, on grounds that repay attention because they mark the school's analytical maturity. The founding book granted that the giant corporation had become the economy's great organised site of technical change, maintaining laboratories and engineering staffs on a scale no competitive firm could match. What it denied was that the direction and pace of that change are governed by technical opportunity. A corporation, the authors argued, develops the innovations that extend its position and suppresses or shelves those that threaten its installed capital; it times introductions to the exhaustion of existing product lines rather than to the readiness of their successors; and it directs its inventive energies disproportionately toward the arts of selling, packaging, and differentiation, where returns are quick and structures safe, rather than toward the arts of production, whose epoch-making variants would render its own plant obsolete. Innovation under monopoly is thus real but rationed, administered like price, and for the same reason: the firm's first obligation is the defence of the margin, and unadministered progress is as dangerous to that margin as unadministered pricing. The patent drawer, in the school's recurring image, holds inventions whose crime was punctuality: they arrived before the capital they would replace had been fully sweated.

The debate between the two accounts is not closed, and the modern evidence speaks in both accents. The superstar-firm literature examined earlier in this article can be read as Schumpeter's posthumous brief, since it attributes concentration to the victory of the productive and treats their margins as innovation's reward; the school's rejoinder asks why, if margins reward innovation, the decades of widening margins have coincided with complaints of slowing productivity growth across the advanced economies, and why the most profitable firms return their earnings to shareholders through buybacks and dividends on a scale that dwarfs their research budgets: behaviour the theory of the surplus predicts precisely, since a firm that cannot place its funds productively must place them financially or return them to owners who will. The buyback, in the school's accounting, is absorption failure wearing a suit: surplus confessing, in the most public way a corporation can confess, that it has run out of uses for itself. Readers weighing Schumpeter against Sweezy should notice that the two theories divide the evidence rather than disputing it: the laboratories are real, and so are the buybacks; the question is which fact governs the system's motion, and the question remains, after three quarters of a century, genuinely open.

## Counter-tendencies: where the school itself hedged

A law stated as a tendency lives or dies by the honesty of its treatment of counter-tendencies, and Monopoly Capital was more candid on this front than its reputation suggests. The book itself catalogues forces that press the surplus down or enlarge absorption beyond expectation: the rise of new industries whose building-out devours investment for a generation; the growth of the state's civilian functions under democratic pressure; the expansion of foreign trade and investment during periods of rapid integration; increases in wages won by organised labour, which compress margins directly and expand the consumption fund that realises output. The school's claim was never that these forces are impotent. It was that they are episodic, externally driven, or self-limiting, while the surplus's rise is continuous and internally driven by the pricing structure itself. A tendency, in this usage, is the resultant that reasserts itself when the episodic forces pause, and stagnation is the name for the intervals. Critics who refute the theory by exhibiting a boom have therefore to show more than the boom: they must show that the boom's engines were internal to the private economy's ordinary working, rather than the war budgets, reconstruction demands, or credit expansions the theory names in advance.

The relation to Marx's own catalogue of counteracting influences is instructive, because the two lists are constructed in mirror image. Marx, stating the falling tendency of the profit rate, enumerated the forces that check it: more intense exploitation, depression of wages below their value, cheapening of the elements of constant capital, relative overpopulation, foreign trade, the increase of stock capital. The monopoly school, stating the rising tendency of the surplus, enumerated the forces that check absorption failure: innovation, war, population, state spending, the sales effort, finance. Each list is the other's problem set. Where Marx's counter-tendencies restore profitability and thereby postpone crisis, Baran and Sweezy's counter-forces restore spending and thereby postpone stagnation. The symmetry reveals the depth of the inversion better than any summary: the two traditions agree that capitalism is a system of tendencies and counter-tendencies whose resultant is historical, not mechanical; they disagree about which tendency sits in the driver's seat. Readers who grasp the symmetry will also grasp why debates between the schools so often pass without contact: each side's evidence of its counter-tendencies working is, to the other side, evidence of the underlying tendency needing rescue.

Wage pressure occupies a special place in the school's hedging, because it connects the theory to the rival profit-squeeze account of the nineteen seventies slump. When the advanced economies stumbled after the long boom, one influential Marxist explanation held that full employment had strengthened labour until wages squeezed profits and accumulation faltered: too little surplus, on that account, not too much. The monopoly school replied that the squeeze, where it occurred, was a conjunctural compression within a structure whose secular tendency ran the other way, and that the sequel vindicated the distinction: the decades after the squeeze were resolved politically, through the weakening of organised labour, were followed not by an investment renaissance but by exactly the pattern the theory predicts, fat margins, feeble capital formation, and recourse to finance. The exchange is recorded here without a verdict, because the data of those decades remain contested terrain between the schools. What the episode shows is the absorption inversion doing its characteristic work: confronted with a crisis narrated as scarcity of profit, the school retells it as a brief interruption in an age of surplus, and asks its opponents to explain the sequel, the long era in which profitability recovered and investment did not.

## How a slump unfolds in the absorption frame

Stagnation is the school's name for the system's weather; a slump is its storm, and the theory's mechanics of the storm deserve statement, since a doctrine of chronic slack must still explain acute collapse. In the absorption frame, a slump begins when one of the major spending channels falters: investment plans are cut as firms confront their excess capacity, or a credit expansion exhausts itself and household spending buckles under debt service, or a financial inflation bursts and destroys the paper wealth against which spending had been planned. The initial shortfall then propagates through the economy's defining feature, its administered prices. Because prices in the core do not fall to clear markets, the adjustment falls on quantities: orders are cancelled, shifts are shortened, lines are idled, and workers are dismissed. Each dismissal subtracts wage income from the spending stream, deepening the shortfall that caused it, in the multiplier sequence Keynes described; but where the Keynesian sequence operates in an economy that will reflate when spending returns, the school's sequence operates in an economy whose surplus has kept rising through the contraction, as surviving firms cut costs into the slump and emerge with wider margins and still less reason to invest. The slump therefore does not purge the condition that produced it. It intensifies the condition, leaving the economy with more surplus relative to its outlets than it had before the storm, which is why recoveries in this framework are credited to outside rescue, state spending or new credit cycles, rather than to any self-healing of the private economy.

The contrast with the classical crisis mechanics is the sharpest practical test between the schools, and readers should hold it clearly. In the falling-rate account, crisis is violent medicine: capital values are destroyed, debts defaulted, wages driven down, and the rate of profit restored on the shrunken capital base, so that expansion resumes from the wreckage by the system's own logic. In the absorption account, the wreckage restores nothing, because the difficulty was never an excess of capital relative to profit but an excess of surplus relative to spending, and destruction of capital leaves the spending problem untouched or worse. Hence the absorption school's characteristic expectation, which the decades since the financial crisis have made familiar far beyond Marxist circles: that a great slump, met without sufficient state absorption, settles not into recovery but into a long twilight of slack demand, idle capacity, and swollen corporate cash, the normal state resumed at a lower level of activity. The expectation was heterodox when the school formed it. Its partial migration into the vocabulary of central bankers and finance ministries, under names like secular stagnation and the savings glut, is among the more ironic tributes the tradition has received, and the school's writers received it in the spirit in which it was offered: as evidence that the condition they named had become impossible for anyone, of any doctrine, to leave unnamed.

## The strongest objections, stated at full strength

No assessment of the school is respectable that does not let its opponents speak in their strongest voices, and the objections cluster in four directions: empirical, theoretical, methodological, and historical. This section states each as its ablest proponents would wish it stated. Replies follow in the school's own terms, with concessions marked where the school itself has made them.

The empirical objection attacks the rising surplus at its measuring rod. The surplus, the critic observes, is not a magnitude the national accounts record; it must be constructed, and its construction involves the analyst's judgement about which expenditures count as costs of production and which as surplus uses. Classify advertising as a cost and the surplus shrinks; classify all government as absorption and it swells; the trend the theory announces can be manufactured by the classification's drift. Attempts to measure the American surplus across the twentieth century, including sympathetic ones from within the tradition, have produced estimates sensitive enough to their assumptions that sceptics conclude the law of rising surplus is an artefact seeking data. The profit rate, for all its own construction problems, at least answers to a concept anchored in firms' accounts. A theory whose central magnitude cannot be pinned down independently of the theorist's choices, the objection concludes, has traded rigour for convenience, and its apparent empirical content is an illusion produced by definitional slack.

The theoretical objection, pressed hardest by other Marxists, targets the abandonment of value. In Marx's construction, the rate of profit and its tendency are not arbitrary choices of variable; they follow from the analysis of the commodity and the working day, and they connect crisis to exploitation by an unbroken chain: the same process that extracts surplus value mechanises production and squeezes its own rate of return. Replace surplus value with an accounting aggregate of output over cost, and the chain snaps. The resulting theory can describe the economy's surface, its margins and expenditures, but it cannot explain why the surface moves as it does in terms of the production relations underneath; it becomes, in the critics' phrase, a Keynesianism with Marxist vocabulary, its stagnation a cousin of the neoclassical Keynesians' demand deficiency, its politics a politics of spending composition rather than of ownership. Ernest Mandel's Late Capitalism, the most systematic Marxist counter-construction of the era, defended the falling rate framework and treated the monopoly school's stagnationism as a failure to grasp the long waves through which accumulation renews itself; Paul Mattick, from a sterner council-communist standpoint, had criticised the school's Keynesian affinities from its early days. The objection's sting is that it is not external: it grants the school's facts and disputes its pedigree, asking whether what remains, once value theory is surrendered, deserves the name of Marxism at all.

The methodological objection concerns falsifiability and has been trailed earlier in this article. Stagnation, the critic notes, is defined as the normal state, yet booms are accommodated through exceptional stimuli whose catalogue is open-ended: technology, war, reconstruction, population, credit, and, when finance arrived, financial explosion, a sequence that invites the suspicion that any expansion whatever will be filed as exceptional and any contraction as normal. A theory that can accommodate every outcome predicts none, and its central law, being a tendency whose counter-tendencies include the main events of economic history, risks the same vacuity. The historical objection completes the set: the theory was framed amid the greatest sustained boom in capitalist history, explained the boom away as exceptional, and then claimed vindication from the slower growth that followed, a sequence the critic reads as the theory's elasticity rather than its foresight, since a doctrine flexible enough to explain the boom as exception and the stagnation as rule has foreclosed nothing.

The school's replies, gathered across decades, run as follows. On measurement, defenders concede the surplus is constructed, deny that construction is arbitrariness, and point to the markup and profit-share literatures as independent evidence that the core magnitude, the margin of price over cost across the economy, has moved as the theory requires; the surplus's precise borders are negotiable, its direction is not. On value, the reply is the one Baran prepared: value categories presuppose the price formation of competitive capitalism, and a theory faithful to Marx's method must rebuild its magnitudes when the economy's structure changes, as Marx himself rebuilt the categories of the classical economists; the chain from exploitation to crisis is not snapped but rerouted, through distribution and spending, and the rerouting is an argument, not an evasion. On falsifiability, the school insists its stimuli are specifiable and its claims comparative: the theory forbids, for example, a long era of vigorous private investment and full employment under conditions of stagnant military budgets, quiescent credit, and no epochal technology, and history has not produced one. On the historical objection, the reply is chronological pride: the stagnation thesis was stated in 1952 by Steindl and in 1966 by Baran and Sweezy, before the long boom ended, and the theory's custodians ask to be judged as forecasters of the post-boom era rather than as rationalisers of it. The reader is entitled to find these replies uneven. The measurement reply borrows strength from a literature whose categories differ from the school's own. The value reply asserts a licence, rebuild your magnitudes, that the tradition's other branches decline to grant so broadly. The falsifiability reply depends on what counts as a fair specification of the forbidden outcome. But the replies are arguments, addressed to the objections as stated, and the debate they form is among the most instructive in modern Marxist economics precisely because neither side is foolish.

## The defence of bigness: the answer from Chicago

The school's external critics include a tradition that defends the giant firm on grounds exactly opposite to Schumpeter's romance of innovation: the Chicago school's cool insistence that monopoly is mostly a mirage of measurement and a memory of temporary advantage. Harold Demsetz fired the foundational shot in the early nineteen seventies, observing that the correlation between industry concentration and profitability, the correlation on which a generation of structure-based reasoning rested, is equally consistent with the superior firm's efficiency: industries concentrate because their best firms grow, and their best firms earn more because they are better, so that the association testifies to competition's success rather than its failure. George Stigler's work on the history of antitrust added the institutional scepticism: measured monopoly, he argued, perpetually dissolves under entry, substitution, and the erosion of cartel discipline, and regulators who hunt it mostly succeed in protecting the inefficient from the efficient. The theory of contestable markets, developed by William Baumol and collaborators, completed the defence by relocating the discipline from actual rivals to potential ones: where entry is easy, even a single seller must price as if competitors stood at the gate, because the gate is the competitor.

The monopoly school's engagement with this defence has been less systematic than its engagement with its Marxist critics, a gap its own historians concede, but the lines of reply are visible in its literature. Entry, the school observes, is easy precisely where it does not matter: the commanding heights of the economy are defended not by law but by the capital requirements, distribution networks, patent estates, and brand accumulations that constitute the modern barrier, and the history of the great industries shows entry at scale occurring mainly by the sponsorship of states or the migration of giants from adjacent markets, which replaces one oligopolist with another rather than restoring competition. Demsetz's efficiency reading is granted its cases and denied its generality: the school asks why, if margins are efficiency's wages, the efficient firms of the markup studies so persistently decline to invest their wages in expanded capacity, preferring financial placement and the repurchase of their own shares, behaviour that sits comfortably in the absorption accounts and awkwardly in the efficiency story. On contestability, the school's rejoinder is empirical and brief: a discipline that operates through hypothetical entrants should show up in prices that behave as if entry threatened, and the administered price's defining trait, its indifference to cost and demand movements that would trigger competitive adjustment, is the record of its absence. The exchange matters for the reader's judgement because Chicago's defence, unlike the value dispute, shares the school's own evidentiary terrain of margins, entry rates, and investment behaviour. It is the objection the theory must beat on data, not on doctrine, and the markup literature's unresolved quarrel between power and efficiency interpretations is, in substance, this older argument continuing by statistical means.

## The dispute over value: the school's internal wound

Among the objections, the one that has never healed is the dispute about value, because it concerns not the school's conclusions but its identity. It deserves a slower hearing than the summary above could give it. The charge, in its full form, runs like this. Marx's economics is not a collection of conclusions about capitalism that might be detached and rearranged; it is an analysis that begins from the commodity, unfolds the value form, locates exploitation in the difference between the value of labour power and the value labour creates, and derives the system's dynamics, accumulation, mechanisation, the falling tendency of the profit rate, from that foundation. Every category earns its place by its position in the derivation. To announce that one will keep the tradition's conclusions about crisis and imperialism while discarding the derivation, substituting an aggregate defined at the surface of prices and costs, is not to develop the analysis but to leave it, retaining the conclusions as opinions. The resulting doctrine may be true or false, but its warrant is no longer Marx's, and its agreements with Marx are coincidences of destination rather than continuities of method. Critics within the tradition have added a sharper edge: the surplus, defined without reference to labour embodied, cannot distinguish the excess a society generates through exploitation from the excess it might generate through any other arrangement, and so the moral and analytical centre of Marxism, the working day and its division, drops out of the theory's foundations even if its rhetoric keeps the words.

The school's answer, given by Baran before the book and refined by Foster after it, deserves equal fullness. It begins by denying the premise that Marx's categories were designed as timeless instruments. The value analysis, on this reading, was built for a capitalism of many competing capitals in which prices gravitate around values and the gravitation does the analytical work. The giant corporation breaks the gravitation: its prices are set by decision, its margins reflect power, and the economy's commanding magnitudes are formed in the boardroom's arithmetic of costs and target returns, not in any equilibration of embodied labour. An analyst who insists on computing values in such an economy computes quantities that no agent in the economy responds to and no data can exhibit, scholastic magnitudes whose only function is to certify orthodoxy. The surplus, by contrast, is formed from the magnitudes the agents themselves use: output, cost, margin, expenditure. Fidelity to Marx, the school argues, lies in his procedure, which was to identify the categories adequate to the capitalism in front of him and to follow those categories to the system's contradictions, not in the preservation of a category set beyond the conditions that made it adequate. Marx revised the classical economists' categories when they failed his capitalism; the school claims the same licence, exercised in the same spirit, toward Marx's.

Then comes the concession, and the school's best writers have made it without flinching. The licence costs something real. What is lost, when surplus value gives way to the surplus, is the derivation: the tight connection by which the rate of exploitation in production governs the possibilities of accumulation and crisis. The monopoly theory can assert that the surplus rests on exploitation, and does, but it cannot demonstrate the quantitative linkages with the rigour the value framework promised, and its explanations of crisis run through demand and expenditure in ways that genuinely do resemble the Keynesian constructions its founders both used and kept at arm's length. A reader must decide whether the resemblance is a scandal or a discovery. The school's position, at its most defensible, is that the resemblance records a fact about the economy rather than a lapse of the analyst: under monopoly conditions the system's contradictions really do migrate to the side of realisation, and a theory honest about the migration will find itself, whatever its pedigree, talking about spending. The wound, on this account, is not in the theory. It is in the object, which changed shape beneath the tradition's inherited instruments. That answer has not persuaded the value theorists, and the argument continues in the journals, which is itself a datum: the school remains the tradition's most productive irritant, the branch whose existence forces every other branch to say what, exactly, it takes Marxism to be.

## Building the surplus estimate: the measurement workshop

Because the empirical objection turns on construction, the construction deserves a workshop visit. A researcher setting out to measure the surplus for a given year begins from the national income accounts and sorts every flow into the theory's three piles: the costs of production, the surplus, and the uses of the surplus. Into costs go the wages of productive labour, the materials consumed, and the depreciation of plant at replacement values. Into the surplus go the profits of corporations and proprietors, interest, rent, the taxes that transfer income to the state, the earnings of the financial sector, and the incomes paid to unproductive activities within firms, the sales forces, legal departments, and supervisory layers whose output is not a saleable good. The uses are then traced: how much of the surplus returns as investment, how much as the consumption of its owners, how much as the sales effort, the military budget, and the other absorbers. The bookkeeping is laborious and every border is an argument. Is a truck driver's wage a cost of production or part of distribution's overhead? Are public school teachers productive, and does the answer change if the school is private? Reasonable constructors differ, and the differences move the total by points of national income.

The tradition's response to this instability of the parts has been to stake its claim on the whole. Foster and his collaborators, updating the surplus estimates across the later twentieth century and into the twenty first, reported that under a range of classification conventions the surplus share rises across the era and the unabsorbed portion, visible in idle capacity and in the financial placements of nonfinancial corporations, rises with it; the level of the estimate moves with the convention, the trend survives it. Critics answer that a trend robust to conventions chosen by sympathisers has not yet met a hostile constructor, and the exchange defines the research frontier with unusual clarity: what the debate needs, and what younger researchers in several traditions have begun to supply, is a surplus estimate built by a published protocol, replicated by scholars with no stake in the outcome, and reported with its sensitivity to each border decision displayed. The mainstream markup literature, whatever its interpretive quarrels, has shown the profession how such protocols change a debate, by making the construction itself a citable object. The school's magnitude awaits its equivalent, and the school's best current writers appear to know it, since their recent empirical work reads increasingly like an application for exactly that discipline.

One measurement sub-dispute deserves separate mention because it ties the workshop back to the theory's centre. Depreciation, the allowance for capital used up in production, sits inside the costs pile, yet its size depends on the prices at which plant is valued and the service lives accountants assume. In an era of rapid technical change, when equipment's economic life is cut short by obsolescence its physical frame survives, the constructor must decide whether the surplus includes the value of machines discarded still serviceable: a real social cost the monopoly economy imposes, the school argues, since the discarding is driven by the product cycle of the sales effort rather than by wear. Decisions of this kind are where the theory hides inside the accounting, and a reader auditing any surplus estimate, friendly or hostile, should ask after them first. The surplus is not found in the accounts. It is built, and the building is the argument continued by other means.

## What would test the theory: evidence and its limits

A theory that invites the statistician into the room owes the statistician a protocol, and the school's commitments can be arranged as a set of expectations against which evidence can fairly be laid. The surplus share of output, however its borders are drawn, should trend upward across the monopoly era and should not collapse back to competitive-era levels in any sustained way. Margins of price over cost in the corporate core should be wider and more stable than competitive models predict, and should widen, or at least hold, through cost reductions. Capacity utilisation should average below the levels the installed capital makes possible, across cycles and not merely in recessions. Private investment should chronically disappoint relative to profitability, with corporations accumulating financial assets in place of plant when outlets narrow. And the growth episodes that do occur should be traceable, in the main, to the theory's catalogue of stimuli and absorbers: military build-ups, credit expansions, epochal technologies, reconstruction, rather than to any spontaneous buoyancy of private spending. Each expectation is checkable in principle, and the school's literature, to its credit, has repeatedly gone to the data with them, publishing series on the surplus share, on utilisation, and on the financial accumulation of nonfinancial corporations.

The limits of such testing are as much a part of the record as the tests. The surplus share requires a constructed magnitude, and constructions differ. Utilisation series rest on engineering estimates of capacity that engineering practice keeps redefining. The attribution of growth episodes to stimuli is a judgement exercised on history, not a regression coefficient, and honest examiners exercise it differently. The mainstream markup literature, as noted, measures cousins of the school's magnitudes rather than the magnitudes themselves. The upshot is a state of evidence unusual in economics: broad configuration confirmed from several independent directions, precise law unsettled. The configuration, wide margins, weak investment, chronic slack, hypertrophied finance and salesmanship, is the one the theory sketches. Whether the configuration arises for the theory's reasons, through administered pricing by monopoly firms, or for the superstar interpretation's reasons, through the victory of the efficient, remains the live contest, and it is being fought with firm-level data the founders could not have imagined. A reader who wants the school's current standing can be given it in one sentence: its description of the economy has aged better than its rivals expected, its explanation of that description remains one contender among several, and its central law awaits a measurement technology all sides can accept.

The school's applied wing carried the stagnation question into the assessment of the system's whole condition in the era after the financial crisis, an application readers can pursue in the article on [the crisis of capitalism assessed as a whole](/2014/01/01/crisis-of-capitalism-today/). There the absorption framework meets rival diagnoses on equal terms, and its characteristic claim, that the slump which began in 2008 was the underlying stagnation reasserting itself through the collapse of its financial remedy, can be compared against accounts centred on profitability, on debt, and on policy error. The comparison is the fairest test the tradition offers its readers: not whether the school's vocabulary is orthodox, but whether its story organises the evidence of the decades with less strain than the alternatives.

## The school and its institutions: a journal as a research programme

Theories survive through institutions, and this school's institution was a journal. Monthly Review was founded in 1949 by Sweezy and the writer Leo Huberman, in the opening chill of the Cold War, as an independent socialist magazine outside both the Communist Party's orbit and the social-democratic mainstream. Its independence was its method: free of any party's line, it could publish the heresy that became the school's doctrine, host the tradition's internal arguments, and survive the political weather that closed larger institutions to Marxist work. The school's history cannot be separated from the journal's, because the journal was where the doctrine was maintained between books: where Sweezy and Magdoff worked out the finance essays, where the surplus measurements were debated, where Foster's monopoly-finance synthesis first appeared, and where the tradition's running argument with its critics has been conducted for three quarters of a century.

The founders' formation shaped the doctrine's character in ways worth tracing. Sweezy had been a Harvard economist of conventional brilliance before his Marxist turn, an expert on the economics profession's own literature who could state the orthodoxy he opposed better than most of its defenders; his Theory of Capitalist Development was for decades the standard English-language exposition of Marxist economics precisely because it refused caricature in both directions. Baran, born in Russia, educated in Germany, Poland, and the Soviet Union, and eventually a professor at Stanford, brought the perspective of the periphery into the school's foundations: his surplus category was forged on the problems of India and Latin America before it was turned on the United States, which is why the theory never treated the American economy as a closed specimen. The collaboration joined a theorist of the core to a theorist of the periphery, and the doctrine bears the double stamp: an account of the advanced economy whose central category was designed to travel, and whose account of the advanced economy keeps glancing outward at the empire that economy maintains.

Institutions also impose disciplines, and the journal's discipline on the school was continuity under revision. A party school protects its doctrine by freezing it; a journal school protects its doctrine by publishing the revisions its own development requires, and the record shows the difference. The finance turn was not smuggled in; it was argued for, issue by issue, against readers attached to the founding text's proportions. The monopoly-finance synthesis was proposed as a development of Baran and Sweezy, debated as such, and adopted gradually enough that its novelty is easy to underestimate. Custodianship of this kind has costs: the school's pace can look slow beside the academy's fashion cycle, and its categories resist the updating-by-rebranding that keeps other traditions visible. But the same custodianship explains the doctrine's unusual coherence across seventy years. To read the school is to read one argument, maintained by identifiable people in an identifiable place, changing its mind in public about the things it got wrong. Few traditions in economics, orthodox or heterodox, can show a cleaner chain of custody.

The chain's later links show the doctrine still working rather than merely being curated. Foster and McChesney's The Endless Crisis, published in 2012, carried the monopoly-finance construction through the financial crisis and its aftermath, arguing that stagnation had become the advanced economies' settled condition rather than their post-crisis convalescence, and extending the analysis to the surplus and slack emerging within China's export economy, a test of the framework's portability its founders would have relished. The school's ecological writing, another late branch, grew from the same root: an economy that must waste to survive will waste the natural world with the same structural indifference it wastes labour, and the potential surplus concept, the gap between what an order does and what it could do, translates without forcing into the language of squandered ecosystems and foreclosed futures. Branches of this kind are how a reader can tell a living tradition from a preserved one. The preserved tradition annotates its founding text; the living one keeps discovering that its founding inversion organises material the founders never touched, and accepts the risk, which the school has repeatedly run, that some of the new material will organise itself against them.

## Reading the founding texts: a route through the school's library

The school's corpus is compact enough to be read whole, and the order matters. Sweezy's Theory of Capitalist Development comes first for most readers, not because the later doctrine is present in it but because its absence is instructive: the young Sweezy states the falling rate of profit fairly, worries at its realisation problems, and introduces Kalecki to English readers, so that the mature inversion can be seen forming against the tradition it would revise. Baran's Political Economy of Growth follows, for the surplus category in its original habitat, where the distinction between actual and potential surplus does its first work on the economics of poor countries and where the reader learns to handle the category before meeting its metropolitan application. Steindl's Maturity and Stagnation then supplies the oligopoly mechanism in its purest form, a short and demanding book whose balance-sheet reasoning rewards the effort it requires. Only then should Monopoly Capital itself be opened, and it should be read as its authors built it: the replacement of the law in the early chapters, the audit of absorption outlets in the middle, and the treatment of the state, the sales effort, and the system's irrationality toward the end, where the book's famous argument that a rational society would convert its potential surplus into welfare and leisure gives the whole construction its point.

The second storey of the library is the finance work. Sweezy and Magdoff's essays of the nineteen seventies and eighties, collected in Stagnation and the Financial Explosion and its companion volumes, show the doctrine meeting the phenomenon it had understated, and they are best read with the question the authors asked themselves: what is finance for, in an economy that cannot place its surplus? Foster's The Theory of Monopoly Capitalism offers the systematic restatement for readers who want the doctrine's logic laid out as theory rather than as commentary, and his monopoly-finance writings bring the construction to the era of the great financial crisis. Against the whole shelf, the critical literature should be read in parallel rather than afterwards: Mandel's Late Capitalism for the falling-rate tradition's most powerful counter-construction, Mattick's essays for the value objection in its least compromising form, and the mainstream markup papers for the convergence and its rival interpretation, because the school's arguments are sharpened by their opponents and a reader who meets only the doctrine will mistake its confidence for consensus.

A practical warning belongs in any honest reading guide for this school. Its central terms are false friends. Surplus sounds like surplus value and is not. Stagnation sounds like depression and is not. Monopoly sounds like a single seller and means, in the school's usage, the whole regime of the giant corporation and administered pricing, whether an industry holds two firms or twenty. Absorption sounds like a metaphor and is a technical claim about expenditure flows. The reader who fixes these four definitions at the outset, ideally in a notebook kept for the purpose, will find the literature transparent; the reader who imports the everyday meanings will find it confused, and the confusion will belong to the importation. Much of the school's public misunderstanding, including several of the misreadings corrected in the next section, reduces to these four equivocations.

## The human register of stagnation: surplus people

A theory of idle capacity is also, whether it says so or not, a theory of idle people, and the school's writers were among the few economists of their era to insist on the symmetry. Marx had written of the relative surplus population, the reserve army of labour that accumulation itself produces, as the lever by which wages are disciplined. Under monopoly conditions, the school argued, the reserve changes character: it ceases to be a cyclical pool, drained in booms and refilled in slumps, and becomes a standing population whose labour the economy's normal operation does not require. The official unemployment figures, counting only those actively seeking work, understate the phenomenon in the theory's telling, because they exclude the discouraged who have stopped seeking, the incarcerated whose numbers grew to historically extraordinary levels in the United States, the conscripted absorbed by the military absorber itself, and the vast penumbra of part-time, casual, and make-work employment by which the sales effort and the service sector park labour the productive economy cannot use. Stagnation's true employment statistic, on this view, is not the unemployment rate but the share of the working-age population the system engages in producing useful output at its capacity, a share the theory expects to sag across the era even as headline figures fluctuate.

The school drew from this a conclusion about the social order that connects its economics to its politics of race and empire, a connection Monthly Review pursued more persistently than most Marxist institutions of its generation. A population surplus to production's requirements does not distribute itself randomly; it concentrates where the system's other exclusions have already fallen, among racial minorities at home, whose neighbourhoods become the reserve's territory, and across the periphery abroad, whose vast informal economies Baran's analysis had already identified as the global form of the same phenomenon: labour power produced and reproduced, at real social cost, for a production system that declines to employ it. The potential surplus and the surplus population are, in this accounting, the same waste measured in two units, the output not produced and the producers not used, and the school's moral argument against the system rests finally on the double entry: an order that wastes things might be merely inefficient, but an order that wastes lifetimes, in the midst of unmet needs its own capacity could supply, stands condemned by the standard the founders kept invoking, its own unused possibilities. Critics of the school's economics should note how much of its tenacity derives from this register rather than from the surplus estimates: doctrines survive their statistical quarrels when they name an experience their rivals' categories cannot phrase, and the experience of being economically unnecessary, administered by a civilisation of unprecedented productive power, is the one this doctrine has always named best.

## Misreadings the record corrects

Half a century of summary and counter-summary has deposited a sediment of errors around the school, and clearing them is part of understanding it. The first and most consequential is the filing of monopoly capital theory as a variant of the falling rate of profit, a filing this article's inversion table exists to prevent. The school did not add monopoly to the classical mechanism; it proposed that monopoly changes which mechanism governs, and any exposition that presents the rising surplus as a footnote to the falling rate has inverted the inversion. The second error treats stagnation as a forecast of permanent recession, refutable by any prosperous decade. As established above, stagnation is a claim about capacity, utilisation, and the sources of growth, and it stands or falls on the composition of booms, not on their existence. The third error holds that the school denies exploitation, a claim its texts refute on their face: the surplus is generated in production, under the wage relation, and the theory's quarrel is with the value-theoretic derivation of crisis, not with the existence of unpaid labour at the system's base. The fourth error conflates the surplus with surplus value, assuming the school merely renamed Marx's category; the categories differ in definition, in measurement, and in the work they do, and the difference is the school's founding decision rather than a slip of its pen. The fifth error, flattering to the school and therefore worth correcting with special care, credits the concentration and markup literature to the tradition's influence, as though mainstream researchers had set out to test Baran and Sweezy. They had not; the convergence is independent, which is precisely what gives it evidential weight, and the school damages its own best argument when its popularisers describe corroboration as discipleship.

A sixth misreading concerns the school's attitude to the postwar boom, sometimes summarised as embarrassment. The record shows the opposite: the boom was the theory's first test and its authors knew it, which is why the book, drafted through the boom's height, insisted on cataloguing the boom's engines, war budgets, the automobile complex, suburbanisation, and foreign reconstruction, as exceptional stimuli rather than as the system's ordinary working. Whether that catalogue was foresight or elasticity is the falsifiability dispute already examined, but embarrassment forms no part of it; a theory embarrassed by prosperity does not build prosperity's explanation into its founding text. The seventh and final misreading casts the school as a purely American doctrine about a purely American economy. Its surplus category was born in the study of underdevelopment, its oligopoly mechanism in Vienna and Oxford exile scholarship, its pricing theory in Warsaw, and its application has always been to a world economy organised by the giant corporation's reach. The United States was its laboratory, not its boundary.

## Monopoly and democracy: the school's political corollary

The school's economics carried a political corollary that its founders considered inseparable and that any complete account must state, with the same neutrality applied elsewhere in this article. If economic power concentrates in corporations whose decisions on investment, pricing, and employment shape the life chances of millions, then the formal equality of the ballot coexists with a substantive inequality of command over the economy, and democratic politics becomes, in significant part, the management of decisions taken elsewhere. Baran and Sweezy pressed the point through their account of the state: the government that must absorb the surplus is staffed, funded, and lobbied by the class that generates it, and the sorting of absorbers described earlier in this article, armaments indulged and social provision resisted, is the corollary's chief exhibit. Later writers in the tradition extended the analysis to the media system, where the concentration of ownership and the dependence on advertising revenue were argued to narrow the range of public debate without any need for censorship: the sales effort finances the channels, and the channels, as businesses, select for content that keeps audiences in a buying frame of mind. Robert McChesney, working in this line, carried the argument into the era of the commercial internet, treating the giant platform firms as the absorption economy's media form.

The corollary has always faced a pluralist rejoinder, and the rejoinder belongs in the record. Political scientists of the pluralist tradition reply that concentrated economic power is checked by countervailing organisation, electoral competition, federal division, and the genuine divisions within the business class itself, whose fractions quarrel over trade, taxation, and regulation too publicly for any monolith theory to survive; the same decades that saw corporate concentration also saw the expansion of suffrage's substance, environmental and consumer regulation imposed over business opposition, and antitrust actions brought by the state against its largest firms. The school's answer distinguishes the terrain: on questions touching the core arrangements, the scale of military spending, the protection of the financial system, the limits of the social wage, the record shows a consistency of outcome across administrations of both parties that pluralism struggles to explain, while on the wide terrain beyond the core, business loses often and visibly. Critics rejoin that a theory which counts only the core questions, defined after the fact as the ones business wins, has insulated itself again. The argument is the falsifiability dispute in political dress, and it is recorded here for the same reason: the school's political corollary is a live research claim about the distribution of outcomes, not a mood about power, and it will stand or fall on the comparative study of who wins, how often, on which questions.

## What follows if the theory is right: waste, rationality, and reform

Every crisis theory smuggles a politics, and this school's politics follow from the absorption inversion with unusual directness. If the system's core problem is a surplus it can dispose of only wastefully, then the indictment of the system is not that it produces too little, the classical scarcity indictment that scarcity economics levels at every order, but that it produces irrationally: that it maintains arsenals while housing decays, manufactures wants while needs go unmet, idles its factories and its people in the midst of potential abundance. Baran had given this indictment its measure in the gap between actual and potential surplus, and the founding book closed on it: a rationally ordered society, the authors argued, would close that gap, converting wasted potential into shorter hours, public provision, and the material basis of a freer life. The standard by which monopoly capitalism is judged, in the school's own texts, is not the standard of a rival economy's output table. It is the standard of the system's own unused possibilities, and the judgement is that the waste is structural: no personnel change at the top of the corporation or the ministry removes the disposal problem that generates it.

The reform implications are correspondingly double-edged, and the school's writers were honest about the edge that cuts their own sympathisers. If absorption is the problem, then demand management of the Keynesian kind can genuinely work, for a time, by expanding the state's absorber; the theory therefore predicts the partial success of the very policies its politics might wish to transcend, and it explains why such successes stabilise the system rather than radicalising it. It also predicts the sorting of state spending described earlier: civilian expansion will be resisted in proportion to its threat to the social order, military expansion indulged in proportion to its innocence, so that a reform politics working through budgets alone will find the menu pre-selected against its intentions. And it predicts that finance, the absorber of last resort, will be rescued at any cost when it fails, because the alternative is the stagnation unmasked. Readers need not accept these implications to learn from them; they are stated here as the theory's own logic, and they illustrate the general rule that the absorption inversion, once granted, reorganises not only diagnosis but expectation, telling its holder in advance which reforms will be easy, which will be fought, and which will succeed in ways that disappoint their authors.

It remains to record, in the same neutral spirit, the counter-implication pressed by the school's critics, since a balance sheet shows both columns. If the falling-rate tradition is right and the monopoly school wrong, then the politics of absorption are a distraction from the system's real motion, and the decades spent analysing waste were decades not spent on the profitability dynamics that actually govern crisis. If the superstar interpretation of the markup evidence is right, the margins the school treats as power are largely prizes of efficiency, and policies aimed at absorption address a misdiagnosed economy. The school's representatives accept the terms of this wager in print, which is more than many traditions do: they have stated what their theory forbids, and they wait on the evidence in the open. The assessment that follows attempts nothing grander than to weigh, for the reader, where the waiting has so far left the account.

## A balance sheet for the school

Set out as a ledger, the school's assets begin with priority. It identified, earlier than any rival framework and in print, the configuration that has become the advanced economies' recognised condition: margins widening over costs, investment lagging profitability, demand chronically short of capacity, and the resulting slack managed by waste, armaments, and credit. Its category of the sales effort founded a research field, the political economy of advertising and commercial media, that scholars outside the tradition now work in without always knowing its source. Its treatment of military spending remains the most developed Marxist account of the arms budget's economic function. Its finance turn anticipated, in mechanism if not in timing, the pattern by which a stagnating productive economy generates an unstable financial superstructure, and the monopoly-finance synthesis gave that anticipation systematic form. Its institutional continuity, a journal maintaining an argument across three quarters of a century, is itself an asset in a field littered with abandoned frameworks.

The ledger's other column is equally definite. The central law rests on a magnitude whose measurement remains contested, and the school's own estimates have never commanded assent beyond its sympathisers; until the surplus can be constructed by a protocol its critics accept as neutral, the law of rising surplus will stand as a well-supported tendency claim rather than a demonstrated regularity. The value question remains open in the form its critics press: the school has a defence of its licence to rebuild categories, and the defence is serious, but the cost it concedes, the loss of the derivation linking exploitation to crisis, is also serious, and readers must judge whether realisation-side explanation can carry the weight the tradition asks of it. The theory of the state is underbuilt relative to the state's role in the argument. The treatment of competition, powerful for the corporate core, says less about the global supply chains through which much production now runs, where buyer power and supplier competition complicate the administered price; the tradition has begun this work, on global value chains and the new imperialism, but it is unfinished. And the theory's elasticity, its catalogue of exceptional stimuli, remains a standing invitation to the falsifiability charge, answerable case by case but never discharged once for all.

The balanced verdict this article offers is therefore a split one, of the kind the evidence supports. As a description of advanced capitalism's characteristic condition, the school has been vindicated to a degree its founders' contemporaries would have found startling, and the mainstream convergence is the certificate. As an explanation of that condition, it remains in open competition with the falling-rate tradition it sought to replace, with the superstar account of the same margins, and with hybrid constructions that borrow from each. As a branch of Marxism, it is the tradition's boldest wager: that fidelity to a method can survive the replacement of its most famous law. The wager's outcome is not in, and the reader who has followed the argument this far holds the materials for judging it, which is all a guide in a contested field should promise. What no reader needs to wait for is the inversion itself, which can be applied at once to any crisis story told in the older vocabulary, by asking the school's one question of it: where, in this account, does the surplus go?

## For students and exam candidates

Hold the inversion and you hold the school. In any crisis-theory question, the monopoly capital answer is the one that runs the film backwards: the problem is not a falling rate of profit but a rising surplus, not generation but absorption, not breakdown but stagnation. Examiners reward the candidate who can set this school against the profit-rate account term by term, which is what the inversion table in this article is for, and who can name the absorption channels in order: capitalists' consumption, investment with its self-defeating capacity effect, the sales effort, civilian state spending with its political limits, military spending without them, foreign placement, and finance. Know the personnel as a chain of contributions: Baran for the surplus category, Steindl for the oligopoly mechanism, Kalecki for the mark-up and the degree of monopoly, Sweezy for the synthesis, Magdoff for the finance essays, Foster for monopoly-finance capital. The trap in this territory is vocabulary: surplus is not surplus value, stagnation is not recession, and answers that confuse them announce themselves. A strong answer also concedes the standard criticisms in a sentence each, the measurement problem and the value objection, before giving the school's replies.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The school teaches well because its founding act is a clean, discussable decision: a law replaced, on stated grounds, by its opposite. Open a seminar by putting the inversion table on the board and asking students to defend each column against the other before any text is read; the argument that follows will organise itself. Pair the early chapters of Monopoly Capital with the falling-rate chapters of a standard crisis survey and let the two mechanisms compete for the same episodes: the postwar boom, the nineteen seventies, the era after the financial crisis each become exercises in attribution. Steindl's balance-sheet reasoning suits a methods session, Kalecki's mark-up a session on price theory's hidden distributional machinery. Set the value dispute as the essay question it deserves to be, requiring students to state the objection that the school abandoned Marx's derivation before they are allowed to answer it, and require the mainstream markup papers in the same week as Foster's restatement, so the convergence is studied as a problem in the sociology of knowledge rather than absorbed as a trophy. The four false-friend definitions, surplus, stagnation, monopoly, absorption, make a reliable opening quiz.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The live research frontier here is the confrontation between the school's interpretation of the markup and concentration evidence and the superstar-firm interpretation of the same data, and the productive questions are empirical: whether high-markup firms' margins persist in ways that indicate entrenched power or erode in ways that indicate contested prizes; whether investment weakness tracks market power sector by sector or tracks the intangibles and globalisation variables rival accounts propose; whether the surplus share, constructed under a published and replicable protocol, trends as the law requires. Researchers keeping command of this fast-moving literature will benefit from a disciplined note system, and the [Marxism study notes tool](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) serves that purpose, letting each study be filed under the magnitude it actually measures, markup, profit share, surplus, so that cousins are not cited as twins. Historians of thought will find the school's archive unusually instructive: a doctrine revised in public across seventy years, with the finance turn documented issue by issue. Journalists should take two cautions from the record. The convergence of mainstream findings with the school's description is a fact; describing that literature as produced in the school's support is not. And stagnation in this theory is a claim about capacity and the composition of growth, never a synonym for collapse, so the school cannot be quoted as predicting the crisis of any particular season. What it can be quoted on is the condition to which economies return when the credit dries: the diagnosis is structural, and its timescale is the era, not the quarter.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is monopoly capital theory?

Monopoly capital theory is the Marxist school founded by Paul Baran and Paul Sweezy, set out in their book Monopoly Capital of 1966 and developed through the journal Monthly Review. It holds that the advanced economies are dominated by giant corporations which administer prices rather than submitting to price competition, that the economic surplus therefore tends to rise as a share of output, and that the system's central problem is absorbing that surplus. Where absorption fails, the result is stagnation: chronic underuse of capacity and labour, relieved by waste spending, armaments, and credit expansion.

### Q: Why did Baran and Sweezy replace the falling rate of profit?

They replaced it because the mechanism behind the falling tendency presupposed the price competition of an earlier capitalism. In Marx's construction, competition forces firms to mechanise and forces the fruits of cost cutting into lower prices, squeezing the rate of profit as capital intensity rises. Baran and Sweezy argued that giant firms in concentrated industries no longer compete on price: they administer prices, retain the fruits of cost cutting as widening margins, and so generate a rising surplus share instead. The replacement was explicit and structural: a new pricing regime, they held, required a new central law.

### Q: What is the law of rising surplus?

The law of rising surplus is the school's central tendency claim: under monopoly capitalism, the economic surplus, meaning all that society produces above the costs of producing it, tends to rise both absolutely and as a share of total output. The mechanism is the scissors of falling unit costs and defended prices: rationalisation drives costs down while administered prices hold, so profits, interest, rent, and the revenues available for unproductive expenditure all draw on a widening stream. The law concerns the surplus share rather than the rate of profit, and it creates a problem of absorption rather than of generation.

### Q: How is the economic surplus absorbed?

The surplus is absorbed through the spending channels that validate it: capitalists' consumption, investment in new capacity, the sales effort of advertising and marketing, government spending in civilian and military forms, foreign investment, and financial expansion. Each channel is limited. Luxury consumption saturates; investment enlarges the capacity that generates future surplus; the sales effort and military budgets can grow greatly but at the price of waste; foreign outlets develop into competitors; finance absorbs by piling claims on future income. Stagnation, in the theory, is what appears when the growth of all the channels together falls behind the growth of the surplus itself.

### Q: Why does monopoly capitalism tend to stagnate?

Because its pricing structure generates surplus faster than its spending channels can absorb it. Giant firms cut costs while defending prices, so the surplus share rises secularly; but investment is restrained by existing excess capacity, consumption is bounded by the distribution of income, and the absorbers that can expand without limit, armaments, salesmanship, and credit, expand only as fast as politics and stability permit. The economy therefore settles below its capacity, with slow growth and persistent unemployment as its normal condition, until exceptional stimuli such as wars, reconstruction, epoch-making innovations, or financial booms temporarily close the gap.

### Q: Did monopoly capital abandon the labour theory of value?

In the judgement of its Marxist critics, substantially yes, and the school's candour makes the charge hard to dodge entirely: Baran and Sweezy replaced surplus value, a category defined through embodied labour, with the economic surplus, an aggregate of output over costs, and derived crisis from expenditure rather than from the value composition of capital. The school replied that value categories presuppose competitive price formation, which administered pricing abolishes, and that fidelity to Marx's method licenses rebuilding the magnitudes. Whether the result remains Marxism is the tradition's deepest unresolved dispute about the school.

### Q: What is monopoly finance capital?

Monopoly finance capital is the concept, developed within the Monthly Review tradition and associated with John Bellamy Foster, for the stage in which giant corporations and giant financial institutions have fused into a single system. The productive sector, stagnating under the absorption problem, throws off surplus that cannot be placed in plant and equipment; the financial sector manufactures the claims, credit, and asset inflations that absorb it and sustain demand. The hybrid's signature is the alternation of long productive slack with financial boom and crisis, and the state's role as rescuer of the financial pole on which the whole arrangement depends.

### Q: Are large technology firms monopoly capital?

By the theory's criteria, the question is structural rather than about size or fame: does the firm operate in a concentrated market where it can administer prices and defend margins, does it retain the fruits of cost cutting rather than passing them into lower prices, and does it dispose of its surplus substantially through sales effort, acquisitions, and financial holdings? Large technology firms in concentrated digital markets meet much of that description, since network effects and control of platforms confer pricing power, and their enormous cash accumulations resemble the surplus seeking placement that Steindl described. The theory classifies by market structure and pricing behaviour, not by sector.

### Q: Does the rising markups literature support the theory?

It supports the theory's description more than its explanation. Mainstream researchers with no connection to the school, including Jan De Loecker and Jan Eeckhout on markups and Simha Barkai on the declining shares of labour and capital, have documented widening margins over cost, rising concentration, and a growing residue of pure profit across the decades since about 1980, a configuration the theory predicts. But the literature measures markups and profit shares, not Baran's surplus; its leading rival interpretation attributes the same margins to efficient superstar firms; and its period covers only part of the era the law claims. The honest verdict is convergence, not vindication.

### Q: What are the main criticisms of monopoly capital theory?

Four criticisms dominate. The empirical criticism holds that the surplus cannot be measured independently of the analyst's classification choices, so the rising surplus may be an artefact of its construction. The theoretical criticism, pressed by Marxists such as Ernest Mandel and Paul Mattick, holds that abandoning surplus value severs the theory from Marx's derivation and leaves a Keynesian demand doctrine in Marxist vocabulary. The methodological criticism charges that stagnation plus open-ended exceptional stimuli can accommodate any outcome and therefore predict none. The historical criticism notes that the theory was framed during the greatest boom in capitalist history and asks whether explaining that boom as exception was foresight or elasticity.

### Q: How does the inversion table turn a falling surplus problem into a rising one?

The inversion table works by comparing the two crisis theories question by question rather than slogan by slogan. Down its rows, the classical column assigns the system a falling rate of profit, a problem of generating surplus, a crisis born in production, and expansion as the normal state; the monopoly column assigns the same system a rising surplus share, a problem of absorbing surplus, a crisis born in realisation, and stagnation as the normal state. Reading across any row shows that the school changed not the parameters but the sign of the difficulty: too little surplus relative to capital becomes too much surplus relative to outlets.

### Q: What counts as absorption in Baran and Sweezy's argument?

Absorption counts any expenditure that realises the surplus without it piling up as unsold output: investment, capitalist consumption, the sales effort, government spending, foreign placement, and the credit-financed spending that financial expansion sustains. The test is functional, not moral. An outlay absorbs whether it builds a school or a bomber, whether it informs consumers or merely restyles a product, because the theory is asking whether spending grows fast enough to validate a rising surplus, and only afterwards asking what the spending was for. The school's critical force comes from holding the two questions apart and then together: absorption explains why the waste exists, and the waste indicts the arrangements that require it.

### Q: Why did military spending matter to the stagnation thesis?

Military spending mattered because it solved the absorber's political problem. Civilian state spending at the scale the rising surplus requires tends to compete with private business, strengthen labour, or expand the social wage, and therefore meets organised resistance; military spending does none of these things, returns no goods to civilian markets, faces no natural satiation point, and enjoys the protection of security politics. When the postwar advanced economies, above all the United States, sustained peacetime military budgets of unprecedented size, the school read the fact as the system selecting the one absorber able to grow unchecked, and built the selection into its explanation of the long boom.

### Q: How does the absorption inversion differ from underconsumption?

Underconsumption theories locate crisis in the insufficiency of workers' purchasing power: wages lag output, goods go unsold, and the remedy implied is redistribution toward consumption. The absorption inversion is broader and differently aimed. The shortfall it identifies is not confined to household consumption; it is the gap between a rising total surplus and all the channels, investment, state spending, sales effort, foreign placement, and finance included, that might absorb it. Surplus can fail of absorption even when consumption is buoyant, if investment falters and credit contracts. The school therefore explains episodes underconsumption cannot, including slumps amid high consumption sustained by debt, and treats redistribution as one influence on absorption rather than the whole story.

### Q: What evidence would test the rising surplus claim?

The claim can be tested against the behaviour of the margins and shares it predicts. A researcher would construct the surplus share of output under a published protocol, classifying profits, interest, rent, and unproductive expenditure consistently over long periods, and check whether the share trends upward across the monopoly era rather than oscillating without direction. Supporting series include price-cost markups in the corporate core, capacity utilisation averaged across whole cycles, the relation between profits and investment, and corporations' accumulation of financial assets. The mainstream markup and profit-share literatures supply partial tests; a full test awaits a surplus construction that critics accept as neutral.
