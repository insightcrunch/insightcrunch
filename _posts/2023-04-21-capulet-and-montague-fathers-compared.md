The fathers of Verona are the play's founding falsehood. The Prologue introduces "two households, both alike in dignity" (Prologue, line 1), and generations of summaries have repeated the formula as if the two patriarchs were matched pieces, twin heads of a symmetrical feud. The performed play delivers nothing of the kind. Lord Capulet is one of the largest roles in the tragedy: he hosts the feast, brokers and then commands his daughter's marriage, rages, bustles, grieves in aria, and closes the feud with his own hand. Lord Montague speaks fewer than forty lines in seven speeches across two scenes, never once addresses his son onstage, and reaches the final scene carrying a death report instead of a decision. The verdict argued here is that the imbalance is not an accident of transmission and not a flaw to be excused. It is the design. Shakespeare builds one household from the inside and the other from the outside, because the tragedy needs two opposite failures of the same office: a father who is everywhere, whose love operates as command, and a father who is almost nowhere, whose love operates as anxious inquiry that never arrives. Juliet is destroyed by a surplus of father. Romeo is destroyed in a vacancy where a father should have been.

The comparison also corrects the feud's arithmetic. The houses are equal in rank, in wealth, in guilt, and in loss, and the play is scrupulous about that heraldic equality from the Prologue to the matched golden statues of the close. What is not equal is dramatization. Capulet receives a hall, a kitchen, a marriage market, a temper, and a grief; Montague receives a street at dawn and a churchyard at night. Readers who want the evidence laid out for teaching or for a presentation can keep the counts and the closing exchange in a single deck through the [ReportMedic PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), but the argument does not depend on presentation. It depends on reading the two parts side by side and noticing that one of them is a character while the other is a function, and that the play knows exactly what it is doing with the difference. ## Two Households, Both Alike in Dignity: The Symmetry the Play Withholds

The Prologue's first line is a heraldic statement, not a casting report. "Alike in dignity" certifies that neither house outranks the other, that the feud is a war between peers, and that no appeal to precedence can settle it. The sonnet then distributes its plurals with care: both houses have "new mutiny," both make "civil hands unclean," both produce a child. What the Prologue never promises is equal stage time, and the play that follows spends its substance with a deliberate unevenness that the heraldry conceals. Of the two fathers, one is given an interior. The audience eats at Capulet's table, hears his servants, watches his wife and his nephew and his daughter move through rooms he commands. Montague's house is never shown from inside at all. No Montague servant speaks, no Montague feast is given, no Montague chamber is entered. Romeo's home exists only as an absence he flees at dawn, described by the father who cannot penetrate it.

The key word of the Prologue's formula rewards a pause. Dignity, in the play's Verona, is not an inner quality but a displayed one, rank made visible in houses, feasts, retinues, and marriages, audited by the city at every public occasion. A dignity that is never displayed cannot be verified, and this is the quiet fraud inside the formula. The audience can verify Capulet's dignity exhaustively, in torches, plate, guest lists, and the deference of a prince's kinsman suing for his daughter. Montague's dignity is asserted by the Prologue and exhibited nowhere: no feast certifies it, no servant embodies it, no suitor courts it. The houses are alike in dignity the way two entries in a herald's roll are alike, as text. As theater, one dignity is a production and the other is a caption, and the play's first audiences, watching one old man fill the stage and the other cross it twice, can hardly have missed the difference their ears were told did not exist.

That distribution shapes how the feud itself is understood. Because Capulet's household is the play's one fully furnished interior, the feud appears onstage chiefly as a Capulet possession, something kept in a great house among plate and torches and marriage plans, while the Montague side of the quarrel is represented by young men in the street. The older generation of Montagues is a rumor; the older generation of Capulets is a regime. This is why accounts of the play so often drift into treating Capulet as the feud's proprietor and Montague as a name on the other banner. The drift is the text's own doing. Shakespeare lodges the system's working parts, hospitality, alliance, inheritance, discipline, in a single patriarch, and leaves his opposite number as a silhouette at the edge of the square.

The thesis of this comparison follows from that lodging. The cliche of the equal houses survives because the ending works hard to restore equality, with a handshake and a matched pair of statues, and because the Prologue announces it in advance. Between those two symmetrical ceremonies, the play is radically asymmetrical, and the asymmetry is where the meaning sits. One father acts on his child until the action kills her. The other father fails to reach his child at all, and the failure kills him just as surely. The comparison restores the imbalance the cliche conceals, and it reads the reconciliation at the end as the moment the imbalance finally closes, at the only price the play will accept: both men childless, both men rich in gold and nothing else.

Even the order of the names conspires. In the Prologue's wake the feud settles into the language as a fixed formula, and the formula, in the Prince's closing summons as in common usage, runs Capulet first: "Capulet! Montague!" (5.3.291). The precedence is not heraldic, since the houses are alike in dignity. It is theatrical. The ear puts first the house it has lived in, the father it has watched govern, rage, and grieve, and appends the other as the formula's necessary completion. A play genuinely built on two equal patriarchs would be expected to vary the order sometimes, in courtesy or in rhythm. The play never does in its summonses, because its loyalties of attention were settled in the casting of the parts: first the father who acts, then the father who is named.

The play's own official language conspires in the cliche, which is one reason the cliche is so durable. Every authority in Verona addresses the fathers as a unit. The Friar marries the lovers in the hope that the alliance may "turn your households' rancour to pure love" (2.3.92), speaking of the rancour as a joint possession with a joint cure. The Prince sentences, pardons, and scourges the houses in paired vocatives from his first edict to his last judgment. Even Mercutio, dying, curses both houses in a single breath, as if the plague could not be bothered to distinguish between them. The institutions of the play, church, state, and street, all perceive a symmetrical feud between symmetrical patriarchs. Only the drama itself declines to cooperate. It gives one of the paired names a body, a voice, a temper, and a biography, and leaves the other as a grammatical partner in other men's sentences. A reader who trusts the play's official language will describe two equal fathers. A reader who counts scenes will describe one father and a formula.

## Capulet and Montague in Act 1 Scene 1: Two Old Men, Two Wives, One Mirror

The fathers enter the play as a matched pair, and the matching is pointed. The opening brawl draws both old men out of their houses in nightgowns, each reaching for a weapon of his youth, each restrained by a wife. Capulet calls for his long sword and is answered with the household's verdict on his dotage: "A crutch, a crutch! Why call you for a sword?" (1.1.73). Montague, entering moments later, is held back with an economy that matches it exactly: "Thou shalt not stir a foot to seek a foe" (1.1.76). For the length of a dozen lines the Prologue's symmetry holds perfectly. Two aging patriarchs, identically belligerent, identically managed, heads of houses so alike that their entrances could be exchanged without disturbing a single cue.

### Who is the first father to speak in Romeo and Juliet?

Capulet speaks first. He enters the brawl calling for his long sword and is mocked by his wife, who offers him a crutch instead, and only afterward does Montague enter, to be restrained by Lady Montague in the same posture. The mirror is exact, but the order matters: the play's first father is the one who will dominate it.

The wives' two restraints, often played as identical jokes, differ in a way that forecasts the households' verbal cultures. Lady Capulet mocks: the crutch she offers her husband is a taunt from inside a marriage fluent enough to tease its master in public, and the mockery works because it is affectionate, a household idiom in which even discipline arrives as banter. Lady Montague forbids: her single complete sentence in the scene is a prohibition laid on her husband's body, stir not a foot, without wit and without appeal. Teasing in one hall, interdiction in the other; a wife who can joke her husband down and a wife who can only hold hers back. The marriages, like the fatherhoods, are drawn at different resolutions, and the audience that laughs at the crutch in the scene's first minute has already been taught which house it will be living in.

The divergence begins inside the same scene. When the Prince has scattered the brawl and the stage clears, Montague stays. He becomes, for some forty lines, the play's first investigator, questioning Benvolio about the son whose dawn wanderings he has watched from a distance. Capulet, by contrast, exits with the crowd and reappears in the next scene in council with Paris, already conducting the marriage policy that will organize the tragedy. The pattern of the whole play is set within a single act. Montague's one substantial scene is an inquiry that produces no action. Capulet's scenes are decisions that produce nothing else. One father asks what is wrong with his child and accepts a report. The other father decides what his child will do and accepts no report at all.

Staging underlines the split. The brawl presents the fathers as bodies, past fighting age, their swords a costume from an earlier Verona. From that shared humiliation the play sends them down separate theatrical roads. Capulet moves indoors, into the architecture of power: hall, chamber, closet, churchyard vault, spaces he owns and fills. Montague is never given an interior to occupy. He speaks in the street and in the churchyard, public ground, the ground of report and judgment. Even his grief arrives as news brought into a public inquiry. The father of the play's heroine is a householder in the fullest sense, a man whose selfhood is coextensive with his house. The father of the play's hero is a citizen, visible only where the city can see him, and the city sees him twice.

## The Servants' Quarrel and the Masters' Entrance: Whose Feud Is It?

The play's first violence is committed by employees. Sampson and Gregory, Capulet's servingmen, open the action trading boasts and biting thumbs, and the Montague servants answer in kind, so that the feud's first blood is risked by men who own no part of it. The masters then enter a fight their households have already started, and the sequence is a small lesson in how each house is governed. Capulet arrives demanding his sword, a proprietor rushing to a disturbance on his own premises, hungry to participate. His household's aggression is an extension of his temperament: the servants' swagger, the nephew's rapier, and the master's temper are one continuous instrument, and the play will spend five acts showing the instrument at work at every social level, from the street to the marriage bed.

Montague's entrance reverses the direction. He arrives asking what happened. His first instinct in his own feud is forensic, a demand for the facts from a nephew who witnessed the start of it, and the instinct governs his entire part. Where Capulet's house generates events for its master to join, Montague's house generates reports for its master to receive. The distinction explains a structural oddity audiences seldom notice: the Capulet side of the feud is always dramatized at its source, in the patriarch's temper and policy, while the Montague side is dramatized at its periphery, in servants, nephews, and a son whose motives his father must hire out the discovery of. The feud belongs, theatrically, to the house that can be seen conducting it.

It is worth registering who speaks the tragedy's first words. Not the Chorus alone, for after the sonnet the play's first voices in action are Capulet's servingmen, swaggering about the house's quarrel in prose before any principal appears. The Capulet establishment thus frames the entire performance: its servants open the action, its feast occupies the first act's climax, its marriage politics drive the middle, its vault receives the ending. The Montague establishment frames nothing, because the play never constitutes it as an establishment at all. Critics speak of the two households as the play's twin settings. The stage knows only one setting with an owner.

## A Past for One Father, None for the Other: Age and Generation

Capulet is the only parent in the play with a past. The feast gives him a youth to reminisce about, the disputed arithmetic of the last masque he danced at, settled with his cousin in a comedy of correction that establishes a whole generation's shared history: weddings attended, beauties admired, decades counted and recounted like entries in a ledger the old men keep together. Act 4 adds the Nurse's teasing portrait of the young Capulet as a merry roisterer, a mouse-hunt in his time, and the old man's half pleased denial. Even his authority has a biography. The audience understands where the master of the feast came from, what Verona formed him, and what he is afraid of losing, because the play keeps showing the long life that the authority summarizes.

Montague has no past at all. Nothing in his seven speeches reaches backward beyond the morning of the first scene. He has no reminiscence, no cousin, no anecdote, no youthful folly recalled by a servant, no account of the feud's origin from the one man who might be expected to remember it. His single question about the quarrel, who set it new abroach, is asked in genuine ignorance of an answer his own generation should possess. The omission is so complete that it reshapes the character's effect: Montague does not read as an old man with a history the play declines to show, but as a figure without duration, a father who exists only in the two presents where the action needs him. Capulet's age is a theme. Montague's is a costume. The generational reading of the play, the old world's government failing the young, is therefore built almost entirely on one household's evidence, and the familiar claim that the tragedy indicts a generation should be amended by the comparison: it indicts one father's generation, in one house, and lets the other house's elder stand as the mere name of an indictment nobody drafted.

## Montague the Questioner: A Father Who Investigates from a Distance

Montague's part, small as it is, has a precise shape, and the shape is interrogative. His first speech is a question: "Who set this ancient quarrel new abroach? / Speak, nephew, were you by when it began?" (1.1.102 to 103). His longest speech is a description offered as evidence in an inquiry he is conducting into his own son. He has watched Romeo at dawn, "with tears augmenting the fresh morning's dew" (1.1.134), stealing home as the sun rises, penning himself in a chamber with the daylight locked out, making "himself an artificial night" (1.1.140). The speech is tender, observant, and entirely helpless. The father can describe the symptoms with a poet's accuracy and cannot name the disease. His conclusion is a conditional that hangs over the whole tragedy: "Black and portentous must this humour prove, / Unless good counsel may the cause remove" (1.1.143 to 144). Good counsel is precisely what Romeo never receives from his house.

The inquiry stalls on the son's privacy, and Montague reports the stall with a diagnostician's frustration. Romeo is "his own affections' counsellor" (1.1.147), a young man who keeps his own counsel so completely that neither his father's direct approaches nor his friends' importunity can sound him. The closing couplet of the investigation is an admission of method and of defeat: "Could we but learn from whence his sorrows grow, / We would as willingly give cure as know" (1.1.155 to 156). Willingness is not the deficiency. Access is. Montague then does the most characteristic thing in his part: he delegates. Benvolio, the nephew, is commissioned to discover the cause, and the father exits the play for four acts, leaving the investigation in the hands of a young man who will extract a name, Rosaline, and mistake it for a cure. The full study of [the Montague parents, Romeo's faint and distant family](/2023/02/03/montague-parents-character-analysis/), traces how completely the household is defined by this faintness; the comparison only needs the mechanism. Montague fathers by proxy or not at all.

Set against the Capulet scenes that follow, the poverty of the instrument is stark. Capulet knows, or believes he knows, everything about his daughter: her age to the fortnight, her marriageability, her feelings, her duty. His knowledge is a form of ownership and much of it is wrong, but it is never delegated and never in doubt. Montague's knowledge is accurate as far as it goes and goes almost nowhere. He perceives that his son is melancholy, secret, and nocturnal. He does not learn of the feud's renewal in his son's life, of the marriage, of the killing, of the banishment, until the catastrophe is complete and the Prince's officers have assembled the survivors. The play withholds from Montague even the dignity of a mistake. Capulet errs grandly, in speech after speech. Montague's error is structural: he is not present when presence was the whole of the job.

There is a further cruelty in the construction. Montague speaks about Romeo to Benvolio while Romeo is offstage, and Romeo enters only after his parents have left the scene, to describe his condition himself in the oxymorons of the Rosaline infatuation. Father and son occupy the same scene in sequence and never in dialogue, a staging of non-communication so quiet that audiences rarely register it. The pattern never breaks. In the final scene Montague addresses his son's corpse. Between the dawn inquiry and the churchyard, across the marriage, the duel, the exile, and the poison, the head of the house of Montague neither speaks to his heir nor is spoken to by him. The part is a study in paternal irrelevance, executed with a restraint that makes it easy to overlook how damning the portrait is.

The construction also makes Montague his son's unwilling prologue. The audience's first knowledge of Romeo, before the young man has spoken a line, is the father's dawn report: the tears augmenting the dew, the sighs adding clouds to clouds, the chamber with daylight locked out. It is the most perceptive account of Romeo anyone in the play ever gives, and it is given by the man who can do least with it. Capulet, by contrast, never introduces Juliet. She is introduced by her mother and the Nurse, in a scene her father does not attend, while he is elsewhere arranging the terms on which she will be viewed. The difference is diagnostic. The Montague household knows its child and cannot reach him. The Capulet household reaches its child constantly and never knows her. Romeo's tragedy grows out of the first condition, a perception without power. Juliet's grows out of the second, a power without perception.

Nor does the inquiry Montague commissions ever reconvene. Benvolio undertakes the discovery, extracts the name of Rosaline within the act, and forms the optimistic plan of curing the infatuation by comparison at the Capulet feast, the very errand that delivers Romeo to Juliet. No scene returns the report to the father. Montague never learns onstage what his investigator found, never approves or forbids the remedy, never hears the beloved's name from the son he described so exactly. The commission simply lapses, the way everything in that household lapses, for want of a second meeting. Shakespeare is careful with the arithmetic of attention: Capulet is granted scene after scene in which his information improves and his decisions worsen, while Montague is granted a single scene of excellent information and is then denied the sequel in which information might have become government.

## Capulet the Commander: Host, Broker, and Master of the House

Capulet's mode is the imperative. Where Montague asks questions and commissions reports, Capulet issues instructions and expects the world to reassemble around them. His first appearance as a father, in council with Paris at 1.2, wears the dress of consultation: Juliet is the sole surviving hope of his house, "the hopeful lady of my earth" (1.2.15), and her consent is solemnly written into the settlement, "my will to her consent is but a part" (1.2.17). The full arc of that conditional tenderness, from the indulgent host to the tyrant of the third act, is traced in [Lord Capulet's study, from loving father to furious tyrant](/2022/12/02/lord-capulet-character-analysis/). What the comparison needs is the clause's other side. Consent, in Capulet's grammar, is a component of his will, delegated downward and revocable from above. The suitor is chosen by the father, the season is set by the father, and the daughter's part is to ratify. It is a kinder arrangement than the period's norm and it is an arrangement, and the play will enforce the distinction at sword point.

The feast at 1.5 shows the same sovereignty in public. Capulet protects the disguised Romeo under his own roof, refusing to let the enemy be touched "for the wealth of all this town" (1.5.71), and in the same breath reduces Tybalt, the house's champion, to a scolded boy for presuming to conduct policy without leave: "Am I the master here, or you?" (1.5.77). Generosity and discipline are one gesture. The host's hall is a theater of his authority, and every act performed in it, the welcome, the reminiscence, the protection of the guest, the crushing of the nephew, demonstrates that a single will governs within those walls. Montague has no walls. He has never been seen to govern anything, not a feast, not a servant, not a conversation with his own son. The contrast is not between a strong father and a weak one in the abstract. It is between a father whose authority has a stage, a staff, and a script, and a father whose authority exists only as a question he asks his nephew in the street.

The feast also exposes the limit that sovereignty cannot see. Capulet's hall admits Romeo because the machinery of display is impersonal: a servant who cannot read carries the guest list into the street, and the enemy's name is spoken aloud by the enemy himself as a favor. The house opens its doors to exhibit its standing, and the exhibition is the breach through which the catastrophe enters. Montague's household suffers the reciprocal defect. It displays nothing, invites nobody, maintains no public machinery at all, and so it is breached by nothing and learns nothing; its catastrophe arrives by report, four acts later, in a churchyard. One father is ruined by the openness of a house run as a theater of power. The other is ruined by the closure of a house that never appears. The play distributes even its accidents along the fathers' dividing line: Capulet's world is porous because it is magnificent, Montague's is sealed because it is absent.

### What does Capulet promise Paris in Act 3 Scene 4 that he would not promise in Act 1 Scene 2?

In the later scene he promises the marriage itself, on a day he names, Thursday, with his daughter's consent treated as a formality he guarantees rather than a condition he awaits. In the earlier scene he had offered only a courtship, with two summers to pass and Juliet's agreement written into the bargain.

The suitor deserves a word of pressure analysis before the bargain, because 1.2 is often misread as Capulet resisting the marriage market when he is in fact setting its terms against a buyer in a hurry. Paris opens with the market's standard argument, that younger girls than Juliet are already happy mothers, and Capulet's reply, that those so early made are too soon marred (1.2.12 to 13), is the prudence of a monopolist, not of a reformer. He is protecting the value and the timing of a unique asset, his only surviving child, and the two summers he demands are a holding period whose terms he alone will later decide to waive. Montague never faces a negotiation remotely like it. No suitor petitions him, no asset of his house is courted by the Prince's kin, and the one marriage his house contracts is concluded behind a friar's door without his signature. The marriage market of Verona conducts its visible business through one father only, which is why the play's economics of alliance, so central to its criticism, are in practice a study of Capulet's ledger.

The midnight bargain of 3.4 is the hinge of the comparison. Tybalt is dead, the house is in mourning, and Capulet, sitting late with Paris, converts grief into policy. His daughter, he reports, has been undone by her cousin's death, and a marriage will settle her: "I think she will be ruled / In all respects by me; nay, more, I doubt it not" (3.4.13 to 14). The consent clause of 1.2 has been silently struck out. Juliet is discussed in the third person, as an asset whose compliance the broker underwrites, and the wedding day is brought forward in the same conversation, Wednesday judged too soon and Thursday fixed, as if speed itself were a poultice. Nothing in Montague's part corresponds to this scene, and the absence is instructive. Romeo's marriage, the act that binds the houses, is arranged by a friar in a cell, without either father's knowledge. Juliet's marriage is arranged twice by her father, once as courtship and once as command. One child is married in a room her father controls the door of. The other is married in a room his father has never entered.

## The Middle Acts: Escalation on One Side, Silence on the Other

The center of the play widens the gap to its full extent. In the sentencing that follows the duel at 3.1, neither father appears. The house of Capulet is represented by its wife, pleading for Romeo's death with a partisan's disregard for the evidence, while the house of Montague has no voice in the proceedings at all: no parent pleads the son's provocation, no parent hears the sentence of exile fall. The feud's proprietors are absent when its bill is presented. For Capulet the absence is a delegation, one more errand run by a subordinate. For Montague it is the condition of his existence in the play. The machinery of Verona, prince, law, feud, wives, nephews, friars, processes both children without ever requiring the younger father's father to attend.

The sentence of exile, handed down in that fatherless court, reaches the Montague household only as weather reaches a distant province, and its casualty there is reported two acts later in a subordinate clause: Romeo's mother dies of grief at his banishment, offstage, unmourned onstage, her death delivered to the audience as an item in her husband's entrance speech at the tomb. Consider what the construction withholds. The one member of the senior Montagues whose feeling might have pleaded with the Prince, visited the exile, or written to Mantua is removed from the play without a scene, and her removal is itself the household's characteristic event, a catastrophe experienced entirely at second hand. The Capulets, by contrast, suffer their losses in the center of the stage with the whole company attending. Even bereavement obeys the comparison's law: in one house it is enacted, in the other it is announced.

What follows belongs to Capulet alone. The rage of 3.5, in which the consent clause is called in and the daughter is threatened with disowning, with begging and starving in the streets, is the domestic center of the tragedy, and its line by line anatomy belongs to its own study. The comparison registers only its place in the fathers' ledger. Here is the surplus of fatherhood at its most destructive: a parent so present, so practiced in command, that a daughter's refusal registers as treason against the house and is answered with the house's full armory of shame. Then, with the same energy, the part pivots into Act 4: the apology accepted without being heard, the father bustling through his own kitchens before dawn, teased by the Nurse, drunk on the bustle of a wedding he believes will crown his policy, until the bride is found apparently dead and the armory collapses into operatic grief: "Death is my son-in-law, Death is my heir; / My daughter he hath wedded" (4.5.38 to 39). Rage, bustle, lament: the registers change, the occupation of the stage does not. Capulet fills the middle of the play the way he fills his hall.

The apology scene of Act 4 Scene 2 deserves its place in the ledger, because it shows the commander's government at its most characteristic and most dangerous: forgiveness as an administrative act. Juliet kneels, performs repentance in the formulas her father requires, and is pardoned at a speed that reveals how little of the transaction concerns her actual state. Capulet's heart, he announces, is "wondrous light" since his wayward girl is "so reclaimed" (4.2.46 to 47), and his first act of restored authority is to advance the wedding by a day, sending for Paris and ordering the knot knit up on the Wednesday morning. The reconciliation lasts one speech and is instantly converted into acceleration. A Montague version of the scene is unthinkable, not because Montague would forgive more slowly but because no one in his house would know what needed forgiving, or would think to kneel, or would find the father at home to kneel to. The Capulet machine processes even remorse into scheduling. Its efficiency is the tragedy's engine, and it never idles.

Act 4 Scene 4 shows the machine at its most attractive, which is why it must be counted in the indictment. Before dawn on the wedding morning the master of the house is in his own kitchens, rousing the servants, calling for logs and dates and quinces, noting the second cock and the curfew bell with a quartermaster's precision. The bustle is lovable, and productions play it for warmth, the old tyrant softened into a bustling father of the bride. The comparison insists on the colder observation underneath the warmth. This is command at its most granular: a government that personally inspects the spices cannot imagine delegating the one inspection that mattered, a look at its daughter's face with a question attached. Montague, at the opposite pole, inspects nothing in person, not even his son's grief, which he observes from a distance and then hands to an agent. Between the father in the kitchen at three in the morning and the father who has not entered his son's room in the whole play lies the entire spectrum of the tragedy's domestic failure, diligence without inquiry on one side, inquiry without presence on the other.

Montague fills nothing. Across Acts 2, 3, and 4 he speaks no line, appears in no scene, and is mentioned only in passing. His son falls in love, marries, kills, is banished, and despairs, and at no point does the action route itself through the boy's father or even pause at his door. The causes of that long silence belong to [the feud's own history, and the question of why the Capulets and Montagues hate](/2022/04/01/capulet-montague-feud-explained/), has its full treatment elsewhere; here the silence is measured as a fact of construction. Romeo's tragedy is assembled entirely from substitutes: a friar for a confessor, a friend for a brother, an apothecary for a last resort. The office of father stands vacant from the first act's dawn to the fifth act's churchyard, and the vacancy is never remarked by anyone onstage, which is the play's quietest and most complete judgment on the house of Montague.

## The Word Father: Who Gets Called It, and Who Does the Calling

The play's distribution of the word itself tells the same story in miniature. Juliet addresses and invokes her father across the action: in supplication, in apology, in terror, kneeling to promise that henceforward she will be ruled by him. Whether she means it or performs it, the word is current coin in her house, spent in scene after scene. Romeo never addresses his father at all, in any scene, in any register, because the two are never given a scene in which address could occur. The vocative exists on one side of Verona and not on the other. A title, like an office, lapses when it is never used, and by the fifth act the word father, spoken in the Montague interest, belongs to someone else entirely.

For Romeo does acquire a father onstage, and the substitution is one of the play's plainest structural rhymes. Friar Laurence is addressed as father by both lovers, receives their confidences, arranges their marriage, devises their escapes, and confesses his part in their deaths, performing in sequence every function that a parent in this world is expected to perform, counsel, consent, provision, rescue, and mourning. Romeo greets him as his ghostly father and brings him, within minutes of their first scene together, the announcement of the Juliet love and the request for the marriage rite, confidences the boy's actual father has been trying and failing to extract through an intermediary for the whole of the preceding act. The speed of the transfer is the measure of the vacancy. A son does not confide his heart's entire revolution to a friar at dawn unless the paternal office at home has been standing empty long enough for the understudy to be a familiar figure. He performs them for the Montague heir in the Montague father's total absence, and for the Capulet daughter against her father's explicit prohibition. The tragedy can be charted as the transfer of fatherhood from the two men who hold the title to the one man who does the work. Capulet loses his daughter to the Friar's cell because his fatherhood was government without confidence. Montague loses his son to the same cell because his fatherhood was concern without presence. The cell receives what both houses failed to hold, and the fathers meet for the last time in a churchyard where the substitute stands between them, giving evidence.

## The Lovers under the Two Fathers: Government Experienced from Below

The comparison completes itself in the children's experience, because the two regimes of fatherhood produce the two halves of the catastrophe with mechanical precision. Juliet lives inside a government. Her father's will reaches her through every channel her world possesses: her mother's embassy, the Nurse's gossip, Paris's suit, the household's calendar, and finally his own voice at her bedside with the disowning threat. She cannot take a step, marry, refuse, confess, or sleep, without the government responding. Her tragedy is consequently a tragedy of counter government: secrets, feigned obedience, a potion administered like a coup, all the apparatus of a subject escaping a state that sees everything except her. The play gives her exactly one room of her own, the chamber of the potion soliloquy, and she spends it rehearsing terrors alone, because solitude is the only privacy a surveilled daughter can obtain.

Romeo lives outside a government, in a liberty so complete it resembles abandonment. No permission is sought for his marriage because no authority exists in his world from whom permission could be sought; his father is not defied, circumvented, or even consulted in imagination. Where Juliet must deceive a father to act, Romeo need only fail to think of one. His tragedy is consequently a tragedy of unassisted improvisation: a secret marriage arranged with a friar, a revenge killing answered to no one, a suicide purchased from an apothecary in a strange city, each decision taken in a single afternoon by a young man whom no senior person has ever been shown advising, restraining, or even addressing. The lovers' famous haste is usually blamed on youth and stars. The comparison assigns it a domestic address. One child had too much government to deliberate under. The other had no government to deliberate with. Deliberation lives between those extremes, in households where fathers are present enough to consult and restrained enough to hear, and Verona contains no such household on either side of its feud.

The difference can be put as a test of imagination. Juliet can predict her father. She knows his temper's timetable, plays to his expectation of obedience, times her feigned apology to his vanity, and calculates, correctly, that a performance of submission will move the wedding machinery exactly as she needs it moved. Her knowledge of him is intimate, operational, and useless for anything except escape. Romeo cannot predict his father, because the play has given him nothing to predict from: no remembered rebuke, no known preference, no household rule is ever quoted in Romeo's speech. When Romeo needs a senior's help he invents the relationship from nothing with the Friar, and when he needs money's worth of death he buys it from a stranger in Mantua. A son who has never been governed cannot even rebel; rebellion requires an edict, and the Montague house issued none that the play records.

The asymmetry also decides what each child dies believing about the old world. Juliet dies in defiance of a father she has outwitted, her last acts a refutation of his settlement. Romeo dies in ignorance of any paternal judgment at all, his final speech addressed to death, to the tomb, and to the beloved, in a world from which the senior Montague has simply been edited out. The fathers then inherit the ending their regimes prepared: the governor receives a corpse and a courtroom in which his government is the unspoken charge, and the absent man receives a corpse and discovers, over it, the one paternal sentence he ever completes, a reproach to the dead for dying untaught by a teacher who never came.

Read down the columns and the pattern is consistent at every level of the construction, from arithmetic to architecture. The counts alone would make the point: a part of over two hundred lines against a part of fewer than forty, nine scenes against two. But the table's deeper rows matter more than its numbers. The two men fail in opposite directions along every axis the comparison can measure. Capulet's knowledge of his child is a fiction of ownership, asserted and enforced; Montague's is a genuine inquiry, delegated and abandoned. Capulet's household is the play's great interior machine, staffed by servants, a nurse, a wife, a nephew with a sword; Montague's household never acquires a single room. Even the nephews rhyme in opposition: Tybalt extends Capulet's will as violence, Benvolio extends Montague's will as investigation, and both instruments fail their principals in the same street fight. The fathers comparison exposes a play that distributes fatherhood the way it distributes everything else, by counterpoint, giving one man the whole office and the other man only its title.

Two rows of the table reward a slower reading. The first is the row of direct speech to the child. Capulet's entries are a tirade, an apology accepted, and a lament over a body: every direct exercise of his fatherhood onstage occurs after the relationship has already been destroyed, as punishment, as pardon for a submission she is feigning, or as grief over a corpse that is not yet a corpse. Even the play's most present father never simply converses with his daughter while anything can still be decided. The second is the row of the wives. Lady Capulet survives to speak at the tomb; Lady Montague dies offstage, of grief, between the acts, and is present in the final scene only as a sentence in her husband's mouth. The asymmetry descends a generation: one household loses its daughter onstage and keeps its mother, the other loses its mother offstage and its son onstage, and the fathers' closing exchange is shadowed by the empty places beside each of them.

## The Fathers in the Sources: What Shakespeare Inherited and What He Changed

The imbalance has a genealogy, and the genealogy clears Shakespeare of the charge of carelessness by showing exactly where his hand worked. The story reached him through a line of tellings, Italian novella and French adaptation and English poem and prose, and in that line the fathers are functionaries of the ending. In Luigi da Porto's version and in the retellings that follow it, the heads of the two houses matter chiefly as the parties to be reconciled; the narrative interest sits with the lovers, the friar, and the machinery of the secret marriage, and the old men arrive at the close to perform the social repair the catastrophe requires. Arthur Brooke's English poem of 1562, Shakespeare's principal source, keeps the same distribution. Its fathers are named, mourned with, and reconciled, but they are not built as characters across the action, and Brooke's moralizing preface distributes blame in general terms rather than through a dramatized patriarch.

William Painter's prose version in The Palace of Pleasure, the other English conduit of the tale, is instructive for the same reason. Painter's fathers are parties to a settlement, figures of the closing pages whose joint appearance ratifies the moral the narrator draws, and the monuments they raise over the lovers are described as civic facts rather than as the climax of two dramatized lives. Shakespeare inherits the monuments and invents the lives, on one side only. Reading backward from the play to its sources produces a surprise that no amount of attention to the play alone can supply: the golden statues, so often taken as the emblem of the fathers' equality, were equal in the sources because the fathers were equally slight. The play keeps the equal statues and spends five acts destroying the equality they commemorate, rebuilding it only as an expenditure after the inequality has done its work.

A small onomastic fact belongs to the genealogy. Neither father is given a personal name anywhere in the play: both are known by the names of their houses, Capulet and Montague, as if the dramatis personae itself regarded them as institutions rather than men. The interest of the fact lies in how unequally the two escape it. Capulet accumulates so much individuating life, the remembered masques, the temper, the kitchens, the tears, that audiences feel they know a man and rarely notice he has no Christian name at all. Montague accumulates nothing, and so the house name is all there is: his part is a surname with seven speeches, an institution that never becomes a person. The sources, which are generous with names for minor figures, cannot be blamed for the effect. It is the play's own distribution of interiority, legible even in the cast list.

Brooke does give the Montague father one distinction Shakespeare quietly drops: in the poem's aftermath, the old man, broken by his son's death, himself dies of grief soon after the reconciliation, so that the feud consumes a third member of his house. Shakespeare transfers the death to Lady Montague and reports it in her husband's voice at the tomb, a change that concentrates the Montague losses into a single entrance speech and leaves the father alive to make the vow. The alteration is small and entirely characteristic. Where the source gives the Montague father a death, the play gives him a line of dialogue about a death, measured out like everything else in his part. His wife's offstage end does the emotional work; he is kept standing because the ceremony needs two hands to clasp.

Against that inherited slightness, Shakespeare's expansion of Capulet stands out as one of the play's largest inventions. Nothing in the sources prepares a father who dominates the first act with a consent speech, presides over a feast, bargains at midnight, rages in the play's most violent domestic scene, runs a wedding from his own kitchens, and collapses over the bride's body. The playwright built that part the way he built the Nurse and Mercutio, by giving a functional figure the run of scene after scene until function became character. He made no corresponding investment on the Montague side. The conclusion the genealogy supports is the one the texts support: Montague is not a diminished Capulet. He is the source's father, preserved at source scale, and Capulet is the anomaly, the patriarch a dramatist invented when he decided that one household would carry the whole weight of the old world.

## Design or Cut Text: Why Is Montague So Thin?

The complication must be faced squarely: a part this small invites the suspicion that something has been lost. Perhaps Montague was fuller in a draft that never reached print. Perhaps the playing company trimmed him, doubling his actor elsewhere and cutting the part to a frame. Perhaps the early texts abbreviate a role that performance once filled out. The hypothesis is respectable and it deserves a verdict on evidence rather than on taste.

The evidence runs against it. The play survives in two early quartos of very different character, the compressed first quarto of 1597 and the fuller second quarto of 1599, and in the Folio text derived from the second quarto tradition. These witnesses disagree about a great deal: whole speeches swell and shrink between them, and the first quarto's economy falls on many parts. On Montague they agree. His share is a sketch in every surviving text, in roughly the same proportion, at the same two stations of the action. A cut imposed for one set of performances might scar one witness. A design present from the start explains why all three preserve the same silhouette. Editors who have worked the text most closely, Weis in the Arden Third Series among them, print the small part without positing lost material, because there is no gap for lost material to fill: the scenes Montague is absent from are complete without him, their business fully staffed by wives, nephews, friars, and the Prince.

Performance explanations, often raised at this point, prove on inspection to support the design verdict rather than the cut hypothesis. A playing company doubling its actors has every reason to keep a part small when the same performer is wanted elsewhere in a crowded play, and the two-appearance shape of Montague, first scene and last, is exactly the shape a doubling schedule would choose. But doubling explains the preservation of a small part, not the amputation of a large one. Had Shakespeare written a Montague as rich as his Capulet, the company that profited from this play's success would have had every incentive to keep him, and at least one of the three early texts, above all the performance derived first quarto, would be expected to preserve some trace of the richer version. No trace exists. The most the stage can explain is why the small part was convenient. The smallness itself was written.

The sources tell the same story from the other direction. In Arthur Brooke's poem and in the prose versions behind it, both fathers are minor figures who matter chiefly at the close, where they reconcile and raise monuments over the children. Shakespeare's transformation of the tale is famous for its enlargements, and the enlargement here is one sided. He took the Capulet father of the sources, a name in a closing reconciliation, and built him into the tragedy's domestic engine, scene by scene, feast, bargain, rage, and lament. He left Montague at approximately the scale the sources gave him. The asymmetry, in other words, is not what remains after something was taken away from Montague. It is what was created when everything was given to Capulet. The sketch is not damaged goods. It is the unexpanded original, kept in frame so that the expansion on the other side can be measured against it.

### Is Montague's thinness in the play a sign that his part was cut?

No, on the evidence that survives. Every early text, the compressed quarto of 1597, the fuller quarto of 1599, and the Folio, gives Montague the same small part at the same two points of the action, and no scene he is absent from shows a gap where a speech has been removed.

The act distribution sharpens the point into a map. Capulet appears in all five acts, the only parent of either lover of whom that is true, and his appearances trace the tragedy's whole arc from brawl to feast to bargain to bustle to tomb. Montague appears in Act 1 and Act 5 only, the frame acts, the acts of public ceremony at the play's two ends, and is missing from the entire middle, the acts of private decision where the catastrophe is actually assembled. A cut part would be expected to leave ragged edges, entrances prepared and not used, references dangling. Montague's distribution is the opposite of ragged: it is architectural, a pillar at each end of the building and open space between, exactly where a dramatist would place a figure meant to frame an action rather than drive it.

Design also explains what cutting cannot: the part's symmetry of placement. Montague opens the fathers' action in the first scene, with the play's first parental inquiry, and he closes it in the last, with the play's last parental vow. Between those stations his absence is total and, this comparison argues, functional. The tragedy requires the feud to have two heads for its heraldry and its closing ceremony, and it requires Romeo to be unparented for its plot: no father must be available to forbid the marriage, shelter the exile, or intercept the poison. A fuller Montague would be a dramaturgical problem, a responsible adult standing in the wings of every catastrophe. Shakespeare keeps him exactly as large as the design can afford, a father reduced to the two moments when fatherhood is ceremonial: the asking and the mourning.

## Feast, Wedding, Funeral: Capulet as the Play's Master of Ceremonies

Count the ceremonies and the comparison sharpens again. Capulet presides over nearly every rite the play contains. He hosts the feast at which the lovers meet, sets the wedding that would have married his daughter to Paris, commands its preparations in his own kitchens, converts it in a single speech into a funeral when the bride is found apparently dead, orders the feast meats to become funeral baked meats and the musicians to silence, and finally co-presides, with Montague, over the rite of reconciliation in the churchyard. Verona's social liturgy runs through his hands. He is the celebrant of his city's joys and of its mourning alike, and the play's deepest irony about him is a celebrant's irony: every ceremony he conducts produces its opposite. The feast of welcome admits the enemy who will ruin the house. The wedding becomes a funeral. The funeral becomes the antechamber of a second, real one. The reconciliation crowns two men who have nothing left to reconcile over except graves.

The conversion of the wedding is performed in his own voice, the celebrant re-consecrating his arrangements in real time: "All things that we ordained festival / Turn from their office to black funeral" (4.5.84 to 85). No other character in the play is permitted to rewrite a ceremony mid performance. The Prince can sentence, the Friar can scheme, the Nurse can mourn, but the power to declare what rite the household is performing, and to turn its office from festival to funeral with a sentence, belongs to the master of the house alone. Shakespeare concentrates the liturgy of Verona in Capulet's hands so completely that the city's emotional weather is whatever this one old man proclaims it to be, sun at the feast, frost at the funeral, a glooming peace at the tomb where for once the proclamation is taken out of his hands and given to the Prince.

Montague presides over nothing. No rite in the play is his: not a feast, not a betrothal, not a funeral, although his house supplies a corpse for the last of them. He attends the closing ceremony as a participant rather than a celebrant, and even there the active verbs, the offering of the hand, the framing of the settlement, belong to Capulet before Montague's single splendid answer. The ceremonial poverty matches the domestic poverty the earlier sections measured. A father who has no rooms has no rites, because rites require rooms, staffs, and occasions, the whole furnished apparatus of household life. Shakespeare's Verona keeps its calendar in one house. The other house keeps only its name, and a name, as the play's most famous speech insists in another context, is the one possession that cannot bury anyone or marry anyone or feed a feud's next course. It can only be carved, at the last, in gold.

## The Fathers and the Prince: Two Governments under a Failing State

Above both households stands the Prince, and his treatment of the fathers is the state's mirror of the Prologue's error. Escalus addresses the patriarchs as the responsible units of Verona, summoning them by name, fining them by implication, and threatening their lives for their servants' brawls, on the theory that the city's disorder flows from two governors who will not govern. The theory is half right, and the play shows which half. Capulet governs too much and in the wrong direction, converting his household into the feud's engine room. Montague does not govern at all, and his house's disorder flows through the vacancy where a governor should be. The Prince's paired edicts cannot distinguish between the two failures, and so they fail with both: his penalties fall on the houses jointly, his mercy at the duel is shaped by a Capulet plea with no Montague answer, and his closing scourge names the pair in one breath for catastrophes they caused by opposite means.

The state's founding image of the fathers is fixed in the first scene and never revised. When the Prince delivers his edict, threatening the patriarchs' lives if their households disturb the streets again, the two men stand before him as they entered, restrained by their wives, indistinguishable in posture and in guilt. Verona's law forms its picture of the feud at that moment, a picture of matched and mutual belligerence, and governs from the picture for the rest of the play. The picture was already false by the second scene, when one of the restrained old men began the marriage policy that would do what no street brawl could do, while the other walked home to a son he could not reach. No institution in the play ever updates the image. The church marries in ignorance of both fathers, the law sentences in absence of both, and the final court, presented at last with the evidence, can only lay a scourge on the pair jointly and accept the gold. The comparison's political finding is that the feud survived not because Verona lacked an account of it, but because Verona's account was of two equal fathers, and no remedy framed for equals can touch a disease of imbalance.

The political moral the comparison extracts is unfashionable and exact. Verona's tragedy is not that its fathers were wicked, nor that its prince was weak, but that authority in the city existed in two defective quantities, concentrated where it should have been questioned and absent where it should have acted, while the law applied its remedies to the symmetry it imagined rather than to the imbalance that was killing people. The final scene's court is the first institution in the play to see the fathers as they actually are, a commander and a mourner, and it sees them only when seeing can no longer save anyone.

## Commands and Questions: The Grammar of the Two Fathers

The contrast survives translation into grammar. Capulet's characteristic moods are the imperative and the appointed future. He summons, forbids, fixes days, and announces what will happen in his house with the confidence of a man issuing a calendar: guests will come, a wedding will be on Thursday, a daughter will be ruled. Even his courtesies are structured as rulings, decisions handed down from the head of the table about who may be welcomed and who must be silent. His sentences end in arrangements. When the arrangements fail, the same grammar produces the tirade, a storm of imperatives and expulsions, grammar trying to restore by volume the obedience that arrangement could not secure.

Montague's characteristic moods are the interrogative and the conditional. He asks who began the brawl, asks what ails his son, asks whether inquiry has been made, and frames his parental hope as a condition awaiting knowledge: could the cause be learned, the cure would follow willingly. His sentences end in questions or in hypotheses, and the play never promotes him out of them. The one speech in which he finally acquires the declarative force of his opposite number is the vow at the tomb, "For I will raise her statue in pure gold" (5.3.299), a future tense as firm as any of Capulet's appointments. The acquisition is the tragedy's last grammatical irony. Montague learns to speak like a patriarch, in binding commitments about the disposition of wealth, at the moment when there is nothing left for a patriarch to govern. Capulet's grammar ruled a living house and killed it. Montague's grammar found its ruling voice only over the dead.

The wives' language measures the same divide from inside the marriages. Lady Capulet argues, pleads, denounces, and reports, a full participant in the verbal economy of her house, while Lady Montague's lines can be counted on one hand and her most consequential act, her death, is performed offstage and reported in her husband's interrogative world as news. Even grief is distributed by the rule of the comparison. The Capulet household experiences everything in speech, at length, in the room where it happens. The Montague household experiences its worst events elsewhere and receives them as information.

The pronouns keep the same account. Capulet's speech is dense with the first person of possession: my child, my house, my will, my friend, the recurring grammar of a man for whom family relations are entries in a personal ledger. Montague's longest utterances run in the first person plural and the conditional, the grammar of a committee of two worried parents and a helpful nephew, hoping that knowledge might be obtainable and cure might follow. Possession against consultation, the singular against the plural: the play's whole argument about the two houses can be heard in their pronouns before it is seen in their scenes. And it is the plural speaker, the man who says we, whose house ends the play extinguished in its senior line, while the singular speaker's house at least retains its mistress and its master to stand in the churchyard. Grammar is not fate, but in this play it keeps predicting the casualties correctly.

## Critics on the Two Households, the Asymmetry, and the Ending

Coppelia Kahn's reading of the play, the most influential account of its households, treats the two families as a single patriarchal system rather than as two portraits. In Kahn's account the feud is a rite of masculine passage policed by fathers, and daughters circulate within it as tokens of alliance between men; the Paris marriage is the system's normal business, and the lovers' secret marriage is a theft from it. On this reading the imbalance between the fathers is secondary, because the system needs only one working embodiment, and Capulet is it. Montague's thinness is what a system looks like when it is not being exercised. The reading has great explanatory force for Capulet's half of the comparison: the consent clause, the midnight bargain, and the rage are the system defending its property at successive levels of alarm.

Harry Levin's formal criticism approaches the pair from the opposite end, from the play's architecture of paired oppositions, high and low, public and private, the world of the fathers set against the world of the children. In a design so fond of symmetry, Levin's approach leads a reader to expect the fathers to be balanced as the lovers are balanced, matched in the sonnet they share and the tomb they share it in. The discovery that the pairing fails at the level of the text is therefore more than a curiosity of casting. It marks the limit of the play's formalism: the pattern of twos that governs the imagery and the structure is deliberately broken in the dramatis personae, as if the play were announcing that symmetry is a property of its language and its ceremonies, not of its human material. The fathers are the broken pair in a play of pairs, and the break is where the life is.

The editorial tradition answers from the documents. Weis's Arden edition, like the Oxford edition of Jill Levenson before it, treats the transmitted text as the object of study, and on the asymmetry the documents are unambiguous: no early witness knows a larger Montague. Where Kahn explains why one father suffices, the editors establish that one father is what Shakespeare's text, in every form in which it reached print, actually contains. The two approaches are often taught as rivals, system against document, but on this question they interlock. The documentary record rules out the lost Montague. The systemic reading explains the Montague who remains: a patriarch whose authority is never exercised onstage because the play has assigned the exercise of patriarchy, in all its registers, to the other house. The class dimension of that assignment, the ranking of households and the servants who carry their quarrels, is mapped in [the account of the two households and the power and class structure of Verona](/2022/04/22/two-households-class-power-verona/).

Susan Snyder's account of the ending takes the argument to the reconciliation. Snyder reads the play through the comic matrix: a comedy of young lovers and blocking elders that turns tragic at its midpoint, when the deaths begin. On that reading the closing reconciliation is comedy's own machinery, the feast of concord in which the old men relent and the generations are joined, arriving after the catastrophe instead of before it. The handshake is thus a generic ghost, the ending of the play that might have been, performed over the bodies of the children it should have saved. The reading is powerful and this comparison accepts half of it. The ceremony is indeed belated to the point of obscenity. But the adjudication offered here qualifies Snyder with the scene's own procedure. The reconciliation does not happen at a feast. It happens inside a judgment, under the Prince's presidency, after witnesses have been heard and sentences implied, and its instruments are not embraces but a contract and a pair of monuments. Comedy's machinery explains the handshake's shape. It does not explain its chill. The chill comes from the fathers themselves, who reconcile in the only currencies they have ever used: a hand offered like a settlement and gold offered like a price.

The three accounts can now be adjudicated in sequence, since each governs a different floor of the structure. The editors govern the ground floor of fact: the asymmetry is authorial, present in every early text, and no lost Montague waits to be restored. Kahn governs the first floor of meaning: the system of patriarchal exchange explains why the play needs only one fully working father, since the machine's operations are identical in both houses and a single demonstration exhausts them. Snyder governs the top floor of feeling: the ending's ceremony is comedy's reconciliation arriving after its deadline, which is why the handshake moves audiences and disturbs them in the same instant. Where the accounts conflict, on the question of whether Montague's thinness is a statement or merely a consequence, this comparison rules for statement. A dramatist who needed only economy had a cheaper option available, distributing the Capulet scenes between two moderate fathers and letting the system show two working parts. He chose instead the extremity of one colossus and one shadow, and extremity in this playwright is always the signature of intent.

## The Reconciliation in Act 5 Scene 3: A Handshake and Two Golden Statues

Before the fathers can reconcile, the scene makes them undergo the experience each had separately inflicted on the action: both must receive the truth of their own houses as a report delivered by others. The Friar narrates the marriage, the potion, and the failed letter; Balthasar produces the letter Romeo wrote; the watch produces the bodies and the instruments. Capulet, who governed his household on information supplied by wives, nurses, and suitors, learns the decisive facts of his daughter's life from a friar's confession in a public inquiry, the report system of his own house turned against him at the last. Montague, who never obtained a report worth the name in his life, finally receives a complete one, and it is the story of his son's death. The symmetry of the scene's procedure, both patriarchs reduced to an audience at the trial of their own regimes, is the precondition of the handshake. Neither man can offer peace as a governor. Both can offer it only as survivors, and the scene is careful to strip the governorship away, in evidence, before it permits the clasp.

The final scene assembles the survivors as a court, and the Prince addresses the fathers as a pair, in the Prologue's own grammar: "Capulet! Montague! / See what a scourge is laid upon your hate, / That heaven finds means to kill your joys with love" (5.3.291 to 293). The pairing is just, and the scene that follows is built to test it, because the two men enter the ceremony from opposite conditions. Montague arrives already diminished past Capulet's state. His first speech in the scene is a death report, and the death is his wife's: "Alas, my liege, my wife is dead tonight; / Grief of my son's exile hath stopped her breath" (5.3.210 to 211). The house of Montague has lost its heir and its mother before the reconciliation begins, and its head speaks as a man tallying a completed ruin. His address to his son's corpse is the part's most piercing moment, a reproach that is really a confession: "O thou untaught! What manners is in this, / To press before thy father to a grave?" (5.3.216 to 217). Untaught is the word the whole comparison has been waiting for. The son was never taught, because the teacher never appeared. Montague speaks the indictment of his own absence over the body it produced, apparently without hearing it.

Capulet speaks first in the exchange that ends the feud, and the order is the last expression of the play's asymmetry. It is the dominant father who initiates the peace, crossing to his enemy with an offer framed in the language of the marriage market he has operated all play: "O brother Montague, give me thy hand. / This is my daughter's jointure, for no more / Can I demand" (5.3.296 to 298). Jointure is the settlement a bride brings or receives, the property term of a marriage contract. Capulet's daughter never received her jointure in life, because her marriages happened in a friar's cell and a tomb. Her father now pays it into the peace, denominating the reconciliation as her marriage portion, the one dowry the feud left her. The word is a whole career in miniature: even Capulet's remorse is transacted in the vocabulary of settlement, and the tragedy of his fatherhood is that the vocabulary was never the problem. The love inside it was real, and it arrived, as everything of his arrived, as a term of contract.

Montague answers, and for the first time in the play the smaller part outbids the larger. "But I can give thee more: / For I will raise her statue in pure gold" (5.3.298 to 299). The vow's object deserves emphasis: Montague promises a statue not of his own son but of Juliet, the enemy's daughter, "true and faithful Juliet" (5.3.302), while Capulet's answering vow raises Romeo. Each father monumentalizes the other's child. The crossing is the reconciliation's true mechanism, more eloquent than the handshake, because it enacts in expenditure what the lovers enacted in marriage: each house takes the other's offspring into its own treasury of honor. Capulet closes the exchange, and the fathers' dialogue in the play, with the matching promise and its bleak summary: "As rich shall Romeo's by his lady's lie; / Poor sacrifices of our enmity!" (5.3.303 to 304).

One word in the exchange carries a weight the handshake alone could not. Capulet addresses his enemy as "brother Montague" (5.3.296), the first kinship term ever applied between the houses by anyone except the lovers, whose marriage made the men brothers in law in a cell two days and several corpses earlier. The word arrives as a ratification of a fact the children created and the fathers refused: the houses were already joined, by the rite Montague never knew of and Capulet never consented to, and the peace merely signs what the marriage had sealed. That the word falls to Capulet is the distribution's last precision. The father who spent the play defending his house's boundaries is the one who finally pronounces its boundary dissolved, while the father who never defended anything, having never been told there was anything to defend, answers with treasure. After Capulet's matching vow the two men speak no more. The fathers' dialogue ends not in an embrace described but in silence, and the state, in the person of the Prince, resumes the play and closes it. Private reconciliation has exactly four speeches in this play: an offer, a vow, a counter vow, and a reckoning of sacrifices. The economy is Montague's scale imposed, at the end, on both of them.

### Who reaches for the other's hand first at the tomb?

Capulet reaches first. After the Prince has laid the scourge of the deaths on both houses, it is the Capulet father who crosses to his enemy, names him brother, and offers his hand as his daughter's jointure, and Montague answers the gesture afterward with the vow of the golden statue.

In performance the handshake is the scene's physical crux, and productions solve the same problem: the two old men must reach each other across a stage occupied by the bodies of their children. The clasp is therefore never a simple greeting. It is negotiated over corpses, in the presence of the Prince's court, and it is usually held in silence long enough for the audience to register what it cost and how little it repairs. The statues complete the problem in another register. A handshake is transient; gold is permanent, and both fathers instinctively convert their reconciliation into the permanent form, as if each distrusted the other's feeling to outlast the morning. The Prince's closing lines accept the conversion without endorsing it: "A glooming peace this morning with it brings" (5.3.305). Peace has been purchased. The play's final judgment on its price is the epitaph he speaks over the lovers, not over the fathers, and the fathers exit into a Verona that will remember their children as monuments and their feud as the cost of raising them.

The statues deserve one further look, because they are the fathers' last acts of parenting and they repeat, in gold, the habits that destroyed the children. Capulet, who treated his daughter as the house's supreme asset, commissions an asset: a figure of Romeo at a rate no other figure in Verona shall match, value specified as a superlative. Montague, who experienced his son as an absence to be wondered at, commissions a likeness, a permanent visible presence of the other child to stand in the city where his own son's presence was never secured. Each man, in grief, does again the thing he always did, the one converting love into expenditure on display, the other converting it into an image of someone finally standing still where he can be seen. The reconciliation is sincere. The play is under no obligation to pretend that sincerity arrives in a new language, and it does not. The fathers make peace in the grammar that made the war, and the gold statues, gleaming in the Prince's glooming morning, are the feud's last and most expensive sentence.

## What Each Father Never Says

A comparison by presence should end by auditing the silences inside the speeches themselves, because each father's part is defined as sharply by its omissions as by its contents. Capulet, for all his hundreds of lines, never asks his daughter a question and waits for the answer. His interrogatives are instruments, rhetorical flourishes in the feast's comedy or weapons in the tirade, demands for submission phrased as inquiries. The girl whose consent he ceremonially wrote into the marriage settlement is never once, in any scene, consulted by him in a form that permits her to reply. Every other principal in her life asks her something real: her mother asks her disposition, the Nurse asks her wishes, Paris asks her love, the Friar asks her resolution. Her father, the play's most voluble parent, asks her nothing. The rage scene's horror is prepared by that lifelong omission, a father arriving at his first real question, will she obey, with a drawn sword behind it because questions were never his instrument. Even his tenderness is declarative: the consent speech of Act 1 is delivered to Paris, in her absence, as an announcement of policy, and the daughter learns its terms from her mother afterward, like a subject informed of a treaty.

Montague's omissions run the other way. He never gives his son an instruction, a prohibition, a blessing, or a confidence; the entire vocabulary of paternal direction is missing from his part, because direction requires the contact his part never achieves. He also never explains the feud. Capulet's speeches, for all their silence about origins, at least perform the quarrel constantly, in hospitality guarded and nephews disciplined and marriages arranged against the enemy's exclusion. Montague, asked directly who set the ancient quarrel new abroach, receives an answer about a street fight's sequence and lets the matter drop, the one senior figure in Verona who might remember the grudge's beginning behaving as its most incurious student. The feud, in his keeping, is not even a grievance. It is weather. And the play reserves its final refinement for his warmest sentence. The most fully characterized speech Montague ever delivers about another person concerns not his son but his enemy's daughter, "true and faithful Juliet" (5.3.302), praised in superlatives as he vows her statue. Romeo, in his father's mouth, remains a puzzle described at dawn and a body reproached at night. Juliet, in the same mouth, becomes an epitaph. The father who could not reach his own child eulogizes the other house's child better than anyone at the tomb except the Prince, as if his capacity for parental speech had been stored, untouched, for a child not his own. It is the comparison's saddest datum, and the play places it in the reconciliation's center, where the gold can glitter over it.

## The Verdict: What the Uneven Fathers Reveal about the Play's Design

The comparison ends where it began, with the Prologue's promise, which can now be read correctly. The households are alike in dignity and in everything heraldic: rank, wealth, guilt, bereavement. They are unalike in everything dramatic. Shakespeare gives the play one father in full because the tragedy is, in its domestic mechanism, the story of a single household's government: its feasts, its bargains, its rages, its griefs. He gives the other father in outline because Romeo's half of the tragedy requires an ungoverned life, a son whose momentous choices are made in rooms no father enters. The imbalance is the meaning. Excess and defect of the same office destroy the two children with a symmetry no equal distribution could have achieved: the daughter is commanded to death, the son is abandoned to it.

### Which father does the play judge more harshly, the one who acts or the one who stays away?

The play judges Capulet more harshly, because judgment follows presence: his acts are onstage, named, and answered, while Montague's failure is an absence the text leaves the audience to notice. The harsher sentence is also the greater honor, since Capulet alone is treated as a moral agent large enough to condemn.

That asymmetry of judgment is the final refinement of the design. Capulet is indicted by name, by his daughter's corpse, by his own remorse, and the play grants him the terrible dignity of a man whose choices mattered. Montague is granted no such dignity. Nobody reproaches him, not the Prince, not the Friar, not the audience's surrogate in the scene, because there is nothing to reproach: a question asked in the first act and a statue vowed in the fifth are the whole of his fatherhood, and the play lets the vacancy speak for itself. "O thou untaught" hangs over his part as its unintended epitaph. The fathers of Verona are reconciled at last, equal at last, in the only scene after the first where they stand together as peers: two childless old men, shaking hands over the evidence, dividing between them a fortune in gold statues and the whole of the blame.

Performance cannot repair the imbalance, and the attempts are instructive. Directors who feel the Montague part's slightness sometimes redistribute lines to him, hold him in dumbshow at the edges of scenes, or keep him visible in the sentencing and the banishment as a silent witness. Each device concedes the comparison's thesis while trying to soften it: the text as written will not let this father act, so the production lends him presence the words refuse. The opposite choice, trimming his dawn inquiry as expendable exposition, is more common and more damaging, because it removes the one scene in which the audience learns that Romeo was watched, worried over, and accurately understood by a parent who then vanished. Without the inquiry, Montague is a name in the churchyard. With it, he is the play's study in the insufficiency of love that never becomes contact.

One caution belongs in the verdict, lest the argument be mistaken for a ranking of the men's worth. Capulet's dominance of the play is not a certificate of superior fatherhood, and Montague's faintness is not a certificate of failure by every measure a parent might be judged. The Montague household produced a son who is, by the testimony of friends, enemies, and the Friar, courteous, gentle, and beloved; the Capulet household produced a daughter who learned, in self defense, to deceive everyone in it. A different tribunal than the theater's might acquit the distant father and convict the diligent one on the evidence of the children alone. The comparison's claim is narrower and, for that reason, harder to evade. Whatever their private merits, the playwright distributed their presence with a five to one hand, and the distribution, not the men's souls, is what an audience experiences, what a reader remembers, and what the tragedy's machinery runs on. Judged as drama, which is the only judgment the text can finally enforce, the fathers of Verona are the most unequal equals in Shakespeare.

For readers and students the comparison offers a discipline as much as a conclusion. The habit of describing the feud as a contest between two houses, two fathers, two equal and opposite tyrannies, survives contact with the text only at the level of heraldry. At the level of scenes the play teaches a harder lesson about how authority actually distributes itself in families and cities: unevenly, by temperament and by accident of attention, with one governor overgoverning and another abdicating, and with the costs of both failures presented, in the end, to the same generation. The lovers grow up, if the word can be used of a story of four days, in the space between a father who would not stop deciding and a father who never started. Measuring that space, in lines and scenes and ceremonies and silences, is what the fathers comparison is for.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How many lines does Lord Montague speak in the whole play, and how does the count compare with Capulet's?

Montague speaks fewer than forty lines, in seven speeches, against Capulet's total of over two hundred lines. Exact totals shift slightly between editions, because editors divide half lines and shared lines differently, but every modern count preserves the same proportion: the Capulet part is more than five times the size of the Montague part. Montague's speeches fall into two clusters, four in the inquiry of Act 1 Scene 1 and three in the churchyard of Act 5 Scene 3, with nothing at all in the three acts between them. His single longest speech, the dawn description of Romeo at 1.1, runs to about a dozen lines, shorter than Capulet's tirade against Juliet by a factor of four.

### Q: Does Lord Montague ever speak directly to Romeo onstage?

No. Montague never addresses Romeo in dialogue at any point in the play. In Act 1 Scene 1 he describes his son's dawn wanderings to Benvolio while Romeo is offstage, and Romeo enters only after both parents have left the scene. In Act 5 Scene 3 he speaks over Romeo's corpse, reproaching the dead boy for pressing before his father to a grave. Between those two moments come the love, the marriage, the duel, and the exile, none of which passes through a single exchanged word between the head of the Montague house and his heir. The omission is unique among the play's parent and child pairs: Capulet addresses Juliet, Lady Capulet addresses Juliet, and Lady Montague at least asks after Romeo's whereabouts, while the Montague father and son share only a stage direction's proximity in the first scene.

### Q: What does Capulet mean by calling the handshake his daughter's jointure in Act 5 Scene 3?

A jointure is the property settlement attached to a marriage, the provision a bride brings to the match or receives under its terms. When Capulet offers his hand and says "This is my daughter's jointure, for no more / Can I demand" (5.3.296 to 298), he is paying into the peace the settlement Juliet never received in life. Her marriages were made in secret and in death, outside every contract her father negotiated. The handshake becomes her one dowry, delivered to the enemy house, and the word exposes the habit of a lifetime: Capulet's love, even in remorse, speaks the language of the marriage settlement.

### Q: Why does Montague answer a handshake with a statue rather than with words?

Because expenditure is the fathers' native language, and Montague's part has never had words to spare. Capulet offers a gesture, the clasped hand, and Montague converts the gesture instantly into a permanent and costly object: a statue of Juliet in pure gold, raised at his charge. The answer outbids the offer, and the outbidding is characteristic of the feud's whole economy, in which honor is measured by display. It is also the one moment in the play where the slighter father exceeds the greater, as if four acts of silence had been saving for a single sentence of gold.

### Q: How are the two fathers staged as mirror images when they first enter in Act 1 Scene 1?

They enter in sequence, dressed for bed, each demanding a weapon and each physically restrained or ridiculed by his wife. Capulet calls for his long sword and is told he needs a crutch; Montague, arriving moments later, is forbidden by Lady Montague to stir a foot toward his foe. The blocking makes the Prologue's symmetry visible: two patriarchs of identical age, temper, and infirmity, heads of houses so alike that their households manage them with the same gesture. The mirror holds for a dozen lines. Afterward their stage lives diverge completely, one into the interior of a great house, the other into absence.

### Q: Why does Capulet get a feast, servants, and a wedding to command while Montague gets no household scene at all?

Because the play concentrates its domestic machinery in one house so that the system of patriarchal government can be examined in a single working model. Capulet's hall contains the feast, the marriage market, the servants, the Nurse, and the wedding preparations: the entire apparatus through which a great household processes a daughter. A second furnished interior would duplicate the lesson without adding to it. Montague's house is left unfurnished because Romeo's story requires the opposite condition, a home with no governing presence inside it, whose heir makes every decision of his life in other men's rooms.

### Q: Through whom does each father try to learn what his child is doing?

Montague works through Benvolio, commissioning his nephew in Act 1 Scene 1 to discover the cause of Romeo's melancholy and report back. Capulet works through the women and servants of his house, receiving his daughter's state from her mother and the Nurse, and through Paris, whose suit gives him a suitor's reports. The difference in instruments is the difference in the men. Montague appoints an investigator because he knows he lacks access. Capulet appoints no one, because he assumes his knowledge is complete, and his household feeds the assumption until the assumption kills his daughter.

### Q: Why are both fathers absent from the sentencing in Act 3 Scene 1, and who speaks for each house instead?

The house of Capulet is represented by Lady Capulet, who pleads for Romeo's death and dismisses Benvolio's evidence as Montague partiality. The house of Montague is represented by no one: no parent pleads Romeo's provocation, and no parent hears the sentence of banishment pronounced. The fathers' joint absence is structural. The feud's owners are missing when its costs come due, and the scene's justice is transacted by a prince, a widow in waiting, and a young reporter, while the two men whose quarrel made the street fight possible are nowhere in the square.

### Q: How do the two nephews, Tybalt and Benvolio, extend their uncles' opposite styles?

Tybalt extends Capulet's authority as violence: he is the household's sword, eager to enforce its honor at the feast and in the street, and his uncle's public rebuke of him is a lesson in whose hand holds the weapon. Benvolio extends Montague's authority as investigation: he is the household's eyes, commissioned to watch Romeo, report his condition, and afterward to give the Prince a sworn account of the duel. Sword and witness, enforcement and inquiry, the nephews embody the two patriarchs' methods so exactly that the street fight of Act 3 Scene 1 can be read as a duel between the uncles by proxy.

### Q: Is Montague's part cut down in the early printed texts, or was it always small?

It was always small. The part appears in the same slight proportions in the compressed first quarto of 1597, in the fuller second quarto of 1599, and in the Folio text, at the same two stations of the action, the dawn inquiry and the churchyard. A cut made for a particular production might mark one witness of the text; it would not produce the same silhouette in all of them. Editors accordingly print the part as complete. What changed across the tradition was not Montague's size but Capulet's, since Shakespeare enlarged the Capulet father far beyond the scale the source story gave him. The first quarto is the sharpest witness here, since its reporting compresses many parts severely, and a Montague once written large would be the likeliest survival in its pages; the part there is the same sketch.

### Q: What news does Montague carry into the tomb scene that Capulet does not have to carry?

Montague enters the final scene reporting a fresh death in his own house: his wife has died that night, her breath stopped by grief at Romeo's exile. Capulet arrives burdened by his daughter's two deaths, the feigned one he mourned and the real one before him, but his wife lives, and speaks at the tomb. The Montague household thus reaches the reconciliation already reduced to a single survivor at its head. Its patriarch speaks his grief as a completed tally, which is one reason his vow of the golden statue lands as a bequest rather than as policy.

### Q: Why does the fathers' exchange at the tomb end with Capulet's voice rather than Montague's?

Because the play's distribution of speech holds to the last exchange. Capulet initiates the reconciliation by offering his hand, Montague answers with the greater vow, and Capulet then caps the answer with the matching promise for Romeo's statue and the summary line, "Poor sacrifices of our enmity!" (5.3.304). The dominant father frames the peace at both ends, opening it and closing it, while the slighter father's single magnificent intervention sits between Capulet's sentences. Only when both men fall silent does the Prince take the play's final lines for the state.

### Q: Which father reverses himself onstage, and why does Montague never get a decision to reverse?

Capulet reverses himself repeatedly: he defers Juliet's marriage for two summers and then advances it to Thursday; he protects Romeo at the feast and then engineers the match that makes Romeo his daughter's destroyer in the world's eyes; he curses his daughter and then buries her in superlatives. Montague reverses nothing because he decides nothing onstage. His part contains an inquiry, a delegation, a report of death, and a vow. A character who is never permitted to act cannot be shown changing course, and the absence of any reversal is the measure of how completely the play withholds agency from him. Reversal is a privilege of the dramatized: only a father whose decisions the audience has watched can be watched abandoning them.

### Q: Why does all the play's household comedy belong to Capulet's side of Verona?

Because comedy in this play lives inside the furnished household, and only one household is furnished. The servants clearing the feast, the Nurse's errands and teasing, the wedding morning bustle, the musicians refusing to play: all of it belongs to Capulet's establishment, the one domestic world the play builds. Montague's house has no servants with lines, no kitchen, no nurse, no morning bustle, and so it generates no comedy at all. The distribution confirms the design from another angle. Where a father's house is fully staffed, the play can laugh inside it. Where the father is a silhouette, there is nothing to laugh with.

### Q: How do directors stage the handshake so the audience reads it as the feud's end?

The staging problem is distance: the fathers enter from opposite sides of a scene filled with the bodies of their children, under the Prince's eye, and must reach each other across that ground. Productions commonly slow the crossing, let the two old men pass or step among the dead, and hold the clasped hands in silence before any speech resumes, so that the audience measures the gesture against its price. The golden statues are then spoken as the handshake's translation into permanence, and the Prince's closing speech frames both gestures as a settlement the state accepts but does not celebrate. Some stagings keep the fathers from touching at all until the vow is spoken, reserving the clasp for the word brother, so that the single physical contact of the two men in the entire play lands as the feud's formal extinction.

### Q: Why does Capulet draw more blame than Montague when both houses kept the feud alive?

Because blame attaches to acts, and Capulet's part is a record of acts while Montague's is a record of omissions. Readers can point to the consent revoked, the wedding advanced, the threats spoken over a kneeling daughter, and assign each consequence its author. Against Montague there is only the empty space where intervention should have been, which is harder to prosecute and easier to forgive. The play itself follows the same logic: the Prince scourges both houses in one breath, but the drama that precedes the scourging has spent four acts exhibiting one father's government and the other father's absence.

### Q: What would the play lose if Montague were written as fully as Capulet?

It would lose the counterpoint that organizes the tragedy. A fully written Montague would have to be given opinions about the marriage, the duel, and the exile, and each opinion would either obstruct the plot, since a vigilant father is the one character the catastrophe cannot survive, or repeat Capulet's scenes in a second register. The play instead sets two failures of fatherhood in opposition: government by excess and government by absence, a daughter crushed by attention and a son lost for want of it. Doubling the first father would halve the design.

The pattern it exposes is consistency of opposition at every level of the construction. ### Q: Does the ending finally make the two fathers equal, or does Capulet dominate even the reconciliation?

The ending makes them equal in loss and in expenditure, and in nothing else. Both men are childless, both are scourged by the Prince in a single sentence, and both leave the stage bound to raise a golden statue of the other's child. Within that equality the old distribution persists to the final line of their exchange. Capulet initiates the peace, frames it as his daughter's jointure, answers Montague's vow, and speaks the fathers' last word over the bodies. The reconciliation equalizes their fortunes. It does not redistribute their voices, and the play's asymmetry survives inside its closing symmetry.

### Q: When does Capulet first speak directly to Juliet, and why does the timing matter?

Capulet does not address his daughter directly until Act 3 Scene 5, the scene of the rage. In Act 1 he discusses her future with Paris while she is absent; in Act 3 Scene 4 he discusses it with Paris again, late at night, while she is in the Friar's hands elsewhere; and his wife is sent to break the news. The first sustained speech the play gives him to Juliet's face is the tirade that threatens to disown her. A daughter who has been governed entirely through intermediaries meets her father's voice for the first time as an instrument of punishment, and the timing explains the scene's desolation better than any theory of sudden temper.
