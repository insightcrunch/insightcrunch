Ask a simple question of Indian labour law, such as what an employer must do before closing a workshop with forty workers, and the answer refuses to be simple. It depends on which state the workshop stands in, on whether the workers are counted on the muster roll or engaged through a contractor, on whether the establishment crossed a headcount line last year or the year before, and on which of several dozen statutes happens to switch on at that size. The Indian labour law framework is not one law. It is an architecture, built over a century and a half out of central statutes, state statutes, state amendments to central statutes, and state rules framed under central statutes, and it cannot be understood statute by statute. It can only be understood by grasping the two structural features that produced the accumulation and that survive every reform of it: labour is a subject on which both Parliament and the state legislatures may legislate, and almost every labour statute applies only above a numerical threshold of workers. Whoever holds those two keys can read the whole body of law. Whoever lacks them will keep mistaking a pile of statutes for a system.

![The Indian labour law framework, showing the Concurrent List foundation and the threshold architecture that decides which statutes apply to a workplace - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-95.webp)

This article is the map of that architecture. It explains why India accumulated dozens of central labour statutes and hundreds of state enactments instead of one labour law, how the constitutional settlement in the Seventh Schedule made that accumulation inevitable, how the threshold mechanism decides whether any given statute reaches any given workplace at all, what the courts did to widen the perimeter of the field, and what the consolidation of twenty-nine central statutes into four labour codes changed and conspicuously did not change. The detailed treatment of individual statutes belongs to the specialist articles in this cluster, and this article links down to them rather than repeating them. What it owns is the connective structure, because in this field the connective structure is where the law actually lives.

## What the Indian labour law framework contains

Begin with the inventory, because the inventory itself is the first fact that public discussion gets wrong. There is no single authoritative count of India's labour laws, and every confident total quoted in debate conceals a counting decision. At the central level, the Second National Commission on Labour, reporting in 2002, worked with a body of some forty-four central labour Acts in the organised sector alone. Of those and their successors, twenty-nine were subsumed when the four labour codes were brought into force, and a residue of central statutes, among them the Apprentices Act 1961, the Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act 1986 and the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace Act 2013, continued outside the codes. Around the central corpus sits a far larger state corpus: each state has its own shops and establishments Act, its own amendments to the central statutes operating in its territory, its own rules under those statutes, and in some cases its own parallel legislation, of which the Maharashtra Recognition of Trade Unions and Prevention of Unfair Labour Practices Act 1971 is the best known. Counted together, central and state labour enactments run into the hundreds. Any single figure offered without that decomposition is a slogan, not a fact.

The central corpus, before and after consolidation, falls into four functional families, and the families are older than the codes that adopted them. The first family governs industrial relations: the formation and registration of trade unions, the certification of standing orders that fix conditions of service in larger establishments, and the machinery for preventing and settling industrial disputes, built around the Trade Unions Act 1926, the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act 1946 and the Industrial Disputes Act 1947. The second family governs wages: the Payment of Wages Act 1936, the Minimum Wages Act 1948, the Payment of Bonus Act 1965 and the Equal Remuneration Act 1976. The third family constructs social security: the Employees' State Insurance Act 1948, the Employees' Provident Funds and Miscellaneous Provisions Act 1952, the Maternity Benefit Act 1961, the Payment of Gratuity Act 1972, the older Employees' Compensation Act 1923, and a set of welfare fund and cess statutes for particular industries. The fourth family governs safety and conditions of work: the Factories Act 1948, the Mines Act 1952, the Plantations Labour Act 1951, the Contract Labour (Regulation and Abolition) Act 1970, the Inter-State Migrant Workmen Act 1979 and a tail of sector-specific statutes for dock workers, motor transport workers, beedi and cigar workers, cine workers and sales promotion employees.

Two features of this inventory deserve emphasis before any analysis begins. The first is that the families were never designed as families. Each statute answered a particular pressure at a particular moment: a royal commission's recommendation, a wartime emergency, a post-independence promise, a sector's agitation, a scandal. The grouping into four is a rationalisation imposed afterwards, most influentially by the Second National Commission on Labour, and then enacted by the codes. The second is that the corpus is shot through with sectoral particularism. Separate statutes exist for mines, plantations, docks, railways-adjacent transport, newspapers, cinemas and beedi manufacture, because the field grew by accretion around specific industries rather than by generalisation from principle. A reader who grasps that the framework is an accretion, not a design, has understood the first thing about it, and has the explanation for nearly every oddity that follows: the overlapping definitions, the inconsistent thresholds, the duplicated inspectorates, and the century-long reform complaint that Indian labour law is at once too much and too little.

Popular accounts of the framework quote rival totals that look irreconcilable, hundreds of labour laws on one side and a few dozen on the other, and the discrepancy is almost entirely a matter of altitude. Count only substantive central Acts and the corpus is the forty-odd the Second National Commission surveyed. Count state Acts as well, the shops Acts, welfare fund Acts, regional and occupational statutes of twenty-eight states and the union territories, and the total runs to several hundred. Count the rules, notifications, registers and returns beneath the Acts and the compliance surface multiplies beyond either figure. All three counts describe the same reality, and the lesson for a reader is to ask of any quoted total what it is a total of. The altitude that mattered for consolidation was the top one, since Parliament can only repeal what Parliament enacted; the altitude that matters for a worker is the bottom one, since rights are met or missed in forms, inspections and notifications far below the title of any Act. The inventory has a penumbra that consolidation left standing, and the map is incomplete without it. The Apprentices Act 1961 still governs the training relation it carved out of employment. The Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act 1986 still carries the framework's prohibitions on child work, the field's one genuinely threshold-free zone, since its bans attach to the child's age rather than the establishment's size. The Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace Act 2013 stands alone by design. Around these stand the constitutional writ jurisdiction, the state welfare boards and the vast subordinate legislation of rules and notifications in which the statutes' promises are cashed. The four codes redrew the core of the map. The penumbra reminds the reader that the core was never the whole territory.

## The four families in detail

The industrial relations family is the framework's political core, and its design expresses a philosophy: industrial peace is a public good that the state will manufacture if the parties cannot. The Trade Unions Act 1926 is the family's permissive member, content to register unions and immunise their lawful activity. The Industrial Disputes Act 1947 is its interventionist member, arming the appropriate government with power to refer disputes to adjudication of its own motion, empowering conciliation officers to mediate, and in its later chapters restricting strikes and lockouts during proceedings and requiring notice before retrenchment and, in larger establishments, permission before it. The standing orders statute is its disciplinary member, converting the employer's unilateral rulebook into a certified public document. The family's internal tension is permanent. Registration without recognition left the strongest union in a plant able to be ignored by the employer, which is why Maharashtra legislated recognition and why the Industrial Relations Code introduces a negotiating union chosen by a fifty-one per cent membership threshold, with a negotiating council where no union crosses it. Recognition machinery had in fact existed all along in one region: the Bombay Industrial Relations Act 1946, a provincial statute continued in Maharashtra and Gujarat, provided for representative unions and approved unions with defined rights in notified industries, so that the bargaining-agent idea the Code generalises was tested for three-quarters of a century in India's most industrialised region before it became national policy. The episode is characteristic of the framework's federalism: the state layer as laboratory, the centre as late adopter. Compulsory adjudication without strong collective bargaining left the courthouse to do the work of the bargaining table, producing the field's famous backlog and its equally famous culture of litigated rather than negotiated industrial relations. The codes keep the philosophy and adjust the machinery: strike notice, once required only in public utility services, is extended to all industrial establishments with a fourteen-day requirement, lay-off and retrenchment procedure is restated, and the permission line is set at three hundred. The family's biography, like the framework's, is one of continuity under new numbering.

The wages family governs the price term of the employment contract, and its founding problem was that the market it regulated included subsistence labour. The Payment of Wages Act 1936 attacked the truck system and arbitrary fines and deductions, insisting that wages be paid in money, on time, and substantially whole. The Minimum Wages Act 1948 attacked the level itself, empowering governments to fix minima for employments listed in a schedule, through committees and advisory boards on which employers and workers sat in equal numbers, with revision required at intervals not exceeding five years and variable dearness allowance linking many fixations to the cost of living. The Payment of Bonus Act 1965 added a share of surplus, the Equal Remuneration Act 1976 attacked sex discrimination in pay, and the Code on Wages 2019 gathered all four, universalised the minimum wage beyond the schedule and erected the national floor wage beneath state fixations. The family's evolution runs from discipline to entitlement: from telling the employer when and how to pay, to telling him how much at the least, to telling both governments that no fixation in India may undercut a common floor. Its administrative signature is the tripartite committee, and its persistent weakness is revision discipline, since scheduled rates in some states and employments have lagged years behind the statutory cycle, a failure of administration rather than of text. The wage family's machinery also produced the framework's most quietly influential institution, the tripartite advisory board, whose recommended rates governments habitually accept, so that the operative minimum wage of a state is in practice co-authored by the unions and employers who will live under it. The pattern extends to the floor wage machinery of the Code, which fixes the national floor after consulting a central advisory board, importing the tripartite habit into the newest instrument of the oldest family. Wage law in India is therefore never merely a command issued to a market; it is a negotiated number promulgated as law, and its chronic enforcement gap, the distance between the notified rate and the rate in the envelope, is the distance between a negotiation the parties attended and a worksite where none of them was present.

The social security family is the framework's insurance wing, and it was built, uniquely among the families, on contributions rather than commands. The compensation statute imposes liability without insurance; the state insurance statute builds a corporation and a fund; the provident fund statute builds an organisation that became, in effect, a national pension and savings utility for the formal sector; the gratuity statute returns to employer liability but regularises it as deferred wages; the maternity statute imposes a wage-replacement duty on the employer of the covered establishment. Around the core statutes grew the cess-funded welfare boards for construction and beedi workers, taxing the transaction rather than the payroll because the payroll, in those trades, could not be found. The family's boundary problem has always been the informal worker who has contributions in no one's ledger, and its history since the Unorganised Workers' Social Security Act 2008 is the history of attempts to reach that worker through registration and schemes rather than through the establishment. The Code on Social Security is therefore the family's most ambitious member and its most criticised: ambitious because it names gig and platform work and designs aggregator financing, criticised because scheme-based provision, dependent on notification and budgets, is entitlement of a lower order than the insured benefits of the covered employee. The distinction between a right and a scheme is the sharpest analytical tool a reader can bring to the whole social security debate.

The conditions of work family is the framework's oldest layer and its most concrete, dealing in the physical circumstances of labour: hours, rest, leave, ventilation, fencing, sanitation, safety committees, canteens, crèches and first aid. The Factories Act 1948 is its capital statute, and around it the sectoral Acts applied the same grammar to mines, docks and plantations, each with a specialist inspectorate. The family's method differs from the others: it prescribes standards for things rather than procedures for relationships, which made it the least litigated and the most inspectable part of the framework, and the part where the gap between text and practice is most plainly a function of inspection capacity. The Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code consolidates thirteen statutes of this family and its neighbours, generalises a single registration for establishments of ten or more, empowers the prescription of workplace standards across sectors, and extends concepts once confined to factories, such as duties of the employer and the right of a worker to warn of imminent danger, across the broader establishment universe. The family's thresholds, ten and twenty under the old factory law and twenty and forty under the Code, are the staircase's ground floor, and its licensing line is the first line an expanding employer crosses.

## Tripartism and the international layer

One design habit runs through every family and deserves separate notice, because it explains machinery that otherwise looks decorative. Indian labour law is tripartite in its bones. India was a founding member of the International Labour Organization in 1919, and the ILO method, standards negotiated among governments, employers and workers, was transplanted into domestic institutions: the Indian Labour Conference, meeting since 1942, and the Standing Labour Committee deliberate policy before legislation; wage boards and advisory committees fix minima; the social security corporations seat the social partners on their boards. The state also legislated against the yardstick of ratified conventions, though sparingly: India has historically preferred to ratify a convention only when domestic law already conforms to it, and its ratification in June 2017 of the two fundamental conventions on child labour, the Minimum Age Convention and the Worst Forms of Child Labour Convention, followed the amendment of its own child labour statute rather than preceding it. Tripartism is the framework's answer to a legitimacy problem: statutes made over the heads of both parties to the employment relation command neither party's cooperation, and the committee rooms of the labour ministry, whatever their delays, are where the framework's measures acquire the consent that enforcement alone could never purchase. The codes preserve the form, constituting national and state boards for social security and advisory bodies for safety and wages, and the observation generalises: in this field, the durability of a rule correlates with the breadth of the table at which it was first negotiated. Tripartism also operates below the conference level, where it is least visible and most continuous. Works committees in establishments of one hundred or more were written into the Industrial Disputes Act itself, and the amendment of 2010 added a statutory grievance redressal committee for establishments employing twenty or more workmen, carrying the tripartite instinct down to the shop floor and setting the twenty-worker line that the Industrial Relations Code adopts for its own grievance committees. The framework's preference is consistent across seventy years: wherever a decision affects both sides of the employment relation, the statute builds a table with both sides at it and the state in the chair, and the codes' negotiating union and safety committee provisions are the instinct in its latest form.

## The second track: law for the unorganised

Alongside the establishment framework there runs a second, younger track of labour law built for workers whom establishments do not organise, and no map of the field is complete without it. Its instruments are different in kind. Where the organised track commands employers, the unorganised track registers workers and funds welfare boards. The Building and Other Construction Workers Acts of 1996 taxed construction cost by cess and channelled the proceeds to state welfare boards for a workforce that changes sites and employers too fast for establishment law. Beedi and cine worker welfare funds followed the cess model. The Unorganised Workers' Social Security Act 2008 created a national board and contemplated schemes covering life, health, maternity and old age, implemented unevenly through state boards and the registration of workers for identity cards, a line of development that runs to the e-Shram database. The National Commission for Enterprises in the Unorganised Sector supplied the track's intellectual charter, arguing that the informal majority required social security designed for them rather than the extension of devices built for factories. The Code on Social Security absorbs this track into its architecture, with boards, registration and scheme powers for unorganised, gig and platform workers under one roof. The framework's mature shape is therefore dual: a rights track for the covered minority administered through establishments, and a welfare track for the uncovered majority administered through boards and databases, with the political argument of the coming decades likely to be about how far entitlements migrate from the second track to the first. The second track also has its own thresholds, of a different design: registration as its gate rather than headcount. A construction worker's access to board benefits depends on being registered as a beneficiary, typically on proof of a minimum period of work in the industry in the preceding year; an unorganised worker's access to schemes depends on appearing in the database. Registration thresholds exclude by documentation what headcount thresholds exclude by establishment size, and the two exclusions fall on overlapping populations, since the worker least likely to work in a large establishment is also the worker least likely to hold the papers registration demands. The second track also has a federal geography of its own. Kerala built the densest welfare board architecture in the country from the 1970s onward, occupation by occupation, financing pensions and benefits for head-load workers, toddy tappers, artisans and others outside the factory system, and Tamil Nadu and Maharashtra developed their own board traditions for manual and mathadi workers. These state systems are the unorganised track's proof of concept and its reproach at once: they show that registration-based welfare can be delivered at scale, and they show by contrast how thin the central track remained for decades, since the states that built boards did so under the same Constitution and the same fiscal constraints as the states that did not. The Code on Social Security's national board architecture generalises the model the pioneering states proved, which is the framework's familiar pattern once more, state experiment first, national text after. Any framework assessment that celebrates the second track's architecture without auditing its registration rates repeats, in a new register, the original error of the first track: mistaking the design of protection for its delivery.

## The constitutional settlement: two legislatures, one workforce

The reason the accretion took the shape it did sits in the Seventh Schedule to the Constitution. Labour is not assigned to the Union List or the State List. It is placed, deliberately and in detail, on the Concurrent List, where three entries divide the field between them in terms that reward close reading, and the full map of the three lists is set out in the series article on [the Seventh Schedule and its three lists](/2018/09/15/seventh-schedule-lists-explained/). Entry 22 covers trade unions and industrial and labour disputes. Entry 23 covers social security and social insurance, and employment and unemployment. Entry 24 covers the welfare of labour, including conditions of work, provident funds, employers' liability, workmen's compensation, invalidity and old age pensions and maternity benefits. Between them, the three entries hand the whole subject to both levels of legislature at once. Parliament may legislate on every one of these matters for the whole country, and every state legislature may legislate on every one of them for its own territory.

A small residue of the field sits elsewhere, and locating it prevents a common examination error. The Union List reserves regulation of labour and safety in mines and oilfields to Parliament under Entry 55, and industrial disputes concerning Union employees under Entry 61, which is why the Mines Act is a purely central statute administered in a central sphere and why the armed forces and central government establishments answer to central machinery. Inter-state migration and quarantine-adjacent matters touch Entry 81 of the Union List. But the gravitational centre of the subject is concurrent, and the Second National Commission on Labour, asked in effect whether that should change, answered that it did not consider it necessary or desirable to move labour off the Concurrent List. The settlement has never been seriously revisited since.

The placement was inherited before it was chosen. The Government of India Act 1935 had already placed the core labour subjects on its concurrent list, and the Constituent Assembly kept the arrangement with its eyes open: the industrial economy was national and migratory, which argued for central power, while conditions of work varied enough across provinces to argue for local room. Concurrency in labour is therefore a considered inheritance rather than a drafting accident, and the Second National Commission's refusal to recommend disturbing it, recorded later in this article, recognises a settlement that had acquired the legitimacy of a fixture. Concurrency has three working consequences, and each of them generates a recognisable stratum of the framework. The first is duplication by design. Because both levels may legislate, India has central statutes and state statutes on adjacent ground, and a workplace answers to both: a factory in Maharashtra complies with the Factories Act 1948, a central statute, with the Maharashtra Factories Rules framed by the state under it, with the state's shops and establishments legislation for its office staff, and with state amendments that alter the central text within Maharashtra. The second consequence is amendment from below. Under Article 254(2) of the Constitution, a state legislature may enact a law on a concurrent subject that conflicts with a central law, and if the state law is reserved for the President's consideration and receives assent, it prevails in that state. This is the constitutional door through which every significant state variation has walked, from Maharashtra's trade union legislation to Rajasthan's threshold amendments of 2014. The third consequence is layering of rules. Central labour statutes are administered substantially by state governments and filled out by state rules, so the operative law for a given establishment is a composite: the central Act, the state's rules under it, any state amendment in force, and the notifications of the appropriate government fixing monetary ceilings and scheduled employments. Two identical factories in two states can therefore stand under materially different obligations without either of them breaking any law.

Where a central provision and a state provision genuinely conflict and the state law has not received presidential assent, Article 254(1) resolves the clash in favour of the central law, which prevails to the extent of the repugnancy. The doctrine matters less in labour than the assent route, because governments that intend a durable variation seek assent rather than litigate supremacy, but the default rule explains why the central text sets the floor of the national conversation even in a concurrent field. Repugnancy, in the courts' working tests, is not mere difference: a state law falls only where obedience to both laws is impossible, where the state law trenches on a field Parliament intended to occupy completely, or where the two provisions collide in direct contradiction on the same matter. A state law more generous to workers than the central law is not for that reason repugnant, and much state welfare legislation more protective than the central floor has coexisted with the central statutes for decades without a supremacy challenge, the two laws operating on the same workers cumulatively. The distinction matters for reading the codes as well: where a code fixes a national floor wage, a state fixation above it is harmony, not conflict, and the repugnancy analysis begins only when a state measure would authorise what the code forbids or forbid what it guarantees. Concurrency's friction, in other words, is engineered to be rare, and the framework's two centuries of accumulated variation rest mostly on coexistence rather than on adjudicated supremacy.

### What do the Directive Principles say about work, wages and conditions?

Articles 39, 41, 42, 43 and 43A of the Constitution direct the state to secure a livelihood and equal pay for equal work, the right to work and to public assistance, just and humane conditions of work with maternity relief, a living wage and cottage industries, and the participation of workers in the management of industries.

The Directive Principles are the framework's stated destination, and they explain the ambitions the statutes were built to serve. Article 39 directs policy towards securing the right to an adequate means of livelihood and equal pay for equal work for both men and women. Article 41 speaks of the right to work, to education and to public assistance in cases of unemployment, old age, sickness and disablement, within the limits of the state's economic capacity. Article 42 requires provision for just and humane conditions of work and for maternity relief, and it is the textual root from which maternity legislation grew. Article 43 directs the state to endeavour to secure a living wage, conditions of work ensuring a decent standard of life, and cottage industries, and Article 43A, inserted by the Forty-second Amendment in 1976, adds the participation of workers in the management of industries. None of this is enforceable by a court, and the gap between these directions and the statutes' narrow coverage is the standing reproach of the field: the Constitution promises in the language of universality, and the statutes deliver in the language of thresholds.

The fundamental rights supply the enforceable edges. Article 19(1)(c) guarantees the freedom to form associations and unions, which is the constitutional shelter of the trade union movement, though it has never been read to guarantee a right to strike or a right to have a union recognised for collective bargaining, both of which remain statutory creations. Article 23 prohibits forced labour, and the Supreme Court read it expansively in People's Union for Democratic Rights v Union of India in 1982, holding that labour exacted for less than the statutory minimum wage could itself amount to forced labour within the article's meaning. Article 24 prohibits the employment of children below fourteen in factories, mines and hazardous work, and stands behind the child labour legislation. Article 21, the right to life, was read in Olga Tellis v Bombay Municipal Corporation in 1985 to include the right to livelihood, and in Consumer Education and Research Centre v Union of India in 1995 to include the health of workers exposed to industrial hazards. The pattern across these provisions is one the courts repeat throughout the field: the constitutional text is broad, the statutory delivery is narrow, and litigation keeps trying to close the distance between the two.

## How the accumulation happened

The framework was not built in one act of statecraft. It accumulated in four distinguishable phases, and each phase left a layer that later phases did not remove. Reading the layers in order is the fastest way to understand why the corpus looks as it does, and why consolidation, when it came, took the form of grouping rather than rewriting.

The first layer is colonial, and its origins are uncomfortable in a way the brief for any honest history must state plainly. The earliest Indian factory legislation was not principally a response to Indian worker demand. A committee of inquiry was appointed in 1875 after sustained pressure from British textile interests, above all the manufacturing chambers of Lancashire and Manchester, who complained that Bombay's mills, running long hours with cheap labour and no factory law, competed unfairly with their own. The Indian Factories Act 1881 that followed applied only to premises using mechanical power and employing not fewer than one hundred workers, and concerned itself chiefly with child labour: a minimum age, limits on children's hours, holidays and the fencing of machinery. Indian reformers such as Sasipada Banerjea and Narayan Meghaji Lokhande had campaigned for factory workers' welfare on their own account, and their pressure mattered; but the legislative energy in London and Calcutta came substantially from the desire to raise the costs of a competitor. The pattern set then has never entirely left the field: labour legislation in India has repeatedly been made about workers, under pressure from interests that were not theirs, at a pace set by someone else's emergency. The Factories Act 1891 lowered the coverage line to fifty workers, and the line fell again in later enactments, which is the threshold history of the whole subject in miniature: protection begins with the largest workplaces and creeps downwards as politics permits.

The second layer is the interwar settlement, in which the modern skeleton appeared. The Royal Commission on Labour under J. H. Whitley, appointed in 1929 and reporting in 1931, surveyed the field comprehensively and recommended much of what was then enacted. The Trade Unions Act 1926 gave unions a registration mechanism and immunities from certain civil and criminal liabilities, borrowing its immunities from the English union statutes and adapting them to a colony where union activity had been prosecuted as conspiracy within living memory. The Trade Disputes Act 1929, the disputes statute of the interwar layer, introduced the public utility distinction and the conciliation idea that the Act of 1947 would generalise. The Workmen's Compensation Act 1923 had already imposed no-fault employer liability for employment injuries. The Payment of Wages Act 1936 disciplined the timing of wage payment and the deductions employers could make. The Factories Act 1934 consolidated the factory code of the day. India was a founding member of the International Labour Organization in 1919, and the conventions and the tripartite habits of that body left a durable mark on the vocabulary and structure of the statutes, a mark visible in everything from hours regulation to the composition of the Indian Labour Conference, the apex tripartite body that has met since 1942. By the end of this phase the recognisable families were all present in embryo: associational law, wage protection, injury compensation, factory regulation.

Parts of the third layer had wartime roots worth recovering. The Industrial Disputes Act 1947 did not spring from nothing in the year of independence. Its adjudication machinery descended from Rule 81-A of the Defence of India Rules, the wartime device by which the colonial state, needing uninterrupted production, had made strikes and lockouts conditional on notice and empowered itself to refer disputes to adjudication and prohibit industrial action while proceedings ran. Provincial experiments such as the Bombay Industrial Disputes Act 1938 had already tested compulsory adjudication before the war. The Act of 1947 converted an emergency instrument into a peacetime philosophy: the state distrusted the strike as an economic weapon in a poor economy and preferred the courtroom to the picket line, a preference constitutionalised by the exclusion of any fundamental right to strike and administered ever since by the conciliation and tribunal machinery the Act created. The third layer is the great post-independence expansion, driven by the industrial policy of a planned economy and by the promises of the freedom movement to the workers who had marched in it. Between 1946 and 1952 Parliament enacted the core of the modern framework in a burst: the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act 1946, the Industrial Disputes Act 1947, the Factories Act 1948, the Minimum Wages Act 1948, the Employees' State Insurance Act 1948, the Employees' Provident Funds Act 1952, and around them the Plantations Labour Act 1951 and the Mines Act 1952. The design philosophy of the burst was consistent. The state would stand between capital and labour as an active third party, disputes would be pulled into conciliation and adjudication rather than fought out industrially, and welfare would be delivered through contributory insurance and funds administered by statutory bodies. The accretion did not stop in 1952, and one later insertion deserves particular notice because the codes' treatment of it is the reform debate in miniature. The prior-permission chapter, Chapter V-B of the Industrial Disputes Act, was not part of the original 1947 settlement at all; it was inserted by amendment in 1976, during the Emergency, initially for establishments of three hundred or more workmen, and extended down to establishments of one hundred or more by the amendment of 1982. The most complained-of rigidity in the entire framework is thus a midlife addition younger than the Act by three decades, imposed in an authoritarian interlude and widened just before the liberalisation argument began. The Payment of Bonus Act 1965 converted an annual ex gratia expectation into a statutory profit-linked entitlement. The Contract Labour (Regulation and Abolition) Act 1970 addressed the spreading practice of engaging workers through intermediaries. The Payment of Gratuity Act 1972 added a terminal benefit. The decade also produced the framework's clearest scandal-driven statute. The Inter-State Migrant Workmen (Regulation of Employment and Conditions of Service) Act 1979 addressed the dadan system, under which labour contractors recruited workers in states such as Odisha for worksites across the country, advancing money against bondage and dissolving accountability at the state border; the Act answered with licensing of contractors, displacement and journey allowances, and equal treatment at the destination, and its enforcement record across four decades is the standard cautionary exhibit in every argument that text without machinery is decoration. Each statute was defensible on its own terms. Cumulatively they produced a corpus in which a single employment relationship could be governed by a dozen Acts at once, each with its own definitions, its own threshold, its own inspectorate and its own forms.

The fourth layer is the long stalemate of the liberalisation decades. After 1991, the economic argument changed around the statutes while the statutes themselves barely changed at the central level. Employer organisations pressed for flexibility: easier retrenchment, wider use of contract labour, fewer inspections. Trade unions pressed for security: wider coverage, stronger penalties, extension of social security to the unorganised majority. Successive central governments, dependent on coalition arithmetic and facing organised resistance from whichever side a proposal displeased, largely declined to amend the central corpus. The First National Commission on Labour under Justice P. B. Gajendragadkar, which reported in 1969, had reviewed the field without recommending consolidation. The Second National Commission, three decades later, recommended exactly that, and then waited seventeen years for enactment and longer for commencement. What moved during the stalemate was the state layer. States competing for investment used their concurrent power to amend central statutes in their own territories, and state governments also competed in the opposite direction, extending benefits and lowering thresholds to court labour. The field thus diverged by state at exactly the moment the national debate froze, which is why any account of Indian labour law that ignores the state layer describes a country that does not exist.

The Centre's one energetic intervention in the stalemate years was administrative rather than legislative, and its vocabulary survives inside the codes. The programme launched in October 2014 under the name Shramev Jayate attacked the inspection raj rather than the statutes: a single online portal, Shram Suvidha, for registrations and returns across central labour enactments; a universal account number intended to make a worker's provident fund portable across employers; and a computerised, randomised allocation of inspections with reports uploaded promptly, replacing the inspector's discretion to choose his visits. The reforms conceded the framework's critics' procedural case while leaving every threshold and every permission exactly where it stood, and their machinery, single registration, single return, randomised inspection, facilitative enforcement, reappears in the codes as statutory design. The stalemate's most instructive episode came from Rajasthan in 2014. The state amended four central statutes as they applied in Rajasthan, securing presidential assent under Article 254(2). The Industrial Disputes Act's prior-permission chapter, which applied at one hundred workmen, was raised to three hundred. The Factories Act thresholds were doubled. The Contract Labour Act line was raised to fifty. Other states followed with variations of their own, among them Madhya Pradesh, Gujarat, Haryana, Maharashtra, Assam and Uttar Pradesh. The constitutional significance of the episode exceeds its industrial significance. It demonstrated that the Concurrent List was not merely a source of duplication but a working mechanism of reform from below, and the numbers Rajasthan chose in 2014, three hundred, fifty, twenty and forty, are recognisably the numbers the Union codes adopted for the whole country six years later. National consolidation, when it arrived, ratified a state experiment.

The accumulation can also be measured from the establishment's chair, and the measurement is worth making once in concrete terms. A mid-sized factory under the old dispensation held, simultaneously, a factory licence renewed on its cycle, a provident fund code number and monthly contribution filings, an insurance code number and its own filings, certified standing orders displayed at the gate, a registration certificate and contractor licences under the contract labour statute if it used contractors past the line, wage registers, bonus registers and leave registers in prescribed forms, annual returns to several authorities in several months of the year, and a welfare officer where its headcount crossed the welfare officer line. Each item belonged to a different statute, many to a different inspectorate, and none of them, individually, was irrational. The stack is the framework as an employer met it, and the political force of consolidation came from the stack rather than from any single burden: repeal twenty-nine statutes into four codes and the stack collapses into a single registration, a common return and a unified inspection regime, which is why employer support for the codes concentrated on their machinery chapters rather than their thresholds. Reforms are usually judged by their rights. This one was campaigned for on its registers.

## The administrative anatomy: funds, inspectorates and tribunals

A framework is not only a set of commands. It is also a set of institutions, and the Indian labour framework built a distinctive administrative anatomy that any map must include. Three kinds of institution recur. The first is the contributory fund administered by a tripartite body. The Employees' State Insurance Corporation administers medical care and cash benefits for insured employees out of employer and employee contributions; the Employees' Provident Fund Organisation administers retirement savings through regional offices under a central board of trustees on which employers and employees sit alongside government. These bodies are among the largest social security administrators in the world by membership, and their design, social insurance financed by payroll contributions and governed tripartitely, descends directly from the International Labour Organization model that shaped the interwar layer of the framework. The second institution is the inspectorate: chief inspectors of factories, certifying surgeons, boiler inspectors, labour inspectors and enforcement officers under each statute, historically separate cadres for separate Acts, so that a single factory could be visited by half a dozen officials each armed with a different statute and a different register. The multiplicity of inspectorates was the administrative face of the statutory accumulation, and the complaint about inspection burden in Indian industry was always, at bottom, a complaint about the number of statutes rather than the zeal of any one official. The third institution is the adjudicatory ladder built by the Industrial Disputes Act: conciliation officers who attempt settlement, boards of conciliation and courts of inquiry in reserve, and labour courts, industrial tribunals and national tribunals that adjudicate disputes and pass awards. Around these three kinds sit the welfare boards of the cess statutes, the registering officers of the trade union law, and the advisory boards that fix minimum wages. The codes consolidate the inspectorates into an inspector-cum-facilitator cadre and push registration towards a single electronic registration and common returns, but they preserve the funds, the boards and the adjudicatory ladder in recognisable form. Institutions, like statutes, were consolidated rather than reimagined. The tribunal rationalisation of 2017, which merged the provident fund's appellate tribunal into the industrial tribunal system, anticipated the codes' institutional logic by three years: fewer, broader forums, on the theory that a worker's grievance does not respect the statutory family boundaries that the forum map once mirrored. The claim machinery tells the same story from below. A wage claim went to an authority under the payment statute, a gratuity claim to a controlling authority under the gratuity statute, a bonus dispute into the disputes machinery, and an insurance question to a specialised court, so that the route to a remedy was as fragmented as the rights themselves, and a worker with three grievances arising from one dismissal filed in three buildings. The codes gather claims within each family under single authorities, which will matter more to the ordinary litigant than any headline threshold, since the price of a right in this field has always included the price of finding its forum.

## The definitions problem: one relationship, many vocabularies

Ask the framework a deceptively simple question, who is protected, and it answers in several vocabularies at once. The Industrial Disputes Act 1947 protects the workman, a defined term covering a person employed in an industry to do manual, unskilled, skilled, technical, operational, clerical or supervisory work for hire or reward, and excluding those employed mainly in a managerial or administrative capacity and supervisors drawing wages above a ceiling that an amendment of 2010 fixed at ten thousand rupees a month. The social security statutes protect the employee, a wider term which in the provident fund statute expressly reaches a person employed through a contractor in connection with the work of the establishment. The Factories Act protects the worker, defined by presence in the manufacturing process. The Minimum Wages Act protected the employee in a scheduled employment, a category that tied protection to a list. Each definition was serviceable for its own statute's purpose, and indefensible as a system: the same human being, on the same day, in the same factory, could be a worker for safety law, an employee for provident fund, outside the workman definition because of a supervisory label for dispute law, and outside minimum wage coverage because the employment was unscheduled. Employers learned to draft appointment letters against the seams between the definitions, and a generation of litigation turned on characterisation, on whether duties were genuinely supervisory, whether a contractor was genuine or a camouflage, whether a trainee was an apprentice or a worker in disguise. The Second National Commission on Labour recommended one definition of worker across the groups of laws, with social security extended to everyone regardless of the label, precisely because the definitional patchwork had become a coverage policy in itself, deciding by vocabulary what Parliament had never decided by vote. The codes move part of the way: each code carries a more uniform definitional scheme than the statutes it replaced, the wage codes share a single definition of wages, and the Industrial Relations Code's definition of worker raises the supervisory wage ceiling to eighteen thousand rupees a month. But the codes retain distinct terms for distinct purposes, worker in the industrial relations code and employee in the wages and social security codes, because the purposes genuinely differ; a person may need insurance without needing retrenchment protection nomenclature, and the framework's settlement is harmonisation within families rather than one word for the whole field. A reader who meets a labour question should therefore always ask the prior question: protected as what, under which statute's vocabulary. The wages half of the definitions problem shows the stakes in rupees. The old statutes defined wages separately for payment discipline, for bonus computation, for insurance contributions and for provident fund, and the definitions diverged on dearness allowance, house rent, overtime and bonus itself, inviting a pay structure engineered definition by definition: a low basic wage for provident fund, swollen allowances for take-home pay, and contribution bases that bore little relation to the cost of the employment. The codes' single definition of wages, anchored on basic pay and dearness allowance and capping all excluded components together at fifty per cent of total remuneration, is aimed squarely at that engineering, and its consequences run through every family's arithmetic: gratuity, contributions, bonus and compensation all rebase onto a definition that pay structuring can no longer hollow out. Of all the codes' technical provisions, this is the one most likely to be felt in monthly payslips across the covered sector.

## The threshold architecture: the mechanism that decides everything

Underneath the accumulation sits the field's defining mechanism, and it deserves to be stated as the single most important fact in this article. Almost every Indian labour statute applies only above a numerical threshold of workers. The Factories Act 1948 applied to premises with ten or more workers where power was used and twenty or more where it was not. The provident fund statute applied at twenty employees. The state insurance statute applied at ten. Standing orders were required at one hundred workmen. The prior-permission regime for retrenchment and closure applied at one hundred. Below each line, the statute simply did not reach the workplace, however harsh conditions inside it might be; above each line, the full apparatus of registration, inspection, contribution and penalty switched on at once. Indian labour legislation, in other words, regulates by headcount rather than by activity. It does not ask what a workplace does to its workers. It asks how many workers there are.

The mechanism deserves analysis rather than mere description, because its consequences drive the whole reform debate. A threshold is a cliff edge, not a slope. The hundredth workman in the old dispensation did not add one unit of compliance; he carried the establishment across the line at which certified standing orders, and at the same figure the prior-permission chapter, attached themselves to every subsequent managerial decision. Rational employers respond to cliff edges predictably: they cluster just below them. Establishments split into multiple legal units, production is fragmented across premises, workers are engaged through contractors so that they count, if they count at all, on someone else's roll, and headcounts are managed as carefully as inventories. The result is visible in the size distribution of Indian industry itself, which is dominated by very small units and very large ones with a comparatively thin band between, the missing middle that every industrial policy document laments. The law does not merely regulate the structure of Indian industry. By attaching its obligations to numbers, it helps to manufacture that structure.

Thresholds also explain why the field's definitions matter so much. A statute that switches on at a number must say precisely what is being counted: workers, workmen, employees, persons employed on any day of the preceding twelve months, contract labour included or excluded. Much of the litigation in this field is counting litigation. Whether contract workers count towards the principal employer's threshold, whether an establishment's separated units are in substance one establishment, whether apprentices and supervisors swell or escape the count: these unglamorous questions decide real coverage. The courts developed a doctrine of functional integrality to police the splitting strategy, asking whether separated units share management, finance, employment and functional unity, and treating them as one establishment where the separation is a device. The doctrine is an admission, embedded in the case law, that the threshold mechanism invites evasion and must be defended by substance-over-form reasoning to work at all.

Thresholds also move without being amended, in both directions, and a reader of the framework's history should watch for drift. A wage ceiling fixed in nominal rupees is eroded by inflation until the legislature revises it: the provident fund ceiling stood at six thousand five hundred rupees a month from 2001 until 2014, and across those thirteen years rising nominal wages carried cohorts of employees over the line out of compulsory coverage, a silent contraction of the fund's reach accomplished without a vote. Headcounts drift the other way: as an economy formalises and average establishment size grows, a static line at twenty or fifty captures a rising share of employment with no amendment at all, a silent expansion. The codes add a safety valve against at least the first drift by leaving the ceilings to notification, where revision is an executive act, at the price of removing the figures from parliamentary view. Either way, the numbers on the threshold map are a snapshot, and the discipline of this field is to date every number one quotes.

A second kind of threshold rides beneath the headcounts and is easy to miss: the wage ceiling, which switches coverage off at the top of an individual's pay rather than at the size of the establishment. The provident fund's ceiling made membership compulsory below a wage and negotiable above it; the state insurance ceiling excluded the better-paid employee of a covered establishment from insurance designed for his juniors; the bonus statute carried both a floor of service and a ceiling of wages; the workman definition's supervisory ceiling lifted whole categories of senior staff out of dispute law by salary alone. The ceilings express a distributive judgement, that mandatory protection is for those least able to bargain for it privately, and they generate their own cliff edges and their own gaming, pay structured to cross a ceiling, allowances recharacterised to escape a definition, which is precisely the behaviour the codes' unified definition of wages, capping excluded allowances at half of total remuneration, was designed to suppress. A complete account of the threshold mechanism must therefore count two staircases, one climbed by establishments as they grow and one descended by employees as they rise, and the social security map of any real workforce is the intersection of the two.

It is equally important to see what thresholds are for, because the reform debate routinely caricatures them as arbitrary. The original case for thresholds was administrative and humane at once. Inspection capacity was scarce, and the state chose to spend it where the most workers stood under one roof. Very small workplaces were often household or artisanal in character, where the full statutory apparatus of registers, returns and certified standing orders would crush the activity it meant to protect, and where the employment relation shade into family production. A threshold is a judgement that below a certain size, the costs of formal regulation exceed its yield, and that the state's protective attention is better concentrated higher up. That judgement was contestable in 1948. What changed over the following decades was not the logic but the evidence about where Indian workers actually are: the overwhelming majority work in establishments far below every threshold, so a mechanism designed to concentrate protection ended up concentrating exclusion. The thresholds did not malfunction. They did exactly what they were built to do, in an economy whose shape they also helped to freeze.

Thresholds of this kind are not an Indian eccentricity, which matters because the reform debate sometimes proceeds as if size-contingent regulation were a local pathology. France attaches a large block of obligations at fifty employees, and economists Luis Garicano, Claire Lelarge and John Van Reenen documented a visible bunching of French firms just below that line, with a corresponding bulge in the size distribution at forty-nine. Germany attaches works council and co-determination rights by headcount. Many American federal statutes exempt employers below fifteen or twenty employees. The Indian distinctiveness lies not in having thresholds but in how much rides on them relative to the shape of the economy: a very high share of employment in very small establishments, so that each threshold excludes more workers than the same device excludes in an economy of mid-sized firms, and a multiplicity of different thresholds across statutes, so that an establishment crosses lines at ten, twenty, fifty and one hundred in a staggered climb, each crossing adding a separate registration, a separate contribution and a separate inspectorate. It is the combination, many lines and a workforce concentrated far below the lowest of them, that made the threshold the framework's operative principle.

The counting rules repay a closer look, because thresholds are only as strong as their arithmetic. The Factories Act did not require ten workers on the day of inspection; it counted a premises as a factory if ten or more workers were working on any day of the preceding twelve months, a drafting choice that blocked the simplest evasion of staffing down for the inspector's visit. Establishments under common ownership on adjacent premises could be treated together where the processes were connected. The provident fund statute counted employees including those engaged through contractors, so outsourcing the headcount did not outsource the contribution. Registration thresholds in the contract labour statute counted the contractor's deployment in the establishment, creating a double registration, establishment and contractor both licensed or registered against the same workforce. The cumulative effect of these rules is that the framework's draftsmen understood evasion early and drafted against it repeatedly, and yet the bunching below the lines persisted, which suggests the motive to stay small was never merely arithmetical. A firm below the lines is also below the notice of unions, of conciliation machinery and of the press; thresholds exclude establishments from a culture of formal industrial relations and not only from a list of dues. Reform that moves numbers without addressing that culture, the reform India enacted, should be expected to change behaviour at the margin rather than transform it, which is what the Rajasthan evidence, discussed below, in fact showed.

Reading the map vertically teaches the field faster than reading any single statute. Take a manufacturing unit with nine workers using power. Under the old law it stood outside factory licensing, outside state insurance if it is counted as an establishment of nine, outside gratuity and maternity coverage as an establishment, and outside the provident fund; its workers' enforceable statutory entitlements reduced mainly to minimum wages in a scheduled employment, payment-of-wages discipline where that Act applied, compensation for employment injury, and the constitutional prohibitions. Take the same unit grown to forty-five workers. Factory licensing attaches, insurance and gratuity and maternity coverage attach, the provident fund attaches at twenty, and the contract labour chapter attaches if the hands are engaged through a contractor past the line; yet the two heaviest industrial-relations obligations, certified standing orders and prior permission for retrenchment, remained out of reach at forty-five under the old law, which reserved them for establishments of one hundred. Take a plant of two hundred and fifty workers. Everything in the preceding sentence applies, and under the code regime it still stands below the three hundred line at which standing orders and the permission regime begin. The practical meaning of the map is that Indian labour law has never been one blanket spread over employment. It is a staircase, and each step is a number.

A hospital makes the map's fourth case, and the most instructive one for the definition of industry. A private nursing home with thirty staff crossed no factory line, since nothing is manufactured, yet it stood inside the Industrial Disputes Act after the Rajappa reading made hospitals an industry, inside state insurance at ten employees, inside provident fund at twenty, inside the shops Act of its state for its administrative staff, and inside the maternity and gratuity lines at ten. Its labour law was assembled from five statutes none of which mentions hospitals, which is the framework working exactly as designed: categories first, sectors second, and the reader's task is always to rebuild the applicable set establishment by establishment. The codes reproduce the pattern with their own categories, and the nursing home's map changes mainly at its edges, which is again the consolidation thesis confirmed at ward level.

Two monetary ceilings ride on top of the headcount staircase and deserve mention because they quietly decide coverage for individuals. The provident fund statute's wage ceiling, fifteen thousand rupees a month from September 2014, meant that an employee joining above that wage could be kept outside mandatory coverage in a twenty-person establishment that was itself squarely inside the Act. The state insurance ceiling, twenty-one thousand rupees a month from January 2017, worked the same way from the other side: the establishment was covered, but the better-paid employee was not insured. The codes retain the technique of notified wage ceilings while redrawing the headcounts, which is one reason a plain statement that the codes simplified labour law needs immediate qualification. The staircase survives. Some of its steps moved.

Minimum wages make the state layer visible in a worker's pocket. Because fixation belongs to the appropriate government employment by employment, the statutory floor for the same occupation has differed across state borders throughout the framework's life, sometimes sharply between an industrialised state and its poorer neighbour, and employers in border districts have always been able to read the difference on a map. The divergence is concurrency working as designed, local cost and local politics priced into local floors, and the national floor wage introduced by the Code on Wages is the framework's first systematic answer from above: not a uniform national minimum, which the federal structure would resist, but a floor beneath floors, set by the Centre after consulting an advisory board, beneath which no state's fixation may fall. The device is a characteristically Indian compromise between uniformity and federalism, and it belongs on the map beside Article 254(2) as one of the framework's two great reconciliation mechanisms.

## The state layer in operation

Concurrency is an abstraction until a reader watches it operate on the ground, where it produces three concrete phenomena. The first is parallel legislation: statutes made by states that occupy space the central corpus leaves thin. Every state has a shops and commercial establishments Act regulating opening hours, weekly holidays and employment conditions in the shops and offices where a growing majority of urban Indians work, a subject the central factory-centred statutes barely touched; the template was set by provincial Acts of the 1940s such as the Bombay Shops and Establishments Act 1948. Maharashtra's Recognition of Trade Unions and Prevention of Unfair Labour Practices Act 1971 remains the leading example of a state building machinery, recognition of unions by verification of membership and a law of unfair labour practices, that the central trade union statute never contained. States have also legislated welfare funds and boards for particular occupations, mathadi boards for head-load workers in Maharashtra being the most studied, and have built the state-level delivery machinery for the unorganised workers' legislation.

The second phenomenon is variation within central statutes by state amendment, of which Rajasthan 2014 is only the most famous instance. States lowered the standing orders line below one hundred in some territories, raised the contract labour line to fifty in several, and amended the wage and bonus statutes' operation in localised ways, each variation surviving in its state by presidential assent. The result was that the Industrial Disputes Act, a single central statute with a single title, did not contain the same law in Maharashtra as it contained in West Bengal, and a national employer with plants in five states administered five subtly different versions of one Act. National consolidation into codes reduces this variation prospectively, but the codes' own commencement position, with state rules framed at different speeds and some states slower than others, reproduces a version of the same geography: for a transitional period, and in truth permanently in the detail, the labour law of India is a federation of labour laws sharing a common skeleton.

The third phenomenon is the quietest and the most consequential for individual workers: the determination, statute by statute, of the appropriate government. Most of the framework's Acts are administered by the Centre for a defined class of establishments and by the states for the rest. Under the Industrial Disputes Act the Centre is the appropriate government for railways, mines, oilfields, major ports, banking and insurance companies and central public sector undertakings, among others, while the state government is appropriate for the generality of factories, plantations and shops in its territory. The same factory can therefore fall to one government for disputes and look to state notifications for its minimum wages and factory licence, and a worker's forum, the conciliation officer to approach, the authority to sanction prosecution, follows the classification rather than the worker's preference. Errors at this step are the commonest practical mistake in the field, and they are unforced: the classification is printed in each statute's definitions, waiting to be read.

## Enforcement: inspectors, prosecutions and the paper state

### Who enforces labour law against an employer who ignores it?

Enforcement runs through inspectors appointed under each statute, who could enter premises, examine registers and launch prosecutions before magistrates, supplemented by claim authorities for wages and gratuity, by the funds' own enforcement officers for unpaid contributions, and by factory licensing authorities who could refuse or withdraw the licence on which the establishment's lawful operation depended.

The framework's chronic weakness was never only its coverage; it was the enforcement of what it covered. Inspection under the old statutes was fragmented by design, each Act appointing its own inspectors with powers of entry, examination of registers and documents, and interrogation, and most offences were criminal, punishable with fines that inflation had reduced to token sums or with short imprisonment rarely imposed, prosecuted before ordinary magistrates after sanction where the statute required it. Compliance therefore rested heavily on the paperwork state: registers in prescribed forms, returns on prescribed dates, notices displayed on prescribed boards. A workplace could be impeccable in its registers and poor in its reality, or decent in its reality and perpetually in default on its forms, and the system's sanctions fell more reliably on the second failure than the first. The codes attack this layer directly. The inspector is redesignated inspector-cum-facilitator, with advice and awareness written into the role alongside inspection; inspection is organised through schemes that may allocate visits by randomised selection and require reports to be uploaded within a set period; many offences are decriminalised into monetary penalties adjudicated administratively; and the establishment-facing obligations of registration, licence and annual return are consolidated towards single points. Whether these changes amount to a lighter touch or to a smarter one is contested, unions reading facilitation as dilution and employers reading the retained penal skeleton as proof nothing changed. What is not contested is the diagnosis both sides share: a framework enforced through a dozen registers under a dozen statutes was enforcing its own paperwork more faithfully than its protections, and the consolidation of enforcement machinery is the codes' most operationally significant act.

## The courts and the widening perimeter

The legislature drew the framework's lines by number. The courts spent decades pushing the framework's reach beyond what the drafters' categories suggested, and the pattern of that expansion is the third structural fact a reader needs. The clearest instance is the definition of industry in the Industrial Disputes Act 1947, the gateway word that decides which establishments the entire disputes machinery serves. In Bangalore Water Supply and Sewerage Board v A. Rajappa in 1978, a seven-judge bench led by Justice V. R. Krishna Iyer laid down the triple test: an activity is an industry where there is systematic activity, organised cooperation between employer and employee, and production or distribution of goods or services calculated to satisfy human wants, with profit motive irrelevant and only genuinely sovereign functions excluded. The reading swept hospitals, universities, research institutes, clubs and charitable projects into a statute drafted with factories in mind, and extended statutory protection to workers whom the drafters had not obviously contemplated. Parliament attempted a legislative narrowing in 1982, enacting an amended definition with express exclusions, and then never brought the amendment into force, leaving the judicial text to govern by default for four more decades. A Constitution Bench in State of Uttar Pradesh v Jai Bir Singh in 2005 doubted the breadth of the 1978 reading and set a reference in motion; the reference was directed to a nine-judge bench in 2017; and the nine-judge bench delivered judgment on 20 August 2026, retaining the triple test's essential framework for disputes governed by the repealed Act while refining its application, and holding that the Industrial Relations Code's own definition of industry must be interpreted on the Code's text independently of the 1978 precedent. The lifecycle is the field in parable form: an expansive judicial reading, an enacted legislative correction that nobody commenced, a reference that outlived the statute it concerned, and a final answer delivered into a legal world that had already replaced the question. The detailed treatment of the definition belongs to [the article on the Industrial Disputes Act](/2013/05/15/industrial-disputes-act-1947-explained/), which owns this doctrine; what matters at framework level is the direction of travel. Again and again, in definition after definition, the courts read labour statutes by their protective purpose and pulled workers at the margin inside.

The same impulse appears wherever a category threatened to become an escape hatch. The definition of workman was read to include categories of employees whom employers had labelled out of it, with the courts insisting that designation follows the nature of the duties rather than the title on the appointment letter. Contract labour arrangements were tested against the reality of control and supervision, and sham contracts were lifted to reveal the true employer beneath. The constitutional cases widened the perimeter from another direction: the reading of forced labour in Article 23 to cover work exacted below the minimum wage, the reading of livelihood into Article 21, and the recognition of occupational health as an aspect of the right to life each supplied rights that operated where the statutes' thresholds did not reach. The public interest litigation era carried the perimeter furthest from the establishment form altogether. In Bandhua Mukti Morcha v Union of India in 1984, the Supreme Court entertained a letter about bonded labourers in stone quarries as a writ petition, read Articles 21 and 23 together to require release and rehabilitation, and supervised the respondent state through commissioners, creating a template by which the framework's most invisible workers, bonded, migrant, home-based, reached constitutional court without passing through any statute's applicability clause. The PIL route never replaced the statutes; it stood behind them as the remedy of last resort for the populations the threshold architecture had left outside every gate, and its existence is part of the framework's honest description. There is a genuine counter-current, and honesty requires recording it. The same Court has also enforced the statutes' textual limits, refused to convert abolition of contract labour into automatic absorption of the workers in Steel Authority of India v National Union Waterfront Workers in 2001, and repeatedly reminded litigants that welfare interpretation cannot rewrite an applicability clause. The perimeter widened by interpretation, but it widened within a structure the courts did not claim the power to redesign. Only the legislature could move the numbers, and for thirty years the legislature mostly declined. The courts also disciplined the framework's harshest device when it overreached. The closure permission provision in its original form was struck down by the Supreme Court in Excel Wear v Union of India in 1978, on the ground that an unguided power to refuse closure, without reasons, hearing or time limit, was an unreasonable restriction on the right to carry on business; Parliament re-enacted the provision in 1982 with the procedural standards the judgment had demanded. The episode completes the pattern of the courts' role at framework level: expansive on definitions, corrective on procedure, conservative on structure. Judges widened doors and insisted they be fairly hung. They did not move walls, and the walls were numbers.

The adjudicatory machinery deserves a word at framework level, because tribunals did not merely apply the framework; over decades they authored a significant part of it. Wage fixation by adjudication developed the region-cum-industry principle, the rule that an employer's paying capacity is judged against comparable concerns in the region rather than in isolation, and the doctrine that a fair wage sits between the statutory minimum and a living wage, with the industry's capacity to pay fixing the point. Dearness allowance jurisprudence built the practice of neutralising cost-of-living increases. Bonus, before its statute, was awarded by tribunals as a share of available surplus. A body of industrial jurisprudence thus grew up inside the tribunals parallel to the statute book, and practitioners of the old system spoke of the law of awards with the same seriousness as the law of Acts. The codes re-enact the tribunal machinery in a leaner form, with industrial tribunals of two members, one judicial and one administrative, and a stated ambition of speedier disposal; what happens to the accreted doctrines of the award era, as definitions change beneath them, is one of the genuinely open questions of the transition, and it will be answered the way the Rajappa question was answered, slowly, by benches, case by case.

### Do the four Labour Codes abolish state labour laws?

No. The codes are central statutes that subsumed twenty-nine central laws. State statutes outside the codes' field, state shops and establishments Acts, and the states' power to legislate on concurrent entries and to frame rules under the codes all survive, so the two-level structure of the framework continues.

## The life of an industrial dispute

The framework's machinery is best understood by following one dispute through it, since the stages, and the restraints that accumulate at each stage, are the industrial relations family in motion. A dispute begins as a grievance or a demand: a charter of demands from a union, or an employer's notice of change in service conditions, for which the Act requires twenty-one days of notice before specified changes take effect. In an establishment of twenty or more workers, the statutory grievance redressal committee is the first forum, a bipartite body required to complete its proceedings within a set period. Failing settlement, the dispute passes to the conciliation officer of the appropriate government, an official whose statutory duty is to mediate and induce a settlement, and whose report to the government, settlement or failure, is the hinge of the system. While conciliation runs, and for a period after it, strikes and lockouts in respect of the dispute are prohibited, the framework's foundational bargain being that industrial action pauses wherever the state's machinery is engaged. The parties may agree to refer the dispute to voluntary arbitration, a route the Act provides and practice rarely uses, or the government may refer it, of its own motion and without the parties' consent, to a labour court or industrial tribunal for adjudication, the power that distinguishes the Indian system from purely voluntarist models. The tribunal hears the dispute as a court, publishes an award, and the award, after a short period in which the government may modify or reject an award against itself as employer, binds the parties, with implementation enforceable and breach punishable. Around this spine sit the system's restraints on the dispute's subject matter: lay-off and retrenchment carry notice and compensation conditions, larger establishments carried the permission requirement, and strikes require notice in public utility services and, under the Industrial Relations Code, in every industrial establishment. Two features of the flow reward emphasis. The government's reference power means that the state decides which disputes the system hears, a gatekeeping discretion that has been litigated as heavily as any merits question in the field. And the flow is deliberately linear, grievance, conciliation, adjudication, each stage narrowing the parties' freedom to act outside it, which is the 1947 philosophy, the courtroom over the picket line, rendered as procedure. The codes preserve the flow with modifications, including time limits aimed at its notorious delays; the philosophy survives the renumbering intact.

## Rigid or narrow: the argument stated fairly

Every general debate about this framework is conducted as an argument between two propositions, and a stance worth taking requires stating each at its strongest before judging either. The first proposition is that Indian labour law is rigid: that the accumulation of protective statutes, above all the Industrial Disputes Act with its permission regime for retrenchment and closure, made it hazardous for an employer to hire permanent workers at scale, and so pushed industry towards capital intensity, contract labour and smallness. The strongest scholarly version of this claim is the work of Timothy Besley and Robin Burgess, published in 2004, which coded the direction of state amendments to the Industrial Disputes Act over the post-independence decades and found that states which amended in a pro-worker direction experienced slower growth in registered manufacturing output, employment and investment than states which amended in a pro-employer direction. The strongest official version is the Economic Survey of 2018-19, which gave the phenomenon a name that stuck. Firms with fewer than one hundred workers that were more than ten years old, which the Survey called dwarfs, accounted for more than half of all firms in organised manufacturing by number, yet contributed only fourteen per cent of employment and a mere eight per cent of productivity, while large firms, about fifteen per cent by number, accounted for three-quarters of employment and close to ninety per cent of productivity. The Survey laid part of the blame squarely on size-based incentives and on inflexible labour regulation containing size-based limitations, and the threshold map in this article shows the mechanism it meant: at one hundred workers the old law hung its heaviest obligations, and firms organised their affairs to stop short of the line.

The second proposition is that the rigidity debate is a debate about a minority. The protections whose rigidity is complained of attach, by the framework's own design, only above thresholds, and the large majority of Indian workers have always worked below them or outside the establishment form altogether. The National Commission for Enterprises in the Unorganised Sector estimated in 2007 that about ninety-two per cent of the workforce was in informal employment, counting workers in the unorganised sector and informal workers within the organised sector together. For that majority there was no standing order, no retrenchment permission, no certified protection against arbitrary dismissal to be rigid in the first place; there was, for most of the period, not even a statutory minimum wage outside the scheduled employments the state governments chose to notify. On this view, to describe the framework as among the world's most rigid is to commit a category error: it takes the experience of the covered minority as the character of the whole, and mistakes a law that is strict in a small room for a law that governs the house.

There is a further body of evidence that belongs in any fair version of the debate, because it complicates the rigidity claim from inside the covered sector itself. Across the liberalisation decades, employers in registered manufacturing obtained much of the flexibility the statutes appeared to deny them, not by amending the law but by reclassifying the workforce: the proportion of contract workers in factory employment rose steadily, fixed-term and trainee categories widened in practice, and production was reorganised across vendor networks that kept each node small. Scholars of the period describe a system of flexibility achieved within formal rigidity, at a price paid not by the employers who obtained the flexibility but by the workers reclassified to supply it, whose wages, security and bargaining power thinned as their legal status blurred. The observation matters for judging the codes. When the Industrial Relations Code recognises fixed-term employment expressly and grants fixed-term workers parity of service conditions with pro-rata gratuity, it is legislating for a practice the market had already normalised, converting an evasion into a category; whether that conversion dignifies the practice or exposes it to standards depends on enforcement the text cannot guarantee. Similarly, the permission regime's bite fell unevenly: large employers with legal departments managed it as process, while mid-sized employers experienced it as prohibition, which is one reason the three hundred line found support well beyond employer federations. The rigidity debate, stated at its most precise, is thus not about whether the framework constrained employers; it is about whom its constraints selected, which employers they selected, and which workers paid for the circumventions.

Both propositions are true, and they are true of different populations, which is why the argument never resolves. The evidence for each deserves its qualifications stated openly. Besley and Burgess measured the effect of amendment direction in registered manufacturing, and later economists have disputed how much of the pattern their coding captures and how far labour law, rather than power, land, credit or demand, explains stunted firm growth; the study of Rajasthan's 2014 amendments by Chaudhary and Sharma found that easing the restrictions reduced the implicit regulatory tax on firms without producing sizable aggregate effects on employment, which cuts against any simple claim that thresholds alone held Indian industry small. The coverage figures, for their part, describe a workforce whose informality long predates and far exceeds anything the thresholds caused; small firms stay small for many reasons, and the threshold is one among them. The verdict this article defends is the one the structure itself supports. India's problem has never been that its labour law is too strict. Its problem is that its labour law is too narrow: a protective code of genuine sophistication addressed to a fraction of the workforce, sitting atop an unprotected majority, with the boundary between the two drawn by headcount. The rigidity complaint and the protection complaint are the same complaint photographed from opposite sides of the threshold line. One refinement completes the verdict, and it concerns the difference between regulating establishments and insuring persons. Establishment regulation, hours, safety, discipline, dispute machinery, is unavoidably local and size-sensitive, and thresholds there are a crude but intelligible rationing of administrative attention. Personal insurance, against sickness, injury, old age and maternity, has no such logic: a worker's need for a pension does not begin at her employer's twentieth hire, and the framework's deepest design error was to route personal insurance through establishment gates borrowed from factory law. The codes half-correct the error, registering unorganised and gig workers in their own right while keeping the contributory funds behind their headcounts, and the unfinished correction is the framework's clearest agenda item. A reader who grasps that distinction, between the law of the workplace and the law of the worker, holds the key that unlocks every family's design, every threshold's rationale and every reform proposal's real content.

### How much of India's workforce do the establishment-based statutes actually reach?

Only a minority. The National Commission for Enterprises in the Unorganised Sector estimated informal employment at about ninety-two per cent of the workforce, so the factory-style statutes, which switch on above headcounts in formal establishments, were written for the small covered segment from the start.

## The dual labour market: what the covered bargain cost and bought

Between the rigidity story and the narrowness story stands a fact both sides use and neither can dissolve: protection under the framework was never only a cost imposed on employers; it was also a bargain that bought the covered sector something real. The permanent worker above the thresholds held a package, job security policed by adjudication, a provident fund, insurance, bonus, gratuity, paid leave and a certified rulebook, that amounted to a private welfare state inside the factory gate, in a country whose public welfare state was thin. That package explains the intensity with which the covered minority defended it, the premium job-seekers placed on formal employment, and the resentment of the uncovered majority, for whom the same labour market offered none of it. Economists describe the outcome as dualism: one labour market, two regimes, with mobility between them obstructed precisely by the value of the package on the protected side, since a formal job is too valuable to leave and too encumbered to multiply. Dualism also explains the politics that froze the framework for three decades. Every proposal to widen coverage by lightening the package met the covered minority's organised defence, and every proposal to lighten the package without widening coverage met the charge of dilution; the codes threaded the needle by lightening industrial relations control while widening the wage floor and the social security register, which is why both the rigidity camp and the protection camp can find their evidence in the same four statutes. The threshold sits at the centre of the dualism, because the line that switches protection on is the same line that prices it, and any honest verdict on the framework must hold both halves of that sentence at once.

Why, then, did reform arrive as codes rather than as amendments, after thirty years of stalemate? The answer is political economy rather than drafting taste. Amending the Industrial Disputes Act alone concentrated all opposition on one symbolically charged statute, while a consolidation exercise, framed as simplification and ease of compliance, distributed attention across twenty-nine repeals and recruited the Second National Commission's bipartisan authority behind the project. Grouping also allowed trade-offs invisible in single-Act reform: a raised permission threshold could be packaged with a universal wage floor, an extended strike notice with recognition of negotiating unions, and the whole carried in two parliamentary sessions. The framework's history teaches the general lesson plainly. In a concurrent field with organised parties on every side, reform travels in bundles or it does not travel at all, and the seventeen years between the blueprint and the first enactment measure not drafting difficulty but the time needed for a bundle large enough to carry.

## The consolidation: a 2002 blueprint and the four codes

The idea of gathering the accumulation into a small number of codes is older than the codes by two decades, and its authorship matters for examinations and for understanding what the codes were trying to be. The Second National Commission on Labour was set up on 15 October 1999 under the chairmanship of Ravindra Varma, a former Union labour minister, with two terms of reference: to suggest rationalisation of the laws relating to labour in the organised sector, and to suggest umbrella legislation for a minimum level of protection for workers in the unorganised sector. It reported to the Prime Minister on 29 June 2002. Its central structural recommendation was that the existing body of labour laws, some forty-four central Acts as it counted them, should be broadly grouped into four or five codes on a functional basis, covering industrial relations, wages, social security, safety, and welfare and working conditions. It recommended that the coverage and the definition of worker should be the same across the groups, that social security should extend to all employees including the managerial and supervisory categories excluded from the definition of workman, and it proposed Labour Relations Commissions as part of a three-tier adjudication structure with Lok Adalats and Labour Courts. It expressly declined to recommend disturbing labour's place on the Concurrent List. Read the four codes enacted seventeen and eighteen years later against this blueprint and the lineage is unmistakable: the functional grouping is the Commission's, the common-definitions ambition is the Commission's, the social security extension to the unorganised is the Commission's second term of reference arriving by statute. The codes are the Second National Commission's report, enacted by a Parliament that took a generation to agree with it.

The enactment itself came in two steps. The Code on Wages 2019, Act 29 of 2019, received assent on 8 August 2019 and subsumed four statutes: the Payment of Wages Act 1936, the Minimum Wages Act 1948, the Payment of Bonus Act 1965 and the Equal Remuneration Act 1976. In September 2020 Parliament passed the remaining three codes, each receiving assent on 28 September 2020. The Industrial Relations Code 2020, Act 35 of 2020, subsumed the Trade Unions Act 1926, the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act 1946 and the Industrial Disputes Act 1947. The Code on Social Security 2020, Act 36 of 2020, subsumed nine statutes, among them the Employees' State Insurance Act 1948, the Employees' Provident Funds Act 1952, the Employees' Compensation Act 1923, the Maternity Benefit Act 1961, the Payment of Gratuity Act 1972, the Employment Exchanges (Compulsory Notification of Vacancies) Act 1959 and the Unorganised Workers' Social Security Act 2008. The Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code 2020, Act 37 of 2020, subsumed thirteen, among them the Factories Act 1948, the Mines Act 1952, the Plantations Labour Act 1951, the Contract Labour (Regulation and Abolition) Act 1970 and the Inter-State Migrant Workmen Act 1979. Four and three and nine and thirteen make twenty-nine, and the arithmetic is the cleanest way to remember the shape of the consolidation.

What the codes changed in substance is best understood through this article's two keys. On the constitutional key, they changed nothing: the Concurrent List settlement survives entire, state labour statutes survive, state rule-making under the codes is indispensable to their operation, and the states' power to seek assent for variations survives with it. On the threshold key, they changed the settings rather than the mechanism. The standing orders line and the prior permission line moved from one hundred to three hundred. The factory line doubled, from ten and twenty to twenty and forty. The contract labour line moved from twenty to fifty. The social security headcounts, ten for insurance, gratuity and maternity and twenty for the provident fund, were carried across essentially unmoved. Against these raisings stand genuine extensions that work by abolishing a threshold rather than moving it. The Code on Wages detaches the minimum wage from the old device of scheduled employments and extends minimum wage entitlement to every employee, with a national floor wage beneath which no state fixation may fall, so the largest single coverage gap of the old framework, the worker in an unscheduled trade in a state that had not scheduled it, is closed in principle. The Code on Social Security writes gig workers and platform workers into the statute book for the first time, defining them and providing for welfare schemes funded in part by aggregator contributions, a recognition that the employment relation the old statutes assumed no longer describes how a visible class of Indians works. Fixed-term employees are recognised as a lawful category with pro-rata gratuity. A single definition of wages, built around basic pay and dearness allowance with excluded allowances capped at half of total remuneration, replaces the divergent wage definitions that each old statute carried for itself. Whether these extensions outweigh the raisings is the live argument about the codes' character, and the comparison article in this cluster owns the full accounting; the framework verdict is narrower. The consolidation simplified the map without redrawing the territory. India still regulates labour by headcount, under two legislatures at once, and the codes are best read as the old architecture renumbered, restated and at selected points extended.

## Enacted, commenced, unfinished: the codes in operation

A code on the statute book and a code in operation are different things, and this field's history punishes anyone who confuses them; the uncommenced amendment of 1982 stood on the books for four decades as the standing warning. The four codes were enacted in 2019 and 2020 and then waited. Their commencement clauses left the date to notification, and the notifications did not come, because commencement required the delegated legislation beneath the codes to exist: the rules that the Centre must frame for its sphere and that every state must frame for the generality of establishments, in a concurrent field where most workplaces answer to state administration. The waiting period itself followed an older Indian rhythm, in which the interval between enactment and operation is where a reform's opponents and its implementers negotiate the real settlement. Central draft rules were published and consulted on; state governments, several of which had elections and administrations of a different political colour from the Centre's, framed their drafts on their own timetables; the trade union movement, which had struck against the codes' passage, pressed the interval as evidence that the reform lacked consent, while employer bodies pressed for certainty about which regime a hiring decision would meet. A framework built by two legislatures can be switched on by neither alone, and the five-year commencement of the codes is the Concurrent List expressing itself in time rather than in text. Draft rules circulated, states moved at different speeds, and the old statutes continued in force in the interim because a repeal that has not commenced has not happened. The codes were finally brought into force together on 21 November 2025, and the twenty-nine subsumed statutes were repealed from that date. The delegated legislation then had to catch up with the commencement rather than precede it: the Central Rules under the four codes were notified on 8 May 2026, and state rules, which carry the operative detail for most private establishments, remained incomplete long after commencement, with rules framed under the repealed statutes continuing to apply in the interim so far as they are consistent with the codes. The durable lesson is structural rather than partisan. In a concurrent field, a central consolidation is only ever half a reform on the day it commences; the other half is written, state by state and rule by rule, afterwards, and a reader who wants to know which labour law governs a workplace on a given day must ask two questions, what the codes say and what the relevant state has made under them.

There is one further transition trap the framework reader should carry. The repeal of the old statutes did not erase the case law built on them, most of which construes concepts the codes re-enacted in altered words, and the Jai Bir Singh bench's direction that the Industrial Relations Code's definitions be read on their own text is the governing caution: precedent under a repealed Act is a guide to its successor only where the successor's language permits. Accrued rights, pending proceedings and benefits crystallised under the old law are preserved by the codes' repeal and savings provisions and by the general law of repeal, so a worker's gratuity earned over decades of service does not depend on the employer's preferred reading of the changeover. The framework's past, as so often in Indian legislation, remains inside its present. One earlier episode of consolidation-that-was-not should be recorded here, because it is the codes' truest precedent. The First National Commission on Labour under Justice P. B. Gajendragadkar, reporting in 1969, reviewed the same accumulation and expressly declined to recommend an integrated code, preferring evolutionary improvement of the existing statutes; the Second Commission's contrary recommendation in 2002 therefore reversed a settled official position, and the reversal itself had to wait for political conditions that the commissions could describe but not create. The arc from 1969 to 2002 to 2020 is the reform history of Indian labour law in three dates: consolidation considered and refused, consolidation recommended and shelved, consolidation enacted and commenced, with the subject's governing structure untouched at every step.

The mechanics of the lag deserve a precise statement, because they determine what an employer or worker actually faced after commencement. A code that has commenced repeals its predecessors, but rights and obligations are administered through rules, forms, authorities and notifications, and until the new rules exist, the savings provisions and the general principles of repeal keep the old machinery turning where it is consistent with the new text: registrations granted under a repealed Act continue, inspectors appointed under it function under the corresponding code power, and proceedings pending at commencement are completed under the law that began them. The Centre framed its rules for its own sphere first; the states, whose labour departments administer the generality of establishments, moved according to their own legislative calendars, several working from published drafts while others had yet to consult their tripartite bodies. The consequence for a multi-state employer was a patchwork transition inside a single commencement: one national text, several state timelines. Enforcement during the transition inherited the same patchwork, since penalties, compounding and inspection schemes under the codes depend on the rules and notifications of the administering government, and an act that is an offence compounded at one penalty in the central sphere may await its state schedule elsewhere. The field has lived through such interregna before, most comparably when the great statutes of 1946 to 1952 commenced in stages and the provincial machinery converted at different speeds, and the historical pattern is consistent: the text settles first, the forms settle second, and practice settles last, over years, in the offices of the inspectorate rather than in the pages of the Gazette. The consequence for the framework's students is a rule of reading: for any commencement question, the answer has three layers, the code section, the notification that brought it into force, and the rules position in the relevant state, and an answer that stops at the first layer is not wrong so much as unfinished.

## Consolidation is not codification

A terminological precision belongs in a map article, because the reform's very name invites a misunderstanding. In the classical sense, a code is a systematic and self-sufficient statement of a field of law, intended to displace the prior learning and be read as a complete text: the Indian Penal Code is the standing Indian example, and France's Code du travail is the standing labour example, a single volume to which the French reader repairs for the whole of the subject. The Indian labour codes are not codes in that sense. They are consolidations: the pre-existing statutes were grouped, their provisions renumbered and restated with amendments at chosen points, and the repealed learning, decades of tribunal awards and court constructions of words the codes often retained, was left largely in place by savings and by the interpretive gravity of identical language. The distinction has practical consequences. A French-style code invites the reader to start from the text alone; an Indian consolidation requires the reader to keep the old case law open beside the new text, asking of each doctrine whether the words that carried it survived. It also calibrates expectations about simplification. Consolidation genuinely reduces the number of books on the shelf, from twenty-nine central statutes to four, and genuinely unifies definitions within each family; it cannot reduce the volume of governing material by anything like the same factor, because the material includes the accumulated interpretation, which consolidation preserves by design. The reform is better described as a reorganisation with amendments than as a fresh start, and measuring it by the fresh-start standard, as much commentary does, mistakes its nature in the act of judging it.

## What consolidation did not settle

A map article owes its reader an honest account of the argument the codes left open, since the consolidation will be judged by examinations and editorials alike on claims its text only partly supports. The codes' defenders point to four achievements: one wage definition where a cluster of divergent ones stood, a universal minimum wage detached from the scheduling device, a national floor wage, and statutory recognition of gig work, platform work and fixed-term employment. The codes' critics answer on the same ground this article has used throughout. The raised thresholds exclude more establishments from standing orders and from the permission regime than the old law excluded, so the codes narrow the industrial relations core even as they widen the social security margin. The gig and platform provisions, on the critical reading, are enabling rather than entitling: the Code on Social Security provides for welfare schemes to be framed and funded partly by aggregator contributions set between one and two per cent of annual turnover, capped at five per cent of the amounts payable to the workers, but it confers no individual right to a pension or insurance that a gig worker could enforce the way an insured employee enforces a benefit. The unorganised worker provisions similarly build a registration and scheme architecture, of which the e-Shram portal, the national database on which unorganised workers register for a universal account number, is the operational centrepiece, rather than a schedule of guaranteed benefits. And the definitional litigation that consumed the old framework will not vanish; it will migrate to the codes' new boundaries, between worker and gig worker, between wages within and above the fifty per cent cap, between establishments above and below the moved lines. The balanced framework verdict is that the codes settled the organisation of Indian labour law, which had been unsettled since 2002 and in truth since 1948, without settling its distributive argument, which is older than the Republic and will be conducted on the new text by the same parties for the same reasons. Consolidation answered the librarian's question, where does everything go. It deliberately left open the citizen's question, how much protection, for whom, at whose cost, and a reader of the framework should distrust any account, favourable or hostile, that claims otherwise.

## Three establishments revisited

The staircase metaphor becomes exact when it is walked up with real institutional types, and three composite establishments, drawn from the framework's ordinary materials, will fix the map in a reader's mind. The first is a powerloom cluster shed in a textile town, one of thousands of small weaving units that supply India's cloth. Twelve looms, eleven hired hands, mechanical power. Under the old law the shed crossed the factory licensing line at ten workers with power, so the Factories Act's hours, safety and leave provisions applied in principle, subject to the practical reach of the factory inspectorate; the workers stood outside standing orders and outside the permission regime by an order of magnitude; provident fund attached if the counted employees reached twenty, which a single shed did not supply, though sheds under one ownership faced aggregation arguments; minimum wages for the textile employment as scheduled and fixed by the state were the floor beneath the piece rates actually negotiated. Under the codes the shed of eleven stands below the twenty-worker line for a factory with power, and its licensing obligation falls away, while the Code on Wages floor and the state's minimum wage continue to govern its pay, an exact illustration of the codes' pattern of lighter establishment law at the bottom of the staircase and unchanged wage law across it.

The second establishment is a components plant supplying vehicle manufacturers, one hundred and eighty permanent workers and ninety engaged through two contractors. Under the old law everything in the staircase applied except that the permanent headcount governed the permission chapter, which attached at one hundred, so the plant could not retrench or close without government permission, and its standing orders were certified; the contract labour chapter applied to both the plant as principal employer and the contractors as employers, at twenty or more contract workers. The plant's managers lived the full framework daily: wage registers, insurance contributions, a works committee, conciliation when the union pressed a charter of demands. Under the codes the plant crosses out of the permission regime and out of standing orders, both of which begin at three hundred under the Code, while retaining its safety, wage, insurance, provident fund and contract labour obligations, and gaining a negotiating union procedure and the obligation to contribute to the re-skilling fund on retrenchment. If a single establishment embodies the codes' net direction, lighter on industrial relations control, undiminished on money and safety, it is this one.

The third establishment is not a factory at all: a software services office of four hundred employees. The factory statutes never touched it. Its governing labour instruments were the state's shops and commercial establishments Act, regulating hours and leave, the wage statutes, the social security statutes at their headcounts, the maternity and gratuity entitlements, the sexual harassment statute, and, for its handful of disputes, the Industrial Disputes Act's workman definition, which most of its highly paid staff fell outside on the supervisory and managerial exclusions. The codes shift its position modestly: the wages and social security codes govern as before with the common wage definition, and the occupational safety code reaches establishments of its size with duties about working conditions, while industrial relations law remains, for most of its workforce, a distant instrument. The office exposes the framework's founding bias in reverse. Built for factories, the law is most complete where work is most physical, and thinnest where a post-industrial workforce actually sits, in services whose conditions problems are real but whose disputes rarely fit the workman mould. The codes narrow that gap without closing it.

## Women's work and the framework's protective layer

The framework carries a distinct protective layer for women workers, assembled across the families rather than in one place, and its architecture shows both the framework's care and its founding assumptions. The Maternity Benefit Act 1961 supplies wage replacement around childbirth, extended by the amendment of 2017 to twenty-six weeks, with crèche obligations attaching at fifty employees and protection against dismissal during absence. The Equal Remuneration Act 1976 prohibited sex discrimination in pay for the same work or work of a similar nature and in recruitment. The Factories Act and its sectoral siblings added safeguards of a more paternalist cast, most prominently the restriction on women's night work in factories, historically a prohibition moderated by state permissions, which the Occupational Safety Code replaces with permission conditional on the woman's consent and prescribed safety measures, a shift from protection by exclusion to protection by condition. The Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace Act 2013 stands outside the codes entirely, a survivor statute administered through internal committees at workplaces of ten or more employees, and its subject, dignity rather than wages or hours, marks the framework's newest frontier. The codes re-enact the maternity and equal pay provisions within the wages and social security codes while leaving the field's structure intact: women's entitlements are not a fifth family but a layer across four of them, and a reader auditing any establishment must check the layer separately, since it attaches by the presence of women workers rather than by the family's usual lines alone.

## How a reader should navigate the framework

For primary material, the framework is unusually accessible and unusually scattered at once. The codes and the repealed Acts are published as bare texts by the legislative department and the labour ministry; the rules that decide commencement questions sit with state labour departments, whose gazette notifications, circulars and minimum wage schedules are the working law of the field and the least collected part of it; and the decisions of the industrial tribunals, where so much of the old jurisprudence lives, were never systematically reported. A researcher of this framework learns to read in layers in the same order the framework was built: Constitution first, code second, state rule third, and the award or judgment that glossed the words last, checking at every step whether the layer beneath has been notified, amended or assented to in the relevant state.

The framework rewards a particular order of inquiry, and stating it plainly is the most useful service a map article can perform. Begin with the constitutional question: is the matter within the concurrent entries, and which government, Centre or state, is the appropriate government for this establishment, since that decides whose rules, whose inspectorate and whose notifications govern. Move next to the counting question: how many workers, of what legal description, counted on which statutory basis, because that decides which statutes switch on. Only then reach the substantive question of what the applicable statute requires. Readers who reverse the order, starting from a right they have heard of and assuming it applies to them, are the readers this framework disappoints.

The depth on each family sits in the cluster's specialist articles. For the industrial relations core, the article on [the Factories Act 1948 and the law of the factory floor](/2013/06/01/factories-act-1948-explained/) treats the conditions-of-work family at statute level, and the article on [the Minimum Wages Act 1948 and the fixing of wage floors](/2013/06/15/minimum-wages-act-1948-explained/) treats the wage family's foundation statute; the side-by-side accounting of what the consolidation altered is the business of [the comparison of the labour codes with the laws they replaced](/2013/09/01/labour-codes-vs-old-laws-comparison/). One caution belongs in any map of a field this fact-sensitive: applicability turns on the counted facts of the individual establishment, and the general position described here is legal information, not a substitute for advice on a particular workplace, because thresholds, state amendments and commencement notifications decide real cases and each of them turns on detail.

## Closing verdict: the architecture is the law

The One Test answer this article set itself was that a reader should leave able to explain why the accumulation happened, what holds it together, and how to tell whether any labour law reaches any workplace. The explanation, gathered up, is short. India accumulated dozens of central labour statutes and hundreds of state ones because its Constitution handed the subject to two legislatures at once and because a century of pressures, colonial competition, royal commissions, planned industrialisation, sectoral agitation and reform stalemate each deposited a statute and none removed its predecessors. The structure that resulted is governed by a mechanism of beautiful simplicity and large consequence: obligations switch on at numbers. That mechanism explains the field's protection and its exclusion, its rigidity for the covered and its absence for the rest, its bunching of firms below the lines and its litigation about counting. The four codes consolidated the text, raised several of the numbers, universalised the wage floor, recognised forms of work the old text never named, and left the architecture itself, concurrent competence plus regulation by headcount, exactly where they found it. Thresholds are the real labour law of India. They were the law under the repealed statutes, they are the law under the codes, and any future reform that does not reckon with them is negotiating with the decoration rather than the building. The reader who has followed the map can perform the framework's essential operation unaided: take any Indian workplace, count its workers as its statutes count them, assign its government as the definitions assign it, and read off the staircase which laws govern it and which pass it by. That operation, mechanical in form and distributive in consequence, is the Indian labour law framework, in every period of its history and under every name its statutes have borne.

What reckoning with them would look like can be stated without fantasy, because the framework's own second track contains the embryo. Obligations that protect persons, insurance, pensions, injury compensation, maternity support, gain nothing from being gated by establishment size, and the direction of the social security code, registration of the worker and portability of the account across employers, points towards benefits that follow the worker the way a bank account does. Obligations that regulate establishments, safety, hours, standing orders, dispute machinery, are sensibly graduated by capacity, and a mature threshold regime would phase them in across bands rather than switch them at lines, so that growing past a number costs a rung and not a regime. The framework India inherited chose lines because lines are administrable by thin inspectorates; the framework India is building, with electronic registration and worker databases, can afford gradients. Whether it chooses them is the question the next commission, whenever it sits, will inherit from this one, together with the map this article has drawn: two legislatures, four families, one staircase, and a workforce still mostly waiting at its foot.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How many separate labour laws did India have before the codes?

The honest answer depends on what is counted. At the central level, the Second National Commission on Labour worked with some forty-four labour Acts in 2002, and the four codes of 2019 and 2020 subsumed twenty-nine of the central statutes between them: four into the Code on Wages, three into the Industrial Relations Code, nine into the Code on Social Security and thirteen into the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code. Alongside the central corpus, the states maintained several hundred enactments of their own, including shops and establishments Acts and welfare fund statutes. Counting rules and notifications beneath the Acts multiplies the total far beyond any of these figures, which is why quoted totals in debate vary so widely.

### Q: Why do both the Centre and the states make labour laws?

Because the Constitution places labour on the Concurrent List in the Seventh Schedule. Entries 22, 23 and 24 of that List cover trade unions and industrial disputes, social security and employment, and the welfare of labour including conditions of work, provident funds and maternity benefits, and both Parliament and the state legislatures may legislate on them. A small residue sits on the Union List, such as labour and safety in mines and oilfields. The arrangement was inherited from the Government of India Act 1935 and kept deliberately: industry and labour migration are national, while conditions of work vary locally. The result is two layers of labour legislation operating on the same workforce, reconciled by Article 254 and by state amendments carried with presidential assent.

### Q: What is the difference between a worker and an employee in Indian labour law?

The words are statutory terms, not synonyms, and each statute defines its own protected person. The Industrial Disputes Act 1947 used workman for a person doing manual, skilled, technical, operational, clerical or supervisory work, excluding managerial staff and higher-paid supervisors. The Factories Act used worker for a person employed in the manufacturing process. The social security statutes used employee, defined widely enough in the provident fund law to reach persons engaged through contractors. A single person could therefore be a worker for safety law, an employee for contributions and outside the workman definition for dispute law on the same day. The four codes harmonise the vocabulary within each family, but the reader must still ask, under each code, protected as what.

### Q: Which labour laws apply to a small business?

Far fewer than apply to a large one, because most Indian labour statutes switch on above worker thresholds. A very small unit generally stands outside factory licensing, standing orders, the prior-permission regime and the contract labour chapter, and may fall below the ten-employee lines for state insurance, gratuity and maternity coverage and the twenty-employee line for the provident fund. What still reaches it are the wage protections: minimum wages for the employment, payment discipline, compensation for employment injury, the prohibitions on child labour, and the constitutional floor of Articles 21 and 23. Its own state's shops and establishments Act will also regulate its hours and weekly holidays. The precise set is counted establishment by establishment, which is the framework's defining habit.

### Q: What did the Second National Commission on Labour recommend?

The Commission, set up on 15 October 1999 under Ravindra Varma and reporting on 29 June 2002, recommended that the existing body of labour laws, some forty-four central Acts, be broadly grouped into four or five codes covering industrial relations, wages, social security, safety, and welfare and working conditions. It recommended a uniform definition of worker across the groups, extension of social security to all employees including categories excluded from the definition of workman, and a three-tier adjudication structure of Lok Adalats, Labour Courts and Labour Relations Commissions. It also declined to recommend removing labour from the Concurrent List. The four codes enacted in 2019 and 2020 follow its grouping closely.

### Q: Are Indian labour laws pro-worker or pro-employer?

The framework resists the label because its benefits and burdens fall on different populations. For the covered minority in larger establishments, the law has been strongly protective: adjudicated job security, permission requirements for retrenchment and closure, statutory bonus, insurance and pension coverage. For the majority in small establishments and informal work, the same framework historically offered little beyond minimum wages and injury compensation, so its strictness never reached them. Economists such as Besley and Burgess found that states amending in a pro-worker direction saw slower registered manufacturing growth, while the Economic Survey of 2018-19 blamed size-based regulation for keeping firms small. The balanced reading is that the law is protective in design and narrow in reach, which serves neither camp's slogan.

### Q: What protection do unorganised sector workers have?

Protection for the unorganised majority runs on a second track built on registration and welfare boards rather than on establishment obligations. The Unorganised Workers' Social Security Act 2008 provided for national and state boards and welfare schemes covering life, health, maternity and old age. Construction workers are served by state welfare boards funded by a cess on construction cost, and several states built occupation-wise boards of their own. The Code on Social Security absorbs this architecture, providing for registration of unorganised workers, a national database in the form of the e-Shram portal, and schemes for gig and platform workers funded partly by aggregator contributions. Minimum wages, injury compensation and the constitutional prohibitions apply irrespective of sector.

### Q: How does a threshold based labour law work?

A threshold based statute applies only to establishments at or above a stated number of workers, so coverage is switched on by headcount rather than by the nature of the work. The Factories Act 1948 applied at ten workers with power and twenty without; standing orders at one hundred workmen; the provident fund at twenty employees. Counting rules decide hard cases, typically counting workers on any day of the preceding twelve months and including contract labour where the statute says so. Below the line, the statute does not reach the workplace at all; above it, the full apparatus of registration, contribution and penalty applies at once. The codes retain the mechanism and move several of the lines, standing orders and prior permission to three hundred among them.

### Q: Why did India accumulate so many separate labour laws instead of enacting one code from the start?

Because each statute answered a particular pressure and no legislature repealed its predecessors. Colonial factory law began in 1881 under pressure from British textile interests; the interwar statutes followed a royal commission; the burst of 1946 to 1952 enacted the freedom movement's promises family by family; later decades added statutes for bonus, contract labour, gratuity and migrant workers as each problem became politically impossible to ignore. Two legislatures legislating concurrently doubled the accumulation, since states added their own Acts beside the central ones. Consolidation required a political moment large enough to repeal twenty-nine statutes in four instruments, and that moment arrived only in 2019 and 2020, seventeen years after the Second National Commission recommended it.

### Q: What happens when a central labour law and a state labour law say different things?

Article 254 of the Constitution governs the clash. Ordinarily, a state law that is repugnant to a central law on a Concurrent List subject is void to the extent of the repugnancy, and the central law prevails. Repugnancy requires a genuine collision: obedience to both must be impossible, or the central law must be intended to occupy the field completely. Article 254(2) supplies the exception that labour law uses most: a state law reserved for the President's consideration and receiving assent prevails in that state despite repugnancy, which is how states such as Rajasthan in 2014 raised central thresholds within their territories. Parliament may legislate afresh to override such a state law, restoring national uniformity when it chooses.

### Q: How does the law decide whether the Centre or the state is the appropriate government for a workplace?

Each labour statute defines its own appropriate government, and the classification follows the character of the establishment rather than the wishes of the parties. Under the Industrial Disputes Act pattern, the Centre is the appropriate government for railways, mines, oilfields, major ports, banking and insurance companies and central public sector undertakings, while the state government is appropriate for the generality of factories, plantations, shops and other establishments within its territory. The classification decides whose conciliation officers, inspectors, notifications and rule-making govern the workplace, and which authority's permission or prosecution sanction is required. Reading the definition clause of the governing statute is the first practical step in any labour matter, since an application addressed to the wrong government fails on that ground alone.

### Q: Why are the same words defined differently in different labour statutes?

Because each statute was drafted at a different time for a different purpose, and its definition was fitted to that purpose. Wages meant one thing for disciplining deductions under the Payment of Wages Act, another for computing bonus on profits, another for insurance contributions, and the draftsmen of each Act excluded or included allowances such as house rent and overtime according to the calculation the statute had to perform. The divergences then acquired a life of their own, since employers structured pay against the seams between definitions and courts built case law on each version separately. The Code on Wages answers with a single definition of wages, built on basic pay and dearness allowance with excluded components capped at fifty per cent of total remuneration.

### Q: What is the difference between the organised sector and the unorganised sector in labour law?

The organised sector is employment in establishments large and regular enough for the establishment-based statutes to grip: registered factories, companies and offices where headcounts cross the thresholds and records exist. The unorganised sector is everything else: small units, casual and home-based work, agriculture and self-employment. The National Commission for Enterprises in the Unorganised Sector estimated informal employment at about ninety-two per cent of the workforce, counting unorganised sector workers and informally engaged workers inside organised establishments together. Labour law treats the two sectors through different instruments: rights enforced against employers in the first, and registration, welfare boards and schemes in the second. The Code on Social Security is the first central statute designed to hold both tracks within one architecture.

### Q: Why do worker thresholds differ from one labour statute to another?

Because each threshold encodes a judgement made at a different time about the administrative cost of covering small workplaces against the protection gained. Safety law reaches furthest down, to ten workers in a powered factory, because physical risk does not wait for scale. Social insurance sets its lines at ten and twenty employees, where payroll administration becomes feasible. Standing orders and the retrenchment permission regime historically sat at one hundred, because certified rulebooks and government permission were thought proportionate only for substantial establishments. The thresholds also reflect the era of enactment, the inspection capacity of the day and the political strength of the interests affected. The differences are untidy but not random, and the four codes redrew several lines while preserving the pattern of graduated coverage.

### Q: What role has the International Labour Organization played in Indian labour law?

India was a founding member of the International Labour Organization in 1919, and the organisation shaped the framework's vocabulary, its tripartite institutions and several of its statutes. The Indian Labour Conference, the apex tripartite body meeting since 1942, follows the ILO model of government, employer and worker delegations deliberating together. Indian practice has generally been to ratify a convention when domestic law already conforms to it, rather than to legislate in order to ratify: the ratifications of the Minimum Age Convention and the Worst Forms of Child Labour Convention in June 2017 followed amendment of the domestic child labour statute. ILO standards also supplied reference points in debates on hours, safety and social security throughout the framework's development.

### Q: Are domestic workers covered by Indian labour law?

Only partially, and the gaps illustrate the establishment model's limits. Domestic work takes place in households rather than establishments, so the factory, standing orders and disputes statutes do not reach it in the ordinary way. Coverage arrives through other doors: several states have included domestic work in their minimum wage schedules, making the notified rate enforceable, and some states have constituted welfare boards for domestic workers. The Unorganised Workers' Social Security Act 2008 expressly included domestic workers within its scheme architecture, and the Code on Social Security continues registration and scheme provision for unorganised workers as a class. The result is protection that depends heavily on the worker's state of residence and on registration, rather than a uniform national entitlement enforceable against the household employer.

### Q: Do apprentices count as workers under Indian labour law?

Generally no, and the exclusion is deliberate. The Apprentices Act 1961 governs the training relation separately, and its scheme treats the apprentice as a trainee rather than an employee, receiving a stipend under a contract of apprenticeship. Labour statutes repeatedly mark the boundary: the Industrial Disputes Act's definition of workman expressly excludes a person employed as an apprentice, and the codes preserve the separation. The boundary is policed by substance rather than label, since courts have treated so-called apprentices who in fact perform regular production work without genuine training as workers in disguise. Employers who use the apprentice label to staff ordinary jobs therefore risk the full application of labour law, while bona fide apprenticeships remain a training channel outside the employment framework.

### Q: Why do mines, plantations and docks have their own labour laws?

Because their working conditions differ enough from the factory floor to need dedicated regimes, and because several of these sectors were regulated early under pressure of their particular dangers. Mining combines underground hazards, occupational disease and remote locations, and sits on the Union List for labour and safety, producing the Mines Act 1952 and a specialist inspectorate. Plantations historically housed resident workforces in isolated estates, so the Plantations Labour Act 1951 regulates housing, medical care and welfare alongside hours. Dock work was casual and gang-organised, answered by dock workers' safety and welfare legislation. The Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code subsumes these sectoral statutes while retaining sector-specific rule-making, since the hazards that justified separate laws survive the consolidation of their texts.

### Q: What is tripartism in Indian labour law?

Tripartism is the practice of making and administering labour policy through bodies on which government, employers and workers sit together. Its summit is the Indian Labour Conference, meeting since 1942 with the Standing Labour Committee, and it descends through wage advisory boards, the governing bodies of the insurance corporation and provident fund organisation, safety committees and works committees, to the grievance committees inside establishments. The institution descends from the International Labour Organization's structure and reflects a judgement that labour rules command cooperation only when both parties to the employment relation helped to shape them. Its resolutions are advisory rather than binding, but governments depart from tripartite consensus at a political cost, which gives the machinery real weight in the framework's operation.

### Q: Can an employer escape labour laws by splitting one business into several small units?

Not reliably, because the courts apply a substance test to artificial fragmentation. Where separated units share common management, finance, employment and functional unity, they are treated as a single establishment, a doctrine known as functional integrality, developed to prevent exactly this evasion of thresholds. Registration and counting rules add further defences: the factory definition counts workers on any day of the preceding twelve months, and provident fund coverage counts employees engaged through contractors. A genuine division, separate businesses independently owned and run, will be respected on its facts. A paper division of one business, same premises, same managers, workers rotated between units, invites aggregation, recovery of dues and penalties, and the split therefore fails at the point it was designed to win.
