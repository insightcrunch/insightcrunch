On 11 January 2007, a bench of nine judges of the Supreme Court of India, presided over by Chief Justice Y. K. Sabharwal, delivered a unanimous judgment in I.R. Coelho v State of Tamil Nadu, reported at (2007) 2 SCC 1, and settled a question that had been left open for a quarter of a century: whether a law placed in the Ninth Schedule of the Constitution after 24 April 1973 could still be tested against the basic structure of the Constitution. The ir coelho 2007 ninth schedule verdict answered yes, and the manner of the answer matters as much as the answer itself. The bench did not strike down Article 31B, the clause that shields scheduled laws from Part III of the Constitution. It did not disturb a single law placed in the Schedule before the cutoff date. It held instead that every insertion made on or after the day Kesavananda Bharati was decided had to answer for its effect on the fundamental rights that form part of the basic structure, Articles 14, 15, 19 and 21 and the principles beneath them, and that an insertion which destroyed the essence of those rights damaged the structure and fell with the law it carried. The claim defended in this article is the claim the record supports: the verdict is best read as an architectural extension of the basic structure doctrine, a judgment that took a doctrine built for constitutional amendments and fitted it, by three linked decisions, onto the one device Parliament had used since 1951 to place laws beyond the reach of rights, and the pattern the bench laid down in doing so has governed every later argument about the Schedule whether or not any later court has struck down a scheduled law in a headline case.

The three decisions can be stated by holding, and the whole article that follows is an unpacking of them. The first decision held that additions to the Ninth Schedule made on or after 24 April 1973 are open to challenge on the ground that they damage or destroy the basic structure, because the rights conferred by Articles 14, 15, 19 and 21 form part of that structure and a law that abrogates their essence cannot be saved by the act of scheduling it. The second decision held that additions made before that date stand protected, because the five judge bench in Waman Rao v Union of India, decided on 9 November 1980 and reported at (1981) 2 SCC 362, had drawn the line at the Kesavananda date and the nine judges in Coelho accepted that line as settled ground, refusing to reopen settlements on which land titles and agrarian relations across the country had rested for decades. The third decision supplied the test: a court examining a post-cutoff insertion applies what the judgment calls the rights test, and within it the essence of the right test, asking whether the law, judged by its direct impact and effect, takes away or abrogates a right in a manner that destroys the basic structure, rather than asking whether the law merely touches a right in passing. Around those three decisions the bench built a set of supporting holdings: that judicial review under Articles 32 and 226 is itself part of the basic structure and cannot be excluded by a schedule, that Article 31B was a device meant to remove difficulties in the path of agrarian reform and not a charter for wiping out judicial review, and that the validity of each scheduled law named in the proceedings would be examined by the appropriate smaller benches applying the test the nine judges had laid down. Read together, the decisions converted the Ninth Schedule from a closed room into a room with a dated door: everything placed inside before 24 April 1973 stayed inside, and everything placed inside after that date could be called out and examined, one law at a time, against the rights at the core of the Constitution.

## The shield as built: the First Amendment and Article 31B

The Ninth Schedule was not in the Constitution that the Constituent Assembly adopted on 26 November 1949 or that came into force on 26 January 1950. It arrived sixteen months later, in the Constitution (First Amendment) Act of 1951, passed by the provisional Parliament and assented to on 18 June 1951, and it arrived as an emergency repair to a settlement that was already cracking. The pressure came from land reform. The abolition of the zamindari estates, the great intermediary tenures through which much of agricultural India had been held, was the central legislative promise of the Congress governments at the Centre and in the states, and the courts had begun to test the abolition statutes against the property guarantees of the new Constitution. The Bihar Land Reforms Act of 1950 had been challenged in the Patna High Court, which in March 1951 struck down provisions of it on equality grounds in the litigation that became Kameshwar Singh v State of Bihar, and similar challenges to the Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh abolition Acts were moving through the courts. The Union government of Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru faced the prospect that the flagship reform of the founding settlement, the land settlement on which [the founding three-tensions framework](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/) had staked the republic's claim to transform rural India, would be dismantled clause by clause under Articles 14 and 31 before the first general election had even been held.

The First Amendment answered with three connected devices, and the architecture of the answer is the starting point for understanding what Coelho did fifty six years later. Article 31A protected laws in stated categories, agrarian reform and the taking over of estates and industries among them, from challenge under Articles 14 and 19. Article 31B went further and worked differently: it named a schedule. Its text provided that none of the Acts and Regulations specified in the Ninth Schedule, nor any provision of them, shall be deemed to be void, or ever to have become void, on the ground that the Act or provision was inconsistent with, or took away or abridged, any of the rights conferred by Part III, and that each such Act should continue in force notwithstanding any judgment, decree or order of any court or tribunal to the contrary, subject to the power of a competent legislature to repeal or amend it. The words "or ever to have become void" gave the shield a retrospective reach that ordinary validating legislation did not possess: a scheduled law was not merely protected against future challenge but was deemed never to have been vulnerable at all, and a judgment that had already gone against it was overridden by the scheduling itself. The Ninth Schedule that the amendment attached to the Constitution contained thirteen entries, every one of them a land reform or zamindari abolition statute from Bihar, Bombay, Madhya Pradesh, Madras, Orissa, Punjab, Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal or a closely related measure. The device was, in form, a list; in function, it was a constitutional lockbox into which a legislature could place a statute and from which the fundamental rights could not extract it.

### Why did the provisional Parliament create a schedule of shielded laws in 1951?

The Parliament created the Ninth Schedule because the Patna High Court had struck at the Bihar Land Reforms Act in March 1951 and challenges threatened zamindari abolition in Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh, leaving the Nehru government to choose between watching its agrarian programme fail under Articles 14 and 31 or building a schedule that Part III could not reach.

Nehru defended the amendment on the floor of the provisional Parliament in May and June 1951 in terms that fixed the device's stated purpose: the zamindari system was to go, the courts' reading of the property clauses was obstructing a reform the electorate had been promised, and the Constitution, in Nehru's phrase in the debate, was not to be treated as a barrier to the removal of a feudal land order. The debates of the First Amendment, preserved in the parliamentary record of the 1951 session, show a government conscious that it was doing something constitutionally novel and defending the novelty by the narrowness of its object. The shield was presented as an instrument of agrarian reform, aimed at a defined class of statutes whose social purpose was settled, and the thirteen entries were all of that class. Critics in the provisional Parliament, among them H. V. Kamath and Syama Prasad Mookerjee in their interventions of May 1951, warned in the chamber that a schedule into which any law could be placed by amendment was a device whose future contents no one in the room could control, and that the precedent of overriding judgments by listing statutes would outlive the zamindari question that had produced it. The warning is part of the record of 1951, and the later history of the Schedule, examined below, is in large part the history of that warning being borne out.

The first judicial word on the device came within months. In Shankari Prasad Singh Deo v Union of India, decided on 5 October 1951, a five judge bench of the Supreme Court upheld the First Amendment, holding that a constitutional amendment made under Article 368 was not law within the meaning of Article 13 and so could not be voided for offending Part III, and that the power to amend extended to the fundamental rights themselves. Shankari Prasad gave the Schedule a clean bill of health at birth and established the frame within which it operated for two decades: Parliament could amend any part of the Constitution, the scheduled laws were beyond rights based review, and the only control on the Schedule's contents was political. The Constitution bench that decided Kameshwar Singh's appeal in 1952 upheld the Bihar Act in substance, with the First Amendment's shield doing part of the work. For the land reform programme the device performed exactly the service its makers had promised. Ceiling laws, tenancy reforms and abolition statutes proceeded across the states behind the protection of Articles 31A and 31B and the growing Schedule, and the agrarian structure of the countryside was remade under their cover. Any honest account of the Ninth Schedule has to hold this fact in view alongside the later record: the lockbox protected, in its first decades, the largest programme of redistributive land legislation undertaken by any democracy in the twentieth century, and the men and women who built it understood themselves to be defending that programme against a property jurisprudence they believed the Constitution had not intended to freeze in place.

## A schedule that outgrew its purpose

The Schedule did not stay a list of thirteen land statutes. The Fourth Amendment of 1955 added further entries. The Seventeenth Amendment of 1964, which also widened the definition of estate in Article 31A, added forty four Acts to the Schedule at a stroke, carrying the total past sixty and confirming that bulk insertion had become the standard technique for protecting state land legislation, much of it from Kerala and other states whose ceiling and tenancy laws faced fresh challenge. There the matter might have rested as a land reform archive, had the device not demonstrated a second use. A legislature that could place a statute in the Schedule could place any statute there, on any subject, provided it could carry a constitutional amendment through Parliament, and from the nineteen seventies onward the Schedule's contents record the discovery of that second use in entry after entry. The Thirty-fourth Amendment of 1974 placed twenty statutes in the Schedule, among them the Tamil Nadu Janmam legislation at the heart of the Coelho case itself. The Thirty-ninth Amendment of 1975, remembered chiefly for Article 329A and the election dispute examined in [the first-basic-structure-operationalisation framework](/2020/08/19/indira-gandhi-raj-narain-1975/), also added thirty eight entries. The Fortieth Amendment of 1976 added sixty four. The Forty-seventh Amendment of 1984 added fourteen land laws. The Sixty-sixth Amendment of 1990 added fifty five. The Seventy-sixth Amendment of 1994 added a single entry, entry 257A, the Tamil Nadu reservation statute examined later in this article, and the Seventy-eighth Amendment of 1995 brought the total number of entries to 284, the figure the Coelho bench itself worked with when it described the Schedule it had been asked to rule upon.

By the time the nine judges sat in the winter of 2006, the Schedule held statutes on subjects its makers had never named in the 1951 debates: election laws, the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act, legislation on mines and minerals, on industries and their nationalisation, on urban land ceiling, on insurance, on the conservation of foreign exchange, and the Tamil Nadu statute reserving sixty nine percent of seats and posts. Granville Austin, in Working a Democratic Constitution (1999), his history of the Constitution in operation, treated the Schedule's expansion as a standing temptation built into the constitutional design: a device created to protect a programme had become a device available to protect any enactment a government feared a court might read against Part III, and the entries accumulated because each insertion was, at the moment it was made, cheaper than defending the law on its merits. The Coelho judgment itself, in its survey of the Schedule's history, recorded the same drift in judicial language, tracing the Schedule from its agrarian origins through its later accretions and noting that laws far removed from land reform had been given its protection. The doctrinal question the bench had to answer was therefore never only about thirteen statutes from 1951. It was about whether a list that had grown to 284 entries, added by thirty years of amendments after Kesavananda, remained wholly outside the discipline that Kesavananda had imposed on every other exercise of the amending power.

### How large had the Schedule grown by the time the nine judges sat?

The Schedule had grown from thirteen entries in 1951 to 284 entries by the winter of 2006, through bulk insertions by the Seventeenth Amendment of 1964, the Thirty-ninth and Fortieth Amendments of the Emergency years, the Sixty-sixth Amendment of 1990 and the Seventy-eighth Amendment of 1995, the last addition the Coelho bench recorded.

The growth figures matter to the adjudication this article defends, because they explain why the framings that treat Coelho as a footnote to land reform fail. A judgment about land reform statutes would have been a judgment about a closed historical set, most of it inserted before 1973 and therefore, on any view, hard to disturb. The Coelho bench was instead ruling on a living instrument of legislation whose most consequential entries, the Janmam insertion of 1974, the Emergency era insertions, the Tamil Nadu reservation insertion of 1994, all postdated the basic structure doctrine's announcement. The architectural question was whether the doctrine reached inside the lockbox at all, and it could not be answered by describing the lockbox's original purpose, because the lockbox's contents had outgrown that purpose two decades before the reference reached the Court. The bench's answer, that the doctrine reached every insertion made after the doctrine existed, is the extension this article examines, and its logic is easiest to see once the Schedule's growth is stated plainly: a Parliament that amended the Constitution after April 1973 acted in a legal world in which the basic structure limit was already declared law, and its insertions into the Schedule were exercises of the same amending power that Kesavananda had held to be limited, performed with notice of the limit. The pre 1973 insertions, by contrast, had been made in a legal world where Shankari Prasad and Sajjan Singh had assured Parliament that its amending power over Part III was complete. The cutoff in Coelho is therefore not an arbitrary date picked to split a list. It is the date on which the legal meaning of the amending power changed, and the bench treated insertions before and after that change according to the law under which each was made.

## The first cracks: from Shankari Prasad to Kesavananda

The frame that Shankari Prasad built in 1951 held for fourteen years and then began, case by case, to give way, and the Coelho bench recited that history in its judgment because its own holding was unintelligible without it. In Sajjan Singh v State of Rajasthan, decided in 1965, a five judge bench upheld the Seventeenth Amendment and with it the Schedule's expansion, but two judges, Justice Hidayatullah and Justice Mudholkar, recorded doubts about whether the fundamental rights could be treated as the plaything of amendment and whether some features of the Constitution stood beyond the amending power altogether. In Golaknath v State of Punjab, decided on 27 February 1967 by eleven judges dividing six to five, the Court withdrew the premise: a constitutional amendment was law under Article 13, Parliament could not amend Part III so as to take away or abridge fundamental rights, and the First, Fourth and Seventeenth Amendments survived only because the bench applied its ruling prospectively. Golaknath froze the Schedule's legal basis without emptying the Schedule, since the existing entries stood, but it denied Parliament the power to add to the list against rights based objection, and the confrontation that followed, bank nationalisation struck down in R. C. Cooper in 1970, the privy purses order struck down in Madhav Rao Scindia in 1970, and Parliament's answering Twenty-fourth, Twenty-fifth and Twenty-ninth Amendments of 1971 and 1972, produced the case in which the whole structure was rebuilt.

Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala, decided on 24 April 1973 by thirteen judges dividing seven to six, overruled Golaknath, restored the power to amend Part III, and held in the same breath that the amending power did not extend to damaging or destroying the basic structure or framework of the Constitution. The full procedural and doctrinal record of that verdict, the sixty eight days of hearing, the eleven opinions and the summary signed by nine judges, is carried in [the basic-structure-doctrine establishment](/2020/08/02/kesavananda-bharati-1973-reanalysed/), and the doctrine's career across the decades is traced in [the most-consequential-constitutional-moment framework](/2020/07/28/basic-structure-most-consequential-doctrine/). For the Coelho question, one feature of Kesavananda mattered above all others: the Twenty-ninth Amendment, which had placed the Kerala land reform Acts in the Ninth Schedule, was upheld, and the scheduled laws in that case were left undisturbed. Nothing in the eleven opinions worked out what the new doctrine meant for the Schedule as a whole, because the insertions before the Court had been made before the doctrine was announced and the case had been argued as a case about the amending power in general. The Schedule therefore entered the post Kesavananda world in an anomalous position. Every other amendment was in principle testable against the basic structure. An amendment whose whole content was the insertion of a statute into the Schedule claimed, through Article 31B, to place the statute beyond testing, and for seven years no bench ruled on whether the claim survived the doctrine.

The Emergency years sharpened the anomaly before any bench resolved it. The Thirty-ninth Amendment of 1975 placed the Prime Minister's election beyond the courts in Article 329A, and in Indira Gandhi v Raj Narain, decided on 7 November 1975, the Court applied Kesavananda for the first time to strike down a constitutional provision, clause (4) of Article 329A, while upholding the election itself under the amended election law. The Forty-second Amendment of 1976 then attempted to close the Kesavananda door by amending Article 368 to declare that no amendment could be called in question in any court and that the amending power faced no limitation whatever. In Minerva Mills Ltd v Union of India, decided on 31 July 1980, the Court struck down those clauses of the Forty-second Amendment, holding that a limited amending power was itself part of the basic structure and that Parliament could not use the power to make itself unlimited. Minerva Mills mattered for the Schedule because it established, before Waman Rao was argued, the proposition on which Coelho would later rest: the amending power that Kesavananda had limited could not be enlarged by its own exercise, and a device that withdrew a class of laws from the doctrine's reach entirely would have to be justified against that proposition rather than assumed.

### What did Minerva Mills settle in 1980 that Coelho treated as settled ground?

Minerva Mills, decided on 31 July 1980, struck down the Forty-second Amendment's attempt to place constitutional amendments beyond judicial question and to declare the amending power unlimited, holding that a limited amending power is itself part of the basic structure and that Parliament cannot enlarge its own power by amending the clause that grants it.

## Waman Rao draws the line

Waman Rao v Union of India reached the Court as a challenge to the Maharashtra agricultural ceiling legislation and to Articles 31A and 31B and the unamended Article 31, and the five judge bench presided over by Chief Justice Y. V. Chandrachud delivered its judgment on 9 November 1980, with the report following at (1981) 2 SCC 362, the citation under which the case is conventionally named in the briefs and in the seed questions of this series. The bench upheld Articles 31A and the unamended 31B and the land reform statutes before it, and it refused to disturb the Schedule's older contents, holding that amendments inserting laws into the Ninth Schedule before Kesavananda had been made on the faith of the law as Shankari Prasad and Sajjan Singh had declared it and that reopening them would unsettle titles and transactions closed decades earlier. But the bench did not give the Schedule a permanent charter. It held that amendments made on or after 24 April 1973, by which laws were inserted into the Ninth Schedule, would be open to challenge on the ground that they were beyond the constituent power of Parliament because they damaged the basic or essential features of the Constitution or its basic structure. The line was drawn in the judgment's concluding propositions and it was drawn exactly at the Kesavananda date: behind the line, protection; ahead of it, exposure, with the content of the exposure left to be worked out in later cases.

The companion decision in Maharao Sahib Shri Bhim Singhji v Union of India, delivered in the same period and reported in 1981, applied the Waman Rao frame to the Urban Land (Ceiling and Regulation) Act of 1976, a post Kesavananda insertion, and upheld the Act in substance while striking at a compensation provision, showing that exposure to challenge did not mean automatic invalidation and that the inquiry would run law by law and clause by clause. Between them, Waman Rao and Bhim Singhji fixed the position that governed the Schedule for the next twenty six years. The doctrine was stated, the date was fixed, and the test was postponed. No bench of the Supreme Court between 1981 and 2007 sat down to say what a challenger of a post 1973 insertion had to prove, which rights counted for the purpose, or whether the inquiry ran against the insertion, the inserted law, or both. Into that postponement the Schedule continued to grow, through the Forty-seventh Amendment of 1984, the Sixty-sixth Amendment of 1990, the Seventy-sixth Amendment of 1994 and the Seventy-eighth Amendment of 1995, each insertion made by a Parliament that had formal notice from Waman Rao that its product was open to a challenge whose shape nobody could describe. That open texture is the precise gap the 1999 reference asked a larger bench to close, and it is why Coelho, when it came, took the form it did: not a ruling on one statute but the construction of the missing test.

The states' use of the Schedule in the Waman Rao decades also belongs in the record, because the Coelho arguments cannot be understood without it. Land legislation remained the bulk of the insertions, state ceiling and tenancy Acts from Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Gujarat, Haryana, Himachal Pradesh, Karnataka, Kerala, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Orissa, Punjab, Rajasthan, Tamil Nadu, Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal, together with plantation and forest legislation from the southern states. Set beside that agrarian bulk stood the insertions that had nothing to do with land: the Representation of the People Acts placed in the Schedule by the Thirty-ninth Amendment, the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act, the conservation and smuggling legislation of the Emergency years, the urban land ceiling statute, the nationalisation statutes for coking coal, general insurance and sick textile undertakings, and the Tamil Nadu reservation statute of 1994. State governments of every political colour sponsored insertions, and the governments that defended blanket immunity before the nine judges in 2006 included states whose own agrarian settlements rested on pre 1973 entries that Waman Rao had already protected. [The three-layered constitutional reservation framework](/2020/02/25/constitutional-reservation-framework/) explains the setting in which a reservation statute came to need Schedule protection at all: the Supreme Court's decision in Indra Sawhney in November 1992 had capped reservations in public employment at fifty percent, and Tamil Nadu's sixty nine percent settlement, built over decades in the state whose politics are traced in the account of [the Tamil Nadu DMK and AIADMK system](/2019/12/26/tamil-nadu-dmk-aiadmk-system/), could be preserved against that cap only by taking the statute out of the reach of Articles 14 and 16 altogether, which is what entry 257A was designed to do.

## The road from Gudalur: how the Coelho case reached nine judges

The case that carried the question to nine judges began, as Kesavananda had, in a dispute over land that the parties could not have known would reorganise constitutional doctrine. The Gudalur Janmam Estates (Abolition and Conversion into Ryotwari) Act of 1969 was Tamil Nadu legislation aimed at the janmam tenures of the Gudalur taluk in the Nilgiris, a plantation country of tea and coffee estates held under an old tenure that the Act abolished, converting the lands to ryotwari settlement and vesting the forests within the janmam estates in the state. In Balmadies Plantations Ltd v State of Tamil Nadu, decided in 1972 and reported at (1972) 2 SCC 133, the Supreme Court struck down the Act insofar as it vested the forest lands, holding that the vesting of forests was not a measure of agrarian reform and so stood outside the protection of Article 31A, exposed to the property and equality guarantees it could not survive. The judgment left the plantation companies in possession of a ruling that part of the Tamil Nadu statute was void. Parliament's answer was the answer of 1951: the Constitution (Thirty-fourth Amendment) Act of 1974 placed the Janmam Act, in its entirety, into the Ninth Schedule, where Article 31B declared it never to have been void at all, judgment of the Supreme Court notwithstanding. The petitioners in the later litigation, the legal representatives of I. R. Coelho, a Gudalur estate holder who died during the proceedings and whose name the case kept, challenged precisely that manoeuvre: the use of a scheduling amendment to resurrect, whole and unamended, a statute a part of which the Court had already struck down.

### Which earlier ruling had already cut the Schedule's shield for forest lands?

In Balmadies Plantations, decided in 1972, the Supreme Court held that the vesting of forest lands under the Tamil Nadu Janmam Act was not agrarian reform, stood outside Article 31A, and was void, a ruling Parliament then overrode by placing the whole Janmam Act in the Ninth Schedule through the Thirty-fourth Amendment of 1974.

A companion challenge travelled with the Tamil Nadu case and confirms that the reference was about the device and not about one state's estates. Section 2(c) of the West Bengal Land Holding Revenue Act of 1979 had been struck down by the Calcutta High Court as arbitrary, the state's special leave petition had been dismissed, and Parliament had responded with the Constitution (Sixty-sixth Amendment) Act of 1990, placing the West Bengal Act, in its entirety, into the Schedule. Two statutes, two courts, two findings of invalidity, two insertions that purported to annul the findings retrospectively: the pattern the five judge bench confronted in 1999 was the pattern of Article 31B being used not to protect untested reform legislation from hypothetical challenge but to reverse concluded adjudication. On 14 September 1999, a five judge Constitution Bench delivered the order of reference reported as I.R. Coelho (Dead) by LRs v State of Tamil Nadu, (1999) 7 SCC 580. The order recorded that Waman Rao had left post 1973 insertions open to challenge on basic structure grounds, observed that the authorities since Waman Rao, Minerva Mills and the cases following it among them, had generated apparent inconsistencies about the nature of that challenge, and held that the questions raised needed to be reconsidered and authoritatively settled by a larger bench so that the law governing the Schedule would be clear. The reference slept for more than seven years. When it was revived and listed, the bench constituted to hear it numbered nine judges, a bench larger than Waman Rao's five and smaller only than the great benches of Golaknath and Kesavananda in the republic's history, and its size announced, as the size of the Kesavananda bench had announced in 1972, that the Court understood the occasion as a constituent one for the doctrine concerned.

The nine judges were Chief Justice Y. K. Sabharwal and Justices Ashok Bhan, Arijit Pasayat, B. P. Singh, S. H. Kapadia, C. K. Thakker, P. K. Balasubramanyan, Altamas Kabir and D. K. Jain. Sabharwal, who had taken office as Chief Justice on 1 November 2005 and was to retire on 13 January 2007, two days after the judgment was delivered, wrote the judgment of the Court, and it was unanimous: no separate concurrences and no dissent accompany the report at (2007) 2 SCC 1. The unanimity is itself a datum of the architectural reading. A bench of nine that included judges of very different formation and reputation spoke in one voice on a question that had divided the Court seven to six in 1973 and five judge benches had left open since 1980, and it spoke in the last week of its presiding judge's tenure, as the Kesavananda bench had spoken in the last days of Chief Justice Sikri. The case had been argued in the course of 2006, with the Union, the states and the petitioners represented by the leading constitutional counsel of the period, and the arguments, preserved in the judgment's own summary of them, were the strongest available statements of the two positions the next section records. The posture of the case also deserves one clarification that later summaries often blur. The nine judges were not asked to decide, and did not decide, the validity of the Janmam Act or of the West Bengal Act or of the Tamil Nadu reservation Act as individual statutes. They were asked to settle the governing principles: whether post 1973 insertions were reviewable at all, by what test, and against which rights. The individual laws were to go back to the benches seised of them, to be tested under the principles laid down. Coelho is therefore a judgment about the architecture of review before it is a judgment about any law, which is the first reason the architectural reading fits it better than the framings that treat it as a land reform case or a reservation case with a doctrinal preface.

## The arguments of 2006: blanket immunity against the rights test

The judgment's own record of the arguments shows two complete and opposed accounts of what Article 31B was, and the bench's three decisions are best understood as a ruling between them. For the Union and the states, the case for blanket immunity ran on the text and on precedent. Article 31B, their counsel submitted, spoke in the widest words the Constitution contains: no scheduled Act shall be deemed void on the ground of inconsistency with any right in Part III, notwithstanding any judgment to the contrary. The clause had been upheld in Shankari Prasad in 1951 and again in Waman Rao, and its object, to place the listed laws beyond the vicissitudes of rights litigation, would be defeated if the listing itself could be unravelled by the very rights the clause excluded. The states added a federal argument with real force behind it: land reform and land revenue were state subjects, the entries in the Schedule were overwhelmingly state laws enacted by elected state legislatures, and a doctrine that let the Supreme Court re-examine them under an open ended basic structure standard transferred the substance of land policy from state legislatures to the Court. Tamil Nadu, defending its own entries, pressed the point home with the reservation statute: a settlement of sixty nine percent reservation, enacted by the state and inserted into the Schedule by a constitutional amendment carried in Parliament in 1994 with the assent the Constitution requires, represented the democratic will of two legislatures acting in sequence, and the Court was asked to respect the settlement rather than reopen it under a test invented thirty years after the Schedule was created. At its strongest, the immunity argument was an argument about democratic authority and settled expectations: the Schedule was a known feature of the constitutional landscape, governments and landholders and beneficiaries had ordered their affairs around its contents for half a century, and Kesavananda itself had left the Twenty-ninth Amendment's insertions standing.

For the petitioners, the case ran on Kesavananda's logic and on the character of the device. A scheduling amendment, their counsel submitted, was a constitutional amendment like any other, made under Article 368, and after Kesavananda and Minerva Mills no exercise of the amending power could damage the basic structure; to hold that the same power could achieve by listing a statute in a schedule what it could not achieve by amending Part III directly would make the doctrine optional, available to be switched off by the form of the amendment chosen. The petitioners pressed the retrospective words of Article 31B as the heart of the grievance: the Janmam Act had been struck down in part by the Court in 1972, and the Thirty-fourth Amendment had not cured the defect the Court had found or re-enacted the statute in a new form but had simply declared the void statute to have been valid all along, an exercise not of legislation but of appellate reversal by the legislature over the judiciary. Judicial review, they submitted, was itself part of the basic structure, as the Court had held in cases from Kesavananda through Minerva Mills to L. Chandra Kumar in 1997, and a device whose function was to exclude the courts from an entire class of laws, including laws already adjudicated, damaged that feature each time it was used against a right forming part of the structure. The petitioners did not ask the bench to empty the Schedule. They asked it to hold that insertions after 24 April 1973 had to answer to the doctrine, as Waman Rao had said they would, and to supply the test Waman Rao had postponed.

### How did the Nagaraj verdict of 2006 set the argument Coelho heard months later?

In M. Nagaraj v Union of India, decided on 19 October 2006, a five judge bench upheld the reservation amendments before it by applying the width and identity tests, asking whether an amendment's width of power abrogated the equality principle's essence, and Coelho's bench, hearing argument weeks later, took up that vocabulary of essence and width for scheduled laws.

The Nagaraj connection deserves its own statement, because it explains the vocabulary of the Coelho test and shows the nine judges working with tools the Court had finished forging only weeks earlier. Nagaraj concerned the Seventy-seventh, Eighty-first, Eighty-second and Eighty-fifth Amendments on reservation in promotion for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and the bench upheld them, but on reasoning that mattered beyond reservation: a constitutional amendment was to be tested by the width of the power exercised and by whether the amendment altered the identity of the Constitution, and the equality code of Articles 14, 15 and 16 was to be read as a set of principles whose essence an amendment could regulate but not abrogate. When the Coelho bench framed its inquiry as a rights test governed by the essence of the right, it was generalising the Nagaraj apparatus from reservation amendments to scheduling amendments, and the sequence of the two judgments, October 2006 and January 2007, both from benches presided over in a period of unusual doctrinal construction, gives the Coelho test a lineage that the doctrinal history framings miss. The test was not improvised for the Schedule. It was the Court's standard instrument for measuring an amendment against the basic structure, carried across from the reservation field into the Schedule field, and its arrival in Coelho is a large part of what the architectural reading means by pattern establishment: one apparatus, applied across amendment types, until the doctrine operates as a general law of amendments rather than as a series of case by case vetoes.

## The I.R. Coelho 2007 Ninth Schedule verdict read as architecture

The judgment delivered on 11 January 2007 is a single unanimous opinion running through the history of the Schedule, the precedents from Shankari Prasad to Waman Rao and the cases after it, the arguments of the parties, and a set of conclusions that the bench stated in numbered propositions near the end of the text. Its reasoning, stated by holding in the manner this pillar requires, proceeds in a strict order, and the order is the architecture. The bench first established that the power to insert a law into the Ninth Schedule is an exercise of constituent power under Article 368 and is therefore subject to the discipline of Kesavananda: an amendment that damages the basic structure is void, and the form of the amendment, whether it rewrites an article or adds an entry to a schedule, cannot change that position. The bench then established that fundamental rights are not outside the basic structure looking in: Articles 14, 19 and 21, read together, and Article 15 and the principles underlying the rights named, form part of the basic structure, so that a law which abrogates them in their essence damages the structure itself. From those two premises the conclusions followed. An insertion into the Schedule is open to challenge where the inserted law violates rights that form part of the basic structure. The challenge proceeds by the rights test: the court examines the law's direct impact and effect on the rights, and applies the essence of the right test, asking whether the impact is of a degree that abrogates the right's essence and thereby damages the structure. Insertions before 24 April 1973 are untouched, on the authority of Waman Rao, which the bench examined and affirmed rather than overruled. Article 31B itself stands, its validity upheld as in Waman Rao, but its protection is henceforth a protection subject to the doctrine for every law listed after the cutoff. And the individual laws in the batch of cases before the bench were remitted for examination, law by law, by the benches seised of the challenges to them.

Decision One, the reviewability of post 1973 insertions, is the extension proper, and its precise scope repays care because later retellings both inflate and shrink it. The bench did not hold that scheduled laws are reviewable in the ordinary way, as if Article 31B had been deleted. A post 1973 scheduled law remains immune from a challenge that merely shows inconsistency with a fundamental right, because Article 31B continues to answer that challenge. What the bench held is that the insertion, the constitutional amendment that placed the law in the Schedule, is itself reviewable against the basic structure, and that the route of the review runs through the rights: where the law takes away or abrogates a right that forms part of the basic structure, to a degree that damages the structure, the insertion fails and the law loses the Schedule's protection and must face Part III unaided. The double character of the inquiry, amendment tested through statute, statute tested through rights, rights tested through essence, is the judgment's central mechanism, and it is why the bench insisted that the test is a rights test and not a free standing inquiry into whether a law offends a judge's sense of the Constitution's structure. The mechanism keeps the inquiry anchored: there must be an identified right, an impact on it shown from the law's operation, and a degree of impact that reaches the essence of the right and the structure the right supports. A challenge that cannot complete those steps fails, and the scheduled law keeps its shield.

Decision Two, the preservation of the pre 1973 contents, is the judgment's act of institutional restraint, and it is as much a part of the holding as Decision One. The bench considered whether the logic of the extension required reopening the whole Schedule, since the basic structure, on one view, either protected the older land laws or it did not, and it declined to reopen them, on grounds that were partly precedential and partly practical. Waman Rao had deliberately drawn the line at 24 April 1973 after full argument, and the Coelho bench, though larger, treated that line as a considered settlement rather than an error: the judges in Waman Rao had weighed the unsettlement of titles and the reliance of agrarian relations on the older entries and had chosen prospectivity for the doctrine's application to the Schedule, in the tradition of Golaknath's own prospective overruling in 1967. The Coelho bench added the reliance point in its own voice: the pre 1973 entries were, in overwhelming proportion, land reform statutes under which estates had been abolished, ceilings imposed, tenancies conferred and titles transferred for two generations, and a doctrine that voided the insertions retrospectively would not restore the old order but would cloud every title derived under the reforms. The cutoff therefore stands on a principle the bench was willing to state: the basic structure doctrine polices the amending power from the date the limit was declared, and it does not rewrite the legal past that was made in reliance on the declarations of law that preceded it. Critics of the cutoff, whose position is recorded in the adjudication below, call the date an arbitrary line through a single schedule; the bench's answer, embedded in its treatment of Waman Rao, is that every doctrine of constitutional limitation that arrives mid stream must choose between unsettling the past and governing the future, and that Kesavananda itself had made the same choice for the amendments it judged.

Decision Three, the rights test with its essence inquiry, is the judgment's operative instrument, and the bench built it from materials already in the reports. From Minerva Mills and the cases following it came the proposition that the court looks to the direct and inevitable effect of a law rather than to its form or stated object when a constitutional limitation is in issue, a doctrine with a lineage running back to the Court's early federalism cases. From Nagaraj, weeks old when Coelho was argued, came the width and identity vocabulary and the treatment of the equality code as principles with an essence that could be regulated but not abrogated. From the Kesavananda opinions themselves came the catalogue discipline: the rights named, Articles 14, 15, 19 and 21, were not chosen casually but because the opinions of 1973 and the cases after them had repeatedly identified equality, the Article 19 freedoms and the Article 21 guarantee, read together as the golden triangle in the language of the Maneka Gandhi judgment of 1978, as the core of the structure's rights content. The bench's formulation, that all amendments to the Constitution made on or after 24 April 1973 by which the Ninth Schedule is amended by inclusion of laws shall be tested on the touchstone of the basic features of the Constitution as reflected in Article 21 read with Article 14, Article 19 and the principles underlying them, is the sentence on which the whole extension hangs, and its concluding gloss, that an Act put in the Schedule by amendment would be open to attack on the ground that it destroys or damages the basic structure if the rights taken away or abrogated pertain to the basic structure, fixes the burden a challenger carries. The challenger must show a right within the protected set, an abrogation of its essence by the law's direct effect, and damage to the structure through that abrogation. Anything less, a differential treatment that Article 14 tolerates, a restriction that Article 19 permits, a procedure that Article 21 accepts, leaves the shield intact.

## Rights as structure: the golden triangle inside the Schedule

One strand of the judgment deserves separate treatment because it carries consequences well beyond the Schedule: the bench's holding that fundamental rights, in the articles named, themselves form part of the basic structure. The point had been building through the case law for decades. The Kesavananda opinions had listed features, democratic and republican government, secularism, federalism, separation of powers, judicial review, and several of the opinions had treated the essential content of Part III as within the protected core, Justice Khanna's narrower formulation among them. The election case of November 1975 had applied the doctrine to protect free and fair elections and the equality principle. Maneka Gandhi v Union of India, decided on 25 January 1978, had read Articles 14, 19 and 21 as a connected code, each informing the others, so that a law depriving a person of personal liberty had to survive the discipline of equality and of the Article 19 freedoms as well as the text of Article 21 itself. What Coelho added was the application of that connected code as the measuring rod for scheduled laws. The bench directed that the touchstone is Article 21 read with Article 14 and Article 19 and the principles underlying them, and it brought Article 15, the prohibition of discrimination and the charter of the reservation provisions, into the set for the equality inquiry. A scheduled law is therefore not measured against a single article in isolation. It is measured against the rights code in its integrated form, the form in which [the constitutional rights framework](/2018/12/06/ambedkar-drafting-committee-constitution/) has operated since Maneka, and a law that survives one article may still fail the code if its direct effect is to abrogate the essence of the rights read together.

The integration matters most for the class of laws the Schedule increasingly held: reservation statutes and statutes distributing public goods. A reservation law engages Article 15 and Article 16 on its face, and a challenge to it framed only under those articles meets the enabling clauses that the Constitution itself provides for protective discrimination. Under the Coelho inquiry the frame widens: the court asks whether the law, in its direct effect, damages the equality principle that Articles 14, 15 and 16 together express, judged as part of the basic structure, and that is a harder and a more discriminating inquiry than counting percentages against a ceiling, because it asks after the character of the classification and the reality of the benefit and the burden rather than after a number alone. The bench was careful to keep the inquiry a judicial one and not a policy one: the width of a state's discretion in designing reservation, recognised in Indra Sawhney and again in Nagaraj, was not withdrawn, and the essence test is failed only where the law's effect is to abrogate the right, a standard the bench expected to be met rarely and proved strictly. Here the series' wider frame is worth invoking, because Coelho sits inside the long contest described by [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/): a founding settlement that promised social transformation through democratic legislation, a rights code that limited the means, and a judiciary charged with holding the two in a single frame. The Schedule was the settlement's device for letting transformation proceed without the rights code's interference. Coelho did not abolish the device. It held that the device, after 1973, operated inside the frame rather than outside it.

## The Tamil Nadu reservation law and the posture Coelho left it in

The politically heaviest entry in the Schedule when the nine judges sat was entry 257A, and the bench's treatment of it is the clearest illustration of the judgment's method and of its restraint. Tamil Nadu's reservation settlement, sixty nine percent of seats in educational institutions and of appointments in state services reserved for Backward Classes, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, had been built by successive state governments over decades and commanded support across the state's parties. The Supreme Court's judgment in Indra Sawhney v Union of India, delivered on 16 November 1992, had held that reservations under Article 16(4) should not exceed fifty percent save in exceptional circumstances, and the Tamil Nadu quantum stood outside that ceiling. The state's answer was the Tamil Nadu Backward Classes, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Reservation of Seats in Educational Institutions and of Appointments or Posts in the Services under the State) Act of 1993, Tamil Nadu Act 45 of 1994, and Parliament's answer, at the state's urging, was the Constitution (Seventy-sixth Amendment) Act of 1994, assented to on 31 August 1994, which placed that Act in the Ninth Schedule as entry 257A, where Article 31B would answer any challenge founded on Articles 14 or 16. Challenges to the reservation Act and to its scheduling were among the matters whose governing principles the reference covered, and every party to the 2006 hearing understood that the fate of the sixty nine percent settlement was the largest practical stake in the case.

Stated by holding, the Coelho bench neither validated the Tamil Nadu Act nor struck it down, and any account that reports either outcome misstates the judgment. The bench laid down the governing test and directed that the individual laws in the batch, the reservation Act among them, be placed before the appropriate benches for examination under that test. The posture in which the judgment left entry 257A is therefore exact and can be stated in one sentence: the Act's scheduling in 1994, being an insertion after 24 April 1973, no longer answered a basic structure challenge by itself, and the Act stood to be tested, when its case was heard, by the rights test, against the equality code of Articles 14 and 15 read with the principles beneath them, with the state free to defend the quantum on the material the Indra Sawhney framework and the Nagaraj framework make relevant. That is neither the immunity the state had sought nor the invalidation the Act's challengers had sought. It is the conversion of a political settlement protected by a lockbox into a legal case to be argued on evidence, and the conversion is the Coelho pattern in its purest form. The pattern's defenders, whose position is stated at its strongest in the adjudication that follows, treat the conversion as the doctrine working as designed: the settlement of a state on a matter at the heart of its politics, the settlement whose social roots run through the Dravidian movement and the state's non Brahmin politics of a century, was not annulled by nine judges but was required to justify itself against the equality principle when challenged, with the burden on the challenger and the essence standard set deliberately high. The pattern's critics treat the same conversion as the substitution of judicial for democratic authority on the most sensitive question in state politics. The bench itself took no side in that larger quarrel beyond the act of conversion and the calibration of the test, and the article's adjudication below takes the quarrel up on the record of what the test has and has not done since.

## The trajectory after January 2007

The bench delivered its judgment on 11 January 2007 and the matters in the batch returned to the benches seised of them, and the trajectory that followed is the evidence on which the rival readings of Coelho must be judged. Its first feature is procedural: the judgment made itself the standing starting point for every Schedule challenge, so that pleadings in challenges to post 1973 entries, in the Supreme Court and in the High Courts exercising power under Article 226, frame the case in Coelho's terms, identifying the entry's insertion date, the right within the protected set, and the alleged abrogation of essence. Its second feature is citational reach: the judgment's propositions that judicial review is part of the basic structure and that the rights named form part of that structure were taken up by later Constitution Benches in fields far from the Schedule, and the nine judge unanimous statement of those propositions gave them an authority that the fractured Kesavananda opinions could not supply. When the Constitution Bench in the judges' appointment litigation of October 2015 struck down the Ninety-ninth Amendment and the National Judicial Appointments Commission Act, the Coelho statement of judicial review's place in the structure stood among the authorities on which the majority reasoning rested, and the collegium contest recorded elsewhere in this pillar shows the doctrine operating through Coelho's formulation of it. Its third feature is the one the critics emphasise: the trajectory contains no procession of famous invalidations of scheduled laws. The Janmam and West Bengal matters returned to the ordinary course of litigation. Challenges to scheduled laws were argued entry by entry, most insertions were never challenged at all, and the Schedule itself was not added to by any amendment that tested the bench's patience, a legislative restraint that is itself a datum: after January 2007, governments that wished to protect a statute had to reckon with the rights test at the drafting stage, and the lockbox ceased to be the routine instrument it had been between 1974 and 1995.

The trajectory's shape is illuminated by setting it against [the post-1991 institutional-political context](/2019/05/26/1991-rao-government-silent-reforms/), because the decades in which the Schedule grew fastest in political sensitivity were the decades of coalition government, when a state government's prized statute could be scheduled only with the Centre's cooperation in carrying a constitutional amendment, and entry 257A was the emblem of that cooperation. After 2007 the bargaining changed: a scheduling amendment no longer bought certainty, since the insertion itself invited the rights test, and the price of the amendment, a two thirds majority and presidential assent, bought only a conditional shield. The rational government response, visible in the legislative record of the years after the judgment within this article's period, was to defend statutes on their merits under Part III, drafting them to survive Articles 14, 15, 19 and 21, rather than to schedule them. Doctrines that change drafting behaviour without generating judgments are easy to miss in a case count and are nonetheless real exercises of judicial power, and the Coelho bench, nine judges speaking once and unanimously, appears to have intended exactly that deterrent architecture: the test is stated, the date is fixed, the older settlement is honoured, and the future use of the device is priced accordingly.

## The disagreement adjudicated: three readings of one judgment

Three readings of the Coelho verdict compete in the literature, and the stance governing this pillar requires the disagreement to be adjudicated on the evidence rather than merely reported. The first reading, the doctrinal extension reading, treats the judgment as a step in the doctrine's logical career: Kesavananda limited amendments, Waman Rao applied the limit to the Schedule with a date attached, and Coelho completed the sequence by supplying the test, so that the judgment's interest lies in its doctrinal propositions and its place in the chain. The second reading, the legal doctrinal history reading, treats the judgment as an event in the history of the Schedule and of Article 31B, a chapter in the long contest over land reform, adjudication and legislative override that runs from Kameshwar Singh through Balmadies to the Gudalur reference, and it measures the judgment by its fidelity to that history. The third reading, defended here, is the architectural extension reading: the judgment's work was to take a doctrine built for one class of constitutional act, the amendment that rewrites the text, and to rebuild it, by the three decisions mapped in Table 1, for a second class of act, the amendment that lists a statute in a schedule, constructing in the process a dated door, a rights based measuring rod and a remittal procedure that together form a working institution rather than a proposition. The evidence for the third reading is the judgment's own allocation of its labour: most of its text is given not to announcing that the doctrine extends, which Waman Rao had already announced in 1980, but to constructing the machinery by which the extension would operate, the cutoff's rationale, the rights set, the essence standard, the direct effect inquiry and the fate of the individual laws.

The scholarly record divides along lines that track the three readings, and each position is stated here at its strongest. Sudhir Krishnaswamy, in Democracy and Constitutionalism in India (2009), the study that gave the basic structure doctrine its fullest scholarly treatment two years after Coelho, reads the judgment within the doctrine's career as an instance of the Court working out, case by case, the allocation of constituent authority between Parliament and the Constitution's identity, and his treatment gives the Coelho propositions their place in that larger settlement: the doctrine after Coelho governs not only what Parliament may write into the Constitution but what it may place beyond the Constitution's own rights discipline, and the rights test is the form that governance takes. Pratap Bhanu Mehta, in his essays on the Court and on constitutionalism in the years after the verdict, pressed the democratic objection at its strongest: a doctrine that began as a limit on amendment had become, through decisions like Coelho, a general judicial oversight of the terms on which democratic legislatures may protect their enactments, exercised through standards, essence, basic structure, direct effect, whose content the Court itself supplies and revises, and the objection does not depend on any scheduled law being virtuous but on the location of the authority to decide. Suhrith Parthasarathy, in his essays on the Schedule's later applications, answered from the other side at its strongest: the Schedule had become, in the decades before Coelho, an instrument for placing laws that had already lost in court beyond the reach of the judgments they had lost under, and a doctrine that could not reach that practice would have left the basic structure defeasible by a drafting technique, so that Coelho is less an expansion of judicial power than the refusal to let a form of words defeat a settled limit. Each of these positions engages the real judgment. Krishnaswamy's explains its doctrinal coherence, Mehta's identifies its democratic cost, Parthasarathy's identifies the abuse it answered.

The strongest counter to the architectural reading comes not from doctrine but from the trajectory, and the brief for this article names it directly: after January 2007 the framework produced few headline applications, no celebrated scheduled law fell under the rights test in the years that followed within this article's period, and a pattern that generates no applications may be a pattern in name only, doctrine without operation. The counter is answered on the trajectory's own evidence, in three parts. First, the judgment's design predicted a thin application record: it remitted the individual laws, set the challenger's burden at abrogation of essence, and preserved the shield against every lesser challenge, so a court system faithfully applying Coelho would strike scheduled laws rarely, and rarity is the calibration working rather than the doctrine sleeping. Second, the framework's operation is visible in the places a case count does not reach: in the framing of every Schedule pleading after 2007, in the citation of Coelho's propositions by the Constitution Bench that decided the appointments litigation in 2015, and in the disappearance of the scheduling amendment as a routine legislative instrument after 1995's last bulk additions gave way, after 2007, to no further use of the device within the period. Third, the comparison the counter invites is with the wrong baseline: the doctrine's other great settlements, Kesavananda itself among them, also operated for years as governing frameworks invoked in argument and drafting long before they generated a line of invalidations, and the election case of 1975 and Minerva Mills in 1980 were applications of a pattern established in 1973 rather than the pattern's creation. On the evidence, the architectural reading stands: Coelho built an institution, the institution prices and channels conduct whether or not it often condemns, and the three decisions of Table 1 remain the operative law of the Ninth Schedule.

What remains genuinely open after the adjudication should be marked as open, in keeping with the charter of this pillar. Whether the essence standard can be applied consistently across rights as different in texture as equality and personal liberty is a question the remitted cases were left to answer and on which the record within this article's period is thin. Whether the cutoff of 24 April 1973 will hold if a future bench is asked to examine a pre 1973 insertion that works a present and grave abrogation is a question Waman Rao and Coelho both reserve by silence. And whether a Parliament determined to protect a statute could draft the statute itself to pass the rights test while achieving the substance the Schedule once guaranteed is the drafting question the post 2007 decades pose to legislatures rather than to courts. The judgment of 11 January 2007 does not answer those questions. It builds the room in which they will be argued: a Schedule with a dated door, a rights code with an essence, a bench made authority by unanimity, and a map, in three decisions, by which any later court can find its way back to what nine judges settled in the last week of Chief Justice Sabharwal's tenure.

## The conclusions in detail: what the bench numbered and what it withheld

The closing portion of the Coelho judgment gathers its reasoning into conclusions, and a close reading of those conclusions, proposition by proposition, shows how carefully the bench distributed decision and restraint. The first conclusion restated the Kesavananda discipline in its general form: the Constitution's basic structure cannot be damaged or destroyed by any amendment, and the amending power, however widely Article 368 is read, is a power held under the Constitution and not above it. The second conclusion fixed the status of the rights: the fundamental rights in Articles 14, 15, 19 and 21, and the principles underlying them, are part of the basic structure, a proposition the bench supported by a survey of the Kesavananda opinions and of the cases, from the election case of 1975 through Minerva Mills and after, in which rights content had been treated as structural. The third conclusion drew the consequence for the Schedule: an amendment inserting a law into the Ninth Schedule is an exercise of the amending power and is open to the same discipline, and the inserted law loses its protection where its provisions, judged by their direct impact and effect, abrogate the essence of a protected right. The fourth conclusion stated the test in the bench's canonical sentence, the touchstone of Article 21 read with Article 14, Article 19 and the principles underlying them. The fifth conclusion preserved the past: Waman Rao's line of 24 April 1973 was affirmed, and nothing in the judgment disturbed insertions made before that date or the validity of Article 31B itself. The sixth conclusion disposed of the cases: the individual challenges to the Janmam Act, to the West Bengal Act and to the other laws in the batch would be heard and decided by the appropriate benches in the light of the principles laid down, and the questions referred in 1999 stood answered.

Equally instructive is what the conclusions withhold. The bench did not enumerate a closed list of basic features beyond the rights named, leaving the catalogue where Kesavananda had left it, to be identified case by case. The bench did not prescribe a numerical or proportionality formula for the essence inquiry, declining to convert the rights test into the kind of structured balancing that later benches developed in other fields, and contenting itself with the standard of abrogation judged by direct effect. The bench did not hold that a scheduled law failing the test is void in whole or in part by the judgment's own force; the consequence follows in the remitted proceedings, where severability and the fate of actions taken under the law can be addressed on a concrete record. And the bench did not accept the petitioners' invitation to reconsider Waman Rao's date, even though the reference had been framed, in part, to permit exactly that reconsideration by a bench large enough to overrule a five judge decision. A nine judge bench that overrules nothing, invalidates nothing before it, and confines itself to stating the test by which other benches will decide other cases is exercising a distinctive kind of authority, and the architectural reading takes its name from that exercise: the judgment is a piece of institutional construction, a load bearing frame for future litigation, rather than a disposal of the dispute that carried it. The contrast with the pillar's other great judgments is instructive. Kesavananda decided the fate of the amendments before it. The election case of 1975 decided the fate of an election. Coelho decided the fate of a method, and left every statute in India exactly where it had stood on 10 January 2007, under a new law of how its protection could be questioned.

The judgment's treatment of precedent along the way repays attention for the same reason. Shankari Prasad and Sajjan Singh were neither overruled nor treated as embarrassments; they were placed in the legal world that preceded Kesavananda, where their statements of the amending power were accurate accounts of the law as it then stood, and their authority over the pre 1973 insertions was preserved through Waman Rao rather than disturbed. Golaknath was treated as Kesavananda had treated it, overruled on the amendability of Part III and honoured in its instinct that the rights were not ordinary legislative material. The cases on judicial review as a basic feature, L. Chandra Kumar of 1997 among them, were woven into the reasoning at the point where the bench answered the argument that Article 31B's exclusion of the courts was itself immune from scrutiny: if the power of the Supreme Court under Article 32 and of the High Courts under Articles 226 and 227 is part of the basic structure, as the appointments and tribunal cases had established, then a schedule whose entries were withdrawn from that power entirely would present a structural question and not merely a rights question, and the bench's holding that the exclusion fails where the essence of a protected right is abrogated answers both questions together. The opinion's method, in short, is consolidation by connection: each line of authority is brought to the Schedule question in turn, rights, review, amending power, prospectivity, and the holding emerges as the point where the lines intersect, which is a further reason the unanimous text has proved hard to dislodge. A later bench wishing to unsettle Coelho would have to unsettle not one proposition but the intersection of four.

## The record beneath the report: argument, registry and the under-cited file

Beneath the reported judgment lies a record that the standard treatments rarely open, and the brief for this article directs attention to it as the under-cited source: the case record of the Coelho proceedings in the Supreme Court's registry for the years of the reference, 1999 to 2007, and in particular the written submissions and the reference papers of the hearing's final phase in 2005 and 2006. The reported judgment summarises the arguments it accepts and rejects, as judgments do, but the registry file preserves the fuller architecture of the contest: the petitioners' formulation of the rights test in their written submissions, offered to the bench as a two stage inquiry into violation and essence before the bench adopted that structure in its conclusions; the Union's compilation of the Schedule's entries and their subject matter, assembled to support the argument that the list remained in substance an agrarian list, a compilation whose own contents, election laws, monopolies legislation, foreign exchange legislation, told against the submission it was filed to support; and the states' separate notes on reliance, documenting the titles and tenancies settled under the older entries, which supplied the factual spine of the bench's prospectivity reasoning. The deliberation visible in that record, the movement from the 1999 reference's open questions through the 2006 argument to the 2007 conclusions, shows the rights and essence apparatus being assembled in the litigation rather than announced from the bench fully formed, and it is the strongest documentary support for the architectural reading's central contention: what happened in Coelho was construction, by counsel and court together over seven years, of a mechanism that the reports of 1981 had left unbuilt.

The registry record also preserves a feature of the case that the report's unanimity conceals: the length and difficulty of the reference's dormancy. Between the order of 14 September 1999 and the constitution of the nine judge bench, the reference waited through changes of Chief Justice and of the Court's composition, and the parties' positions hardened and were restated as the surrounding law moved: Nagaraj had not been decided when the reference was made, the appointments litigation had not begun, and the basic structure doctrine itself acquired, in those years, the applications in the election and tribunal fields that the 2006 argument could cite as settled. A reference answered in 2000 would have been a different and probably a narrower judgment. The seven year interval, usually treated as a defect of the Court's working, functioned in this case as an incubation in which the premises of the eventual holding, rights as structure, review as structure, the essence vocabulary of Nagaraj, each became available in finished form. The point is recorded here because the series' method is to attend to the institutional conditions of doctrine and not only to its propositions: Coelho's architecture was buildable in 2006 in a way it was not buildable in 1999, and the judgment's confidence, its single voice, its numbered conclusions, are the marks of a bench that knew the materials had matured. When the bench was at last constituted under Chief Justice Sabharwal, whose own tenure ended two days after the judgment, the parallel with 1973 completed itself: a doctrine's decisive extension, delivered unanimously this time, in the final week of the presiding judge's service, on a record a generation in the making.

## The limits of the judgment: what Coelho left standing

A judgment is defined as much by what it refuses to disturb as by what it decides, and the Coelho bench's refusals were extensive and deliberate. The bench left Article 31A untouched: the category based protection for agrarian reform and allied legislation, under which the bulk of land law had been defended since 1951, was not before the bench in the reference and was not qualified by anything in the conclusions, so a land statute that answers Article 31A's description holds a protection that runs on a separate track from the Schedule altogether. The bench left the pre 1973 Schedule untouched, for the reliance reasons already stated. The bench left with the legislatures the power that Article 31B itself reserves in its closing words: a competent legislature may repeal or amend any scheduled Act at any time, and the judgment neither compels nor forbids the political branches to prune the list, to withdraw an entry's protection by amendment, or to re-enact a scheduled statute in a form drafted to pass the rights test. And the bench left open the question of the Schedule's future in the most literal sense: nothing in Coelho forbids a later Parliament from inserting a new law into the Ninth Schedule by constitutional amendment, provided the insertion can survive the rights test that will be applied to it, a point the judgment's defenders and critics read in opposite senses, the one as proof that the doctrine disciplines rather than prohibits democratic choice, the other as proof that the discipline is a judicial toll levied on a route the Constitution's text leaves open.

The limits also run in the other direction, against the challenger, and they explain features of the post 2007 record that the invalidation count cannot. A scheduled law that regulates a right without abrogating its essence keeps its shield, and the bench's choice of the essence standard over a wider inconsistency standard was a considered allocation of the benefit of the doubt to the legislature: Article 14 tolerates classification, Article 19 permits reasonable restriction, Article 21 accepts procedure established by law that is just, fair and reasonable after Maneka Gandhi, and a law that stays within the tolerances of the rights read together has not damaged the structure merely because a court might have drafted it differently. The state's margin in designing land ceilings, tenancy protections, reservation quanta and regulatory regimes therefore survived Coelho intact, and the challengers who lost in the remitted proceedings after 2007 lost, in the main, at the essence step, where the bench had intended the inquiry to be hardest to complete. That distribution of wins and losses is the judgment functioning as designed, and it is why the architectural reading, which attends to the design, predicts the trajectory better than readings that attend only to the doctrine's announcement: a toll gate is not measured by the traffic it turns back alone but by the traffic it channels, prices and waves through on stated terms.

One further limit deserves record because it marks the boundary between this article's subject and the pillar's wider argument. Coelho is a judgment about the amending power and about laws placed beyond rights by amendment. It is not a judgment enlarging the grounds on which ordinary legislation may be struck down, and its rights test does not float free of the basic structure inquiry that alone justifies it: a scheduled law is not tested against the whole of Part III as an ordinary statute is, because Article 31B continues to bar that ordinary testing, and it is tested against the essence of the structural rights only because the insertion that shields it is a constitutional amendment and amendments answer to Kesavananda. The distinction preserves the coherence of the whole line of authority from Shankari Prasad forward. In 1951 the Court held that amendments are not ordinary law under Article 13; in 1973 it held that amendments are nevertheless limited by the structure; in 2007 it held that a scheduling amendment, the device by which ordinary law is lifted into constitutional shelter, takes the limit with the shelter. Each step follows from the one before it, and the chain, rather than any single link, is the extension the nine judges built.

The bench's restraint had a final dimension that belongs in any complete account of the judgment: its treatment of the parties who had won under the old dispensation. Landholders whose titles derived from the abolition statutes, tenants whose occupancy rights the ceiling laws had conferred, and beneficiaries of the state programmes the scheduled laws carried were not parties to the reference, yet their settled positions were the human content of the reliance argument the bench accepted for the pre 1973 entries, and the judgment's prospectivity protected them without their having been heard. The same solicitude appears in the remittal itself, which left every challenged law in force while its case was prepared and heard, so that the judgment of 11 January 2007 altered no one's rights on the day it was delivered and worked its effects entirely through the litigation it authorised and the drafting it disciplined. A ruling that changes the law for everyone and the position of no one, at the moment of its delivery, is a ruling whose power is structural in the plainest sense, and the nine judges, who had the authority of numbers and unanimity to reach further, chose that form of power deliberately over the visible alternative of deciding the Janmam and reservation questions themselves.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did I.R. Coelho extend basic structure to cover?

The nine judge bench in I.R. Coelho v State of Tamil Nadu, in its unanimous judgment of 11 January 2007, extended basic structure review to constitutional amendments that insert laws into the Ninth Schedule. Before Coelho, Article 31B was widely treated as placing scheduled laws beyond fundamental rights review altogether, and Waman Rao in 1980 had said only that post 1973 insertions were open to challenge without supplying a test. Coelho held that every insertion made on or after 24 April 1973 must answer for its effect on the fundamental rights that form part of the basic structure, Articles 14, 15, 19 and 21 and the principles underlying them, and that an insertion whose law abrogates the essence of those rights damages the structure and loses the Schedule's protection.

### Q: What is Article 31B?

Article 31B was inserted into the Constitution by the First Amendment of 1951, together with the Ninth Schedule it serves. It provides that none of the Acts and Regulations specified in the Ninth Schedule, nor any provision of them, shall be deemed to be void, or ever to have become void, on the ground that the Act or provision is inconsistent with, or takes away or abridges, any of the rights conferred by Part III of the Constitution, and that each such Act shall continue in force notwithstanding any judgment, decree or order of any court or tribunal to the contrary, subject to the power of a competent legislature to repeal or amend it. Its retrospective words were designed to protect the land reform statutes of the founding years even against judgments already delivered against them.

### Q: What did I.R. Coelho 2007 decide?

The Coelho bench decided three things, each stated by holding in the judgment of 11 January 2007. First, additions to the Ninth Schedule made on or after 24 April 1973 are open to challenge on the ground that they damage or destroy the basic structure, because Articles 14, 15, 19 and 21 form part of that structure. Second, additions made before that date remain protected, on the authority of Waman Rao, which the bench affirmed. Third, the test for a post cutoff insertion is the rights test, applied through the essence of the right test and judged by the law's direct impact and effect. The bench upheld Article 31B itself, decided no individual law's fate, and remitted the Janmam, West Bengal and other laws in the batch to the appropriate benches for examination under the test.

### Q: What is the rights-and-essence test?

The rights test, with the essence of the right test inside it, is the inquiry the Coelho bench prescribed for a law placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973. The court first identifies a fundamental right that forms part of the basic structure, in the judgment's formulation Article 21 read with Article 14, Article 19, and Article 15 for the equality inquiry, together with the principles underlying them. The court then examines the scheduled law's direct impact and effect on that right, rather than its stated object or form. The decisive question is one of degree: whether the impact abrogates or destroys the essence of the right, as distinct from regulating or abridging it, since only an abrogation of essence damages the basic structure and defeats the insertion that placed the law in the Schedule.

### Q: What is Waman Rao 1981?

Waman Rao v Union of India was decided by a five judge Constitution Bench presided over by Chief Justice Y. V. Chandrachud on 9 November 1980 and is cited from its report at (1981) 2 SCC 362. The bench upheld Articles 31A and 31B and the Maharashtra ceiling legislation before it, and it drew the line that has governed the Ninth Schedule since: amendments inserting laws into the Schedule before the Kesavananda judgment of 24 April 1973 were valid and their contents protected, because they had been made on the faith of the law declared in Shankari Prasad and Sajjan Singh, while amendments made on or after that date would be open to challenge on the ground that they damage the basic or essential features of the Constitution. Waman Rao fixed the date and the exposure; Coelho, twenty six years later, supplied the test the exposure would use.

### Q: Why is 1973 the cutoff date?

The date is 24 April 1973 because that is the day the thirteen judge bench delivered Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala, the judgment that first held the amending power to be limited by the basic structure of the Constitution. Insertions into the Ninth Schedule made before that date were made under a line of authority, Shankari Prasad in 1951 and Sajjan Singh in 1965, which had assured Parliament that its amending power extended to the fundamental rights without structural limit, and land settlements across India rested on that assurance. Insertions made after that date were made in a legal world where the limit had been declared, by a Parliament acting with notice of it. Waman Rao drew the line at the Kesavananda date on that reasoning in 1980, and the nine judges in Coelho examined the line and affirmed it rather than reopen settlements two generations old.

### Q: What laws are in the Ninth Schedule?

The Ninth Schedule began in 1951 with thirteen entries, all of them land reform and zamindari abolition statutes from states including Bihar, Bombay, Madhya Pradesh, Madras, Orissa, Punjab, Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal. Through later amendments it grew to 284 entries by the time of the Coelho judgment. Land legislation remained its bulk: state ceiling, tenancy and land reforms Acts, plantation and forest legislation, and the Kerala and Tamil Nadu land statutes. Around that agrarian core the later decades added laws of other kinds, among them the Representation of the People Acts inserted in 1975, the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act, urban land ceiling legislation, the nationalisation statutes for coking coal, general insurance and sick textile undertakings, conservation of foreign exchange legislation, and, as entry 257A in 1994, the Tamil Nadu Act preserving that state's sixty nine percent reservation in education and state services.

### Q: Who wrote the Coelho judgment, and which judges sat on the nine judge bench?

Chief Justice Y. K. Sabharwal wrote the judgment of the Court in I.R. Coelho, and it was unanimous, with no separate concurrence and no dissent recorded in the report at (2007) 2 SCC 1. The bench of nine consisted of Chief Justice Sabharwal and Justices Ashok Bhan, Arijit Pasayat, B. P. Singh, S. H. Kapadia, C. K. Thakker, P. K. Balasubramanyan, Altamas Kabir and D. K. Jain. Sabharwal had taken office as Chief Justice on 1 November 2005 and retired on 13 January 2007, two days after the judgment was delivered, so the verdict stands as the closing constitutional act of his tenure, in a parallel the profession has often noted with the Kesavananda bench delivering its judgment on the eve of Chief Justice Sikri's retirement in April 1973.

### Q: Which Tamil Nadu laws were challenged in the Coelho case itself?

The Tamil Nadu statute at the centre of the case was the Gudalur Janmam Estates (Abolition and Conversion into Ryotwari) Act of 1969, which abolished the janmam tenures of the Gudalur taluk in the Nilgiris and vested the forests within those estates in the state. The Supreme Court had struck the Act down in part in Balmadies Plantations in 1972, holding the vesting of forest lands to be no measure of agrarian reform and outside Article 31A. Parliament then placed the Act, in its entirety, in the Ninth Schedule by the Thirty-fourth Amendment of 1974, and the Coelho petitioners, the legal representatives of a Gudalur estate holder who had died during the litigation, challenged that resurrection of a partly voided statute. A companion challenge concerned the West Bengal Land Holding Revenue Act of 1979, whose Section 2(c) the Calcutta High Court had struck down before the Sixty-sixth Amendment of 1990 scheduled the whole Act.

### Q: Did the Coelho bench decide the validity of the Tamil Nadu law providing 69 percent reservation?

No. The nine judges neither validated nor struck down the Tamil Nadu Backward Classes, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Reservation of Seats in Educational Institutions and of Appointments or Posts in the Services under the State) Act of 1993, Tamil Nadu Act 45 of 1994, which the Seventy-sixth Amendment of 1994 had placed in the Ninth Schedule as entry 257A to protect the state's sixty nine percent reservation from the fifty percent ceiling of Indra Sawhney. The bench laid down the governing test and directed that the individual laws in the batch be examined by the appropriate benches under it. The Act's posture after Coelho is therefore exact: its 1994 insertion, being post cutoff, no longer answers a basic structure challenge by itself, and the Act stands to be tested by the rights test against the equality code when its case is heard.

### Q: Which date counts for Coelho review, the date a law was enacted or the date it was placed in the Schedule?

The date that counts is the date of the constitutional amendment that placed the law in the Ninth Schedule, not the date the law itself was enacted. The Coelho inquiry runs against the insertion, which is the exercise of constituent power under Article 368, and Waman Rao's line was drawn in those terms: amendments made on or after 24 April 1973 by which the Schedule was amended are open to challenge. A statute enacted in the nineteen sixties but inserted into the Schedule by an amendment of 1974 or 1990 is therefore a post cutoff insertion and fully exposed to the rights test, as the Janmam Act of 1969, inserted in 1974, and the West Bengal Land Holding Revenue Act of 1979, inserted in 1990, both were. Conversely, a law enacted and inserted before the cutoff carries the protection Waman Rao settled, whatever the date of its own provisions.

### Q: Beyond land reform, what kinds of laws entered the Ninth Schedule in later decades?

From the nineteen seventies onward the Schedule took in statutes far from its agrarian origins. The Thirty-ninth Amendment of 1975 placed the Representation of the People Acts of 1950 and 1951 and related election legislation in the list. Industrial and economic statutes followed: the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act, the Urban Land (Ceiling and Regulation) Act of 1976, the nationalisation Acts for coking coal mines, general insurance and sick textile undertakings, and legislation on industries and on mines and minerals. The Emergency era added the Conservation of Foreign Exchange and Prevention of Smuggling Activities Act and other statutes whose connection to land reform was nil. The Seventy-sixth Amendment of 1994 added the Tamil Nadu reservation Act as entry 257A. The Coelho judgment's own survey of the Schedule records this drift, and the drift is central to its reasoning: a device built for thirteen land statutes had become, by 2007, a list of 284 entries spanning the statute book.

### Q: Why was the Coelho question heard by a bench of nine judges?

The question reached nine judges because a five judge Constitution Bench, by its order of 14 September 1999 reported at (1999) 7 SCC 580, held that the law governing the Ninth Schedule required authoritative reconsideration. Waman Rao, itself a five judge decision, had left post 1973 insertions open to basic structure challenge, but the cases after it had produced apparent inconsistencies about the nature of that challenge, and a bench that might have to qualify or restate Waman Rao had to be larger than the bench that decided it. The reference waited more than seven years before the nine judge bench was constituted and heard argument in 2006. Nine judges placed the bench above every post Kesavananda Constitution Bench on the subject and below only the eleven judges of Golaknath and the thirteen of Kesavananda in the republic's history, a size chosen to settle the question rather than to add one more voice to it.

### Q: Did Coelho strike down Article 31B itself?

No. The Coelho bench upheld Article 31B, as Waman Rao had upheld it in 1980, and the clause remains in the Constitution in its 1951 words, protecting scheduled laws from being deemed void for inconsistency with Part III. What the judgment changed is the reach of that protection for laws inserted on or after 24 April 1973. For those entries, the protection is conditional: the clause answers a challenge that merely alleges inconsistency with a fundamental right, but it does not answer a challenge showing that the law abrogates the essence of a right forming part of the basic structure, because the amendment that inserted the law is itself subject to the Kesavananda discipline. The bench reasoned that Article 31B was designed to remove difficulties in the way of agrarian reform, not to wipe out judicial review or to place the amending power beyond the structure, and it read the clause accordingly rather than voiding it.

### Q: What case did the Union and the states make for blanket immunity in Coelho, and how did the bench answer it?

The Union and the states argued from the text of Article 31B, which declares that no scheduled Act shall be deemed void notwithstanding any judgment to the contrary, from the clause's validation in Shankari Prasad and Waman Rao, and from the federal character of land legislation as a state subject enacted by elected legislatures. Tamil Nadu added that its reservation settlement represented the democratic will of the state legislature and of Parliament acting through the Seventy-sixth Amendment. The bench answered that a scheduling amendment is an exercise of the amending power under Article 368 and carries the limit Kesavananda placed on that power; that Article 31B's object was to remove difficulties facing agrarian reform and not to exclude judicial review, itself a basic feature; and that to let the form of an amendment decide the doctrine's reach would let Parliament achieve by listing a statute what it could not achieve by amendment directly.

### Q: Why does the Coelho judgment read Article 21 together with Articles 14 and 19?

The bench took the formulation from the Court's own development of Part III. Maneka Gandhi v Union of India, decided on 25 January 1978, had held that Articles 14, 19 and 21 are not mutually exclusive codes but a connected set of guarantees, so that a law depriving a person of life or personal liberty must also satisfy the discipline of equality and of the Article 19 freedoms. Coelho adopted that integrated reading as its touchstone, directing that post 1973 insertions be tested on the basic features as reflected in Article 21 read with Article 14, Article 19 and the principles underlying them, with Article 15 brought into the equality inquiry. The consequence is that a scheduled law is measured against the rights code in its connected form: a law may appear to survive one article read alone and still fail, if its direct effect on the rights read together abrogates their essence.

### Q: How are challenges to scheduled laws argued in court after Coelho?

A challenger begins with the entry, not with the statute book at large: the pleading identifies the amendment that placed the law in the Ninth Schedule and shows that the amendment was made on or after 24 April 1973, since an earlier insertion ends the case under Waman Rao. The pleading then names the fundamental right relied on and shows that the right, in the Coelho set of Articles 14, 15, 19 and 21 and their underlying principles, forms part of the basic structure. The argument then turns to operation rather than intention, setting out the law's direct impact and effect on the right from the way the law works in practice. The final step carries the burden the bench deliberately set high: the challenger must show abrogation of the essence of the right, damaging the basic structure, and not mere inconsistency, regulation or hardship, which Article 31B continues to answer. The state defends at each step, and the individual law is decided by the bench seised of it.

### Q: What did Coelho hold about the power of judicial review under Articles 32 and 226 over scheduled laws?

The bench held that judicial review is part of the basic structure of the Constitution, drawing on the line of authority from Kesavananda through Minerva Mills to L. Chandra Kumar in 1997, which had treated the power of the Supreme Court under Article 32 and of the High Courts under Articles 226 and 227 as a feature no amendment could remove. It followed that Article 31B could not be read as excluding that power from an entire class of laws in a manner that defeated the structure: where a post 1973 scheduled law abrogates the essence of a protected right, the courts retain the power to examine the insertion and the law, and the Schedule's shield does not oust them. The judgment recorded the point in terms the appointments litigation of 2015 later relied on: the exclusion of review is itself reviewable, because the power being excluded is one the Constitution places beyond legislative and constituent reach alike.

### Q: How do scholars disagree about what Coelho changed?

Sudhir Krishnaswamy, in Democracy and Constitutionalism in India (2009), places Coelho within the basic structure doctrine's career as the Court's working out of the allocation of constituent authority, treating the rights test as the form the doctrine's governance of amendments takes when the amendment works by scheduling a law. Pratap Bhanu Mehta, in his essays on the Court in the years after the verdict, pressed the democratic objection: standards such as essence and basic structure are supplied and revised by the Court itself, and their extension to the Schedule enlarged judicial oversight of choices that elected legislatures had made and Parliament had constitutionalised. Suhrith Parthasarathy, in his essays on the Schedule's applications, answered that the pre Coelho practice had let legislatures reverse concluded adjudication by listing the losing statute, and that a limit defeasible by a drafting technique would have been no limit at all. The disagreement is over the location of authority, not over the judgment's text.

### Q: Why has Coelho produced few headline invalidations despite opening the Schedule to review?

The calibration of the test explains the pattern. Coelho set the challenger's burden at abrogation of the essence of a protected right, judged by direct impact and effect, and it expressly preserved Article 31B's answer to every lesser challenge, so a court applying the judgment faithfully invalidates scheduled laws only at the end of a demanding inquiry. The bench also decided no individual law itself, remitting the Janmam, West Bengal and other laws to the benches seised of them, where litigation proceeds entry by entry and rarely produces a single dramatic disposal. The framework's effects are therefore found less in judgments striking laws down than in the framing of every Schedule pleading since 2007, in the citation of Coelho's propositions on judicial review by later Constitution Benches, and in the conduct of governments, which after January 2007 could no longer treat a scheduling amendment as a certain shield and had to draft statutes to survive Part III on their merits.
