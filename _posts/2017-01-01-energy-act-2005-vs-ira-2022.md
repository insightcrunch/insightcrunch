Every student of federal energy law eventually faces the same sorting problem. Two statutes dominate the shelf, one signed in the summer of 2005 and one signed in the summer of 2022, and the second is routinely described as the largest climate investment in the nation's history while the first is remembered, if at all, as the last time both parties agreed on an energy bill. The comparison sounds simple until a reader tries to state it precisely. What exactly differs between a statute that authorizes programs and a statute that creates tax credits? Why did one measure pass with votes to spare from both parties while the other passed on a tie broken by the Vice President? And the question that matters most for anyone trying to predict what survives: which design keeps working when Congress moves on to other things?

![The Energy Policy Act of 2005 and the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 compared across coalition, procedure, instrument and durability - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-02.webp)

The honest answer is that these two measures are not two versions of the same thing. Public Law 109-58, the Energy Policy Act of 2005, was an authorizing statute built the old way, through authorizing committees and a conference committee, and it assembled its majority by giving something to every constituency that had a seat at the table. Public Law 117-169, the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022, was a reconciliation measure whose climate title was built almost entirely from fiscal instruments, tax credits, fees and grants, because the reconciliation process admits fiscal instruments and screens out most everything else. One statute asks Congress to come back every year and fund it. The other statute does its work without any further action from Congress at all. That single structural choice, authorization versus automaticity, predicts more about each measure's survival than the size of the majority that passed it.

That claim needs defense, because it runs against the intuition that a bigger majority means a sturdier law. The 2005 measure drew 75 House Democrats and lost 31 House Republicans on the conference vote, while the 2022 measure received zero minority-party votes in either chamber. By the familiar logic of legislative durability, the bipartisan measure should be the sturdier one and the party-line measure should be the fragile one. The record of how federal programs actually live and die suggests the opposite ranking, and this article works through the four axes on which the two statutes differ to show why. The comparison is restricted to institutional durability and instrument design. This article takes no position on whether expanding fossil fuel production, nuclear power or renewable deployment is good policy; it asks only which machinery keeps running and which machinery needs help.

## Why This Pair of Statutes

The two measures compared here are the natural pair for anyone studying how Congress makes energy policy, because each one represents the high-water mark of a different legislative era. The Energy Policy Act of 2005 was the last comprehensive energy statute to pass with broad bipartisan support, the product of a Congress in which energy legislation still moved through authorizing committees and conference committees and still assembled majorities by satisfying every region with a stake in the outcome. The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 contains the largest climate program ever enacted into federal law, and it arrived through an entirely different channel: a party-line reconciliation bill whose climate title is, as a matter of statutory mechanics, a tax title. Between them sits a seventeen-year gap in which the Senate's procedural terrain changed, the parties' energy priorities diverged, and the instrument of choice for federal energy policy shifted from the appropriated program to the tax expenditure.

Students meet this pair the way they meet every famous comparison in legislation courses: as two names that blur together until someone forces the differences into the open. The titles invite confusion. One is called an energy policy act and the other is called an inflation reduction act, and neither title describes what the measure actually does with any precision. The dates are far enough apart to demand separate explanations and close enough together to fuse in a survey of twenty-first century lawmaking. Both measures address the same underlying subject, how the nation produces and consumes energy, and both were described at signing as historic. Without a structural framework, a reader can memorize both sets of vote tallies and still miss the point, which is that the two statutes embody opposite theories of how policy survives contact with later Congresses.

The stakes of getting the comparison right are practical rather than academic. Legislative staff who draft the next energy bill need to know which instruments keep working when attention moves elsewhere. Advocates who want durable policy need to understand why some programs fade without ever being repealed while others persist without ever being reauthorized. Journalists who cover the inevitable arguments about repeal need the vocabulary to distinguish a program that can be starved from a credit that must be affirmatively undone. And students who will one day write, defend or dismantle federal programs need the habit of asking not just what a statute does but what it requires Congress to do next, because the answer to the second question predicts the first one's lifespan.

This article restricts its judgment to institutional durability and instrument design, and it takes no position on the merits of either policy direction. Whether subsidizing fossil fuel production was wise, whether the renewable fuel standard was well designed, whether the 2022 credits aim at the right technologies, all of those are policy questions on which reasonable people disagree, and none of them is decided here. What is decided here is narrower and, for the student of legislation, more useful: which machinery keeps running with the least help, which machinery needs the most help, and what the historical record says about the kind of help that most often fails to arrive.

### The Two Theories of Survival

Beneath the four axes lies a simpler contrast between two theories of how policy survives. The first theory says that survival comes from breadth: a statute that satisfies many constituencies acquires many defenders, and many defenders make repeal politically costly. The 2005 statute embodies this theory. Its drafters maximized the number of stakeholders with a reason to protect the measure, trading provisions across regions and industries until the coalition was broad enough to pass comfortably. On this theory, the 2022 law looks fragile, because a statute with one party's fingerprints invites the other party to erase them.

The second theory says that survival comes from automaticity: a policy that executes itself without further congressional action cannot be undone by congressional inaction, and undoing it by action is expensive. The 2022 law embodies this theory. Its drafters, constrained by reconciliation into fiscal instruments, built a program that needs nothing from future Congresses except the absence of a repeal they must affirmatively organize. On this theory, the 2005 statute looks fragile, because every one of its programs must survive an annual appropriations process that rewards inaction.

The article's verdict is that the second theory better describes how federal programs actually live and die, because the historical record shows neglect killing more programs than reversal does. That verdict does not deny the first theory's logic; broad coalitions do complicate repeal, and the 2005 statute's bipartisanship was a genuine asset on that dimension. The verdict claims only that the asset protects against the rarer threat while leaving the commoner threat unaddressed. A statute can have many defenders and still die quietly if none of them sits on the appropriations subcommittee in the year the funding lapses. A statute can have few defenders and still persist if ending it requires opponents to mount a costly affirmative campaign. The four axes that follow make this abstract contrast concrete, and the comparison table at the center of the article states each statute's answers side by side.

## The One Test for This Comparison

A reader who finishes this article should be able to state the four structural differences between the last bipartisan energy statute and the largest climate statute, understand that one authorized programs requiring annual appropriations while the other created tax expenditures that execute themselves, and reach a defended verdict on which design is more durable, with the deciding factor named. The four differences are the coalition that passed each measure, the procedure that carried it, the instrument it used to deliver policy, and the durability that instrument implies. The deciding factor, stated plainly at the outset and defended at length below, is this: self-executing tax expenditures are more durable against passive obstruction and equally vulnerable to active repeal, so the right design depends on whether the expected threat is neglect or reversal, and the historical record suggests that neglect is the more common of the two.

The test is deliberately demanding. It does not ask the reader to memorize vote tallies, though the tallies are given below, and it does not ask the reader to hold a position on climate policy, which is excluded from the inquiry. It asks for a structural explanation, the kind that transfers to other comparisons. Once a reader sees that an authorized program and a tax credit are not two ways of saying the same thing but two different theories of how policy survives, the comparison becomes a tool rather than a trivia exercise. The same lens applies to health legislation, education legislation and any other field where Congress chooses between telling agencies to do something, telling taxpayers they may claim something, and simply hoping the money shows up.

## Axis One: The Coalition

### Was the 2005 statute actually bipartisan?

Yes, by the vote record. The House agreed to the conference report 275 to 156 on July 28, 2005, with 75 Democrats voting yes and 31 Republicans voting no, and the Senate agreed 74 to 26 the next day. That cross-party spread makes it the last broadly bipartisan energy statute of its era.

The path to that vote ran through regular order, and the shape of the majority tells the story of how the bill was assembled. H.R. 6 was introduced by Representative Joe Barton of Texas on April 18, 2005, and it moved through multiple authorizing committees in each chamber before a conference committee reconciled the differences in House Report 109-190. The House had passed its version 249 to 183 on April 21, and the Senate had passed its version 85 to 12 on June 28. President George W. Bush signed the conference agreement into law on August 8, 2005, at Sandia National Laboratories in New Mexico, and the measure became Public Law 109-58, cited at 119 Stat. 594.

Those tallies describe a coalition built by provision trading. The statute combined a renewable fuel standard requiring 7.5 billion gallons of ethanol by 2012, nuclear provisions including renewal of the Price-Anderson liability framework and authorizations for nuclear research and development, fossil fuel provisions such as royalty relief and refinery expensing, and a set of efficiency and conservation measures. Each constituency found something in the package, which is the standard account of how a broad energy bill gets to 275 votes in the House and 74 in the Senate. The vote record supports describing the result as a bipartisan deal that subsidized fossil production, nuclear power and renewables simultaneously, and the breadth of that support is exactly what the word bipartisan means in this context. Readers who want the provision-by-provision anatomy of the earlier measure can consult the companion guide to the Energy Policy Act of 2005 (/2016/09/01/energy-policy-act-2005-guide/), which walks through the titles in sequence.

The 2022 measure was built on the opposite theory of coalition. The Senate passed H.R. 5376 on August 7, 2022 by 51 to 50, with Vice President Kamala Harris breaking the tie, after a vote-a-rama that ran roughly sixteen hours; every Democrat voted yes and every Republican voted no. The House agreed to the Senate amendment on August 12, 2022 by 220 to 207, with all Democrats in favor and all Republicans opposed. President Joe Biden signed the bill on August 16, 2022, and it became Public Law 117-169. The House had earlier passed the same bill number as the Build Back Better Act on November 19, 2021 by 220 to 213, but that earlier passage covered a different and larger package, and the final 220 to 207 tally belongs to August 12, not to any August 7 House vote, because the House took no action on August 7. In neither chamber did a single member of the minority party vote for the final measure.

The contrast is the first column of the comparison. One statute was a coalition, the other was a program. The 2005 measure drew substantial majorities from both parties because it was assembled through trades across constituencies, while the 2022 measure drew its entire majority from one party and broke a Senate tie because it was a program that one party wanted and the other party opposed. The practical consequence follows directly. A coalition spreads political ownership across parties, which matters if the statute later needs defenders in both camps, but it also obliges the drafters to include contradictory provisions, which is why the earlier measure subsidized fossil fuels, nuclear plants and renewable fuels at the same time. A party-line program can be coherent, because it answers to a single set of priorities, but it concentrates political ownership in one party, which matters if the other party later holds the power to repeal it.

### The 2005 Coalition, Provision by Provision

The bipartisan vote on the 2005 conference report becomes legible once the reader sees what each bloc of votes was buying. The renewable fuel standard, which required 7.5 billion gallons of renewable fuel, chiefly corn ethanol, by 2012, spoke to the farm states and their representatives in both parties; Midwestern Democrats and Republicans alike could vote for a bill that guaranteed a market for their constituents' crops. The nuclear provisions, including renewal of the Price-Anderson liability framework and authorizations for nuclear research and development, spoke to the utilities, the national laboratories and the members whose districts hosted reactors. The fossil fuel provisions, royalty relief for producers, expensing for refinery investments and consolidated permitting for liquefied natural gas facilities, spoke to the producing states and to the members who defined energy policy as supply policy. The efficiency and conservation measures, from appliance standards to federal building requirements, gave the environmental constituency a reason to stay in the coalition rather than oppose a bill they could not stop.

Each of those provisions had a price in votes, and the conference committee paid it. The House vote of 275 to 156, with 75 Democrats crossing over and 31 Republicans defecting, maps neatly onto this account: the yes votes came from members who found their provision in the package, and the no votes came disproportionately from members whose priorities were absent or whose objections to other members' provisions outweighed their own gains. The Senate's 74 to 26 margin tells the same story with less drama, because the Senate's earlier 85 to 12 vote on its own version had already shown that a supermajority for some energy bill existed; the conference negotiation was about which provisions would fill it. Nothing in this account requires cynicism about the participants. Logrolling, the exchange of support across provisions, is how broad legislation has always been assembled in a legislature of 535 members with divergent regional interests, and the 2005 statute is a clean example of the form.

The consequence for program design was a statute that pulled in several directions at once. A single measure that mandates renewable fuels, subsidizes fossil extraction, underwrites nuclear research and tightens efficiency standards does not express a theory of the energy future; it expresses a theory of the vote count. That is not a flaw in the coalition logic, because the coalition logic was about passage rather than coherence, and on its own terms the logic succeeded. But it means that anyone looking to the 2005 statute for a consistent policy direction will be disappointed, and anyone citing its bipartisanship as evidence of its wisdom is confusing a fact about votes with a claim about design. The bipartisanship is real and verified. What it purchased was breadth, not clarity.

### What the Earlier Votes Show

The final conference votes were not the only votes that mattered in 2005, and the earlier tallies add texture to the coalition story. The House passed its version of H.R. 6 on April 21, 2005 by 249 to 183, a solid but unremarkable majority that showed the bill had room to grow. The Senate passed its version on June 28, 2005 by 85 to 12, a strikingly lopsided margin that revealed how little organized opposition a comprehensive energy bill faced when every constituency saw something for itself in the package. The conference committee then reconciled the two versions, and the conference report drew 275 House votes, twenty-six more than the House's own earlier version, which suggests the conference trades added supporters rather than losing them.

The progression from 249 to 275 in the House is the quantitative trace of provision trading at work. Conference committees do not merely split differences; they assemble the final coalition, adding the provisions that bring marginal members aboard and removing the provisions that drive them away. The 2005 conference succeeded on those terms, and the bipartisan character of the final vote, 75 Democrats yes and 31 Republicans no, was the product of that assembly work rather than of any preexisting consensus. Readers who treat the final tally as evidence that everyone agreed on energy policy mistake the output of a negotiation for the input to one. The votes show what the coalition cost, and the cost was a statute that contained something for everyone and a consistent direction for no one.

### The Vote-a-Rama and the Tie-Break

The Senate passage of the 2022 law deserves a closer look, because the mechanics of that night illustrate what reconciliation demands of a narrow majority. A vote-a-rama is the marathon amendment session that precedes final passage of a reconciliation bill: senators may offer unlimited amendments, each receiving a few minutes of debate and a rapid roll-call vote, and the minority uses the session to force politically painful votes on the majority. The August 2022 session ran roughly sixteen hours, through the night and into the morning, with dozens of amendments offered, debated and defeated on near-party-line votes. The session's function is partly substantive, since some amendments are adopted, and partly theatrical, since most are designed to create campaign material rather than to improve the bill. For the majority, the task is endurance: keep all fifty members present and voting together through every amendment, because a single defection on final passage ends the enterprise.

The final vote, 51 to 50 on Record Vote 325, with Vice President Kamala Harris breaking the tie, was the narrowest possible margin for enactment. Every Democrat voted yes, every Republican voted no, and the Vice President's constitutional role as the Senate's presiding officer supplied the fifty-first vote. The closeness matters for the coalition analysis because it shows how little slack the program had: any single Democratic senator could have demanded changes or blocked the bill, which is why individual members held outsized leverage during the drafting. It also matters for the durability analysis, because a statute that passed by a single vote has, by definition, no bipartisan cushion. The House's agreement to the Senate amendment five days later, 220 to 207 on August 12, repeated the party-line pattern with the same discipline and the same absence of minority support.

### The 2022 Coalition and Its Limits

The 2022 coalition was the mirror image: narrow, disciplined and coherent, with limits that the durability analysis must take seriously. The Senate's 51 to 50 vote, with the Vice President breaking the tie after a sixteen-hour vote-a-rama, meant that every Democratic senator was necessary and every Republican senator was opposed. There was no margin for defections, no room for a member to demand a contradictory provision in exchange for a vote, and no need to court the other party's constituencies. The House's 220 to 207 vote repeated the pattern with the same party-line discipline. The result was a climate title that pointed in one direction, because it answered to one set of priorities, and the absence of trades with fossil-state legislators is visible in the statute's contents: credits for clean electricity, clean manufacturing, clean vehicles and efficiency, plus a methane fee, with no royalty relief or refinery expensing to balance them.

The limits of such a coalition are straightforward. A statute that passes with zero minority-party votes has no cross-party defenders, and every member of the minority party has a recorded vote against it that a future campaign can cite. If control of Congress changes hands, the new majority faces no awkwardness in moving against a program its members unanimously opposed; the political cost of repeal is lower than it would be for a program with bipartisan fingerprints. The 2022 law's supporters understood this at the time, which is one reason the credits were designed to create constituencies quickly: businesses planning investments, workers building projects and communities hosting facilities all become defenders of the policy regardless of party, and that interest-group armor partially substitutes for the bipartisan armor the statute lacks. Whether that substitution suffices is a question about politics rather than procedure, and this article does not pretend to settle it.

What the vote record does settle is the descriptive claim. One statute was a coalition and the other was a program. The coalition bought breadth at the price of coherence; the program bought coherence at the price of breadth. The durability question is which of those bargains survives contact with later Congresses, and the answer turns less on the coalition than on the instrument, which is the subject of the third axis.

### What Bipartisan Means Here

The word bipartisan does a lot of work in discussions of the 2005 statute, and it is worth fixing its meaning precisely, because the word is often stretched beyond what the vote record supports. In this article, bipartisan means one thing: substantial voting support from both parties on final passage. The 2005 conference report meets that definition cleanly, with 75 House Democrats voting yes, 31 House Republicans voting no, and a 74 to 26 Senate margin. The definition is deliberately narrow. It does not mean that both parties agreed on the statute's direction, because the statute had several directions. It does not mean the drafting was consensual in any deliberative sense, because conference negotiations are exercises in vote assembly rather than seminars. And it does not mean the statute was moderate, because a bill can satisfy the extremes of both parties while alienating each party's center, which is roughly what provision trading produces.

The narrow definition matters because the broader, honorific sense of bipartisan smuggles in a durability claim that the vote record cannot support. When commentators call a statute bipartisan and mean it as praise, they usually imply that the statute will last, because both parties own it and neither will undo it. The implication confuses political ownership with institutional mechanics. Ownership complicates active repeal, and on that dimension the 2005 statute genuinely had an advantage over the 2022 law. But ownership does nothing against the quiet death, which requires no one's permission and no one's vote. A bipartisan authorization can be starved exactly as easily as a party-line authorization, because appropriators do not check party labels before leaving a program unfunded. The 2005 statute's bipartisanship was real, verified and fairly described. What it purchased was insurance against one threat, and the article's verdict turns on the frequency of the other.

## Axis Two: The Procedure

### Why did the 2022 law travel through reconciliation?

The majority lacked 60 Senate votes but held 51, and reconciliation allows passage with a simple majority. The FY2022 budget resolution supplied the directives, and the climate program was drafted to fit the vehicle. The cost was the Byrd rule, which screened out non-budgetary provisions.

Procedure is the axis most readers skip and the axis that explains the most. The 2005 statute moved under regular order, which is the ordinary legislative path through authorizing committees and, when the chambers disagree, a conference committee whose report each chamber then accepts or rejects. That path imposes no special constraint on the form of the policy. A committee with jurisdiction over energy can write grants, mandates, loan programs, standards and tax provisions alike, subject only to finding the votes. The result is visible in the 2005 measure's mixed contents: a renewable fuel standard that operated as a mandate, loan guarantee authority that operated as a financing program, royalty provisions that operated as fiscal policy, and tax incentives that operated as tax policy, all in one vehicle.

The 2022 measure moved through reconciliation, which is the fast-track procedure the Congressional Budget Act provides for legislation that changes spending or revenues. Reconciliation bills need only a simple majority in the Senate, which is why a 51 to 50 vote can enact one, but the procedure carries the Byrd rule, named for Senator Robert Byrd and codified at Section 313 of the Congressional Budget Act, as its gatekeeper. The rule's key test, at subsection (b)(1)(D), treats a provision as extraneous, and therefore subject to a point of order, when its changes in outlays or revenues are merely incidental to its non-budgetary components. The Senate Parliamentarian enforces the rule through points of order, and in the 117th Congress that office was held by Elizabeth MacDonough.

The Byrd rule's effect on the climate program is documented but requires care to state. During the 2021 drafting of the Build Back Better package, the vehicle that eventually became H.R. 5376, the Clean Electricity Performance Program was designed as a carrot-and-stick grant program that paid utilities for hitting clean-electricity targets and penalized them for missing those targets, and contemporary reporting described it as a reconciliation-friendly substitute for a straightforward clean electricity standard. Senate Republicans announced plans to challenge the program under the Byrd rule, arguing that its policy effects outweighed its budget effects. But no parliamentarian ever struck the program, because Senator Joe Manchin told the White House in October 2021 that he would not support paying utilities for decarbonization they would undertake anyway, and the White House removed the program before any ruling. Readers who want the mechanics of the rule in full can consult the explainer on the Byrd rule (/2020/03/15/byrd-rule-explained/), which quotes the statutory text and walks through the extraneousness tests.

What survived was a climate title built almost entirely from budget instruments: tax credits, fees and grants that could pass the Byrd test because their fiscal effects were the point rather than an incident of the policy. The interpretive claim that the Byrd rule shaped the 2022 law's tax-credit form is well supported by the drafting record, the announced challenges and the shape of what survived, but it must be stated as analysis rather than as a reported ruling, because no ruling on the performance program ever issued. The practical consequence is the second column of the comparison. Regular order let the 2005 drafters choose any instrument for any goal, and they chose many instruments for many goals. Reconciliation let the 2022 drafters move with 51 votes, and it dictated that the climate program take the form of revenue provisions, which is why the measure that is remembered as a climate law is, as a matter of statutory mechanics, a tax law.

### Regular Order and the Conference Committee

Regular order is the name for the ordinary legislative path, and the 2005 statute is a textbook illustration of how it works when it works. A bill is introduced, in this case H.R. 6 by Representative Joe Barton of Texas on April 18, 2005, and referred to the committees with jurisdiction over its subject matter. For a comprehensive energy bill, that meant a long list: Energy and Commerce, Ways and Means, Resources, Science, Transportation and Infrastructure, Agriculture, Education and the Workforce, and Financial Services in the House, with the Senate Energy and Natural Resources Committee handling the Senate's version through late June. Each committee marks up the provisions in its jurisdiction, the chambers pass their versions, and when the versions differ, a conference committee of members from both chambers reconciles them into a single report that each chamber then accepts or rejects without amendment.

The conference report for H.R. 6, House Report 109-190, was filed on July 27, 2005, and both chambers acted within days: the House agreed 275 to 156 on July 28, the Senate 74 to 26 on July 29. The speed of that sequence is worth noting, because it shows what regular order can do when the coalition is already assembled. The conference committee is sometimes described as the third chamber of Congress, and the description fits: the conferees decide which provisions survive, the chambers get a single up-or-down vote, and the resulting package reflects the conferees' judgment about what can pass both chambers rather than either chamber's ideal bill. The 2005 conference produced exactly that kind of package, with something for every constituency that had leverage in the room.

Regular order imposes no special constraint on the form of the policy, and that freedom is visible in the statute's mixed contents. An authorizing committee with jurisdiction over energy can write a mandate like the renewable fuel standard, a financing program like the Title XVII loan guarantees, fiscal provisions like royalty relief, and tax incentives like the energy credits, all in one vehicle, because the committee's jurisdiction is defined by subject matter rather than by budgetary form. The only binding constraint is votes: sixty in the Senate to defeat a filibuster, a majority in the House, and a president willing to sign. The 2005 coalition cleared all three with room to spare, which is why the statute could afford to be eclectic. The procedure permitted any instrument, and the coalition demanded many of them.

### Reconciliation as a Fiscal Straitjacket

Reconciliation is the fast-track procedure the Congressional Budget Act provides for legislation that changes federal spending or revenues, and its defining feature is the vote threshold: a reconciliation bill needs only a simple majority in the Senate, which is why a 51 to 50 vote can enact one. The procedure begins with a budget resolution containing reconciliation directives, which instruct specified committees to report legislation achieving particular budgetary effects. For the 2022 law, the vehicle was the FY2022 budget resolution, S. Con. Res. 14, whose directives covered the ten-year window from FY2022 through FY2031. H.R. 5376 was introduced as the reconciliation vehicle, passed the House in November 2021 as the larger Build Back Better package, was rewritten in the Senate through the summer of 2022, passed the Senate with the Vice President's tie-breaking vote on August 7, and was agreed to by the House on August 12 before the President signed it on August 16.

The price of the 51-vote threshold is the Byrd rule, and the rule functions as a straitjacket on the form of the policy. Section 313 of the Congressional Budget Act, at 2 U.S.C. 644, defines six tests for extraneous matter, and the most consequential is the merely-incidental test at subsection (b)(1)(D): a provision is extraneous if its changes in outlays or revenues are merely incidental to the non-budgetary components of the provision. The Senate Parliamentarian enforces the rule through points of order, and any senator can raise such a point against any provision. The practical effect is that reconciliation bills can carry tax provisions, spending provisions and revenue provisions, but they cannot easily carry regulatory provisions whose budgetary effects are secondary to their policy commands. A mandate that tells utilities what to do, with budgetary effects that follow from compliance, is the paradigmatic casualty of the test.

The Clean Electricity Performance Program lived and died on exactly this logic. During the 2021 drafting of the Build Back Better package, the program was designed as a carrot-and-stick scheme: grants to utilities that hit clean-electricity targets, penalties for those that missed them. Contemporary reporting described it as a reconciliation-friendly substitute for a straightforward clean electricity standard, which everyone understood could not survive the Byrd rule. Senate Republicans announced plans to challenge the program under the rule, arguing that its policy effects substantially outweighed its budget effects. The challenge never reached the Parliamentarian, because Senator Joe Manchin told the White House in October 2021 that he would not support paying utilities for decarbonization they would undertake anyway, and the White House removed the program before any ruling issued. The episode is sometimes misremembered as a parliamentarian's ruling striking the program; no such ruling occurred. What the record supports is the interpretive claim that the Byrd rule's merely-incidental test shaped the climate title's form: the regulatory program that could not clearly pass the test was removed under political and procedural pressure, and what survived was built almost entirely from tax credits, fees and grants whose fiscal character was beyond dispute.

The straitjacket metaphor should not be overstated. Reconciliation did not prevent Congress from enacting a large climate program; it channeled the program into fiscal form. Tax credits for clean electricity, manufacturing, vehicles and efficiency, plus a methane fee, constitute a substantial climate policy, and the fiscal form turned out to have durability advantages that the drafters may or may not have intended. But the channeling was real, and it explains the central oddity of the 2022 law: that the largest climate statute in the nation's history is, as a matter of statutory mechanics, a tax bill. The procedure dictated the instrument, and the instrument dictated the durability profile, which is why the second axis leads directly to the third.

### Deficit Reduction as the Price of the Vehicle

The 2022 statute was not only a climate bill; it was a reconciliation bill, and reconciliation bills must generally reduce the deficit over the budget window. The official score's finding of about 90 billion dollars in deficit reduction over fiscal years 2022 through 2031, from the Congressional Budget Office and the Joint Committee on Taxation estimate of the July 2022 text, was the procedural credential that made the 51-vote path legitimate. The climate title's spending and tax breaks, commonly summarized at about 369 billion dollars over the same ten-year window, were therefore paired with revenue measures and savings provisions that more than offset them. The revenue side of the bill, including the corporate minimum tax and other revenue provisions, exists in the text because reconciliation required it, not because energy policy demanded it. A reader who studies only the climate title misses the fiscal logic: the credits could take the uncapped, self-executing form they took because the bill's overall deficit arithmetic satisfied the procedure.

### The Parliamentarian's Quiet Power

The Senate Parliamentarian is an unelected official whose rulings shape what legislation can become law, and the Byrd rule is the instrument through which that power most visibly operates. The Parliamentarian advises the presiding officer on points of order, interprets the Congressional Budget Act's extraneousness tests, and determines, provision by provision, whether a reconciliation bill's contents belong in the vehicle. In the 117th Congress the office was held by Elizabeth MacDonough, whose rulings on the Byrd rule during the Build Back Better drafting were closely watched by both parties. Her power is formally advisory, since the presiding officer issues the ruling, but in practice her advice is followed, which makes her the gatekeeper of reconciliation's boundaries.

The gatekeeping works through a process both parties call the Byrd bath, in which the Parliamentarian reviews the bill's provisions against the six extraneousness tests before floor consideration. Provisions that fail are struck unless sixty senators vote to waive the rule, a threshold the majority using reconciliation by definition cannot meet. The merely-incidental test does most of the work: a provision whose budgetary effects are secondary to its policy commands cannot ride in a vehicle whose justification is budgetary. Regulatory standards, mandates with incidental fiscal effects and programmatic commands dressed in budgetary language all face skepticism in the bath, while tax provisions, direct spending and revenue measures pass through cleanly because their fiscal character is the point.

The Clean Electricity Performance Program's fate illustrates the gatekeeper's shadow even without a formal ruling. The program's designers knew the merely-incidental test would be a problem, which is why they built it as grants and penalties rather than as a direct standard; the grants-and-penalties form was itself an attempt to give the program a fiscal character the rule would accept. Senate Republicans announced their intention to raise a Byrd point of order anyway, arguing that the program's policy effects dwarfed its budgetary ones. Whether the Parliamentarian would have sustained the point became moot when Senator Manchin withdrew his support and the White House removed the program in October 2021. But the episode shows how the rule shapes legislation before any ruling issues: drafters write to survive the bath, opponents threaten points of order to narrow the drafters' options, and provisions that cannot clearly pass are negotiated away. The Byrd rule governed the 2022 climate title as much through anticipation as through adjudication.

## Axis Three: The Instrument

### What makes a tax credit self-executing?

A qualifying taxpayer claims it on a tax return with no agency award and no annual appropriation. Eligibility comes from the statute and Treasury rules, and the fiscal cost scales automatically with the number of qualifying claims. Congress must pass a new law to stop it.

This is the axis on which the two statutes differ most deeply, and it is the axis the article's central claim rests on. The 2005 statute was predominantly an authorizing measure. It created programs, authorized appropriations for them, established loan guarantee authority under Title XVII for advanced energy technologies, and added a set of modest, time-limited tax incentives with varying expirations and per-facility caps. The central mechanical fact about an authorization is that it is permission to spend, not money spent. The Congressional Budget Office's July 27, 2005 letter on the conference agreement estimated that enactment would increase direct spending by 2.2 billion dollars over fiscal years 2006 through 2010 and by 1.6 billion dollars over fiscal years 2006 through 2015, while reducing revenues by 7.9 billion dollars over 2005 through 2010 and 12.3 billion dollars over 2005 through 2015, and the same letter stated plainly that the office had not completed an estimate of the legislation's potential discretionary costs. The tax title's net cost over ten years was 11.525 billion dollars, per the Joint Committee on Taxation figures summarized in the Columbia energy center's analysis. Everything else the statute authorized, from weatherization assistance to nuclear research programs, needed a later appropriations act to become real money.

The 2022 statute worked the other way. Its climate title created and extended a suite of clean-energy tax credits with no aggregate cap: the production tax credit at section 45 and the investment tax credit at section 48 for clean electricity, the technology-neutral credits at sections 45Y and 48E, the hydrogen credit at 45V, the carbon sequestration credit at 45Q, the advanced manufacturing credit at 45X, the vehicle credits at 30D, 25E and 45W, and the efficiency credits at 25C, 25D and 179D. Because the credits are uncapped, the fiscal cost scales with deployment: the more qualifying investment taxpayers undertake, the more credits the Treasury honors. No appropriator votes on the total, no agency rations a fixed pot, and no annual action is required to keep the mechanism running. The companion guide to the 2022 measure's energy title (/2016/10/15/inflation-reduction-act-2022-energy/) catalogs the credits provision by provision for readers who want the statutory detail.

Two refinements made the credits reach taxpayers who owe little or no tax. Elective pay, at section 6417 of the Internal Revenue Code, lets tax-exempt and governmental entities, and businesses for the 45X, 45Q and 45V credits, receive a direct cash payment from the government in lieu of the credit. Transferability, at section 6418, lets businesses sell eligible credits to unrelated taxpayers for cash, with the seller's payment excluded from gross income, the buyer's payment not deductible, and only a single transfer permitted. These are tax positions, not appropriated funds, which is why they do not appear in any agency's grant ledger. The Treasury and the Internal Revenue Service still write the eligibility rules, including the prevailing-wage and apprenticeship conditions and the domestic-content and energy-community bonuses, so self-executing does not mean rule-free; it means the rules govern who qualifies rather than whether the money exists.

The route these credits took through Congress is worth understanding as a general matter, because tax expenditures are one of the two great channels of federal policy and the reconciliation process is their usual vehicle. Readers unfamiliar with how revenue provisions move can consult the guide to how tax bills move through Congress (/2013/08/15/how-tax-bills-move-through-congress/), which explains why the tax-writing committees and the budget process give tax credits a procedural home that grant programs do not always enjoy. The practical consequence is the third column of the comparison. The 2005 statute authorized programs, loan guarantees and modest credits that then depended on annual appropriations and agency discretion, so each program faced a second legislative gauntlet every year. The 2022 statute created uncapped credits that operate automatically for any qualifying taxpayer without further congressional action, so the policy executes itself unless Congress affirmatively intervenes to stop it.

### Authorization versus Appropriation: The Core Distinction

The distinction between authorization and appropriation is the single most important concept in this comparison, and it repays careful study because it is the mechanism through which the 2005 design fails. Authorization is the work of the authorizing committees: Energy and Commerce, Energy and Natural Resources and their counterparts, which write the substantive law, create programs, define eligibility and set the terms under which the government may act. Appropriation is the work of the Appropriations Committees, which decide how much money the government will actually spend, divided into twelve annual bills under allocations that reflect the budget resolution's totals. The Constitution requires that no money be drawn from the Treasury except through an appropriation, which means the authorizers' permission is necessary but never sufficient.

The two-step structure exists for reasons of institutional design rather than accident. The framers of the modern budget process wanted substantive policy and funding decisions to be made by different committees under different procedures, so that no single committee could both create a program and fund it without check. In practice, the division creates the vulnerability this article analyzes: a program's supporters must win twice, once in the authorizing committee and once in the appropriations subcommittee, and the second victory must be repeated every year. The authorizing victory is durable, since authorizations often run for multiple years or indefinitely, but the appropriations victory expires annually, which means the program's opponents need win only once, or rather need only arrange for the program's supporters to lose once, for the funding to lapse.

Tax expenditures escape the two-step structure entirely. A tax credit is enacted by the tax-writing committees, Ways and Means and Finance, usually in the same bill that defines its substance, and it requires no appropriation because it operates as foregone revenue rather than as an outlay. The appropriations committees have no jurisdiction over it, no annual vote on its total, and no mechanism to starve it by inaction. This is why the 2022 law's choice of instrument was so consequential for durability, whether or not the drafters chose it for that reason: by writing the climate program as tax expenditures, they moved it out of the jurisdiction of the committees that practice the quiet death and into the jurisdiction of the committees whose products persist until affirmatively repealed. The core distinction, authorization versus appropriation on one side and tax expenditure on the other, is the machinery beneath the article's central claim.

### What the 2005 Statute Actually Built

The 2005 statute's authorizing character is clearest in its largest titles, which created programs and permissions rather than money. Title XVII established the Department of Energy's loan guarantee program for advanced energy technologies, giving the department authority to guarantee loans for qualifying projects. The authority was real, and it later became the institutional home of the department's clean-energy financing work, but authority is not funding: the program needed appropriated credit subsidy and administrative resources before it could guarantee anything, which meant its scale would be decided in later appropriations cycles rather than in the authorizing statute. The renewable fuel standard worked differently but illustrated the same dependence on later action in a different form: it set a mandate, 7.5 billion gallons of renewable fuel by 2012, and left the Environmental Protection Agency to write the implementing rules, a task that required the agency's own appropriated resources and rulemaking capacity.

The statute's research and development titles followed the authorization pattern most purely. Multi-year authorizations for nuclear research, fossil energy research, efficiency programs and weatherization assistance gave the department permission to spend at specified levels, and the specified levels looked substantial on paper. But an authorization is a ceiling on what appropriators may provide, not a floor on what they must, and the appropriations committees treated it accordingly in the cycles that followed. The Congressional Budget Office's conference letter captured the resulting uncertainty in the driest possible language: the office had not completed an estimate of the legislation's potential discretionary costs, because those costs would be determined by appropriations acts that did not yet exist. A statute whose main costs cannot be estimated at enactment is a statute whose main costs are decided later, by different committees, under different pressures.

The tax title, Title XIII, was the exception that proved the rule. Its incentives for energy production and efficiency were tax provisions, which meant they did not need annual appropriations to operate; a qualifying taxpayer claimed them on a return. But the title was modest by design, with varying expirations and per-facility caps that limited both its fiscal cost and its policy reach. The Joint Committee on Taxation figures put the title's net ten-year cost at 11.525 billion dollars, a small sum beside the authorizations the statute is remembered for. The modesty was not an accident. In a coalition bargain, tax provisions are expensive in budgetary terms relative to authorizations, which cost the scorekeepers nothing until appropriated, so the drafters bought their coalition with the cheap currency of authorizations and spent the expensive currency of tax provisions sparingly. The result was a statute that looked large at signing and depended on later Congresses for most of its substance.

### The 2022 Credit Stack in Detail

The 2022 law inverted that bargain by making the expensive currency the main instrument. Its climate title created and extended a stack of clean-energy tax credits with no aggregate cap, which means the statute's fiscal footprint would be determined by the economy's response rather than by any committee's allocation. The production tax credit at section 45 and the investment tax credit at section 48 rewarded clean electricity generation and investment; the technology-neutral credits at sections 45Y and 48E extended the same logic to any qualifying clean generation technology, replacing the older source-specific credits with a single emissions-based test; the hydrogen credit at section 45V, the carbon sequestration credit at section 45Q and the advanced manufacturing credit at section 45X reached into fuels, industrial processes and supply chains; the vehicle credits at sections 30D, 25E and 45W and the efficiency credits at sections 25C, 25D and 179D reached households and buildings. Each credit had its own eligibility rules, phase-down schedules and bonus conditions, but all shared the self-executing character: a qualifying taxpayer claims the credit on a return, and the Treasury honors valid claims without any appropriator's intervention.

Two refinements extended the credits beyond the population of taxpayers with large tax liabilities. Elective pay, at section 6417, allows tax-exempt organizations, state and local governments and tribal entities, along with businesses claiming the 45X, 45Q and 45V credits, to receive a direct cash payment from the government in lieu of the credit. The mechanism matters because the entities best positioned to build clean-energy projects, municipalities, rural cooperatives, public power authorities and nonprofit institutions, are precisely the entities with no tax liability to offset. Transferability, at section 6418, allows a business to sell an eligible credit to an unrelated taxpayer for cash, with the seller's proceeds excluded from gross income, the buyer's payment not deductible and only a single transfer permitted. The mechanism matters because it creates a market: a developer with credits but no tax appetite can sell to a profitable company that has appetite but no project, and both parties benefit from the trade.

The Treasury and the Internal Revenue Service write the rules that determine who qualifies, and those rules are where the policy's conditions live. The prevailing-wage and apprenticeship requirements attach labor standards to the full credit value; the domestic-content bonus rewards projects built with American-made components; the energy-community bonus directs additional value to communities with histories of fossil-fuel employment or closed facilities. These conditions mean that self-executing does not mean unconditioned: the credits steer behavior through eligibility rather than through agency award discretion. But the steering happens within a mechanism that needs no annual funding vote, which is the structural fact the durability analysis depends on. A credit with labor standards and bonus conditions still executes itself; an authorized program with the same conditions still needs its appropriation.

The contrast with the 2005 instrument could not be sharper. The earlier statute bought its coalition with authorizations that cost the scorekeepers nothing and depended on later appropriators for everything. The later statute bought its program with tax expenditures that cost the scorekeepers hundreds of billions on paper and depended on later Congresses for nothing. One design front-loads the political fight and back-loads the fiscal reality; the other back-loads nothing, because the fiscal reality arrives automatically with the qualifying activity. That difference is the article's central claim in its concrete form, and it is what the durability axis now evaluates.

### Why the Credits Could Be Uncapped

The uncapped character of the 2022 credits deserves separate attention, because it was a policy choice rather than a procedural requirement. Reconciliation did not demand that the credits be uncapped; the Byrd rule tests whether budget effects are merely incidental to policy effects, and an uncapped credit has very real budget effects. The drafters chose the open-ended form, betting that the policy goal justified uncapped fiscal exposure. That choice is what separates the two statutes most sharply on the instrument axis: a capped credit, like the bounded incentives in the 2005 tax title, limits the Treasury's exposure to a fixed set of facilities, while an uncapped credit makes the fiscal cost a function of uptake. The procedure made the uncapped form possible; the policy choice made it real.

### Fees, Grants, and the Non-Credit Instruments

The 2022 climate title is overwhelmingly a tax-credit title, but it is not exclusively one, and the exceptions confirm the procedural rule. The methane fee, grants for air pollution and transportation infrastructure, conservation and forestry funding, and Energy Department programs appear as spending and fee provisions, the budget instruments the Byrd rule admits. These provisions operate through the familiar machinery of agency programs and appropriated or directly appropriated funds, and they face the familiar vulnerabilities of that machinery, including dependence on later action. Their presence is a reminder that reconciliation permits spending as well as tax provisions; the climate title leaned on credits because credits execute themselves, not because spending was unavailable. The instrument comparison is therefore not between a pure tax bill and a pure authorization bill. It is between a statute whose center of gravity is self-executing tax expenditures and a statute whose center of gravity is authorization-dependent programs.

## Axis Four: Durability

### Can Congress undo the 2022 law's credits with 51 votes?

Procedurally, yes. A provision enacted through reconciliation can be repealed through reconciliation by a bare majority, subject to the same Byrd-rule constraints. That requires a willing majority, a budget resolution with directives, and the political will to absorb the cost of taking credits away.

Durability is where the comparison pays off, and it cuts in both directions, which is why the verdict needs a deciding factor rather than a slogan. Start with the case for the later design. An authorized program that never receives an appropriation spends nothing. That is not a metaphor; it is how the appropriations process works. Authorization creates the legal authority to obligate funds, and appropriation provides the funds, and a program that has the first without the second is a legal shell. The 2005 statute's own scoring history demonstrates the point: the Congressional Budget Office never completed an estimate of the measure's discretionary costs, because those costs depended on appropriations acts that had not been written yet, and the office's letter described the resulting spending as subject to appropriation action. Every program the 2005 measure created therefore faced an annual test in the appropriations committees, where it could be funded at the authorized level, funded below that level, or left unfunded without any new legislation and without any recorded vote to kill it. Starvation by appropriators is passive obstruction. It requires no majority, no vehicle and no debate. It requires only that nobody acts.

A tax credit inverts the burden. Once a credit exists in the Internal Revenue Code, it continues until Congress changes the statute, subject only to the credit's own statutory sunsets and phase-down schedules. No annual appropriations vote is needed to keep the credits flowing, because there is no appropriation to vote on. The fiscal cost appears on the revenue side of the ledger rather than the spending side, and it scales with taxpayer behavior rather than with committee decisions. This is why the uncapped design matters so much to the durability question. A capped credit creates a pot that can be exhausted or left unreplenished; an uncapped credit creates an entitlement-like mechanism that runs as long as qualifying activity continues. The practical effect is that passive obstruction, the slow starvation that kills so many authorized programs, has almost nothing to grip. An appropriator who wants to stop a tax credit cannot simply decline to fund it. Congress must pass a law.

That is the case for the later design, and it is strong. Now the case the other way, which is equally real. A tax provision enacted through reconciliation can be repealed through reconciliation, and the vote threshold is the same 51 that created it. The Byrd rule constrains what a reconciliation bill can carry, but repealing a revenue provision is itself a revenue provision, so the rule that shaped the credits' creation does not protect them from a repeal drafted to the same standard. The 2022 measure's party-line character concentrates the political risk here. A statute that passed with zero minority-party votes has no bipartisan ownership to complicate a repeal effort, and every member of the minority party has a recorded vote against it. Active repeal, unlike passive starvation, requires a willing majority and a legislative vehicle, but the reconciliation vehicle is available to any majority that can assemble one, and the threshold is a bare majority rather than the supermajority that regular order would demand.

So the durability comparison is genuinely two-sided, exactly as the four-axis framework requires. Self-executing tax expenditures are more durable against passive obstruction and equally vulnerable to active repeal. The 2005 design, authorization plus appropriation, is vulnerable to the quiet death: funded below its authorized levels, or not funded at all, year after year, until the program exists only on paper. The 2022 design, uncapped credits in the tax code, is immune to the quiet death and exposed to the loud one: a future majority with 51 Senate votes and a reconciliation vehicle can repeal it outright. The verdict therefore depends on which threat the historical record suggests is more common, and the article must name the deciding factor rather than leaving the reader with a tie.

### How Starvation Works

The quiet death of an authorized program deserves a precise description, because it is the threat the 2005 design cannot escape and the threat the 2022 design was built to avoid. The appropriations process divides federal discretionary spending into twelve annual bills, each written by a subcommittee working under an allocation set by the full committee. Every program in the subcommittee's jurisdiction competes for a share of that allocation, and the competition is zero-sum: a dollar for one program is a dollar unavailable to another. In that environment, the path of least resistance is continuity. Subcommittees carry forward the prior year's numbers with small adjustments, respond to the loudest constituencies and the most urgent crises, and leave unfamiliar or unloved programs at whatever level, including zero, that the previous cycle assigned them.

An authorized program enters this competition with a permission slip and no money. The authorization may specify a handsome figure, and the program's supporters may cite that figure in speeches, but the subcommittee is bound by its allocation rather than by the authorization, and it routinely funds programs below their authorized levels. Nothing in the process requires a vote against the program or a finding that it has failed. The program's line in the bill simply carries a number smaller than the authorization, or no number at all, and the bill moves on. Year after year, the unfunded authorization remains on the books as a legal curiosity while the program it was meant to support withers for lack of resources. Agency staff assigned to the program are reassigned, institutional knowledge disperses, and the constituency that might have defended the program never forms, because a program that never operated has no beneficiaries to lobby for its revival.

This is not a hypothetical failure mode. It is the modal outcome for authorized-but-unfunded programs across decades of appropriations history, and it is why the Congressional Budget Office's 2005 conference letter matters as more than a scoring footnote. When the office reported that it had not completed an estimate of the legislation's discretionary costs, it was reporting that the statute's substance would be decided later, by appropriators, under the competitive pressures described above. Every program the 2005 measure authorized, from weatherization assistance to nuclear research to the Title XVII loan guarantees, faced that gauntlet. Some would be funded, some would be funded below their authorizations, and some would be left to the quiet death. The statute's survival as a going concern depended on winning the appropriations fight every year, in every subcommittee with jurisdiction, against every competing claim on the allocation. Winning once is an achievement. Winning annually for decades is a much harder trick.

### How Repeal Would Work

The loud death, by contrast, requires affirmative legislation, and its mechanics are worth stating precisely because they define the 2022 design's real vulnerability. A tax credit in the Internal Revenue Code continues until Congress changes the statute. Ending it means passing a bill that repeals or curtails the credit, which means finding a legislative vehicle, assembling a majority and surviving the procedural constraints that apply to the vehicle chosen. For a future majority that wants the 51-vote path, the vehicle would be reconciliation: a budget resolution with directives to the tax-writing committees, committee-reported legislation, Senate consideration under expedited procedures, and compliance with the Byrd rule. The rule would not be much of an obstacle, because repealing a revenue provision is itself a budgetary provision rather than a merely incidental one, so the merely-incidental test that channeled the credits' creation would not block their repeal.

The political economy of repeal is where the real constraints lie. By the time a repeal effort is mounted, the credits will have created the standard defenses of any established program: businesses that planned investments around the credits' availability, workers hired to build qualifying projects, communities that sited the facilities and the supply chains that serve them. Those interests lobby against repeal with the intensity that any threatened investment brings to Washington, and they do so regardless of party. The repeal effort must also pay the opportunity cost of floor time and political capital, both scarce resources for any majority, and it must do so in the face of the standard asymmetry that taking something away is harder than declining to give it. None of this makes repeal impossible, and the article does not claim otherwise. The 2022 law's party-line passage means no minority-party members have ownership stakes that complicate a repeal vote, and a disciplined future majority with 51 Senate votes could, as a matter of procedure, do it.

The comparison between the two deaths is therefore a comparison between a threat that requires nothing and a threat that requires everything. Starvation requires no majority, no vehicle, no floor time and no debate; it requires only that appropriators do what the appropriations process already incentivizes them to do. Repeal requires a majority, a vehicle, floor time, procedural compliance and the willingness to absorb the political cost of taking benefits away from organized constituencies. Both threats are real, and the article's verdict does not deny the second. But the frequency with which legislatures choose inaction over action, the path of least resistance over the costly fight, is what makes the first threat the more common one, and that frequency judgment is the deciding factor the verdict names.

### The Sunsets the Statute Wrote for Itself

Self-executing is not the same as permanent, and the 2022 statute illustrates the difference. The technology-neutral credits under sections 45Y and 48E are written to phase down when power-sector emissions fall to specified targets, and the statute's various credits carry their own expiration and phaseout schedules. These are statutory sunsets: the law ends its own provisions on its own terms, without any later Congress acting. A credit can therefore expire by design, be amended by a later Congress, or be repealed outright, and none of those outcomes requires an appropriations vote. The durability comparison turns on this distinction. The question is not whether the credits last forever; nothing in the statute books lasts forever. The question is what it takes to stop them, and the answer is affirmative legislative action, which is a higher bar than the passive inaction that starves an authorized program.

### The Interest-Group Armor

Programs that survive tend to acquire defenders, and the two statutes differ in how quickly and reliably their instruments generate those defenders. An authorized program generates defenders only if it is funded and operates: beneficiaries must experience the program, organize around it and lobby for its continuation. A program that is starved in its first appropriations cycles never gets the chance, because there are no beneficiaries to defend what never existed. The quiet death is therefore self-reinforcing: non-funding prevents the formation of the constituency that might have demanded funding, and each unfunded cycle makes the next one easier to justify. The 2005 statute's programs faced this trap from the day the President signed the bill, because every one of them needed an appropriator's affirmative decision before it could create a single defender.

Tax credits generate defenders through a different and faster mechanism. The moment a credit is available, businesses begin planning investments around it, developers site projects to capture it, workers are hired to build the qualifying facilities, and communities compete to host the activity. Each of those actors acquires a financial interest in the credit's continuation, and financial interests organize quickly in Washington. The interest-group armor forms before any repeal effort can be mounted, because the credits execute themselves from the date of enactment while a repeal effort must wait for a future Congress, a future vehicle and a future majority willing to choose the fight. By the time the fight arrives, the credits' defenders are entrenched, experienced and well funded.

The armor is not a complete defense, and the article does not present it as one. Interest groups lose legislative fights regularly, and a determined majority can repeal credits over organized opposition, as the history of expired tax provisions attests. But the armor changes the repeal calculus from an abstract procedural question into a concrete political one: repeal means taking money from identifiable businesses, workers and communities that will appear at hearings, run advertisements and fund challengers. That transformation is the mechanism through which self-executing instruments convert their procedural immunity to starvation into political resistance to repeal. The 2005 design, which could be killed without ever creating defenders, enjoyed no such conversion. The asymmetry is another reason the frequency judgment favors the later design.

## Agency Discretion Under the Two Designs

### Discretion in the Authorization Model

The 2005 model gives agencies wide discretion, and the discretion runs in both directions. An authorized program leaves the implementing agency to write rules, set priorities, select grant recipients, and pace disbursement. A supportive agency can stretch an authorization toward its most ambitious reading; an indifferent one can implement it narrowly and slowly. The loan guarantee authority under Title XVII is the type case: the statute created the authority, and the Department of Energy's subsequent choices about credit policy, project selection, and risk tolerance determined what the authority meant in practice. Agency discretion is therefore a second source of vulnerability for the authorization model, alongside the appropriations gauntlet. Even a funded program can be blunted by implementation choices that no statute can fully control.

### Discretion in the Self-Executing Model

The 2022 model does not eliminate agency discretion; it relocates it. Treasury and the Internal Revenue Service write the eligibility rules for the credits, and the bonus conditions, prevailing wage and apprenticeship, domestic content, energy community, are defined through guidance that the executive branch controls. A supportive Treasury can interpret eligibility broadly and issue taxpayer-friendly safe harbors; a hostile one can interpret narrowly, delay guidance, and create compliance uncertainty that chills investment. The difference from the 2005 model is the default. Under the authorization model, agency inaction stops the money, because the money must be affirmatively disbursed. Under the credit model, agency inaction creates friction but the statutory entitlement remains, because the credit is claimed on a return and the burden of disallowing it falls on the government. Discretion exists in both designs; it is fatal by default only in one.

## The Verdict: Name the Deciding Factor

The deciding factor is the relative frequency of the two threats, and the record of federal program management points in one direction. Neglect is more common than reversal. Appropriations committees routinely fund authorized programs below their authorized levels or decline to fund them at all, and they do so through the ordinary, low-visibility work of writing twelve annual bills under tight allocations, where every program competes with every other and the path of least resistance is to leave the prior year's number in place or trim it. No vote is taken to kill the unfunded program. No debate is held on its merits. The program simply never receives the money, and after enough cycles of non-funding it becomes a historical curiosity rather than a going concern. This is the quiet death, and it is the modal way that federal programs end.

Reversal is rarer and harder. Repealing an enacted tax provision requires a majority willing to spend scarce floor time and political capital on taking something away, a budget resolution with reconciliation directives if the 51-vote path is wanted, and compliance with the same Byrd-rule constraints that governed enactment. Majorities that possess the votes for repeal often discover that the credits have created constituencies, businesses that planned investments around them, workers hired to build the qualifying projects, and state officials who sited the projects, and those constituencies lobby against repeal with the same intensity that any threatened interest brings to Washington. None of this makes repeal impossible. It makes repeal an active, visible, costly political act, which is a fundamentally different proposition from the passive non-action that starves an appropriated program.

The verdict follows. The 2022 design is the more durable of the two against the threat that most often kills federal programs, because self-executing tax expenditures cannot be starved by inaction and can only be ended by action. The 2005 design is the more vulnerable, because its survival depended on winning the appropriations fight every single year, and winning a fight every year for decades is a much harder trick than winning a single enactment fight once. The party-line character of the 2022 measure is a genuine offsetting risk, and the article does not minimize it: a statute with zero minority-party votes has no cross-party defenders if a future majority moves to repeal it. But the repeal scenario requires a future majority to choose the fight, assemble the vehicle and pay the political price, while the starvation scenario requires only that appropriators do what appropriators do in every cycle. On the historical frequency of the two threats, the automaticity of the later design beats the authorization structure of the earlier one.

This is the namable claim of the article, and it is worth stating in its compact form. Authorization versus automaticity: the deepest difference between the two statutes is not partisanship or size but whether the policy requires Congress to act again every year, and that single structural choice predicts more about a statute's survival than the size of the majority that passed it. A reader who internalizes that sentence has the tool this comparison was built to provide, and can apply it to any future statute that presents the same choice.

### Why Frequency Decides

A reader might object that the verdict compares a certainty with a possibility: starvation is common, repeal is possible, and comparing their frequencies is comparing different kinds of things. The objection has force, and the verdict should answer it rather than evade it. The answer is that durability is always a probabilistic judgment, because no statute is repeal-proof and no program is starvation-proof. The question is never whether a design can fail; every design can fail. The question is which failure mode the institutional environment selects for, and the appropriations process selects for starvation with remarkable consistency.

Consider what each design asks of its defenders. The 2005 design asks its defenders to win every year: to show up in every appropriations cycle, in every relevant subcommittee, and secure funding against every competing claim. A single loss, a single cycle in which the subcommittee has other priorities, and the program goes unfunded for the year; enough such cycles and the program is gone. The 2022 design asks its defenders to win only when attacked: to defeat a repeal effort if and when a future majority mounts one. Between those two burdens lies the entire difference. Annual defense against an always-operating gauntlet is a heavier lift than occasional defense against a sometimes-mounted assault, because the gauntlet never takes a year off and the assault requires its organizers to choose the fight.

The verdict also survives the strongest version of the counterargument, which runs as follows: the 2022 law's party-line character makes repeal more likely than the baseline, because the minority party that unanimously opposed the credits has every incentive to repeal them when it holds power. That is true as far as it goes. But likelihood is not frequency, and the institutional record shows that even motivated majorities repeal less than they promise. Repeal efforts collide with the constituencies that credits create, with the scarcity of floor time, with the procedural thicket of reconciliation, and with the simple fact that governing majorities usually prefer to spend their capital on their own priorities rather than on undoing their predecessors'. The 2022 design's vulnerability to active repeal is genuine, and the article states it plainly. The claim is only that the vulnerability materializes less often than the starvation that the 2005 design invites by its very structure.

### The Asymmetry of Action

The verdict's logic can be compressed into a single institutional observation: in a legislature, inaction is always cheaper than action. Doing nothing requires no coalition, no floor time, no procedural compliance and no political capital; doing something requires all four. The appropriations process exploits this asymmetry relentlessly, because its default outcome, the continuation of the prior year's numbers with small adjustments, is itself a form of inaction toward any program that was not funded the prior year. A program seeking its first appropriation, or seeking restoration after a lapse, must overcome the asymmetry by organizing a coalition for action in every cycle. A credit seeking to avoid repeal must overcome the asymmetry only when an opponent organizes a coalition for action against it, which happens rarely and at great cost.

The asymmetry also explains why the two designs' vulnerabilities feel so different to their defenders. Defenders of an authorized program live with chronic anxiety: every appropriations cycle is a potential loss, the losses accumulate silently, and there is no single moment at which to rally because there is no single vote to oppose. Defenders of a tax credit live with acute anxiety: most of the time nothing threatens the credit, but a repeal effort, when it comes, arrives as a visible legislative campaign with hearings, amendments and a final vote. Chronic anxiety is harder to manage than acute anxiety, because it demands constant vigilance without clear focal points, and vigilance is a scarce resource for any coalition. The 2005 design imposed chronic anxiety on its supporters by structure; the 2022 design imposes acute anxiety only when attacked.

None of this denies that the attack can succeed. A future majority with 51 Senate votes, a reconciliation vehicle and the willingness to pay the political price can repeal the credits, and the article's verdict would then be proven wrong in that instance. Verdicts about durability are probabilistic, not prophetic, and they are judged by the frequency of outcomes across many cases rather than by any single case. But the institutional environment that produced the two statutes, the appropriations gauntlet on one side and the reconciliation vehicle on the other, has been stable for decades, and within that environment the asymmetry of action consistently favors the instrument that needs nothing from Congress over the instrument that needs something every year.

## The Coherence Trade-Off

A natural objection runs the other way, and the article should meet it directly rather than letting it linger. The objection says that bipartisanship is itself a design virtue, that a statute assembled from both parties' priorities is more balanced and therefore better, and that the 2022 measure's party-line passage is a defect that the durability analysis understates. The honest answer is that the trade between coalition breadth and program coherence is real, and it cuts against the earlier statute as much as for it.

The 2005 measure's bipartisan character produced internal contradictions. To assemble 275 House votes and 74 Senate votes, the drafters subsidized fossil fuel production, nuclear power and renewable fuels simultaneously, pairing a renewable fuel standard with royalty relief, pairing nuclear research authorizations with refinery expensing, and pairing efficiency measures with provisions that made fossil extraction cheaper. Each provision had a constituency and each constituency had votes, which is how the coalition was built, but the resulting statute pulled in several directions at once. A reader who asks what energy future the 2005 measure envisioned gets no single answer, because the measure envisioned all of them. That is not an accusation of bad faith; it is the predictable output of provision trading across constituencies, and the vote spread, 75 Democrats yes and 31 Republicans no in the House, is the evidence that the trades happened.

The 2022 measure's party-line character produced coherence and fragility together. Because it answered to a single party's priorities, its climate title pointed in one direction: tax credits for clean electricity, clean manufacturing, clean vehicles and efficiency, with fees on methane as the fiscal complement. There is no internal contradiction to manage, because no trades with the other party's constituencies were needed. But the coherence came at the price the durability axis already identified: zero minority-party votes means zero minority-party ownership, and a future majority faces no cross-party awkwardness in moving against a program its party unanimously opposed. The trade is therefore symmetrical and honest. Coalition breadth buys political insurance and sells program coherence; program coherence buys directional clarity and sells political insurance. Neither bargain dominates the other in the abstract. The durability verdict above chooses between them only because the historical record says the threat that coherence-without-breadth invites, active repeal, arrives less often than the threat that breadth-without-automaticity invites, passive starvation.

The trade also illuminates why the familiar praise of bipartisanship can mislead. Bipartisanship is a fact about votes, and it is often treated as a fact about quality, as though a statute that satisfied both parties must be better designed than one that satisfied only one. The 2005 statute shows why the inference fails. Its bipartisanship was purchased with contradictory provisions, each one the price of a bloc of votes, and the resulting measure subsidized the energy sources it also sought to displace. A reader who values coherence, a statute that knows what it wants, will find more of it in the party-line measure than in the bipartisan one. A reader who values political insurance, a statute that both parties have reason to defend, will find more of it in the bipartisan measure than in the party-line one. The honest lesson is that coalition breadth and program coherence trade against each other, and that neither virtue implies the other.

That lesson generalizes beyond energy law, which is why the article states it as the complication rather than burying it. Every broad statute assembled through provision trading faces the same bargain: each constituency's price is a provision, and provisions accumulate into contradictions. Every narrow statute enacted on party lines faces the inverse bargain: coherence comes cheap, because only one set of priorities must be satisfied, but insurance is expensive, because only one party owns the result. The durability verdict chooses between the bargains on frequency grounds, not on aesthetic ones. Coherence is genuinely valuable, and the 2022 law has more of it; breadth is genuinely valuable, and the 2005 law has more of it. What decides is not which virtue is prettier but which threat arrives more often, and the historical record answers that question in favor of the self-executing design.

## A Decision Framework for the Reader

### The Two Threats, Stated as a Matrix

The verdict of this article rests on the claim that neglect is the more common threat to energy statutes than reversal, and a reader applying the framework should test that claim against the two designs directly. Consider the authorization model first. Its threats are threefold: appropriators can underfund it, agencies can implement it narrowly, and a later Congress can repeal or amend it. The first two require no affirmative legislative act; they are the default outcomes of inattention. The third requires a majority willing to spend political capital. Now consider the self-executing model. Its threats are also threefold: Treasury can interpret eligibility narrowly, a later Congress can amend the credit's terms, and a later Congress can repeal it through reconciliation. The first is friction, not stoppage; the second and third require affirmative legislative action. The matrix shows the asymmetry: the authorization model's most likely failure modes require nothing to happen, while the credit model's most likely failure modes require something to happen. That asymmetry is the entire durability argument in one table of the mind.

### How the Framework Generalizes

The four axes are portable. Any reader comparing two statutes, on energy or on any other subject, can ask the same four questions: who passed it and how broad was the coalition, what procedure carried it and what constraints did the procedure impose, what instrument does the policy use and does the instrument require Congress to act again, and what does it take to stop the policy once enacted. The answers will differ by subject matter, but the structure of the inquiry does not. That portability is the reason the study path in this series places the comparison article where it does: after the reader has the two statute guides and the procedure explainers, the four axes become a reusable tool rather than a one-time exercise.

## A Note on the Numbers

Every cost figure in this section is attributed to a named source with its scoring window, because the most common error in this comparison is to set headline dollar figures from incompatible accountings side by side. The two statutes were scored under different conventions, in different eras, for different instruments, and a direct dollar comparison misleads more than it informs.

For the 2022 measure, the official score at enactment came from the Congressional Budget Office and the staff of the Joint Committee on Taxation, estimating the July 27, 2022 text of H.R. 5376: the legislation decreased federal deficits by about 90 billion dollars over fiscal years 2022 through 2031. Within that score, the energy and climate provisions are commonly summarized at about 369 billion dollars over the same ten-year window, a shorthand derived from the CBO and Joint Committee tables. That shorthand is a static ten-year estimate built on conventional uptake assumptions, and it is the figure the official score supports. It is not the only figure in circulation, because the credits are uncapped and later analysts modeled higher uptake. Goldman Sachs estimated about 1.2 trillion dollars of climate and clean-energy incentives by 2032 in an April 2023 report. Credit Suisse estimated about 800 billion dollars in November 2022. The Brookings Institution's analysis using the US-REGEN model put total fiscal costs of the climate provisions through 2031 at 365 billion to 1.19 trillion dollars, with a central estimate of 902 billion dollars, and tax credits alone at 244 billion to 1.07 trillion dollars against about 392 billion in the official score. The Joint Committee on Taxation itself estimated 515 billion dollars for the tax credits over 2023 through 2033 in April 2023. The range reflects uptake assumptions, not arithmetic errors: higher deployment of clean electricity, electric vehicles and hydrogen pushes the fiscal cost up, because uncapped credits scale with the activity they reward.

For the 2005 measure, the scoring conventions were different because the instrument was different. The Congressional Budget Office's July 27, 2005 letter on the conference agreement estimated that enactment would increase direct spending by 2.2 billion dollars over fiscal years 2006 through 2010 and by 1.6 billion dollars over fiscal years 2006 through 2015, while reducing revenues by 7.9 billion dollars over 2005 through 2010 and 12.3 billion dollars over 2005 through 2015. The tax title's net cost over ten years was 11.525 billion dollars. Decisively, the same letter stated that the office had not completed an estimate of the legislation's potential discretionary costs, which is to say that the bulk of what the statute authorized was never scored at enactment because it depended on appropriations that had not yet been written. No single official total authorization figure for the 2005 measure exists, and this article does not invent one. Labeled examples from the statute's summaries, such as multi-year authorizations for weatherization assistance and nuclear research, illustrate the scale of what authorization meant, but they are examples, not a total.

The accounting lesson is the point of the section. The 2005 figures are small because most of the statute's cost was never scored; the 2022 figures are large because the credits were scored on the revenue side and later re-estimated under higher uptake. Comparing the 11.525-billion-dollar tax title of the earlier measure with the 369-billion-dollar shorthand of the later one compares a scored fragment with a scored whole, and comparing either with the trillion-dollar independent estimates compares official static scoring with modeled uptake scenarios. The honest comparison is structural, not arithmetic: one statute authorized spending that later Congresses could provide or withhold, and the other created tax expenditures that execute themselves at whatever scale qualifying taxpayers claim them.

### Reading the Scorekeepers

A final caution about the numbers concerns what official scores can and cannot tell the reader. The Congressional Budget Office and the Joint Committee on Taxation score legislation over a ten-year window using conventional assumptions about economic behavior, and their estimates are the best available guide to what a bill does to the budget under those assumptions. But the conventions have limits that matter for this comparison. For the 2005 statute, the conventions meant that discretionary costs dependent on future appropriations went unscored, so the official figures understated the statute's ambitions by exactly the amount that appropriators would later decide. For the 2022 statute, the conventions meant that uncapped credits were scored on assumed uptake, so the official figures understated the fiscal cost under every scenario in which deployment exceeded the assumptions. In both cases, the score described the statute's interaction with the scoring window rather than the statute's ultimate footprint.

Independent analysts filled the gaps with models, and their ranges are informative precisely because they disagree. When Goldman Sachs, Credit Suisse, Brookings and the Joint Committee on Taxation produce estimates spanning hundreds of billions of dollars, the spread is not a sign that someone made an arithmetic error; it is a sign that the underlying instrument makes cost a function of behavior, and behavior is hard to forecast. The reader's takeaway should not be any single number but the structural reason the numbers vary: authorization leaves cost to appropriators, uncapped credits leave cost to the economy, and neither leaves cost to the scorekeeper's window. Attribute every figure to its source, note its window, and resist the temptation to crown a winner by dollars. The comparison is about machinery, and the machinery is what the four axes describe.

### Scoring Windows and Their Tricks

The ten-year scoring window shapes every figure in this comparison, and its tricks deserve explicit attention because they flatter both statutes in different ways. For authorizing legislation like the 2005 statute, the window captures only direct spending and revenue effects, which means the discretionary programs that constitute the statute's substance fall outside the score entirely. The official figures, 2.2 billion dollars in direct spending over five years and 12.3 billion in foregone revenue over ten, describe the statute's budgetary shadow rather than its ambitions. A reader who takes those figures as the measure of the statute's size will underestimate what Congress thought it was doing, because what Congress thought it was doing was authorizing programs whose costs would be decided later.

For tax-expenditure legislation like the 2022 law, the window captures the credits' conventionally estimated cost, but the conventions assume a particular path of uptake over the ten years. If deployment runs hotter than assumed, the actual cost exceeds the score, potentially by multiples; if deployment runs cooler, the cost falls short. The official 369-billion-dollar shorthand is therefore a point estimate drawn through a cloud of possibilities, and the independent estimates, from 800 billion to 1.2 trillion dollars, map the cloud's extent under different assumptions. A reader who takes any single figure as the measure of the law's cost will mistake a forecast for a ceiling, because the statute sets no ceiling.

The window also treats the two instruments' timing differently. Authorization costs appear in the score only when appropriations follow, which may be years after enactment or never; credit costs appear in the score from the first year of availability, because qualifying taxpayers claim them immediately. This timing difference means the two statutes' scores are not just different in magnitude but different in kind: one is a partial snapshot of decisions not yet made, the other is a forecast of behavior not yet observed. The honest use of the numbers is comparative only in the structural sense the article defends. One statute left its costs to appropriators, and the other left its costs to the economy, and the scorekeepers' window could fully capture neither.

## The Long View: What Seventeen Years Changed

### What the Migration Costs and What It Buys

The migration from regular order to reconciliation costs the legislative process something real. A conference committee can reconcile any differences between the chambers' bills, and a regular-order bill can carry regulatory provisions, program authorizations, and tax measures in a single text, as the 2005 act did. Reconciliation cannot do either freely: it is bound to fiscal provisions that survive the Byrd rule, and its product must reduce the deficit over the window. What the migration buys is passability. A policy that cannot command 60 Senate votes can still become law with 51, provided it can be written in fiscal form. The 2022 climate title exists because its authors accepted that bargain: they gave up the regulatory instrument and the programmatic form in exchange for a vehicle that could pass. The 2005 authors never faced the bargain, because their coalition supplied the votes for the textbook path. The four axes, read across seventeen years, thus tell a story about what the Senate's procedures permit as much as a story about what two Congresses wanted.

### The Durability Lesson the Migration Teaches

The migration teaches a durability lesson that runs deeper than either statute. When major policy moves through reconciliation, it takes fiscal form, and fiscal form is self-executing in a way that programmatic form is not. The tax credit needs no annual appropriation; the authorization needs one every year. A procedural shift that was adopted for passability therefore has a durability side effect: the policies that survive the Byrd rule are disproportionately the policies that survive neglect. The 2022 statute's credits will flow without further votes precisely because they had to be written as credits to pass at all. The 2005 statute, passed the hard way through regular order, got the easier instrument to kill by neglect. Procedure giveth, and procedure taketh away.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the difference between the Energy Policy Act and the 2022 climate law?

The differences run along four axes. Coalition: the 2005 statute passed with substantial bipartisan majorities, 275 to 156 in the House and 74 to 26 in the Senate, while the 2022 law passed on party lines, 51 to 50 in the Senate with the Vice President breaking the tie and 220 to 207 in the House, with zero minority-party votes. Procedure: the earlier measure moved through regular order and a conference committee, while the later one moved through reconciliation, which screened out non-budgetary provisions under the Byrd rule. Instrument: the 2005 statute mostly authorized programs that needed later appropriations, while the 2022 law created uncapped tax credits that execute themselves. Durability: authorized programs can be starved by appropriators without new legislation, while tax credits continue until repealed, though reconciliation provisions can be repealed through reconciliation by a bare majority.

### Q: Which was bipartisan, the Energy Policy Act or the 2022 law?

The Energy Policy Act of 2005 was the bipartisan one. The House agreed to the conference report on July 28, 2005 by 275 to 156, with 75 Democrats voting yes and 31 Republicans voting no, and the Senate agreed the next day by 74 to 26. That spread across both parties reflects a coalition assembled through provision trading, with something for fossil, nuclear, renewable and efficiency constituencies. The 2022 law was not bipartisan by any vote-based measure. Every Democrat voted yes and every Republican voted no in both chambers, and the Senate result required the Vice President's tie-breaking vote. The contrast matters for the durability analysis, but it does not settle it: bipartisanship spread political ownership across parties while producing a internally contradictory statute, and the party-line vote produced a coherent program with concentrated ownership.

### Q: Did the Energy Policy Act use tax credits like the 2022 law?

Both statutes used the tax code, but in very different ways. The 2005 measure included a set of modest, time-limited tax incentives with varying expirations and per-facility caps, alongside its larger authorizing titles; the tax title's net cost over ten years was 11.525 billion dollars, a small fraction of the statute's ambitions. The 2022 law made tax credits the main instrument rather than a sidecar: it created and extended an uncapped suite of clean-energy credits spanning electricity production, investment, hydrogen, carbon sequestration, advanced manufacturing, vehicles and efficiency, plus elective pay under section 6417 and transferability under section 6418 so that entities with little tax liability could still benefit. The 2005 credits needed no appropriation either, but their caps and sunsets limited their scale, while the 2022 credits scale automatically with qualifying activity.

### Q: Is the Energy Policy Act or the 2022 law harder to repeal?

The answer depends on what kind of repeal is meant, which is why the article treats durability as two-sided. The 2022 law is harder to kill by neglect: its uncapped credits need no annual appropriation, so appropriators cannot starve them by inaction, and ending them requires Congress to pass a repeal. The 2005 statute's programs are easier to kill by neglect: any authorized program that never receives an appropriation spends nothing, and that outcome requires no vote at all. Against active repeal, the ranking flips in form if not in likelihood. A tax provision enacted through reconciliation can be repealed through reconciliation by a bare Senate majority, so the 2022 credits are procedurally exposed to a future majority in a way that a broadly bipartisan authorization is not. The article's verdict is that neglect is the more common threat, which favors the 2022 design.

### Q: Why did the 2022 law use reconciliation and the Energy Policy Act did not?

The 2022 majority used reconciliation because it allowed Senate passage with 51 votes instead of 60, and the majority did not have 60 votes. The FY2022 budget resolution, S. Con. Res. 14, carried reconciliation directives for the ten-year window from FY2022 through FY2031, and the climate program was drafted to fit that vehicle. The 2005 majority did not need reconciliation because it had votes to spare under regular order: 74 in the Senate and 275 in the House on the conference report. Regular order also imposed no constraint on the form of the policy, which is why the 2005 statute could mix mandates, loan programs, royalty provisions and tax incentives freely. Reconciliation's Byrd rule, by contrast, screened out provisions whose budget effects were merely incidental to their policy effects, which is why the 2022 climate title was built almost entirely from fiscal instruments like tax credits, fees and grants.

### Q: Which delivered more clean energy, the Energy Policy Act or the 2022 law?

This question cannot be answered from the enactment record alone, and an honest comparison says so. Measuring delivered clean energy requires post-enactment deployment data, which this comparison excludes by design, so any ranking would rest on evidence outside the comparison's scope. What the statutes themselves show is a difference in design intent and scale mechanism. The 2005 statute combined a renewable fuel standard requiring 7.5 billion gallons of ethanol by 2012 with nuclear and efficiency provisions, but it also subsidized fossil fuel production, so its net direction was mixed. The 2022 law's climate title pointed in a single direction, with uncapped credits designed to scale clean electricity, manufacturing, vehicles and efficiency in proportion to qualifying investment. The scored estimates reflect the design difference: the 2022 credits were scored in the hundreds of billions over ten years, while the 2005 tax title netted 11.525 billion dollars, with most of the earlier statute's programs left unscored pending appropriations.

### Q: Does the Energy Policy Act authorize spending while the 2022 law creates credits?

Essentially yes, with qualifications on both sides. The 2005 statute was predominantly an authorizing measure: it created programs, authorized appropriations for them, established Title XVII loan guarantee authority, and added modest time-limited tax incentives, and nearly everything it authorized needed a later appropriations act to become real money. The Congressional Budget Office never completed an estimate of its discretionary costs for exactly that reason. The 2022 law's climate title was predominantly a tax-expenditure measure: uncapped credits that qualifying taxpayers claim on returns, plus elective pay and transferability, none of which require annual appropriations. The qualifications are that the 2005 statute did include some tax provisions and some direct spending, and the 2022 law did include some grants and fees alongside the credits. But the dominant instrument of each is as the question states, and that is the structural difference the article's claim rests on.

### Q: Which should a student study first, the Energy Policy Act or the 2022 law?

Study the 2005 statute first. Authorization, appropriation, conference committees and loan guarantee authority are the older concepts in federal energy law, and the 2005 measure displays them in their classic form: a big authorizing bill, built through regular order, that means little until appropriators fund it. Once that vocabulary is secure, the 2022 law's machinery becomes vivid by contrast: uncapped credits that need no appropriation, elective pay that reaches tax-exempt entities, transferability that creates a market in credits, and a reconciliation vehicle constrained by the Byrd rule. Reading them in this order also teaches the article's central lesson in the right sequence, because the student first learns what it means for a program to depend on annual funding and then sees what it means for a policy to execute itself. The study-path section above orders the companion readings the same way, from statutory anatomy to procedural mechanics.

### Q: What does an uncapped tax credit mean for the federal budget?

It means the fiscal cost is determined by taxpayer behavior rather than by a congressional appropriation. A capped credit sets a maximum total, and once claims reach the cap, the program stops or waits for Congress to raise the limit. An uncapped credit has no aggregate limit: every taxpayer who meets the statutory eligibility criteria may claim it, and the Treasury must honor every valid claim. The budgetary consequence is that the cost scales with deployment, which is why estimates for the 2022 law's climate provisions range from the official 369-billion-dollar ten-year shorthand to independent estimates above one trillion dollars under higher uptake assumptions. For budget scorekeepers, uncapped credits are conventionally estimated on assumed uptake over the ten-year window, but the estimate is a forecast, not a ceiling. For the durability comparison, the key point is that no appropriator can hold the total down by declining to fund it.

### Q: What happens when Congress authorizes a program but never appropriates money for it?

Nothing happens, which is precisely the point. Authorization creates legal authority for the government to obligate funds, but appropriation provides the funds themselves, and the two steps are handled by different committees under different procedures. A program with an authorization but no appropriation is a legal shell: the agency may have statutory permission to act, but it has no money to act with, so it spends nothing. This outcome requires no vote to kill the program and no debate on its merits; it results from the ordinary appropriations cycle, in which twelve annual bills divide tight allocations among competing programs and the path of least resistance is to leave an unfamiliar or unloved program unfunded. The 2005 statute's scoring history illustrates the mechanism: the Congressional Budget Office never completed an estimate of its discretionary costs because those costs depended on appropriations acts that had not been written, and every program it authorized faced that annual test.

### Q: What procedural steps would repeal of a reconciliation tax provision require?

Repeal would require new legislation, and the most likely vehicle would be reconciliation itself. The steps run as follows: Congress adopts a budget resolution containing reconciliation directives to the tax-writing committees; the committees report legislation repealing or curtailing the credits; the Senate considers the bill under reconciliation's expedited procedures, which limit debate and allow passage with 51 votes; and the provisions must survive the Byrd rule, which they generally would, because repealing a revenue provision is itself a budgetary provision rather than a merely incidental one. None of this is automatic. It requires a majority willing to spend floor time and political capital on taking credits away, and by the time repeal is attempted, the credits will typically have created constituencies, such as businesses that invested around them and workers hired for qualifying projects, that lobby against repeal. The procedure is available; the politics are the constraint.

### Q: How did the Byrd rule shape the form of the 2022 law?

The Byrd rule, at Section 313 of the Congressional Budget Act, treats a reconciliation provision as extraneous when its budgetary effects are merely incidental to its non-budgetary policy effects, and the Senate Parliamentarian enforces the test through points of order. That constraint pushed the drafters toward fiscal instruments, because a tax credit's budget effect is the point of the provision rather than an incident of it. The clearest illustration is the Clean Electricity Performance Program, a grant-and-penalty scheme for utility clean-electricity targets that was widely described during 2021 drafting as a reconciliation-friendly substitute for a clean electricity standard. Senate Republicans announced plans to challenge it under the Byrd rule, and Senator Joe Manchin separately refused to support it, so the White House removed it in October 2021 before any parliamentarian ruling. What survived was a climate title built almost entirely from tax credits, fees and grants that could pass the Byrd test, which is why the measure remembered as a climate law is mechanically a tax law.

### Q: What happened to the Clean Electricity Performance Program?

The Clean Electricity Performance Program was a grant-and-penalty scheme for electric utilities, paying them for hitting clean-electricity targets and penalizing them for missing those targets, designed during the 2021 drafting of the reconciliation vehicle that became H.R. 5376. Contemporary reporting described it as a reconciliation-friendly substitute for a straightforward clean electricity standard, and Senate Republicans announced plans to challenge it under the Byrd rule. What ended it was Senator Joe Manchin's refusal: he told the White House he would not support paying utilities for decarbonization they would pursue on their own, and the White House rewrote the bill to exclude the program in October 2021, before any parliamentarian ruling. There was no Byrd-rule ruling striking the CEPP, and drafters should never describe one.

### Q: Did bipartisanship make the 2005 statute more durable than the 2022 law?

Not in the sense that matters for survival. Bipartisanship gave the 2005 statute broad political ownership: 75 House Democrats voted yes and only 31 House Republicans voted no, so both parties had fingerprints on the measure. That kind of ownership can complicate later repeal efforts, because repeal would mean attacking a program one's own party helped create. But the 2005 statute's dominant instrument was authorization, and authorization is vulnerable to a threat that bipartisanship does not address: passive starvation by appropriators, which requires no vote, no debate and no majority. A program that both parties supported at enactment can still be left unfunded year after year. The 2022 law had no bipartisan ownership at all, which is a genuine vulnerability to active repeal, but its self-executing credits are immune to the quiet death that most often kills programs. Durability, in this comparison, turns more on instrument than on coalition.

### Q: Why do cost estimates for the 2022 law's climate provisions vary so widely?

The estimates vary because the credits are uncapped, so every estimate is a forecast of uptake rather than a reading of a fixed total. The official score at enactment, from the Congressional Budget Office and the Joint Committee on Taxation, put the energy and climate provisions at a shorthand of about 369 billion dollars over fiscal years 2022 through 2031, using conventional ten-year uptake assumptions. Later analysts modeled higher deployment and got larger numbers: Goldman Sachs estimated about 1.2 trillion dollars of incentives by 2032, Credit Suisse about 800 billion dollars, the Brookings Institution's US-REGEN modeling 365 billion to 1.19 trillion dollars through 2031 with a central estimate of 902 billion dollars, and the Joint Committee on Taxation 515 billion dollars for the tax credits over 2023 through 2033. None of these are arithmetic errors; they are different assumptions about how much qualifying investment taxpayers will undertake. The range itself is the lesson: uncapped instruments make fiscal cost a function of behavior.

### Q: Why is there no single official cost figure for the 2005 Act?

Because the Congressional Budget Office never completed the estimate. Its July 27, 2005 preliminary letter on the conference agreement scored direct spending effects, 2.2 billion dollars over fiscal years 2006 through 2010 and 1.6 billion over 2006 through 2015, and revenue effects, minus 7.9 billion over 2005 through 2010 and minus 12.3 billion over 2005 through 2015, but it stated explicitly that the estimate of potential discretionary costs was not complete. The bulk of the act's program spending depended on future appropriations and was therefore unscored at enactment. The statute contains labeled authorization examples, such as 1.8 billion dollars over three years for weatherization and 2.25 billion over five fiscal years for nuclear research and development, but authorizations are permissions, not expenditures, and no official total exists. This article does not invent one.

### Q: Which statute created loan guarantees for energy projects?

The 2005 statute did. Title XVII of the Energy Policy Act of 2005 established the Department of Energy's loan guarantee program for advanced energy technologies, giving the department authority to guarantee loans for qualifying projects. Like the rest of the statute's authorizing titles, the program depended on later appropriations and on agency implementation to become operational, which places it squarely on the authorization side of the article's central distinction. The 2022 law did not create a loan guarantee program; its climate title worked through tax credits, elective pay and transferability instead, with a smaller set of grants. The comparison is instructive because loan guarantees and tax credits are two different answers to the same financing question. A guarantee needs an agency to evaluate applications, obligate credit authority and monitor the portfolio, all of which requires appropriated administrative resources; a credit needs only a qualifying taxpayer and a tax return.

### Q: How did the two statutes treat fossil fuel production?

The 2005 statute actively supported it. Alongside the renewable fuel standard and the nuclear provisions, the measure included royalty relief, refinery expensing and other provisions that lowered the cost of fossil fuel production, which is why the article describes the statute as subsidizing fossil production, nuclear power and renewables simultaneously. That mix was the price of the bipartisan coalition: fossil-state legislators had votes the drafters needed, and the provisions for their constituencies were part of the trade. The 2022 law took a different approach. Its climate title directed its credits toward clean electricity, clean manufacturing, clean vehicles and efficiency, and it included a fee on methane emissions as a fiscal complement. The contrast is not an accident of drafting taste; it follows from the coalition and procedure axes. A bipartisan bill built by trades includes provisions for every constituency at the table, while a party-line reconciliation bill answers to one party's priorities and can point in a single direction.

### Q: Why did the 2005 Act go through a conference committee and the 2022 law not?

The 2005 act moved under regular order, where the House and Senate pass different versions of a bill and a conference committee reconciles them into a single text both chambers accept without further amendment. House Report 109-190 was that reconciliation, reported July 27, 2005, and agreed to within two days. The 2022 law moved through reconciliation, a different procedure entirely: the Senate passed H.R. 5376 with an amendment, and the House simply agreed to the Senate amendment on August 12, 2022, so no conference was needed. The deeper reason is the one the procedure axis states: the 2005 coalition had the votes for regular order and used its full machinery, while the 2022 majority needed the 51-vote budget path and accepted its constraints.

### Q: Is neglect or reversal the more common threat to an enacted program?

Neglect, by a wide margin, and that frequency judgment is the deciding factor in the article's verdict. Neglect is the ordinary product of the appropriations cycle: twelve annual bills, tight allocations, every program competing with every other, and the path of least resistance running toward leaving the prior year's number in place or trimming it. An authorized program can go years without funding while no vote is ever taken against it and no debate is ever held on its merits, until it exists only on paper. Reversal is an extraordinary event by comparison. It requires a majority willing to spend scarce floor time and political capital on taking something away, a legislative vehicle, and compliance with procedural constraints, all in the face of the constituencies the program has created. The asymmetry is structural, not partisan: inaction is always cheaper than action in a legislature. That is why self-executing tax expenditures, which can only be ended by action, outlast authorizations, which can be ended by inaction.
