Rajni Kothari's Congress System is the most influential framework ever proposed for the politics of independent India, and it remains the most persistently misread. In the popular telling, the phrase survives as a label, a shorthand for the decades when the Indian National Congress won election after election and no rival could displace it from office in New Delhi. In the academic telling, the framework survives as one entry in a catalogue, placed beside the typologies of Giovanni Sartori and the field studies of Myron Weiner, and cited politely before the argument moves elsewhere. Both tellings miss what Kothari actually built. The Congress System, as he stated it in Asian Survey in December 1964 and developed it in Politics in India in 1970, was not a description of who won. It was an account of how a political order worked: a four-component absorptive mechanism, in which single-party dominance, pluralism among the dominant party's own factions, the structural marginality of opposition parties, and the disciplined autonomy of regional leaders operated together so that pressures which would have ruptured other party systems were absorbed inside one. Read that way, the framework also tells us how the order ended. The verdict of 1989 did not gradually weaken the mechanism. It ended each of its four components, and with them the analytical work the framework had performed for a quarter of a century.

## The Essay of 1964 and the Book of 1970: How the Framework Was Stated

The framework arrived in two instalments, and the interval between them matters. Kothari published "The Congress 'System' in India" in Asian Survey, volume 4, number 12, in December 1964, on pages 1161 to 1173. Jawaharlal Nehru had died on 27 May 1964, barely six months earlier, and the question hanging over Indian politics was whether the order the Congress had built since 1947 was a durable structure or a personal inheritance that would dissolve with its maker. Kothari's answer was that the order was structural. He placed the word system at the centre of the argument and the word Congress inside quotation marks in his title, because his claim was that India possessed neither a one-party state nor a conventional competitive party system, but a distinct arrangement in which one party of consensus stood at the centre and parties of pressure worked at the margins, influencing policy less by defeating the Congress than by pressing upon its internal factions. The essay's timing gave the claim its edge: written at the moment of the first succession, it insisted that dominance belonged to the architecture and not to Nehru's person.

The book that followed six years later, Politics in India, published by Little, Brown of Boston in 1970 in its comparative politics series, turned the essay's insight into a full analytical architecture. By 1970 Kothari could draw on four general elections, the state-level faction studies that younger scholars were producing, and his own institutional base at the Centre for the Study of Developing Societies in Delhi, which he had founded in 1963 and which made the empirical study of Indian politics an organised scholarly enterprise rather than a commentary trade. Politics in India treated the Congress as the product of a historical sequence, a movement that had become a party and then a government without ever surrendering its claim to speak for the whole society, and it read the party's internal life, its factions, its state units, its relations with caste associations and regional elites, as the real site where Indian politics happened. The argument that elections merely ratified was that the substantive contests occurred earlier, inside the dominant party and between its factions and the interests pressing upon them.

Two features of the articulation deserve emphasis because later readings flattened both. The first is that Kothari's claim was comparative. He knew the literature on one-party states and set India apart from it deliberately: opposition parties in India were legal, organised and vocal, elections were free, and governments could and did lose in the states, as the Communist ministry in Kerala in 1957 demonstrated before its dismissal in 1959. The system's distinctiveness lay precisely in competition without alternation. W. H. Morris-Jones, working independently at the University of London, reached the neighbouring formulation in the same years and gave it a sentence that Kothari's readers have quoted ever since: India exhibited dominance coexisting with competition but without a trace of alternation. Morris-Jones developed the point in The Government and Politics of India in 1964 and in his 1967 essay "The Indian Congress Party: A Dilemma of Dominance" in Modern Asian Studies, volume 1, number 2. The convergence of two scholars with different methods on the same structural fact is part of the evidence that the framework described something real rather than something convenient.

The second feature is that Kothari wrote as an analyst of a living mechanism, not as its memorialist. He expected the system to change as the social forces it had absorbed matured, and he returned to it repeatedly: in the essays he published in Economic and Political Weekly through the late 1960s and early 1970s, in Caste in Indian Politics, the 1970 volume he edited on the traffic between caste associations and party politics, and most explicitly in "The Congress System Revisited: A Decennial Review" in Asian Survey in December 1974. That willingness to revise is sometimes used to demote the framework to a period description. The rereading offered here takes the opposite view. A framework whose author kept testing it against events is a framework with testable content, and its architectural claims, dominance, factional pluralism, opposition marginality and regional autonomy within discipline, can be checked component by component against the record of 1947 to 1989. The four components are separable in analysis but were joined in operation, and the sections that follow take them in turn before reassembling the mechanism.

One more piece of the articulation context belongs to this series' own architecture. In the four-phase organisational history traced by the pillar's hub article, Kothari's framework is the analytical instrument built for the third phase, the phase of governmental dominance that followed the movement phase and the founding phase, because [the four-phase organisational framework](/2019/10/27/indian-national-congress-long-arc-1885-2020/) reads the post-1947 Congress as an organisation whose governmental weight changed its internal physics. Kothari, who had watched the party from Jaipur and then from Delhi, supplied the vocabulary for that change. What he described was a party that had absorbed the state into its own internal bargaining, so that the boundary between party politics and public politics ran through the Congress rather than between the Congress and its rivals.

The articulation had a social mechanism as well as a party architecture, and Kothari stated it in the companion work of the same year, the volume Caste in Indian Politics, which he edited and introduced in 1970. His argument there ran against the settled assumption that caste and democratic politics were antagonists, the one destined to dissolve the other. The traffic, he argued, ran in both directions: politics politicised caste, drawing caste associations into electoral organisation, candidate selection and demands on the state, and caste in turn gave politics a ready-made network of communication and solidarity through which new entrants could reach the system. For the Congress System the point was structural. Caste associations were among the principal transmission belts of absorption, the channels along which demands from the countryside entered the Congress's factional bargaining and along which the party's arbitration flowed back as recognition, tickets and office for the associations' leaders. A Maratha association in Maharashtra, a Nadar association in Madras, a Kshatriya sabha in Gujarat, each could deal with the Congress as a constituency with negotiators, and the Congress could deal with each as a faction-adjacent interest to be balanced inside its own ticket distribution. The social pluralism of the country was thereby translated, association by association, into the party's internal pluralism, which is the second component's raw material. The framework's later critics who charged Kothari with describing a superstructure without foundations had not reckoned with this volume, in which the foundations are the argument.

## Component One: Dominance That Was Structural, Not Arithmetic

The first component is the one every reader knows and most readers stop at. The Congress won 364 of 489 Lok Sabha seats at the first general election of 1951 to 1952, on 45 percent of the vote. It won 371 of 494 seats in 1957 on 47.8 percent, and 361 of 494 in 1962 on 44.7 percent. Even in 1967, its worst result of the period, it took 283 of 520 seats on 40.8 percent and formed the government in New Delhi. Those figures, from the Election Commission's statistical reports, establish dominance as a fact. Kothari's argument was that dominance was also a structure, and the difference between the fact and the structure is the difference between the popular reading and his. A contingent majority can be assembled by a popular leader, a favourable electoral cycle or a divided opposition, and it dissolves when the conditions change. The Congress majority had a different source. It rested on an organisation that reached from the All India Congress Committee down through the Pradesh Congress Committees to district and block committees in nearly every part of the country, an inheritance of [the post-1920 mass-membership architecture](/2019/11/12/gandhi-reorganisation-congress-1920/) that no rival could replicate in a generation, and on the legitimating capital of the freedom movement, which made the Congress the default party of nationhood for voters who agreed on little else.

The structural character of the dominance showed in three ways that arithmetic alone cannot express. First, the Congress majority was socially comprehensive. The party contained, within one organisation, landlords and tenants, industrialists and trade unionists, upper castes and the Harijan Sevak Sangh's constituency, Hindi-speaking heartland notables and the regional elites of Madras, Bengal and Maharashtra. Kothari called it a party of consensus because its electoral coalition was not a segment of society but a cross-section of it, and a cross-section cannot be outflanked by a party that represents one segment, however intensely. Second, the dominance was reproduced at the state level. The Congress formed governments in nearly every state through the 1950s, and where it lost, as in Kerala in 1957, the loss was treated by all parties as an interruption of a pattern rather than the pattern's negation. Third, the dominance survived its founder. Nehru's death in May 1964 was followed not by fragmentation but by two managed successions, in 1964 and 1966, executed by the party's own regional leadership acting as a syndicate of power brokers. The election of 1967 cut the majority, and the analysis of [the first general election of 1951 to 1952](/2019/01/30/first-general-election-1951-1952-india/) shows how deep the original implantation ran: the machinery that Sukumar Sen's Election Commission administered returned Congress majorities state after state because the party's organisation matched the country's administrative geography almost exactly.

Kothari's comparative point followed from this. In Sartori's later typology in Parties and Party Systems of 1976, India fell into the predominant-party category, a system in which the same party wins an absolute majority of seats over a sustained run of elections while competition remains genuine. Kothari, writing a decade earlier, had already seen what the typology missed. Predominance in India was not merely a property of election results. It was a property of the space between elections, where the dominant party's internal pluralism performed the work that alternation performs elsewhere. That is why the first component cannot be assessed alone. A party that wins three-quarters of the seats on less than half the vote, as the Congress did in 1952 and 1957, holds a majority that first-past-the-post arithmetic inflates; a party that also contains its own opposition, disciplines its own regions and defines the terrain on which outside parties must compete holds something more durable, a system. The remaining three components describe that something.

There is a harder test of the structural reading, and Kothari's framework passes it. If dominance had been arithmetic only, the decline of the Congress vote share from 47.8 percent in 1957 to 40.8 percent in 1967 should have ended the system in 1967, when the seat majority thinned to 283 and eight state governments fell to opposition coalitions. The system did not end in 1967, as the later sections show, because the other components kept working for another two decades. Arithmetic weakened; the architecture bent and held. That distinction, between a majority and a mechanism, is the analytical property that separates Kothari's framework from the label it is usually reduced to, and it disciplines everything that follows in this rereading.

## Component Two: Factions as the System's Internal Opposition

The second component is the framework's most original claim, and the one that converts dominance from a result into a mechanism. Kothari argued that the Congress contained within itself a plurality of factions, organised around leaders, regions, castes and shades of ideology, and that these factions competed for office, patronage and policy with a vigour that made the internal contest the principal arena of Indian politics. The claim had a precise implication. In a system with a dominant party and marginal opponents, the citizen who wished to oppose a policy, remove a minister or advance an interest did not need to defeat the Congress. The citizen needed a faction inside the Congress. Opposition thus flowed into the ruling party and was processed there, and the party's internal pluralism supplied the substantive debate that the weakness of external opposition would otherwise have denied the polity. Factionalism, treated in the journalistic idiom of the time as a pathology of Indian parties, became in Kothari's hands a functional element of the system, the substitute for alternation.

The evidence for the component was visible from the first years of independence. Between 1947 and 1950 the Congress held, in Jawaharlal Nehru and Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, two leaders whose followings inside the organisation were distinct and whose views on economic policy, on the place of religion in public life and on the treatment of the former princely order diverged in ways that party members debated openly. When Purushottam Das Tandon, identified with the Patel wing, was elected Congress president at the Nashik session of September 1950 against Nehru's preference, the episode demonstrated that the party presidency could be won against the Prime Minister's wishes by organising delegates, a fact about the system that no account of a monolithic Congress can explain. Tandon's resignation in September 1951 and Nehru's assumption of the presidency closed the episode, but the pattern it revealed, arbitration among organised internal forces, recurred at every level. At the state level the pattern was the system's daily life: in Uttar Pradesh the factions around Govind Ballabh Pant, Charan Singh and others contested the chief ministership and the Pradesh Congress Committee as rival governments in waiting; in Bombay and then Maharashtra the organisation balanced Maratha, Gujarati and other blocs; in Madras, K. Kamaraj built a machine whose internal discipline still left room for competing lieutenants to appeal over his head to the Working Committee in Delhi.

Equally instructive is the case that seems to contradict the component. The Congress Socialists, organised inside the party since 1934, left in 1948 when the Congress amended its constitution to bar members of other political formations, and by 1952 they had merged with the Kisan Mazdoor Praja Party to form the Praja Socialist Party. The exit is sometimes read as proof that the Congress could not absorb organised dissent. Kothari's framework reads it more exactly. The system absorbed unorganised and semi-organised plurality, the faction, the caste association, the regional interest, and it expelled only the attempt to build a rival party inside the party, because that attempt denied the premise on which absorption rested, that the Congress was the single arena. The distinction mattered for the opposition's structure, as the next component shows: parties outside remained parties of pressure partly because the system tolerated every form of opposition except a counter-organisation within its own ranks, and drew the line there.

Factional pluralism also did the system's ideological work. The ideological spectrum of Indian politics in the 1950s, from the socialist left of the party to the conservative right associated with figures such as Rajendra Prasad's sympathisers and later the Swatantra defectors, was largely represented inside the Congress before it was represented outside it. When the All India Congress Committee adopted the socialist pattern of society at Avadi in January 1955, the resolution was carried through an internal debate in which the party's right wing registered its reservations and stayed, expecting to contest implementation through the factions that controlled state governments and ministries. A voter or an interest that lost at Avadi did not need to found a party. It needed to wait for the next internal round. Kothari saw that this waiting, repeated across thousands of disputes, was a stabilising technology: it converted every grievance into a claim on the ruling party's internal process, payable in the currency of factional support, and it postponed the founding of rival organisations by making rivalry inside the Congress more promising than opposition outside it.

The component had a cost, which Kothari recorded and which later mattered. A system that processes conflict inside one party makes the party's own health the health of the polity. The factional balances of the 1950s were held by leaders of stature who treated arbitration as a craft: the Working Committee and the Central Parliamentary Board functioned as courts of appeal, and the syndicate of state leaders who managed the successions of 1964 and 1966 were themselves products of the factional system, men who had risen through it and believed in its rules. The arrangement presupposed that defeated factions would accept arbitration and remain inside. So long as that presupposition held, pluralism within one party could do the work of a party system. The stress tests of 1967 to 1977 examined what happened when it began to fail, and they are taken up after the remaining components are in place.

The arbitration had machinery, and the machinery deserves a paragraph of its own, because pluralism without procedure is merely noise. The Congress Working Committee, the party's highest executive body, was composed substantially of the system's principals, the prime minister, senior ministers, the party president and the great state leaders, so that its meetings were negotiations among the factions' heads rather than instructions issued to them. The Central Parliamentary Board cleared candidate selections for Parliament and the state assemblies, and its clearance process was the clearing house where factional claims were weighed, balanced and traded: a faction denied a ministry could be compensated with tickets, a state leader denied a policy could be compensated with a Pradesh presidency. Organisational elections, held for the party's committees at district, Pradesh and all-India levels, were genuinely contested through the 1950s and into the 1960s, and they functioned as periodic censuses of factional strength whose results the arbitration then respected. A defeated Pradesh president had lost an election, not a purge, and the distinction kept him inside. The Congress presidency itself rotated among figures of independent standing, Acharya Kripalani's successors through U. N. Dhebar, Neelam Sanjeeva Reddy, Kamaraj and S. Nijalingappa, men who could arbitrate because they were not the prime minister's clerks. The decay of each element can be dated: organisational elections became irregular after the split of 1969 and lapsed for long stretches thereafter, the Parliamentary Board's clearing function yielded to nomination by the leader's secretariat, and the presidency became an office the leader filled. The second component did not fade. Its machinery was dismantled piece by piece, and the dismantling preceded the ending that the machinery's absence made inevitable.

## Component Three: Opposition Parties Kept at the Margin by Structure

The third component completes the triangle. The opposition parties of the post-Independence decades were real, competitive and, Kothari argued, structurally marginal. The Praja Socialist Party, the Communist Party of India, the Bharatiya Jana Sangh founded by Syama Prasad Mookerjee in October 1951, the Swatantra Party founded by C. Rajagopalachari in 1959, and regional formations such as the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam in Madras existed in a political space whose dimensions the Congress defined. Their marginality had three sources, and Kothari's claim was that each source was structural rather than accidental. The electoral source was plain: on the figures set out under the first component, no opposition party in the elections from 1952 to 1962 won more than 29 Lok Sabha seats, the Communist tally of that year, and a party with 29 seats in a house of 494 cannot credibly bid for national government. The organisational source was equally plain: outside a few strongholds, Kerala and West Bengal for the Communists, the Hindi belt districts for the Jana Sangh, opposition parties lacked the district-level machinery that converts votes into a governing alternative. The third source was the subtlest and the most Kotharian. Opposition parties were marginal because the system gave them a function that did not require them to win: they acted as parties of pressure, articulating interests and ideologies, keeping issues alive, and feeding their demands into the Congress through sympathetic factions. A party of pressure that begins to win ceases to be a party of pressure; the system's stability rested on the opposition accepting, in practice, the pressure role.

### Why did opposition parties remain marginal under the Congress System?

The opposition parties remained marginal because the structure denied them the three assets a governing alternative needs. They lacked the seats, holding only a small fraction of the Lok Sabha. They lacked organisation across most districts. And they lacked a motive to unite, since each could press its demands through sympathetic Congress factions more cheaply.

The pressure role was not impotence, and Kothari was careful on the point. Opposition criticism in Parliament and the press, the Communist victory in Kerala in 1957, the Jana Sangh's municipal strength in Delhi, the Swatantra Party's capture of the business and former-princely constituency after 1959, all registered in Congress calculations. The mechanism of registration is what the framework specifies. When an opposition party raised an issue, a minimum wage demand, a language grievance, a price complaint, the issue entered the Congress's internal debate, where a faction could adopt it to strengthen its hand against a rival faction. The opposition thus influenced policy out of proportion to its seats, while remaining incapable of forming a government. Morris-Jones, in The Government and Politics of India in 1964, described the same arrangement from the opposition's side: parties outside the Congress performed the functions of criticism and ventilation within a system whose centre of gravity lay elsewhere. The arrangement also explains a puzzle of the period, the opposition's chronic fragmentation. Unity against the Congress was repeatedly urged and repeatedly failed, in the socialist movement's splits and the stillborn united fronts of the 1950s, because the parties of pressure competed with each other for access to the dominant party's factions as much as they competed with the Congress for votes. Fragmentation was not a failure of opposition leadership. It was the rational product of the structure Kothari described.

Myron Weiner's field studies supplied the micro-evidence. In The Politics of Scarcity, published in 1962, and in Party Building in a New Nation of 1967, Weiner showed how district-level Congress organisations mediated between the administration and local interests, leaving opposition parties to protest decisions they could not shape except by finding a channel into the Congress machine. The district was where marginality was manufactured: a cultivator with a grievance against a canal officer or a cooperative society approached the Congress notable, not the opposition worker, because the notable could act. Stanley Kochanek's The Congress Party of India, published in 1968, documented the same mediation from inside the organisation, tracing how the party's structure linked governmental and organisational wings. The scholarly convergence across methods, Kothari's system analysis, Morris-Jones's institutionalism, Weiner's fieldwork, Kochanek's organisational study, is why the marginality component is the least contested part of the framework. What later scholarship contested was the framework's dynamism, its account of its own ending, and that contest is adjudicated in the closing sections of this article.

One implication of the component deserves isolation, because it governs the dating of the system's end. Marginality was structural, which meant it could end only structurally. An opposition party winning an extra twenty seats, or a charismatic leader embarrassing the government, would leave the component intact. The component ended when opposition parties could form governments, at the centre as well as in the states, and when interests stopped routing their demands through Congress factions because the Congress no longer monopolised the channels of action. By that test the component survived 1967, when opposition coalitions took office in eight states but the centre and the channels held, and it did not survive 1989, when a coalition of former opposition parties formed the government in Delhi and the Congress itself became one claimant among several. The framework thus contains its own ending test, stated in advance by the logic of its components. Few analytical frameworks in political science supply so clean a criterion for their own expiry.

## Component Four: Regional Leaders, Autonomous Within Discipline

The fourth component is the one that kept a centralised party from becoming a brittle one. The Congress of the 1950s and 1960s was run, in the states, by leaders who were powers in their own right: K. Kamaraj in Madras, Bidhan Chandra Roy in West Bengal, Yashwantrao Chavan in Bombay and then Maharashtra, Partap Singh Kairon in Punjab, Mohanlal Sukhadia in Rajasthan, S. Nijalingappa in Mysore, Morarji Desai in Bombay before his move to Delhi. Kothari's formulation was autonomy within discipline. The regional leaders controlled candidate selection in their states in practice, distributed patronage through their own networks, settled their own factional disputes and spoke for their regions in Delhi. In return they accepted the discipline of the national party: they delivered their state units to the Congress's national decisions, accepted arbitration by the Working Committee when disputes escaped state-level settlement, and did not carry their quarrels into rival organisations. The bargain was the federalism of the party system, and it mirrored the federalism of the Constitution that the party governed under.

The bargain's operations were concrete, not ceremonial. When the Central Parliamentary Board endorsed a candidate list, it did so on the recommendation of the state leadership, and a state leader who lost control of his Pradesh Congress Committee lost the substance of his power even if he kept his ministry. When the party needed to discipline its own governmental wing, it used organisational instruments wielded by regional men: the Kamaraj Plan of August 1963, under which senior ministers resigned to work for the organisation, was devised by Kamaraj himself and executed through the party's own hierarchy, an act of self-discipline that no external opposition could have imposed. The instrument discussed in [the Kamaraj Plan of 1963](/2019/02/27/kamaraj-plan-1963-congress/) shows the component at work, because the Plan's resignations were accepted selectively by Nehru in consultation with the very regional leaders whose autonomy the Plan reaffirmed. And when the national leadership fell vacant, in May 1964 and again in January 1966, the regional leaders acting together as the syndicate chose the successor, installing Lal Bahadur Shastri and then Indira Gandhi. A party whose state bosses pick its prime ministers is not a party run from a single centre. It is a federation of baronies bound by arbitration, which is what Kothari described.

The discipline side of the bargain was equally real, and the framework loses its point if the autonomy is romanticised. The high command could and did intervene: chief ministers were replaced, state ministries were constructed and reconstructed from Delhi, and the dismissal of the Kerala ministry in July 1959, executed by the central government on the advice of the Congress president, Indira Gandhi among the advocates, showed the outer limit of regional autonomy when a non-Congress government held a state. Within the Congress family the discipline was accepted because it was reciprocal and predictable. The Working Committee arbitrated between factions rather than annihilating them; a defeated state leader expected to survive, diminished, inside the system, and to fight the next round. That expectation, that losers remained members, was the load-bearing element of the whole component. It distinguished the Congress's internal federalism from the winner-take-all habits that later centralised parties displayed, and it explains why the system's regional leaders defended an arrangement that formally subordinated them. They were subordinated to a process, not to a person, and the process left them masters in their states.

The contrast with what followed is the sharpest in the framework. In the Congress of the 1970s, after the split, chief ministers were nominated from Delhi, changed at the leader's convenience and denied independent bases, a pattern the closing sections examine as the hollowing of the fourth component. In the post-2014 Bharatiya Janata Party, chief ministers are likewise selected and replaced by a central leadership whose authority rests on the parliamentary majority and the organisation's cadre discipline, not on the state leader's own following. Both configurations are coherent ways to run a party. Neither is Kothari's fourth component, because in neither do regional leaders hold autonomy as a right of the system's constitution rather than as a revocable grant from the centre. The component's presence or absence is therefore a diagnostic: a dominant party with autonomous regional leaders inside a discipline both sides accept is operating the Congress System; a dominant party whose state leaders serve at the centre's pleasure is operating something else, whatever its seat tally.

## The Mechanism Assembled: How the Four Components Absorbed Pressure

The components earn the name system only in combination, and the combination is the framework's core claim. Dominance gave the Congress the seats and the state-level governments. Factional pluralism gave the polity an arena in which interests and ideologies could contend without defeating the government. Opposition marginality channelled external pressure into that arena, because parties of pressure found their demands taken up by Congress factions competing for advantage. Regional autonomy within discipline distributed the work of absorption across the federation, so that a grievance in Madurai or Patna could be processed by leaders who knew its caste arithmetic and its factional history, under arbitration that losers accepted. The mechanism's product was governability without alternation: policies could be contested, ministers removed, state governments changed and ideologies fought over, while the party in office in Delhi endured. Kothari's word for the product was consensus, and the party of consensus was the mechanism's name for itself.

Each row names a component, the way it operated across the decades from 1947 to 1989, and the manner of its ending in 1989, because the map's purpose is double: it summarises the architecture and it registers the framework's ending test, component by component.

### What are the four components of the Congress System?

The four components are single-party dominance, pluralism among the dominant party's own factions, the structural marginality of opposition parties, and the autonomy of regional leaders held inside national party discipline. Kothari presented them as one absorptive mechanism: pressures that would rupture other party systems entered the Congress and were settled among its factions and regions.

The mechanism's operation can be traced in a single episode. Consider the language question that ran through the 1950s and 1960s, the demand for linguistic states and the resistance to Hindi imposition from Madras. A unitary dominant party might have imposed a settlement and reaped a secessionist politics. The Congress System processed the question through every component in turn. Dominance meant the Congress governed both in Delhi and in most of the states where the question burned. Factional pluralism meant the party contained the question's partisans on both sides, the Hindi advocates of the northern units and the regional-language advocates of the southern and eastern units, and their contest inside the Working Committee and the Parliamentary Board produced the Official Languages Act of 1963 and its 1967 amendment as arbitrated outcomes. Opposition marginality meant the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, the loudest external voice, pressed its demand into the system as pressure, winning Madras in 1967 but framing its victory inside the federation rather than outside it, and Annadurai's announcement of 1963 abandoning the Dravida Nadu demand registered the absorption in a single act. Regional autonomy meant the settlement was administered by state leaders, Kamaraj in Madras until 1963, whose local authority made the centre's arbitration acceptable. No component alone explains the outcome. The four together describe a machine for converting potentially system-breaking demands into intra-system bargains, which is the service Kothari claimed the Congress System performed for the republic.

Scholars who teach the framework often redraw Table 1 as classroom slides, and a browser-based [PPT viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) opens such a deck without local software, which is a fitting fate for a map designed to be cited. The map's analytical point, however, is not pedagogical. By stating the ending of each component as a dated event of 1989 rather than as a drift, the table commits the framework to a falsifiable position. If the components ended at different times, or if some survived 1989 intact, the definitive-ending claim fails. The remaining sections take up that commitment: the stress tests of 1967 to 1977, which bent the components without ending them, the verdict of 1989, which ended all four, and the two objections, that Kothari meant description rather than architecture, and that post-2014 India rebuilt the system under new management.

## The System in the States: Absorption Below the Centre

A framework built on national election results would deserve the label reading. Kothari's was built substantially on the states, and the state evidence is where the mechanism's operation is most visible, because absorption is a daily practice before it is a national outcome. The Centre for the Study of Developing Societies, which Kothari founded in Delhi in 1963, made the empirical study of state politics one of its founding enterprises, and the state studies of the 1960s, including the essays collected in Weiner's State Politics in India of 1968, supplied the framework with its working material: district Congress committees mediating between cultivators and the administration, Pradesh Congress Committees functioning as parliaments of regional interests, chief ministers balancing caste blocs in their cabinets with the care of coalition managers. The Congress System was not imposed on the states from Delhi. It was assembled in the states and summarised in Delhi, which is why its fourth component, regional autonomy within discipline, carries as much weight in the framework as the first.

Consider the mechanism at work in Madras under K. Kamaraj, chief minister from 1954 to 1963. Kamaraj governed a state where the Congress faced a rising Dravidian movement with a separatist past, a strong Communist organisation in the industrial districts and a caste politics of great complexity. The absorptive method handled each pressure in kind. The Dravidian demand was answered by incorporation, Tamil cultural pride honoured inside the Congress's own idiom, the state's Congress organisation Tamilised under a chief minister of unimpeachable regional standing, until the space for a separatist politics narrowed to the electoralism the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam eventually chose. The Communist pressure was answered by welfare and land measures that competed for the same constituency, administered through Congress-controlled local bodies that made the party the visible author of benefits. Internal Congress dissent was answered by factional management, Kamaraj's lieutenants rotated, balanced and arbitrated in a continuing internal politics that substituted for the opposition the state-level system lacked. When Kamaraj left the chief ministership in 1963 under his own Plan, he left a state the Congress held comfortably, and the machine he built won Madras again in 1967 for his successor's party organisation even as the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam took the government amid the national anti-Congress wave, a sequence that shows both the mechanism's strength in the man and its dependence on the architecture he personally operated.

Uttar Pradesh shows the mechanism's factional face. The state's Congress contained, across the 1950s, organised followings attached to Govind Ballabh Pant, to the organisational wing around the Pradesh Congress Committee, to Charan Singh's agrarian constituency and to the socialist-leaning younger members, and the state's politics consisted substantially of their contests over the chief ministership, over candidate selection and over land policy. The contests were not decorative. The zamindari abolition settlement, the consolidation of holdings legislation and the community development programme all passed through factional bargaining before they reached the assembly, and interests that lost in the assembly could reopen their case in the Pradesh Congress Committee or before the Working Committee in Delhi. A cultivator caste pressing for a higher procurement price, a teachers' association seeking a pay revision, a sugar-mill constituency resisting a levy, each learned the same lesson: the road to the government ran through the Congress's internal geography, and the toll was factional allegiance. That lesson, taught in every district of the largest state, is the micro-foundation of the third component. Opposition parties in Uttar Pradesh, the socialists with their distinguished leadership and the Jana Sangh with its urban and trading-caste base, could embarrass the government and win municipalities; they could not become the road, because the road was already built and tolled by the ruling party's factions.

The state evidence also corrects a metropolitan misunderstanding of the framework, the idea that absorption meant passivity, a sponge soaking up whatever politics offered. The state machines absorbed actively, by co-optation of leaders, by timing of concessions, by the deliberate promotion of counterweights, a rising caste's leader advanced against an entrenched one, a district faction funded against the Pradesh president's candidate, and the activity required the constant arbitration that the Working Committee and the Parliamentary Board supplied. Arbitration is the component the label reading cannot see and the architectural reading cannot do without, because arbitration is what made pluralism safe for dominance. Where arbitration failed, in the Congress's Bihar and Andhra organisations at intervals in the 1960s, the system locally produced the pathologies of the post-system decades, defections, ministry instability, the direct appeal from faction to electorate. Kothari's model accommodates the failures as evidence about the mechanism's conditions: absorption is a practice that must be performed by skilled arbiters holding a shared expectation that the game continues inside. The stress tests that follow examined what happened when the arbiters at the centre stopped performing it.

## The Stress Tests, 1967 to 1977: Bending Without Breaking

The decade from 1967 to 1977 subjected every component to the severest pressure of the period, and the framework's claim depends on reading that decade correctly. The popular reading treats 1967 as the beginning of the end: the Congress lost its majority in eight states, its Lok Sabha tally fell to 283, its vote share slid to 40.8 percent, and the defections that followed, christened by the Haryana legislator Gaya Lal's repeated crossings in 1967, made a byword of floor-crossing. The academic reading treats the same decade as a long transition in which the system decayed by stages. The architectural reading defended here is different. Between 1967 and 1977 the components were damaged, one of them gravely, but the mechanism kept performing its defining operation, the absorption of pressure inside the dominant party or the recovery of dominance after a shock, and the proof is that the system's crisis decade ended not with a new system but with the old one's restoration in altered form. The ending, when it came in 1989, had a different signature.

Take the tests in order. The election of 1967 struck the first component and the fourth. Dominance thinned at the centre and broke in eight states, where Samyukta Vidhayak Dal coalitions of opposition parties took office, and regional autonomy changed its meaning where the regional leaders by then led opposition governments rather than Congress baronies. Yet the centre held, Indira Gandhi formed a government, and the opposition coalitions in the states collapsed within two years under their own contradictions, most of them built from defections that the absorptive system had trained politicians to practise inside parties rather than between them. Kamaraj's own defeat at Virudhunagar in 1967, at the hands of a Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam candidate, marked the depth of the shock; the syndicate's survival as the party's arbiter marked its limit. The system bent. It did not break, because the opposition remained incapable of holding the states it had won, and incapacity to govern is precisely what marginality means.

The split of 1969 struck the second component, and struck it hardest. When the syndicate and Indira Gandhi fought over the presidential election of August 1969, over bank nationalisation and over the party's direction, the dispute was not arbitrated to a settlement that losers accepted. It was resolved by schism, the expulsion of the Prime Minister by the party president S. Nijalingappa in November 1969 and the division of the Congress into Congress (O) and Congress (R). The episode is treated in its own right in [the 1969 split's structural-cultural rupture](/2019/03/26/1969-congress-split-decisive-moment/), and its organisational sequel belongs to the next article in this pillar; for the framework, the question is what the schism did to the mechanism. A faction had exited rather than accept arbitration, the first time at the national level since the socialists' departure of 1948, and this time the exiting faction carried the government with it. Factional pluralism as Kothari described it depended on the expectation that defeated factions remained inside to fight the next round. After 1969, the expectation was damaged. The Congress (R) that won the election of 1971 with 352 seats was a party whose internal pluralism had been replaced by loyalty to a leader, and its factions behaved increasingly as followings rather than as ideological and regional formations with standing of their own. The component was not ended, the 1971 victory rebuilt dominance and the party still contained multitudes, but its pluralism had changed in kind, from arbitration among standing factions to management by a leader who could overrule them.

### How did the Emergency stress-test the architecture of the Congress System?

The Emergency of 1975 to 1977 suspended the conditions on which every component rested: elections, factional contest, opposition activity and regional bargaining stopped at once. Absorption failed because its channels were closed by decree. Yet the failure registered as suspension rather than replacement, since no rival architecture governed in its place and the 1977 verdict restored electoral politics.

The Emergency itself, proclaimed on 25 June 1975 and lifted in March 1977, is the third test, and the one Kothari lived through as a critic of the regime. With opposition leaders imprisoned, the press censored and the Congress's internal life frozen under the Twenty-Point Programme and Sanjay Gandhi's parallel authority, the absorptive mechanism had nothing to absorb with: the channels were closed by decree. Kothari's writings of those years, in Economic and Political Weekly and in the seminar circuit that survived the censorship, diagnosed the stress in the framework's own terms, a party of consensus converting itself into an instrument of command, and his 1974 decennial review, published in Asian Survey months before the proclamation, had already recorded the drift, the decline of the consensus idiom, the personalisation of authority, the weakening of the organisational mediations that the 1964 essay had treated as the system's skeleton. The framework thus diagnosed its own patient's illness in advance, a point in favour of reading it as analysis rather than as celebration.

The verdict of March 1977 then performed the system's last great act of absorption, in reverse. The Congress was defeated nationally for the first time, reduced to 154 seats, and swept from the northern states while it retained much of the south, [the 1977 verdict's geographically uneven character](/2019/04/28/1977-janata-verdict-explained/) preserving the regional base from which recovery could be organised. The Janata Party's victory ended Congress rule but not the Congress System's logic: the victors were a coalition assembled in the opposition's old parties-of-pressure manner, plus the Congress (O) and the defectors of the Congress for Democracy, and within thirty months the coalition had disintegrated and the Congress, reunified under Indira Gandhi's leadership, won the election of January 1980 with 353 seats. A system whose defining mechanism is absorption had, in effect, absorbed its own defeat: the opposition's turn in office produced no alternative architecture, and the electorate returned to the party of consensus. That restoration is the strongest evidence against dating the system's end to 1967, 1969 or 1977. What ended in those years were phases of the mechanism's health. The mechanism itself still stood in 1980, damaged in its second component, unbalanced in its fourth, but recognisably the arrangement Kothari had described, which is why the common scholarly habit of pronouncing the system dead in 1969 cannot survive the election returns of 1971 and 1980.

The baseline against which all of this was measured had been set in the Nehru years, and [the Nehruvian-era institutional fragility framework](/2019/01/25/nehruvian-era-settlement-three-tensions/) explains why the stress tests found the structure wanting in the specific places they did: the settlement had concentrated legitimacy in the party without building institutions that could outlast the party's internal discipline. Kothari, the settlement's most exact analyst, saw the concentration clearly. His framework's four components were, among other things, a list of the mediations the settlement depended on, and the stress decade damaged them in the order of their dependence on voluntary restraint: factional arbitration first, regional autonomy next, dominance and marginality last. The order of the damage matters for what follows, because 1989 struck the components in the reverse order and struck all of them at once.

## 1989: The Year All Four Components Ended

The general election of November 1989 returned the Congress with 197 seats and 39.5 percent of the vote, its poorest seat tally since independence, and it made possible a government, the National Front administration of V. P. Singh, formed by parties that the Congress System had defined as marginal. The claim defended here, following the brief of the framework itself, is that [the 1989 verdict's structural rupture of the Congress System](/2019/05/20/vp-singh-1989-verdict-india/) ended each component, and that the four endings can be specified separately, as Table 1 registers. The first component, dominance, ended in arithmetic and in expectation together. The Congress had lost seats before, in 1977, and had recovered. In 1989 it lost something else: the presumption, held by voters, politicians and itself, that it was the natural party of government around which all calculations were made. The proof is behavioural. In 1977 the Congress's opponents had united to remove it; in 1989 they united and then governed without it, and the Congress, offered the arithmetic possibility of bargaining, chose opposition, a choice unintelligible inside the old system, in which the party of consensus never vacated the centre of the board.

The second component ended in the manner of its damaging, completed. Factional pluralism inside the Congress had been narrowing since 1969, and by the late 1980s the party under Rajiv Gandhi was organised around a leader and a circle of advisers, its state units headed by nominees, its internal elections long in abeyance, its factions functioning as court groupings competing for access rather than as formations with independent bases. The decisive episode is the career of the dissent that destroyed the government the Congress had won in 1984. V. P. Singh, Rajiv Gandhi's finance minister and then defence minister, resigned from the cabinet in April 1987, was expelled from the party, and instead of being absorbed, arbitrated or even merely contained, he built a rival organisation, the Janata Dal of October 1988, assembled from the fragments of the old Janata parivar. Inside the Congress System, a leader of V. P. Singh's standing was exactly the material the absorptive mechanism existed to process: a factional chief with a grievance, to be settled by negotiation, office and delay. The mechanism did not process him. It expelled him, and the expulsion created the alternative that ended the system. A mechanism is known to have ended when its characteristic operation fails on the characteristic case.

The third component ended with the government formation of December 1989. The National Front took office supported from outside by the Bharatiya Janata Party, with 85 seats, and by the Left Front, two forces whose simultaneous support of one government the old system had no category to describe, since parties of pressure do not sustain governments. The opposition had not merely won an election; it had occupied the centre, and the occupation was not reversed. Between 1989 and 2014 no single party won a Lok Sabha majority, and the governments of the period, United Front, National Democratic Alliance and United Progressive Alliance, were coalitions in which former parties of pressure held ministries, wrote common programmes and set terms. Marginality, the structural condition Kothari had specified, was gone, and its going was registered inside the Congress itself: the party that had once absorbed the opposition's demands had itself been reduced to competing to join coalitions on the opposition's terms, supporting the United Front from outside in 1996 in the posture its own framework had assigned to the parties of pressure of the 1950s. The wheel had come full circle, and a framework is definitively ended when its subject takes up the position it reserved for its subject's opposite.

The fourth component ended by migration. Regional power did not disappear in 1989; it changed address. The state-level leaders who mattered after 1989, N. T. Rama Rao and then N. Chandrababu Naidu in Andhra Pradesh, M. Karunanidhi and J. Jayalalithaa in Tamil Nadu, Jyoti Basu in West Bengal, Lalu Prasad in Bihar from 1990, led parties of their own, accountable to no national high command and disciplined by no Working Committee. The Congress's own state leaders, by contrast, had been reduced by two decades of central nomination to managers of Delhi's franchises, unable to bargain as Kamaraj had bargained because they commanded no independent following. Autonomy within discipline had been replaced by autonomy without discipline on one side and discipline without autonomy on the other, and the space between, the bargain that Kothari's fourth component named, was empty. Each of the four endings is a dated, checkable event of the years around 1989, not a mood. That is what justifies the strong word in the framework's defence: the post-1989 era ended the Congress System definitively, and gradual-weakening language, appropriate to 1967 or 1974, understates the discontinuity that the component-by-component record shows.

## The Coalition Decades as the Framework's Negative Image

The quarter-century after 1989 supplies the control case. If the Congress System had merely weakened, the decades that followed would show its components operating at reduced strength; if it ended, the decades would show a politics organised on different principles, in which the old components are not diminished but absent, their places taken by substitutes that work differently. The record, surveyed in [the coalition era of 1989 to 2014](/2019/05/15/coalition-era-1989-2014-explained/), shows the second pattern with unusual clarity, because the substitutes can be named component by component and each is the old component's opposite in structure, not merely in degree.

Where dominance had stood, coalition management stood. The governments of 1989 to 2014, National Front, United Front, National Democratic Alliance and the two United Progressive Alliance administrations, were assembled from parties that retained their separate organisations, their own state bases and their right of exit, and their common programmes were negotiated contracts among sovereigns rather than arbitrations inside a family. The National Democratic Alliance's decision to shelve the Ayodhya temple, the Uniform Civil Code and the abrogation of Article 370 for the duration of the Vajpayee governments is the cleanest illustration. The Congress System had processed such demands by absorbing them into the dominant party's interior, where factions fought them to a draw; the coalition processed them by exclusion from the agenda, a contract term, because the partners who objected could not be arbitrated, only bought off or obeyed. Agenda-shelving is what a polity does when it has no absorptive interior. Its prevalence after 1989 is the first component's absence, photographed from the negative side.

Where factional pluralism had stood, party fragmentation stood. The Congress System had kept India's ideological and regional plurality inside one organisation, where it could be arbitrated; the post-1989 decades distributed the same plurality among dozens of organisations, where it could only be bargained. The Janata Dal's dissolution into the Rashtriya Janata Dal, the Samajwadi Party, the Janata Dal (United), the Biju Janata Dal and other state formations between 1990 and 1999 is the type case: a single party's factions, unable to arbitrate their succession dispute, became separate parties, each with its state, its caste base and its leader, reproducing at the level of the party system the factional map that the Congress had once contained within itself. The Congress's own interior followed the same logic in milder form. The party's factions after 1989 increasingly behaved as claimants on a leadership that decided by nomination, and the party's most consequential internal dissent of the period, the 1999 expulsion of Sharad Pawar, P. A. Sangma and Tariq Anwar over Sonia Gandhi's leadership, produced not an arbitrated settlement but a new party, the Nationalist Congress Party, within the month. Factions that exit and incorporate are not the second component at low strength. They are its negation.

Where opposition marginality had stood, opposition government stood, and with it a transformation of the state's own habits. The parties of pressure of the 1950s had influenced policy through the ruling party's factions; their successors after 1989 influenced policy by holding the ministries that made it, the finance and railway portfolios passing to regional party nominees whose first accountability ran to their own organisations. The Congress itself learned the new grammar from the receiving end: supporting the United Front from outside in 1996 and 1997, withdrawing support twice and bringing down two prime ministers, it occupied precisely the position of the outside pressure party that its own system's third component had described, and discovered what Kothari's opposition had always known, that pressure from outside a government one does not control is a wasting asset. Where regional autonomy within discipline had stood, regional sovereignty stood. The chief ministers who anchored the coalition decades, Karunanidhi in Tamil Nadu, Naidu in Andhra Pradesh, Lalu Prasad and then Nitish Kumar in Bihar, Basu and then Mamata Banerjee in West Bengal, held their states as principals. They negotiated with Delhi over finance commission devolution, over river waters, over the terms of their support to central governments, and no Working Committee arbitrated their disputes with the centre, because they acknowledged no committee above their own parties. The fourth component's bargain, autonomy exchanged for discipline, had been replaced by autonomy retained and discipline refused, which is to say by federalism conducted between parties rather than within one.

The negative image is completed by the Congress's own returns to office. The party governed from 1991 to 1996 as a minority that bought support vote by vote, and from 2004 to 2014 as the largest member of a coalition whose partners held it to a common minimum programme and whose supporting parties, the Left between 2004 and 2008, exercised from outside a veto that no Congress faction of the old system ever wielded against its own government. A party that had once been the arena of Indian politics had become a participant in an arena built by others. Nothing in this record resembles a weakened Congress System. It resembles, in each component, the system's structural opposite, which is what a definitive ending looks like when it is photographed across twenty-five years.

The minority government of P. V. Narasimha Rao, formed after the election of 1991 in which the Congress returned as the largest party with well under half of the house, is the negative image in its purest single frame. Rao governed for a full five-year term by the new grammar: assembling majorities vote by vote, purchasing abstentions, conceding committee inquiries and policy pauses to parties whose support he needed, and carrying the economic reforms of 1991 through a Parliament his party did not control by bargaining with the very opposition the reforms alarmed. A Congress prime minister of the old system had managed a Lok Sabha his party dominated by arbitrating among its factions. Rao managed a Lok Sabha his party did not dominate by negotiating among sovereign parties, and the skill he displayed, for which he is rightly praised, was the skill of a coalition manager two decades before the word became the period's common description of its prime ministers. The Congress in office after 1991 was therefore not the Congress System at reduced strength. It was the Congress party, staffed by the system's heirs, operating a different system, and the difference is visible in the single fact that its government's survival was a weekly parliamentary calculation rather than a premise of the political order. Kothari's framework, applied to its author's own party in its author's own lifetime, returned the verdict of absence on every component.

## Kothari Against Kothari: The Revisions of 1974 and the Emergency Years

The least-read part of Kothari's corpus is the part where he revised his own framework, and the neglect has distorted the framework's reception, because the revisions show the model functioning as an instrument rather than standing as a monument. The foundational texts of 1964 and 1970 dominate the citations; the essays of 1972 to 1985, in which Kothari tracked the Congress System's mutations year by year, are treated as footnotes. Restoring them changes the picture in one decisive respect. The definitive-ending claim does not have to choose between Kothari's model and Kothari's later judgement, because the later judgement, delivered in instalments, recorded the mechanism's progressive disablement and named the disabling agents: the personalisation of authority inside the Congress, the decay of its organisational mediations, the conversion of the party of consensus into an electoral machine commanded from a single household. The author of the model supplied the autopsy reports as the patient declined.

The decennial review of December 1974 is the hinge document. Returning to the framework ten years after the Asian Survey essay, in "The Congress System Revisited: A Decennial Review", Kothari wrote with the split of 1969 and the victory of 1971 behind him, and with the Gujarat and Bihar movements of 1974 gathering around him. The review's argument, compressed, is that the system's form persisted while its idiom changed: dominance had been re-established by the election of 1971, but the consensus on which the party of consensus rested was being replaced by a plebiscitary relationship between a leader and the electorate, in which the mediating structures, factions, state organisations, the Working Committee's arbitration, counted for less each year. In the vocabulary of the four components, the review records the second component mutating, pluralism draining out of the party's interior into a direct leader-mass circuit, while the first and third components still registered as intact. A gradualist reading of the framework's end usually stops at this review and treats the mutation as the ending in slow motion. The architectural reading accepts the diagnosis and contests the inference: a component mutating is not a component ended, and the review's own evidence shows arbitration replaced by command at the centre while the states, the opposition's pressure role and the electoral dominance continued to behave as the model described. Kothari in 1974 described a system under a new management style. He did not describe a new system, and he was right not to, because the election of 1977 and the restoration of 1980 lay ahead, events inexplicable if the architecture had already died in 1974.

The Emergency essays extend the autopsy under the hardest conditions. Between June 1975 and March 1977, with the censorship in force and the opposition in prison, Kothari used the space that Economic and Political Weekly and the seminar circuit still afforded to argue that the regime's significance lay in the collapse of mediations: a politics that had once processed conflict through layered institutions was being conducted through a single chain of command, and the Congress itself, the historic bearer of the mediations, had become the instrument of their suspension. The framework's categories do the analytical work in those essays even where the framework is not named, the party of consensus measured against its own standard and found converted into its opposite. After the Emergency, Kothari's essays of the late 1970s and early 1980s, and the work around Lokayan, the forum for dialogue between activists and intellectuals that he helped to found in 1980, turned from the system's defence to the search for its successor values, decentralisation, humane governance, the themes of State Against Democracy in 1988. The trajectory of the corpus is itself the periodisation this article defends, written by the framework's author across twenty years: construction in 1964 and 1970, revision under stress in 1974, diagnosis of suspension in 1975 to 1977, and thereafter no return to the model as a description of the present, because the present no longer contained the model's object. Scholars who cite the 1964 essay as a label for an era and ignore the author's twenty-year engagement with his own model's decay are not reading Kothari more faithfully than the architectural rereading. They are reading less of him.

## The Descriptive Objection: What Kothari Meant and What the Framework Does

The strongest counter to the reading defended here is not factual. It is interpretive, and it deserves to be stated at full strength. Kothari, the objection runs, was describing a system, not prescribing one and not building an architecture with load-bearing parts whose failure could be dated. His 1964 essay and his 1970 book were works of political description, empirically rich and conceptually suggestive, and to speak of the framework's analytical work being ended in 1989 is to impose on a descriptive text a theoretical ambition its author did not claim. On this view, the four-component map of Table 1 is the rereader's construction, not Kothari's, and the definitive-ending claim tells us about the rereader's taste for clean periodisation rather than about Indian politics.

The reply distinguishes intent from implication, and it does so on Kothari's own evidence. An author's intent governs what a text may be praised for attempting; it does not limit what the text's claims entail. Kothari's description was not a chronicle but a model, with named elements and specified relations among them: dominance maintained through internal pluralism, pluralism made safe by arbitration, opposition contained by the pressure role, regions held by the autonomy bargain. A model with specified relations has implications that can be tested independently of the author's purposes, and the model's central implication is that the elements stand or fall together, that the arrangement is a system and not a list. When the relations ceased to hold, in the component-by-component manner set out for 1989, the model's object ceased to exist. To call that cessation the ending of the framework's analytical work is not to attribute intentions to Kothari. It is to report what happened to the thing he modelled, in the vocabulary he supplied for it.

The objection also underestimates Kothari the reviser. The author of the 1974 decennial review did not treat his own model as a snapshot to be admired; he treated it as an instrument to be recalibrated, recording the consensus idiom's decline and the mediations' weakening as data against the model. His Emergency-period writings extended the practice under censorship, and his later work, from the essays gathered around the founding of Lokayan in 1980 to State Against Democracy in 1988, continued to measure Indian politics against the standard of a system that could absorb conflict without suppressing it, and to find the successor arrangements wanting. A scholar who spends three decades revising a model and judging reality by its absence has, whatever his disclaimers, built an architecture. The rereading's four components are not pasted onto his text. They are the joints at which his text itself says the system's weight was carried, and the ending claim is falsifiable at each joint, which is more than can be said for the label reading or for the one-framework-among-several reading, neither of which risks a dated claim at all.

There is a related caution, which the rereading accepts. Kothari wrote about a system in which he was also a participant, a Congress-sympathetic intellectual of the Nehruvian generation, and his model's warmth toward its object, the suggestion that absorption was a civilisational virtue of Indian politics, has drawn the charge that the framework rationalised dominance. Pradeep Chhibber's Democracy without Associations of 1999 pressed a version of this charge from an unexpected direction: the Congress's dominance, Chhibber argued, rested less on an absorptive architecture than on the weakness of associational life and the state's penetration of society, so that when the Congress organisation decayed, no party, including the Congress's successors, could rebuild linkage with social associations, and the post-1989 fragmentation followed. The charge is a genuine rival account of the mechanism, and the adjudication section weighs it. It does not, however, touch the point at issue in this section. Whether the architecture was admirable, and whether its foundations were as Kothari described them, are questions about the model's truth. The descriptive objection is a question about the model's kind, and on that question the text is clear: a description with joints, relations and a named mode of operation is a model, and models end when their objects do.

## A Second Congress System? The Claim Made for the Years After 2014

The framework's most consequential afterlife is the claim that India after 2014 rebuilt it. The claim has a distinguished sponsor. Yogendra Yadav, the psephologist and political scientist who had earlier periodised the post-1989 decades as a second system of democratic upsurge and coalition politics, argued after the verdict of 2019 that the country had entered a third system, and commentators extended the argument into the suggestion that the Bharatiya Janata Party under Narendra Modi had become a new party of consensus, occupying the centre of the board as the Congress once had, reducing opponents to pressure and absorbing social groups into a capacious national coalition. Suhas Palshikar's essays on the Bharatiya Janata Party's hegemony, and the third-system essays of Yadav and Palshikar collected in Indian Democracy: Meanings and Practices, gave the suggestion its scholarly form. The suggestion is the strongest available test of the four-component reading, because if the post-2014 configuration satisfies the framework's components, then 1989 ended a party's tenure and not a system's life, and the framework survives as a general theory of Indian dominant parties. If the configuration fails the components, the ending claim is confirmed by the hardest case.

Begin with the component the new configuration most nearly satisfies, dominance. The Bharatiya Janata Party won 282 Lok Sabha seats in 2014 on about 31 percent of the vote and 303 seats in 2019 on about 37 percent, the first single-party majorities since 1984, and [the 2014 inflection's relation to the post-1989 system](/2019/07/03/2014-inflection-election-reanalysed/) rightly treats the 2014 result as the coalition era's close. But the dominance differs from the first component in its construction. The Congress's majorities of 1952 to 1967 rested on vote shares between 40.8 and 47.8 percent gathered by a party that also governed nearly every state; the Bharatiya Janata Party's majorities rest on lower vote shares converted by a divided opposition and on an alliance, the National Democratic Alliance, whose partners govern Bihar and, until 2018, Andhra Pradesh, states the dominant party does not itself hold. Dominance as seat arithmetic has returned. Dominance as the Congress practised it, a cross-sectional party governing centre and states on two-fifths to one-half of the national vote, has not, and Kothari's component specified the latter, a social comprehensiveness that made the party the polity's default. A party that wins three seats in five on three votes in eight is predominant in Sartori's sense. Kothari's first component demands more, and the demand is the framework's, not the rereader's.

### What does the third-system claim say about politics after 2014?

The third-system claim, advanced by Yogendra Yadav and developed with Suhas Palshikar, holds that the verdicts of 2014 and 2019 opened a new party system centred on the Bharatiya Janata Party, succeeding the Congress System and the coalition decades. It claims systemic novelty for the new dominance, not a restoration of the old architecture under new management.

The remaining components fail more decisively. The second component, factional pluralism as the arena of substantive politics, has no post-2014 counterpart. The Bharatiya Janata Party is a cadre party whose internal life runs through an organisational hierarchy continuous with the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh; its disagreements are settled by an organisational leadership, and its parliamentary party does not contain organised ideological factions competing for the direction of policy in the manner of the Congress's socialist and conservative wings at Avadi. Dissent within the ruling formation after 2014 has been processed by exit, marginalisation or silence, not by arbitration among standing factions whose contests substitute for opposition. The third component fails in the opposite direction: the opposition after 2014 is not marginal in Kothari's structural sense, because opposition parties govern West Bengal, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Delhi and Punjab among other states, command their own regional machines, and won three large state elections in December 2018, in Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh, in the middle of the dominant party's national ascendancy. Parties of pressure do not take turns governing a third of the country. The fourth component fails most clearly of all. The Bharatiya Janata Party's chief ministers are chosen by the central leadership and changed at its direction, in Gujarat in 2016 and repeatedly in the smaller states, and no syndicate of state leaders chose the prime ministerial candidate of 2014 or could veto the choice. Autonomy within discipline, the bargain in which regional leaders held power as of right inside a national discipline they helped to operate, is inverted: discipline is total and autonomy is a revocable administrative convenience.

Atul Kohli's essays on the post-1989 reorganisation supply the connecting judgement. Kohli argued, in Democracy and Discontent of 1990 and in his subsequent essays, that the crisis of the old order was a crisis of governability produced by the decay of the Congress organisation and the mobilisation of groups the old system had kept at the margins. On that account, the post-2014 dominance is not the old system's return but a different solution to the governability problem, majoritarian mobilisation allied to a disciplined cadre organisation, adopted after the absorptive solution failed. The four-component test reaches the same conclusion by a different road. Yadav's third system is real as a period. It is not a second Congress System, and calling it one obscures precisely the features, centralised leadership, cadre discipline, a governing alliance with autonomous regional partners outside the party, an opposition entrenched in the states, that separate the present architecture from the one Kothari mapped. The framework thus performs analytical work even in refusal: it specifies what would have to exist for the analogy to hold, and the specification shows that it does not.

The specification is worth stating positively, because it converts the refusal into a test any future configuration can be put to. A genuine second Congress System would require a dominant party whose internal factions conduct the country's substantive ideological argument in public, whose state leaders hold their offices by their own followings and choose national leaders by their collective weight, whose opponents influence policy chiefly by feeding demands into the ruling party's interior, and whose social coalition cuts across caste, region and class so completely that segmental parties cannot outflank it. Nothing in the record of the years after 2014 answers to that description, and the description's precision is the framework's continuing gift to the analysis of whatever system India is building in the Congress System's place.

## The Adjudication: Label, Framework Among Frameworks, or Architecture

Three readings of Kothari's work have competed across five decades, and the rereading can be judged against its rivals. The popular reading takes the Congress System as a descriptive label for the decades of Congress victories, a piece of period shorthand. Its strength is mnemonic economy; its weakness is that it explains nothing. A label cannot say why the dominance survived Nehru's death, why it bent in 1967 and recovered in 1971, why the opposition's victory of 1977 produced no successor system, or why 1989 differed in kind from every earlier shock. Worse, the label reading misdates the ending by sentiment, attaching the system's death to whichever Congress defeat the writer remembers most vividly. A reading that cannot date its object's death has not understood its object's life.

The academic reading takes the framework as one model among several, to be shelved beside Sartori's predominant-party type, Morris-Jones's dominance-with-dissent, Weiner's politics of scarcity and the later party-system literature. Its strength is scholarly caution; its weakness is a false symmetry. The rival models do not in fact compete at the same level. Sartori's typology classifies by election results and is silent about the machinery between elections. Weiner's scarcity framework explains the content of political demands, the competition for divisible goods in a poor society, without modelling the party architecture that processed them. Morris-Jones came closest, and his convergence with Kothari is evidence for the architecture rather than an alternative to it, since two scholars approaching from institutional description and from system analysis arrived at the same load-bearing fact, that India's politics ran through the dominant party's interior. The one-framework-among-several shelving dissolves these differences of level into a survey course's table of contents. Against it, the architectural reading claims priority on a simple ground: Kothari's model alone specifies the mechanism connecting the levels, the electoral, the organisational and the governmental, and a mechanism that connects levels explains facts at every level, which is why the model, and not its shelf-mates, generated the periodisation the discipline still uses, down to Yadav's numbering of first, second and third systems, a vocabulary that presupposes Kothari's founding act of treating a party configuration as a system with a life and a death.

The serious rival is not the label or the shelf but the counter-mechanism, and it comes from Chhibber. Democracy without Associations argued in 1999 that the Congress's dominance rested on the state's displacement of social associations and on the party's role as the sole broker between citizens and government, and that the post-1989 fragmentation followed from the absence of associational linkages beneath every party, not merely beneath the Congress. Weighed against the four-component record, Chhibber's account explains the depth of what ended better than it explains the fact of the ending. The brokerage he describes is close kin to the absorption the framework specifies, the channelling of demands through the dominant party, and his evidence of associational thinness sharpens rather than replaces the third component: parties of pressure stayed marginal partly because the associations that might have sustained them as governing alternatives had been crowded out of the space the Congress occupied. Where Chhibber genuinely corrects Kothari is on foundations. Kothari's model took the absorptive architecture as a political achievement of the Congress; Chhibber shows it was also a social condition, dependent on a configuration of state and society that economic change and mobilisation from below were bound to disturb. A corrected architectural reading absorbs the correction: the mechanism was real, and its foundations were eroding under it, which is why, when the political shocks of 1989 arrived, the components failed together instead of bending separately as they had in 1967.

Kohli's governability essays supply the second correction, on the demand side. The groups whose mobilisation overwhelmed the system in the 1980s, the backward castes of the northern states organising for reservations, the regional formations hardened by linguistic and agrarian grievances, the religious mobilisation that the Ayodhya campaign carried, were not absorbed because their demands had become claims for a share of power itself rather than for benefits within an unchanged distribution. Absorption works on divisible demands; it fails on demands for recognition and office that the absorber would have to concede its own position to satisfy. V. P. Singh's Mandal decision of August 1990, taken by the government the 1989 verdict installed, dramatised the failure from the other side: the demand that ended the system's world was enacted by its successors as state policy, over the protests of those the old mechanism would once have arbitrated into quiet. Read with Kohli and Chhibber as correctives, the architectural claim survives in its exact form. The Congress System was a four-component absorptive mechanism. Its components ended in and around 1989, for reasons that include the erosion of its social foundations and the transformation of the demands addressed to it. What the correctives do not sustain is the gradualist alternative, the picture of a system fading across three decades, because the component record refuses it: dominance, pluralism, marginality and the regional bargain were all functioning, in damaged but recognisable form, in 1984, when the Congress won the largest majority of its history, and none was functioning in 1991, when the Congress returned to office as the largest party in a hung house and governed as a minority by the new rules of coalition management. Between those two elections the mechanism ended. Frameworks, like the systems they describe, die at dates, not in decades.

What survives the death is the analytical habit Kothari founded. Indian political science periodises by systems because Kothari taught it to see a party configuration as an architecture with components, relations and a lifespan, and the liveliest arguments in the field, Yadav's third system, Palshikar's hegemony thesis, Chhibber's associational account, are arguments conducted in the grammar he built. The popular label and the academic shelf both borrow that grammar while declining its discipline. The rereading offered in this article has tried to restore the discipline: four components, each checked against dated evidence, an ending specified component by component, the hardest successor case tested and refused, the author's own revisions treated as evidence, and the rival mechanisms weighed rather than waved away. On that record the namable claim stands. The Congress System was a four-component absorptive mechanism, and the post-1989 era ended it definitively, not gradually, leaving Indian politics to be conducted by other architectures whose description still awaits a framework as exact as the one Rajni Kothari built for the order that raised him.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the Congress System?

The Congress System is Rajni Kothari's name for the party system of post-Independence India, first stated in Asian Survey in December 1964 and developed in Politics in India in 1970. It describes an arrangement in which one party of consensus, the Indian National Congress, dominated elections and government while containing within itself a plurality of factions that performed the work of opposition, while parties outside it acted as parties of pressure and regional leaders governed with autonomy inside national party discipline. Kothari's claim was that these elements formed a single absorptive mechanism: demands and grievances entered the Congress and were settled through its internal bargaining rather than through alternation in office. The system operated from 1947 until the verdict of 1989 ended its components together.

### Q: Who was Rajni Kothari?

Rajni Kothari, born in Udaipur in 1928 and deceased in New Delhi in April 2015, was the political scientist who gave Indian politics its most durable analytical vocabulary. He taught political science in Rajasthan before moving to Delhi, where in 1963 he founded the Centre for the Study of Developing Societies, the institution that made empirical research on Indian elections, parties and state politics a scholarly discipline. His Politics in India of 1970 was the first comprehensive analytical account of the post-1947 political order, and his edited volume Caste in Indian Politics, published the same year, mapped the traffic between caste associations and party competition. He later founded the Lokayan forum in 1980 and wrote State Against Democracy in 1988 and Rethinking Democracy in 2005, returning across five decades to the questions his Congress System framework had opened.

### Q: What is single-party dominance?

Single-party dominance is the condition in which one party wins an absolute majority of seats across a sustained run of free elections while rival parties remain legal, organised and competitive but unable to form a government. Kothari introduced the term for India, where the Congress won between 40.8 and 47.8 percent of the vote and absolute Lok Sabha majorities at every general election from 1952 to 1967, governing at the centre and in nearly every state. Giovanni Sartori later classified such arrangements as predominant-party systems in Parties and Party Systems of 1976. Dominance differs from a one-party state, where rivals are banned, and from an ordinary majority, which a party may win once and lose at the next election. Its defining mark is repetition under competition: the same party keeps winning, and everyone organises their politics around the expectation that it will.

### Q: How did the Congress absorb opposition?

The Congress absorbed opposition through the structure of its own organisation rather than through repression. A citizen, interest or region with a grievance could press it through a faction inside the ruling party, since the party contained organised followings of the left, the right, the regions and the major caste blocs, and its Working Committee arbitrated among them. Opposition parties outside, which Kothari called parties of pressure, found their demands taken up by sympathetic Congress factions competing for internal advantage, which gave the opposition influence over policy without office. Regional leaders absorbed local pressures in the states and settled them under national arbitration, and defeated factions stayed inside to contest the next round. The result was that building a rival organisation was usually less rewarding than working through the Congress's internal geography, so opposition remained pressure rather than alternative until the mechanism failed around 1989.

### Q: When did the Congress System end?

The Congress System ended with the general election of November 1989, when the Congress won 197 seats, short of a Lok Sabha majority, and a coalition of its opponents formed the government in New Delhi. Each of the framework's components ended in that conjuncture: dominance gave way to a hung house and then to twenty-five years without a single-party majority; factional pluralism had already been displaced by centralised leadership, and the dissent of V. P. Singh was expelled rather than absorbed, producing the Janata Dal; opposition parties ceased to be parties of pressure by forming and sustaining governments; and regional power migrated to autonomous regional parties outside any national discipline. Earlier shocks, the losses of 1967, the split of 1969 and the defeat of 1977, damaged the system without ending it, as the recoveries of 1971 and 1980 demonstrated.

### Q: What is the post-1989 Indian party system?

The post-1989 Indian party system is the configuration that replaced the Congress System: a multiparty order in which no party won a Lok Sabha majority between 1989 and 2014, governments at the centre were coalitions or minority administrations sustained by outside support, and regional parties holding their own states became indispensable partners in forming and running national governments. Its landmarks include the National Front of 1989, the United Front of 1996 to 1998, the National Democratic Alliance governments of 1998 to 2004 and the United Progressive Alliance governments of 2004 to 2014. Yogendra Yadav periodised these decades as a second system, succeeding Kothari's first. In the vocabulary of the Congress System framework, its defining feature is the absence of an absorptive centre: plurality that the Congress once contained within one organisation is distributed among many parties and reconciled by bargaining among them.

### Q: Is the Modi era a second Congress System?

No, on the test Rajni Kothari's framework itself supplies. The verdicts of 2014 and 2019 restored single-party Lok Sabha majorities, 282 seats for the Bharatiya Janata Party in 2014 and 303 in 2019, and Yogendra Yadav has described the period as a third party system. But the four components do not match. The new dominance rests on lower vote shares and an alliance whose partners govern states the dominant party does not hold. The ruling party's interior is cadre-disciplined rather than factionally plural, so internal contests do not substitute for opposition. Opposition parties govern large states and are not structurally marginal. And chief ministers of the ruling party are selected and replaced by the central leadership rather than holding autonomy within discipline as Kamaraj or Chavan did. A new system exists; it is not the Congress System rebuilt.

### Q: What did Kothari mean by the party of consensus?

Kothari called the Congress the party of consensus because its electoral and social coalition was a cross-section of Indian society rather than a segment of it. The party contained landlords and tenants, industrialists and trade unionists, upper castes and Scheduled Castes, the Hindi-speaking heartland and the regional elites of Madras, Bengal and Maharashtra, and its internal factions organised that plurality into a continuing negotiation. Consensus, in his usage, did not mean agreement. It meant that the major social forces accepted the Congress as the arena in which their disagreements would be processed, so that even groups hostile to a particular Congress policy treated the party's internal process as the route to changing it. The phrase distinguishes the Congress from a cadre party with a defined doctrine and from a catch-all party assembled for one election: the consensus was historical, inherited from the freedom movement, and it was the social foundation of single-party dominance.

### Q: What were parties of pressure in the Congress System?

Parties of pressure is Kothari's term for the opposition parties of the Congress System era, among them the Praja Socialist Party, the Communist Party of India, the Bharatiya Jana Sangh and the Swatantra Party. He argued that their function in the system was not to alternate in office, which their seat tallies made impossible, but to articulate interests and ideologies, ventilate grievances and press demands that sympathetic factions inside the Congress could adopt for their own internal contests. The arrangement gave them influence disproportionate to their seats: an issue raised by an opposition party could enter the ruling party's factional bargaining and emerge as policy. It also kept them fragmented, since each party competed for access to Congress factions as much as for votes against the Congress. Their marginality was therefore structural, a role the system assigned them, rather than a verdict on their leadership or their ideas.

### Q: Where did Kothari first publish the Congress System argument?

Kothari first published the argument in "The Congress 'System' in India", in Asian Survey, volume 4, number 12, December 1964, pages 1161 to 1173. The essay appeared six months after Jawaharlal Nehru's death in May 1964, at the moment when the durability of the Congress order was most in doubt, and its claim was pointed: dominance belonged to the party's architecture rather than to Nehru's person. He developed the essay into a book-length treatment in Politics in India, published by Little, Brown of Boston in 1970, where the framework received its full statement with the evidence of four general elections behind it. He returned to the argument in "The Congress System Revisited: A Decennial Review" in Asian Survey in December 1974, and in essays in Economic and Political Weekly across the following decade.

### Q: What share of seats did the Congress win in the general elections from 1952 to 1967?

The Congress won 364 of 489 Lok Sabha seats at the first general election of 1951 to 1952, 371 of 494 in 1957, 361 of 494 in 1962, and 283 of 520 in 1967, on the Election Commission's statistical reports. Its vote shares across the same elections were 45 percent in 1952, 47.8 percent in 1957, 44.7 percent in 1962 and 40.8 percent in 1967. The pattern is the arithmetic face of single-party dominance: a party winning between two-fifths and one-half of the vote converted, under first-past-the-post, into between three-quarters and one-half of the seats, while the largest opposition party of the period, the Communist Party of India in 1962, held 29 seats. The 1967 result, a bare majority with eight state governments lost, was the dominance component's first serious thinning, though the system survived it.

### Q: How did Morris-Jones describe India's one-party dominance?

W. H. Morris-Jones, the British scholar of Indian government at the University of London, arrived at Kothari's conclusion independently and gave it its most quoted formulation: India exhibited dominance coexisting with competition but without a trace of alternation. He developed the analysis in The Government and Politics of India in 1964 and in "The Indian Congress Party: A Dilemma of Dominance", in Modern Asian Studies, volume 1, number 2, in 1967. Morris-Jones stressed the dilemma inside the dominance, that the Congress's very inclusiveness left it without a clear governing identity, and he analysed the idioms through which Indian politicians addressed different audiences. The convergence matters for the framework's standing: a scholar working from institutional description and a scholar working from system analysis, in London and in Delhi, described the same architecture in the same years, which is evidence that the Congress System named a real structure.

### Q: Which opposition parties operated at the margins of the Congress System?

The principal opposition parties of the period were the Praja Socialist Party, formed in 1952 from the Socialist Party and the Kisan Mazdoor Praja Party; the Communist Party of India, the largest opposition party in the first three Lok Sabhas, which also formed the Kerala government of 1957; the Bharatiya Jana Sangh, founded by Syama Prasad Mookerjee in October 1951 and strongest in the Hindi-speaking belt and Delhi; the Swatantra Party, founded by C. Rajagopalachari in 1959 as a conservative challenge to the licence and permit regime; and regional parties such as the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam in Madras. None won more than a small fraction of Lok Sabha seats in the elections to 1962, and Kothari classified them as parties of pressure. Their strongholds were real but bounded: the Communists in Kerala and West Bengal, the Jana Sangh in northern cities, Swatantra among business and former princely constituencies.

### Q: How did Congress chief ministers exercise autonomy within party discipline?

Chief ministers of the Congress era held their states as regional leaders with followings of their own. K. Kamaraj in Madras, Yashwantrao Chavan in Maharashtra, Partap Singh Kairon in Punjab and S. Nijalingappa in Mysore controlled candidate selection in practice, distributed patronage through their own networks and settled most factional disputes inside their state units. The discipline they accepted in return was real: they delivered their organisations to national decisions, submitted disputes that escaped state settlement to the arbitration of the Congress Working Committee, and did not carry defeats into rival parties. The arrangement was visible at the summit in January 1966 and May 1964, when the regional leaders acting as the syndicate selected Indira Gandhi and Lal Bahadur Shastri as prime ministers. Autonomy was thus a right inside the system's constitution, not a favour from the centre, and its later conversion into nomination from Delhi marks the fourth component's decay.

### Q: What did the 1967 general election change inside the Congress System?

The general election of 1967 thinned the system's dominance without ending the system. The Congress fell to 283 of 520 Lok Sabha seats on 40.8 percent of the vote and lost office in eight states, where opposition coalitions of the Samyukta Vidhayak Dal type took power. K. Kamaraj himself was defeated at Virudhunagar, defections became a recognised political trade after Gaya Lal's repeated crossings in Haryana, and the opposition governed states for the first time on a large scale. Yet the components held in damaged form: the Congress formed the central government, the state opposition coalitions collapsed within about two years, factional arbitration continued, and the recovery of 1971 restored a large majority. In the framework's terms, 1967 was a stress test that bent the architecture, demonstrated in the states' instability that opposition marginality had a governing dimension, and left the system's ending to the different conjuncture of 1989.

### Q: How did Kothari revise the framework in his 1974 decennial review?

In "The Congress System Revisited: A Decennial Review", published in Asian Survey in December 1974, Kothari argued that the system persisted in form while its idiom changed. Dominance had been restored by the election of 1971, but the consensus on which the party of consensus rested was giving way to a plebiscitary relationship between Indira Gandhi and the electorate, in which the mediating structures of the party, its factions, its state organisations and the Working Committee's arbitration, counted for less. The review is the hinge document of the corpus: it records factional pluralism mutating under centralised leadership while dominance and opposition marginality still behaved as the 1964 essay described. Readers who cite the review as the framework's obituary misread it. Kothari described a system under a new management style and under strain, and the restoration of Congress rule in 1980 after the defeat of 1977 showed that the architecture he had modelled was still standing.

### Q: What did Kothari write about the Congress System during the Emergency?

During the Emergency of June 1975 to March 1977, Kothari wrote as a critic of the regime in the spaces that remained open, in Economic and Political Weekly and in seminar discussion conducted under censorship. His diagnosis used the framework's own categories. A party of consensus, built to process conflict through layered mediations, had been converted into an instrument of command; opposition was imprisoned, the press was censored and the Congress's internal life was frozen, so the absorptive mechanism had no channels through which to work. He treated the Emergency as the collapse of the mediations rather than as one leader's aberration, which is why his later work, from the Lokayan forum founded in 1980 to State Against Democracy in 1988, searched for successor values in decentralisation and humane governance. His Emergency-period essays are the least cited part of the corpus and the part that best shows the framework operating as an instrument of criticism.

### Q: What separates the Congress System from a one-party state?

Three things separate them. First, legality and competition: under the Congress System, opposition parties were legal, organised, financed and free to campaign, elections were administered by an independent Election Commission, and governments could lose, as the Congress lost Kerala in 1957 and eight states in 1967. Second, the locus of contest: in a one-party state, the ruling party suppresses organised disagreement; in the Congress System, disagreement was organised inside the ruling party itself, among factions whose contests decided ministries and policies. Third, the basis of rule: the one-party state rests on prohibition, the Congress System on a consensus that the Congress was the natural arena of politics, a consensus the electorate could and did withdraw, in 1977 and definitively in 1989. Kothari's whole construction depended on the distinction, which is why he insisted that India had produced neither a one-party state nor a conventional competitive system but a third thing that required its own name.

### Q: When did Kothari found the Centre for the Study of Developing Societies?

Kothari founded the Centre for the Study of Developing Societies in Delhi in 1963, a year before the Asian Survey essay that announced the Congress System. The Centre mattered to the framework because it supplied the empirical machinery the framework presupposed: systematic election studies, state-level field research and a generation of scholars trained to treat Indian politics as a subject for evidence rather than commentary. Kothari directed the Centre through its formative decades and made it a base independent of both the government and the Congress, at a time when the party he analysed also governed the country. The institutional fact disciplines a common misreading of the framework as an insider's rationalisation. Its author built an independent research institution, published his revisions as the system decayed, criticised the Emergency while it lasted and spent his later career on decentralisation and democratic theory, in State Against Democracy of 1988 and Rethinking Democracy of 2005.

### Q: How does Pradeep Chhibber explain the party system that followed the Congress System?

In Democracy without Associations, published in 1999, Pradeep Chhibber argued that Indian parties, the Congress included, had always been weakly linked to social associations, because the post-Independence state crowded out the associational space in which parties elsewhere build durable ties to unions, churches, business bodies and civic organisations. On his account, the Congress's dominance rested on its position as the sole broker between citizens and the state, and when the Congress organisation decayed, no party possessed the associational linkages that would let it rebuild a stable national following. The fragmentation after 1989 followed: parties became electoral machines competing for state-level coalitions of castes and communities without organisational depth. Chhibber's account corrects Kothari on foundations, since absorption depended on a configuration of state and society that was itself eroding, while leaving the four-component description of the mechanism, and the dating of its end to 1989, substantially intact.
