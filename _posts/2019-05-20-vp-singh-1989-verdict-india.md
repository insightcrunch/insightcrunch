V.P. Singh entered the 1989 general election as a dissident whom the Congress had expelled, and he left it as Prime Minister of a coalition that his former party could neither join nor bring down on its own. The standard account of his career treats that election as a prelude, the necessary doorway to the eleven months in office during which he implemented the Mandal Commission report in August 1990. This article inverts the priority. The lasting consequence of Vishwanath Pratap Singh was the 1989 verdict itself: the first general election since 1952 in which the Congress lost power to a coalition that contained neither a single dominant party nor any prospect of one, and the moment at which the Congress System, the absorptive one-party-dominant order that Rajni Kothari had anatomised in 1964, ended as a working description of Indian politics. Mandal would have found another vehicle. The verdict had exactly one.

## The Standard Reading: Mandal First, the Verdict as Backdrop

The standard reading has a clean narrative shape, and its cleanliness is the first thing to distrust. In that reading, V.P. Singh matters because on 7 August 1990 he announced in Parliament that the Union government would implement the Mandal Commission's central recommendation, reserving 27 per cent of central government posts for the Other Backward Classes. The announcement detonated a season of protest, the self-immolation of the Delhi student Rajeev Goswami on 19 September 1990 became its emblem, and the reservation order reshaped the caste arithmetic of north Indian politics for a generation. On this account, the 1989 election is context. It explains how a man born in 1931 in Allahabad, a former chief minister of Uttar Pradesh and a former finance minister, came to hold the office from which the Mandal order issued. The election is the staircase; Mandal is the room at the top.

Biographical treatments reinforce the ordering. Inder Malhotra, whose political biography of Indira Gandhi appeared in 1989 and who chronicled the succession struggles of the decade in his journalism, consistently framed V.P. Singh's career through the question of what he did with power rather than how power came to be available to a man outside the Congress. The textbook treatments of the 1989 election compound the effect by listing it as one hung verdict among several, distinguished mainly by producing a short-lived government. Christophe Jaffrelot, in India's Silent Revolution, published in 2003, supplied the scholarly version of the standard reading: the rise of the lower castes through democratic politics is the long story, V.P. Singh is a pivotal agent inside it, and the Mandal implementation is the hinge on which the story turns.

The inversion defended here rests on a distinction between two kinds of historical event. Some events are necessary in substance but replaceable in agency: the substance would have arrived through another decision-maker, another session of Parliament, another coalition arithmetic, because the pressures producing it had accumulated beyond any single government's capacity to refuse. Other events are contingent and singular: they depended on a precise sequence of resignations, disclosures, expulsions and alliances inside a narrow window, and had any link in the sequence failed, the event itself would not have occurred in a form capable of changing the structure around it. The Mandal implementation belongs to the first kind. The Mandal Commission had submitted its report on 31 December 1980, nine years before V.P. Singh took office. The Janata Party government of Morarji Desai had appointed the Commission in January 1979. Bihar under Karpoori Thakur had implemented 26 per cent reservation for backward classes in state services in November 1978, a full decade before the 1989 verdict, on the basis of the Mungeri Lal Commission's report. Karnataka and Tamil Nadu operated extensive backward-class quotas through the same period. The substance of Mandal was circulating through state politics, commission reports and party manifestos long before it reached a prime ministerial desk in Delhi.

The 1989 verdict belongs to the second kind. Nothing in the structure of Indian politics in 1986 made a non-Congress, non-BJP coalition government inevitable. The Congress had won 414 seats in December 1984, the largest mandate in its history. The BJP held two seats. The Janata experiment of 1977 had collapsed in public recrimination within thirty months, and its memory taught most politicians that anti-Congress unity dissolved on contact with office. Producing the 1989 verdict required V.P. Singh to resign from Rajiv Gandhi's cabinet in April 1987, to survive expulsion from the Congress in July 1988, to build the Jan Morcha in October 1987 and then dissolve it into the Janata Dal in October 1988, and to hold together a National Front whose parliamentary survival depended simultaneously on the BJP to its right and the Left Front to its left. Each step was avoidable. Each step had identifiable opponents working to prevent it. The verdict, not the eleven months that followed, is the contingent achievement, and the claim of this article is that it is therefore the consequential one.

### Did V.P. Singh plan to become Prime Minister when he left the Congress in 1987?

No evidence from 1987 shows V.P. Singh organising a bid for the prime ministership. His resignation from the cabinet in April 1987 followed his transfer from finance to defence in January 1987 and his order of an inquiry into the HDW submarine purchase, and his statements framed his quarrel with Rajiv Gandhi as a quarrel over corruption in defence procurement.

The Jan Morcha, founded in October 1987, described itself as a forum for clean politics rather than a party of government, and V.P. Singh's own public position through 1988 was that of a campaigner against corruption, not a candidate assembling a cabinet.

The distinction matters for everything that follows, because it separates the verdict's causes from the verdict's effects. V.P. Singh did not set out to end the Congress System, and his admirers and his detractors have both, at different times, found that fact inconvenient. His admirers in the Janata Dal presented the 1989 result as the harvest of a principled crusade. His detractors in the Congress presented it as the harvest of a calculated ambition dressed in the language of principle. The documentary record supports neither presentation in full. What the record of 1987 to 1989 shows is a politician converting a procurement scandal into an organisational exit, and then discovering, election by election and negotiation by negotiation, that the exit had created a space in Indian politics that no one, including its creator, had fully designed.

## The Man: A Raja's Heir Who Made a Career of Resigning

The biography matters because the verdict's chain runs through it at every joint, and because V.P. Singh's political persona, the asset the 1989 campaign actually traded on, was manufactured by a series of earlier resignations and confrontations that taught the electorate how to read him. Vishwanath Pratap Singh was born on 25 June 1931 in Allahabad and was adopted in childhood by Raja Bahadur Ram Gopal Singh of Manda, an estate in the Allahabad region, succeeding to the title and becoming, in the vocabulary of the period, the Raja of Manda. He studied at Allahabad University and at Fergusson College in Pune, joined the Congress, and rose through the Uttar Pradesh organisation in the approved manner of the Congress System: legislative assembly member from 1969, deputy minister and minister in Uttar Pradesh governments through the 1970s, a reputation for personal austerity that set him apart in a state machine not famous for it.

His first government taught him the uses of resignation. Appointed chief minister of Uttar Pradesh in June 1980 by Indira Gandhi, he inherited a state convulsed by dacoity in the Chambal ravines and by the massacre at Behmai in February 1981, where Phoolan Devi's gang murdered twenty Thakur men in reprisal for atrocities against her. V.P. Singh had publicly committed his government to ending the dacoit menace, and in July 1982, with the menace unended and criticism mounting inside his own party, he resigned the chief ministership, accepting moral responsibility for the failure. The gesture entered his political capital. Supporters read it as proof of a conscience rare in the profession; opponents read it as an early exhibition of a talent for converting failure into theatre. Both readings travelled with him to Delhi, where Indira Gandhi made him commerce minister in 1983 and Rajiv Gandhi, succeeding his mother in December 1984, gave him the finance ministry.

The finance ministry years, from the last day of 1984 to January 1987, built the reputation on which the 1989 campaign would draw. V.P. Singh's budgets of 1985 and 1986 carried the liberalising signature of the Rajiv Gandhi government's first phase, lowering tax rates and licensing barriers. Simultaneously, and in visible tension with that signature, he unleashed the enforcement agencies on the industrial houses that the licence system had enriched. The Enforcement Directorate, directed by Bhure Lal, raided or investigated a roster of the country's largest business families, and the finance minister authorised the engagement of the Fairfax Group, an American investigative firm whose head, Michael Hershman, was tasked with tracing Indian funds held illegally abroad. The Fairfax engagement was the detonator inside the government: its inquiries pointed toward accounts and intermediaries uncomfortably close to the political executive, and the government's embarrassment was compounded when the arrangement's details reached the press. In January 1987 Rajiv Gandhi transferred V.P. Singh from finance to defence. As defence minister he ordered an inquiry into the commissions alleged in the 1981 HDW submarine contract. In April 1987 he was out of the cabinet altogether, resigning days before the Swedish Radio broadcast turned defence procurement into the central question of Indian politics.

Read in sequence, the years 1980 to 1987 show a politician repeatedly choosing the dramatic exit over the quiet accommodation that the Congress System prescribed for its ambitious men. The system's senior figures, the men who had waited out Indira Gandhi's displeasure and returned to favour in the factional rotation, regarded the pattern as a character flaw: a man who resigns cannot be relied on to hold a coalition's line. The electorate of 1989 read the same pattern from the other side, as the curriculum vitae of the one senior politician who had repeatedly paid a price for refusing complicity. That reading, fair or unfair to the colleagues he had left behind in the cabinet room, was the Janata Dal's principal campaign asset, and it had been forty years in the making.

## The Arithmetic of the 1989 Verdict

The Election Commission of India conducted polling for the ninth general election on 22 and 26 November 1989, the first election held after the Sixty-First Amendment lowered the voting age from twenty-one to eighteen and added tens of millions of first-time voters to the rolls. The Commission's Statistical Report on the 1989 general election records the result that restructured Indian politics. The Congress, which had won 414 seats and 49.10 per cent of the valid vote in 1984 according to the Commission's report on that election, fell to 197 seats on 39.53 per cent of the valid vote. The Janata Dal, a party thirteen months old, won 143 seats on 17.79 per cent of the valid vote. The Bharatiya Janata Party rose from two seats in 1984 to 85 seats on 11.36 per cent of the valid vote. The Communist Party of India (Marxist) won 33 seats and the Communist Party of India won 12, giving the Left parties a combined bloc that the brief arithmetic of the period usually rounds, with the smaller Left parties included, to about 52. The remaining seats scattered among regional parties and independents, from the Telugu Desam Party's two seats to the Shiromani Akali Dal (Mann) group's six in Punjab.

Read the distribution, not the ranking, because the distribution is where the rupture sits. The Congress remained the single largest party in the Lok Sabha, and it remained by a wide margin the largest party by vote share: 39.53 per cent against the Janata Dal's 17.79 per cent. Under every previous hung or near-hung outcome in the republic's history, that combination of plurality in seats and overwhelming plurality in votes had translated into a Congress government or a Congress-supported arrangement, because the Congress was the only party with legislators in nearly every state and the only machine capable of assembling a majority from fragments. In 1977, when the Congress lost, it lost to a single merged opposition party, the Janata Party, which won 295 seats and formed a government with its own majority. In 1989 no such successor existed. The Janata Dal's 143 seats were 129 short of the majority mark in a House of 543. The verdict therefore posed a question that the political system had never had to answer outside the states: could a government be constructed entirely by negotiation among parties, none of which could govern alone, and could such a government be headed by a party holding barely a quarter of the seats in the House?

The answer the politicians of November and December 1989 produced was yes, and the construction they produced it with deserves attention precisely because it had no precedent at the Union level. The National Front, a pre-poll alliance formed in August 1988 whose core was the Janata Dal and whose members included the Telugu Desam Party, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, the Asom Gana Parishad and the Indian Congress (Socialist), commanded on its own only a minority of the House. The BJP, with 85 seats, and the Left Front, with its roughly 52, agreed to support a National Front government from outside, voting with it on confidence and supply without joining the cabinet. The two outside supporters stood at opposite poles of the ideological field. The BJP had fought the election on its own manifesto, had contested against Janata Dal candidates in parts of the Hindi belt, and was committed to positions on Ayodhya and on Article 370 that the Left regarded as anathema. The Left parties had fought the BJP as their principal enemy in West Bengal and Kerala politics for a decade. The only proposition uniting the three blocs was negative: the Congress should not govern. Rajiv Gandhi, invited first by President R. Venkataraman as leader of the largest party, declined to attempt a government he could not sustain, and on 2 December 1989 V.P. Singh was sworn in as Prime Minister at the head of a cabinet drawn from the National Front alone, kept in office by two forces that agreed on nothing except his presence there.

That configuration is the verdict's signature, and it is why the verdict outlasts the government it produced. A coalition of convenience between a centrist party and one flank would have been an improvisation. A government held up simultaneously by the Hindu nationalist right and the communist left, each calculating that Congress rule was the greater evil, demonstrated that the centre of Indian politics had become a space of negotiation rather than a property of one party. [The coalition-era renegotiation framework](/2019/05/15/coalition-era-1989-2014-explained/), developed across the quarter-century that followed, begins at this point: December 1989 is where bargaining among parties replaced absorption within a party as the operating principle of government formation in New Delhi. Every subsequent Union government of the era, from Chandra Shekhar's minority administration of 1990 to the United Progressive Alliance of 2004, worked inside the grammar that the 1989 verdict wrote.

It is worth recording what the verdict was not. It was not a vote of confidence in a programme. The Janata Dal's 17.79 per cent vote share was less than half the Congress share, and the National Front's combined vote did not approach a majority of the electorate. It was not a regional revolt alone, although regional parties held the balance in several states. It was not, despite the campaign that produced it, purely a referendum on corruption, because the Congress retained two-fifths of the national vote in the middle of the Bofors storm. The verdict was a structural event wearing the clothes of an ordinary election: the moment the seat arithmetic of the Lok Sabha stopped being a distorted mirror of Congress dominance and became an invitation to permanent negotiation.

The translation of votes into seats records the mechanism in its purest form. In 1984, on the Election Commission's figures, the Congress converted 49.10 per cent of the valid vote into 414 of the seats decided, a seat share of roughly four-fifths, because its opponents contested against one another in most constituencies and split the anti-Congress vote into losing fragments. In 1989 the Congress vote fell by less than ten percentage points, to 39.53 per cent, yet its seat tally fell by more than half, to 197. The disproportion between a moderate vote decline and a catastrophic seat decline is the statistical signature of opposition coordination: where the Janata Dal, the BJP and the regional parties divided the field between them, the Congress plurality that had sufficed for decades stopped sufficing. Political scientists describe such outcomes as manufactured majorities unwinding; the 1989 tables show the unwinding in a single step. The Congress had not lost the country's allegiance in five years. It had lost the divided opposition that had made its pluralities look like majorities, and the loss proved permanent because the coordination that caused it taught every opposition party the value of the lesson.

## Reading the Result across the States

The national arithmetic concealed a geography, and the geography explains why no reconstruction of the old order was available. The Election Commission's Statistical Report records a verdict that split the republic into zones of different verdicts. In the northern heartland that had decided Indian elections since 1952, the Congress collapsed as a seat-winning machine: the Janata Dal and its allies carried the greater part of Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, the two states that together send 139 members to the Lok Sabha, and the BJP made its first great advances across Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Gujarat, Himachal Pradesh and Delhi. In the south and in parts of the east, the Congress held much of its ground: it remained the leading party in Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka, held its position in Maharashtra in alliance arithmetic, and watched the Left Front defend West Bengal under Jyoti Basu with its organisation intact. The Congress's 39.53 per cent of the national vote was thus not a uniform decline of two-fifths of the country's allegiance. It was a fortress vote in the south and east joined to a rout in the north, and a party in that condition cannot form a government in a House where the majority must be assembled from the north's new holders.

The state results also certified the arrival of regional parties as arbiters of Union power. The Telugu Desam Party, which had won thirty seats in 1984 on N.T. Rama Rao's Telugu-pride platform, fell to two seats in 1989 as the Congress recovered Andhra Pradesh, yet the Front's federal structure meant that Rama Rao's stature as the Front's chairman survived his party's defeat, an early proof that the new order priced seniority and convening power alongside seats. The Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, returning to office in Tamil Nadu under M. Karunanidhi in the state election of January 1989, drew a blank in the Lok Sabha poll despite its state victory, a result that underlined the period's volatility and the weakness of straight-line projections from state to centre. The Asom Gana Parishad, born of the Assam Accord of 1985, carried the grievances of the Assam movement into the Front's councils. In Punjab, where the election was held in the shadow of militancy, the Shiromani Akali Dal (Mann) group won six seats on a platform that the Union government would have to negotiate with rather than administer past. A Congress System reading of the map would have treated each of these as a local disturbance to be managed by the centre. The verdict made them shareholders.

The caste geography underneath the state map is the terrain of Jaffrelot's silent revolution, and it was already legible in the 1989 counting. In Bihar, the Janata Dal's victory rested on the Yadav and broader OBC mobilisation that Lalu Prasad Yadav would convert into a fifteen-year ascendancy after he became chief minister in March 1990. In Uttar Pradesh, the Dal's performance drew on the organisational inheritance of Charan Singh's Lok Dal among the Jat and OBC peasantry of the west joined to the socialist networks of the east, while Mulayam Singh Yadav, who became the state's chief minister in December 1989, embodied the transfer of power from the upper-caste Congress leadership to the backward-caste leaderships the Mandal announcement would soon electrify. The Congress System had managed caste by ticket distribution inside one party, balancing factions so that every major group received a share of nominations and none received a programme. The 1989 map showed caste organising itself into parties of its own, in the open, with the share of power to be settled by negotiation between them. That settlement process, begun in the counting halls of November 1989, is the social content of the phrase coalition era.

## Bofors: The Issue That Built the Opposition

Every structural rupture has a proximate instrument, and the instrument of the 1989 verdict was a howitzer. On 16 April 1987, Swedish Radio broadcast the disclosure that the Swedish arms manufacturer Bofors had paid commissions, routed through Swiss bank accounts and codenamed intermediaries, to secure the Indian Army's contract of March 1986 for 410 FH-77B 155-millimetre howitzers, a contract valued at roughly 1,437 crore rupees. The disclosure named no Indian recipient on air, but its implication was immediate, because the Rajiv Gandhi government had publicly committed itself, during the contract negotiations, to a policy of no middlemen in defence purchases. A payment to intermediaries was therefore not merely an irregularity. It was evidence against the government's own declared standard, available to any opponent willing to keep repeating it.

V.P. Singh's entanglement with the procurement question predated the Swedish broadcast, and the sequence is essential. As finance minister from 1984, he had authorised Enforcement Directorate action against industrial houses, including the celebrated 1985 raids, and had built a public reputation as a minister willing to pursue the politically connected. In January 1987 Rajiv Gandhi moved him from finance to the defence portfolio. As defence minister, V.P. Singh ordered an inquiry into allegations of commissions in the 1981 HDW submarine contract with a West German yard. In April 1987, days before the Swedish Radio broadcast, he resigned from the cabinet. His stated ground, repeated in his statements of the period, was that the government was unwilling to let the inquiries proceed where the evidence led. The Congress leadership's counter-account, maintained consistently thereafter, held that he had resigned in pique at the transfer from finance and had then constructed a corruption crusade around his grievance. Both accounts agree on the chronology, and the chronology is what mattered politically: when the Bofors disclosure landed on 16 April 1987, the minister who had just resigned over an arms-deal inquiry was standing outside the cabinet, available as its interpreter.

The press converted the disclosure into a dossier. Through 1988, The Hindu published a sustained investigation, led by its associate editor N. Ram and the correspondent Chitra Subramaniam, drawing on Bofors documents, diary notations and the records of the Swedish National Audit Bureau, which in June 1988 confirmed that payments of at least 319 million Swedish kronor had been made and that a portion had gone to accounts whose beneficial ownership the Bureau could not publish. The Indian Express under Arun Shourie pursued the parallel story of the government's investigative paralysis. The parliamentary track ran through the Joint Parliamentary Committee appointed in August 1987 under the chairmanship of B. Shankaranand. The committee's report, tabled in April 1988, concluded that no evidence established payments to Indian politicians or officials, a conclusion the opposition members refused to endorse; the dissent notes appended to the report, written by members drawn from the BJP and the Janata formation among others, are the under-used primary record of how the issue was converted into a campaign weapon, since they rehearse, in official prose, the exact argument the opposition carried to the electorate in 1989: that the question was not only who received the money but why the government had disabled every instrument that might have found out.

The government's defensive errors compounded. The Comptroller and Auditor General's report on the Bofors purchase, tabled in July 1989, criticised the evaluation process and the haste of the selection, giving the opposition an official Indian document to quote in the election season. The Congress campaign of October and November 1989 never found an answer to the accumulated record, in part because answering required litigating details that voters had long since compressed into a single judgement about the government and honesty. Inder Malhotra's retrospective assessments of Rajiv Gandhi's decline return repeatedly to this compression: by the autumn of 1989, Bofors functioned less as an allegation than as a synonym, in the bazaars and in the press, for a governing style. A scandal becomes structurally dangerous to a dominant party when it gives scattered opponents a common vocabulary. Bofors gave the Janata Dal, the BJP and the Left the same sentence to say in three different accents, and the 1989 campaign was the first national campaign in which the incumbent Congress was defined, across most of the country, by a word it did not control.

## Exit over Absorption: The Dissidents Leave the Congress

The Congress System, as Kothari described it in his 1964 essay in Asian Survey and in Politics in India in 1970, processed dissent by ingestion. A Congress faction that lost an internal contest did not, in the normal run of the system, walk out and found a rival; it bargained, sulked, intrigued and waited for the factional wheel to turn, because the Congress organisation was the only road to office and its central leadership arbitrated among the factions that composed it. Opposition parties, in Kothari's famous formulation, were parties of pressure, influencing a Congress that was the party of consensus. The system had survived defections before, and it had survived one great schism, [the 1969 structural-cultural rupture](/2019/03/26/1969-congress-split-decisive-moment/), by the simple device of letting the departing organisation wither while the leader who kept the mass following kept the name, the symbol and the state. Exit, in the system's experience, was a mistake the exiter paid for.

The resignations and expulsions of 1987 and 1988 were engineered, by both sides, in the belief that the old law still held. V.P. Singh resigned from the cabinet in April 1987. In October 1987 he and his associates, among them Arun Nehru, Rajiv Gandhi's former ministerial colleague and cousin, Arif Mohammad Khan, the former minister who had resigned in 1986 over the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, and Satya Pal Malik, founded the Jan Morcha, a political forum that announced clean politics as its programme and made no secret of its intention to become the nucleus of an anti-Congress alignment. The Congress expelled V.P. Singh from the party's primary membership in July 1988, after his campaign had begun drawing crowds the leadership could no longer dismiss. The expulsion was meant as a demonstration of the old law: the dissident outside would fade, as dissidents had faded since the 1950s.

The Allahabad by-election of June 1988 supplied the first hard evidence that the law had been repealed. V.P. Singh contested the Lok Sabha seat vacated by Amitabh Bachchan and defeated Sunil Shastri, the Congress candidate and son of Lal Bahadur Shastri, in a constituency the Congress regarded as its own, in a campaign run explicitly on the Bofors record. A by-election is a small instrument, but its political reading was large: an expelled dissident, campaigning on corruption against the full weight of the ruling party in its heartland state, had won. The verdict of Allahabad is the point at which exit stopped being a threat the Congress leadership could discount, and it fed directly into the consolidation that followed. On 11 October 1988, the Jan Morcha merged with the Janata Party and the Lok Dal to constitute the Janata Dal, with V.P. Singh as its president. The National Front, formed in August 1988 with the Andhra Pradesh chief minister N.T. Rama Rao as its chairman and V.P. Singh as its convener, supplied the federal shell inside which the new party could negotiate with regional forces that would never have merged into a Delhi-centred formation.

Seat adjustment did the rest of the work. In the 1989 election the Janata Dal contested a limited slate, of the order of two hundred and forty seats, and reached understandings, formal in some states and tacit in others, that reduced the three-cornered contests through which the Congress had so often slipped back to power on a divided opposition vote. The BJP contested its own slate of about two hundred and twenty-five seats and benefited in the northern states from the same anti-Congress concentration. The mechanism was unglamorous and decisive. The Congress System had depended, at the level of the constituency, on the opposition's inability to agree on a single challenger; the factional energies that the system once absorbed inside the Congress were, in 1989, arrayed outside it in a rough but workable division of the electoral field. When the counting finished on 26 November 1989, the party of consensus held 197 seats and no path to 272 that did not run through parties campaigning explicitly against it.

### How did the Jan Morcha become the Janata Dal in October 1988?

The Jan Morcha, founded in October 1987 as a forum rather than a party, merged on 11 October 1988 with the Janata Party and the Lok Dal to form the Janata Dal. V.P. Singh became the party's president, and the merger gave the dissident platform a party's machinery, state units and a recognised place in the seat adjustments of 1989.

The merger ended a year of negotiation among the non-Congress opposition's fragments and converted a protest forum into the vehicle that would head the Union government thirteen months later, with V.P. Singh, the Jan Morcha's most prominent figure, at its head.

The deeper change the merger registered is easy to miss because mergers were a familiar feature of opposition politics. The Janata Party itself had been a merger, in January 1977, of the Congress (O), the Bharatiya Lok Dal, the Jana Sangh and the Socialist Party, and it had disintegrated by 1979 under the weight of its dual loyalties and leadership rivalries. The Janata Dal of 1988 was built by men who had watched that disintegration from inside: Chandra Shekhar had been the Janata Party's president, Devi Lal had governed Haryana under its banner, and V.P. Singh's recruits included veterans of the 1977 experiment alongside the Congress dissidents of 1987. What distinguished the 1988 consolidation was not its components but its federal restraint. The Janata Dal did not demand that regional parties dissolve into it. The Telugu Desam, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam and the Asom Gana Parishad stayed outside the party and inside the Front, keeping their state machines and their chief ministers while accepting a common prime ministerial candidate in waiting. The absorptive instinct, the demand that allies become subordinates, had been the Congress System's signature. The National Front's founding bargain was its negation, and the bargain held through the election precisely because it asked so little of the pride of its members.

The contrast with the system's earlier great exit fixes the scale of the change. When the Congress split in 1969, the departing organisation, the Congress (O) of Morarji Desai, K. Kamaraj and S. Nijalingappa, carried away much of the old party's machinery and many of its most experienced organisers, and in the general election of 1971 it won sixteen seats. The remnant faded into the Janata merger of 1977 and disappeared as an independent force, teaching a generation of politicians that the leader who kept the mass following kept everything and the organisers who walked out kept nothing. The exiters of 1987 and 1988 inverted the lesson's every term. V.P. Singh carried away no machinery worth the name, a forum without state units and a party thirteen months old at polling day, and he won the prime ministership. What had changed between 1971 and 1989 was not the quality of the men who left but the condition of the vessel they left: the Congress of 1971 could still mobilise a majority behind a leader, while the Congress of 1989 was a leader, a family and a flag, with the organisation that once converted following into seats reduced to nominated committees. Exit succeeds when the centre has already hollowed, and the hollowness, accumulated through the two decades since 1969, is the precondition the verdict exposed.

## The Congress System and Its End: A Seven-Indicator Comparison

Kothari's framework had always contained the seed of its own expiry date. A system in which the ruling party performs the functions of both government and opposition, absorbing every organised grievance into its factional interior, requires two conditions: the ruling party must be present everywhere as a plausible vehicle for ambition, and exit from it must be visibly costlier than patience inside it. [The Nehruvian-era Congress System baseline](/2019/01/25/nehruvian-era-settlement-three-tensions/) had rested on the organisational inheritance of the freedom movement and on the absence of any rival all-India machine. By 1989 both conditions had been quietly consumed. Indira Gandhi's centralisation after 1969 had converted state Congress units into nominated committees; organisational elections inside the party had lapsed for the better part of two decades; the arbitration that once made factional patience rational had been replaced by the high command's favour, which is a currency that buys obedience but not loyalty. The 1989 verdict did not destroy the Congress System's machinery. It revealed, in the only forum whose verdicts cannot be argued with, that the machinery had already been hollowed, and that a sufficiently determined set of exiters could beat the shell.

Pradeep Chhibber, in Democracy without Associations, published in 1999, gave the post-1989 fragmentation its party-system reading: the state's expanding regulatory and distributive reach had raised the returns to capturing it, while the collapse of encompassing party organisation left the field to parties mobilising narrower, more intense constituencies. Jaffrelot's account in India's Silent Revolution complements the institutional reading with a social one, tracing how the political awakening of the Other Backward Classes in the northern states supplied the fragmentation with its human material. Both readings describe a transformation that the 1989 verdict ratified rather than initiated, and both are consistent with the verdict-priority claim of this article: a ratifying election can be the consequential event of an era precisely because ratification at the ballot box is what converts drift into structure.

The comparison below, which this article calls the seven-indicator Congress System comparison, sets the system's working condition before the 1989 verdict against its condition after it. Each indicator names a feature of the absorptive order, the form that feature took in the era of Congress dominance, and the form the 1989 verdict left behind.

| Indicator | Congress System before the 1989 verdict | Indian politics after the 1989 verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Single-party dominance | The Congress won Lok Sabha majorities in 1952, 1957, 1962, 1971, 1980 and 1984, and governed alone as the default party of the Union | No party won a Lok Sabha majority in 1989, 1991, 1996, 1998, 1999, 2004 or 2009; the era closed without a single-party majority |
| Intra-party absorption | Dissent was processed inside the Congress organisation, whose factions performed the role of an internal opposition | The dissidents of 1987 exited, organised the Janata Dal and were rewarded with office; exit displaced absorption as the dissident's rational move |

| Organisational-discipline architecture | The Congress high command arbitrated factional disputes and distributed tickets through an organisational hierarchy | Ticket distribution and alliance management became inter-party negotiations; no single high command could arbitrate the system |

Seven indicators, seven inversions. The table's discipline is deliberate: each row records a change in how politics was conducted, not merely a change in who won. Governments had fallen before 1989, and the Congress had lost power before, in 1977. What had never happened before was the simultaneous inversion of the rules by which governments were made, oppositions imagined themselves, dissidents calculated their futures and regional parties priced their support. The 1989 verdict is the only election in the republic's history, up to the close of the coalition era in 2014, that inverts all seven.

## 1977 Was the Rehearsal: Why the Janata Precedent Misleads

The obvious objection to everything argued so far is that India had seen a non-Congress government before, and within living political memory. In March 1977, twelve years before the verdict at the centre of this article, the Janata Party swept the post-Emergency election in the north, the Congress was reduced to 154 seats, and Morarji Desai became the first non-Congress Prime Minister. If the Congress System ended anywhere, a reasonable reader might say, it ended then, and 1989 merely repeated 1977 with weaker actors. The objection is the strongest available to the standard reading, and it deserves a direct answer built on the institutional difference between the two moments, a difference [the 1977 Janata coalition's institutional fragility](/2019/04/28/1977-janata-verdict-explained/) makes visible.

The Janata Party of 1977 was a merger, not a coalition. Its constituents dissolved their separate organisations into a single party, contested on a single symbol and formed a government holding its own majority of 295 seats. Structurally, Janata aspired to become a replacement Congress: one big tent, one parliamentary party, one internal factional economy. When its factions quarrelled, they quarrelled inside a single organisation, and when the organisation split in 1979 over the dual-membership question and the leadership ambitions of Charan Singh and Jagjivan Ram, the government fell and the system reverted. The Congress returned to office in January 1980 with 353 seats, and by 1984 it held 414. The 1977 rupture, in other words, was absorbed by the larger pattern: a consolidated challenger had displaced the Congress for thirty months and then handed the polity back, its organisational inheritance spent. The Congress System as a description of how power was organised survived 1977 damaged but recognisable, because the challenger had copied the system's form and the form itself was not repudiated.

The 1989 verdict repudiated the form. The National Front did not merge its constituents. The Janata Dal remained one party among allies; the BJP and the Left, the government's outside props, remained not merely separate but ideologically armed against each other. Government formation became a public negotiation among sovereign parties, conducted in the open, priced in portfolios and policy commitments, and terminable by any party that judged its price unmet. That is the institutional content of the phrase multi-party negotiation, and it has no analogue in 1977. The proof of the distinction lies in what each rupture left behind. The fall of the Janata government in 1979 restored single-party rule for a decade. The fall of the National Front government in November 1990 restored nothing: it produced another negotiated government under Chandra Shekhar, then the Congress minority government of P.V. Narasimha Rao sustained by abstentions and outside understandings, then the thirteen-day BJP government of 1996, the United Front experiments, the National Democratic Alliance and the United Progressive Alliance. After 1989 the negotiation never again stopped being the method. After 1977 it had barely started.

There is a personal thread inside the institutional one. [The office-rebuilding's legible end](/2019/05/09/operation-blue-star-1984-assassination/) came in October 1984, and the 1984 election that followed was the Congress System's last full performance: a single party, a single leader, a sympathy wave converted into 414 seats. The five years between that performance and the 1989 verdict are the shortest distance any dominant system in a democracy has travelled from its largest mandate to its structural end. Rajiv Gandhi's government supplied the causes: the Shah Bano reversal of 1986, which alienated constituencies in both directions; the IPKF deployment in Sri Lanka from July 1987, which bled men and money into an unwinnable intervention; the Bofors record; and a prime ministerial style that treated Parliament and party organisation as audiences rather than institutions. The verdict was contingent, as this article argues, but the contingency operated on ground that the 1984 to 1989 government had thoroughly prepared.

### Why did the BJP and the Left Front support the same government in 1989?

Both blocs calculated that keeping the Congress out of office outweighed their hostility to each other. The BJP, risen to 85 seats in the Election Commission's count, gained national standing as guarantor of a non-Congress government. The Left Front gained a Union government committed to federal restraint and secular administration, and neither bloc joined the cabinet.

Each preserved the right to withdraw from the arrangement at its own choosing. The Left Front, anchored in West Bengal and Kerala, kept its distance from the government's economic management; the BJP kept its Ayodhya programme in reserve, and exercised the withdrawal right in October 1990, when the arrest of L.K. Advani at Samastipur converted the party's reserve position into the government's death warrant.

The support arrangement's oddity was its honesty. Earlier Indian governments had depended on unacknowledged understandings; the December 1989 arrangement announced its own contradictions and governed inside them for eleven months. The Lok Sabha debates of the session, from the confidence discussion of December 1989 onward, record opposition speakers from the Congress benches taunting the treasury with the incompatibility of its props, and ministers replying, in substance, that a government formed to end one-party dominance could not be expected to reproduce one-party coherence. The taunt and the reply together define the era the verdict opened: coherence had stopped being the test of a government, and negotiability had replaced it.

## The Campaign of 1989: A Negative Mandate by Design

Campaigns usually promise. The Janata Dal campaign of October and November 1989 accused, and the accusation was a strategy rather than a failure of imagination. V.P. Singh's managers understood the arithmetic of the anti-Congress field: the Janata Dal, the BJP and the regional parties held incompatible positive programmes, from the BJP's Ayodhya commitment to the Left's land and labour agenda to the regional parties' federal demands, and any attempt to draft a single affirmative platform would have reopened the quarrels that the seat adjustments had papered over. Bofors was the one text every anti-Congress voter could read. The campaign therefore ran on the procurement record, on the figure of the middleman, and on V.P. Singh's personal biography as the minister who had resigned rather than bury an inquiry. The National Front's manifesto and the Common Programme adopted after the election contained the expected commitments on federalism, electoral reform, agricultural prices and the autonomy of the broadcast media, but the campaign's energy, its slogans and its stump oratory ran on corruption.

The strategic choice had a price that the verdict's defenders should acknowledge, because the price explains the government's later poverty of programme. A coalition assembled against a scandal inherits no agreement about anything except the scandal. When the National Front government took office on 2 December 1989, it held a cabinet of considerable administrative experience and a legislative agenda of remarkable thinness, beyond the promises every opposition makes about commissions of inquiry, a Lok Pal bill and the strengthening of the panchayats. The government fell behind even on the Bofors investigation itself: the Joint Investigation Team's work through 1990 produced charge-sheets against intermediaries but no resolution touching the political questions the campaign had posed, and the CBI's handling of the case, including the controversy over the January 1990 release of the Swiss account details to the press, kept the government entangled in the very machinery of insinuation it had campaigned against. None of this diminishes the verdict, on the argument of this article. It sharpens the argument: the verdict's significance never depended on the quality of the government it installed, because the verdict's work was done on the day the results were counted, when the seat distribution made single-party dominance arithmetically impossible and made negotiation the standing method of Indian government.

Rajiv Gandhi's campaign deserves its share of the record. The Congress fought on its record of technological missions, the panchayati raj bill that the Rajya Sabha had defeated in October 1989, and the claim that the opposition offered instability as its only programme. The claim about instability was, as a prediction, entirely accurate. Its failure as an argument is the measure of the moment: the electorate of 1989, including the eighteen-year-old voters enfranchised by the Sixty-First Amendment who had no adult memory of any government but Rajiv Gandhi's, preferred the risk of negotiation to the certainty of incumbency. Political scientists describing the result have sometimes called it a negative verdict, and the phrase is fair as far as it goes. What the phrase misses is that negativity, in 1989, was itself a constitutional position: the refusal, by the largest combined vote in the republic's history to that point, to return the instrument of single-party dominance to the party that had held it for forty years with one interruption.

## Making a Government: December 1989 and the Common Programme

President R. Venkataraman left the fullest first-person record of the transition in his memoir My Presidential Years, published in 1994, and the memoir's account of the last week of November 1989 is a document of the old order discovering its own end. Venkataraman records his consultations with constitutional authorities and party leaders, his attention to the precedent of inviting the leader of the largest party, and his awareness that the largest party's leader might not accept the burden of a minority he could not sustain. The memoir is equally valuable for what it shows about Rajiv Gandhi's calculation. The Congress president commanded 197 seats and a party machine present in every district of the country; a bolder or more desperate leader might have attempted a government and dared the House to refuse it supply. Rajiv Gandhi declined, and his stated reason, that a government formed without a workable majority would serve neither the country nor the party, was constitutionally impeccable and politically fateful. By declining, the Congress conceded the premise of the new era, that office belonged to whoever could assemble a negotiation, and conceded it at the very moment of the era's founding. When the Congress next held the prime ministership, in June 1991, it did so as a minority government practising the arts of survival that the 1989 verdict had made the common curriculum of Indian politics.

The fortnight between the counting of votes and the swearing-in of 2 December 1989 is the first worked example of the new grammar, and it repays attention because every participant behaved as if the old rules might still apply. President R. Venkataraman followed the convention of inviting the leader of the single largest party. Rajiv Gandhi, whose Congress held 197 seats, assessed the House, found no combination willing to sustain him, and declined the attempt, a decision his party's critics inside and outside have debated ever since. The arithmetic then ran through the National Front's parliamentary party, which elected V.P. Singh its leader, and through the separate decisions of the BJP parliamentary party and the Left parties to extend outside support. Devi Lal, the Haryana chief minister whose claim to the leadership had its own following inside the Janata Dal, became Deputy Prime Minister, a settlement of ambition by portfolio that the Congress System would once have conducted inside a single party and that the new order conducted between the two most powerful men of a coalition. Chandra Shekhar, the senior socialist who had expected the leadership and been passed over, stayed inside the party, dissenting in public from the first month. The government's internal tensions were thus not an accident of its second year. They were installed at its christening, the price in personnel of a verdict that had rewarded a coalition for existing rather than for agreeing.

The National Front's Common Programme, adopted by the Front's constituents and reflected in the President's address to the joint session, committed the government to a recognisably opposition-era agenda: a Lok Pal to investigate corruption at high levels, electoral reforms including state funding and a curb on defections, implementation of the Sarkaria Commission's federal recommendations, autonomy for Doordarshan and All India Radio, a national agricultural policy, and the strengthening of panchayati raj institutions. Readers working through the Programme alongside the Election Commission's seat tables, and assembling both into a teaching deck on the verdict, can open the resulting presentation in a browser through a [PowerPoint viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) without installed software, which is how the document circulates among students of the period. The Programme's interest for this article lies in its silences. It contained no commitment on the scale of the Mandal implementation that would define the government's reputation eleven months later, and no machinery for arbitrating the disputes among the Front's own constituents, let alone between the government and its two outside props. The Front had written a programme for ending Congress rule and had omitted, because it could not have written, a programme for governing together.

### What did the National Front Common Programme actually promise?

The Common Programme committed the National Front government to a Lok Pal against corruption in high office, electoral reform, implementation of the Sarkaria Commission's recommendations on centre-state relations, statutory autonomy for Doordarshan and All India Radio, a new agricultural policy and constitutional strengthening of the panchayats.

The document reflected the coalition's anti-Congress origins, emphasising accountability and federal restraint above any programme of social engineering, and it contained no commitment to implement the Mandal Commission's reservation recommendations, the decision that would define the government in August 1990.

Measured against its promises, the government's eleven months delivered fragments. The Prasar Bharati Act, granting statutory autonomy to the broadcast media, passed in 1990, although its operationalisation waited for a later decade. The Lok Pal was introduced and lapsed. The panchayat amendments that the Congress had failed to carry in 1989 were reintroduced in altered form and also waited. The Lok Sabha debates of December 1989 to November 1990, the third of the mandatory primary records for this article, show a government spending its debating capital on survival: on the Bofors investigation's pace, on the Kashmir deterioration that consumed its first spring, on the dismissal politics of state governments, and from August 1990 on the reservation order and the temple movement. A reader who comes to the debates expecting the deliberative record of a government executing a programme will be disappointed. A reader who comes expecting the record of a new political method being improvised under fire will find the debates inexhaustible.

## Governing inside the Contradiction: The Government's Working Record

Before its two terminal decisions, the National Front government accumulated a working record that the standard reading, in its hurry to reach Mandal, tends to skip, and the record matters to this article because it demonstrates the new method operating as routine administration rather than as crisis improvisation. The government's first crisis arrived from Kashmir within weeks of its swearing-in. The kidnapping of Rubaiya Sayeed, daughter of the home minister Mufti Mohammad Sayeed, by the Jammu and Kashmir Liberation Front on 8 December 1989, and the government's decision on 13 December 1989 to release five detained militants in exchange for her freedom, was judged harshly at the time and since, as the moment the Union signalled negotiability to an armed insurgency at the insurgency's opening hour. In January 1990 the government appointed Jagmohan as governor of Jammu and Kashmir; Farooq Abdullah resigned as chief minister in protest, and the valley slid into the full insurgency whose shadow lay across the government's whole tenure. The episode is recorded here because it shows the coalition's decision structure under stress: the home minister belonged to the Janata Dal's Kashmir-facing wing, the governor's appointment carried the BJP's assent, and the price of the exchange decision was paid by a government that could not survive a resignation from any of its three directions.

The federal file shows the method working in the opposite direction. A government dependent on regional parties and on a Left Front entrenched in West Bengal and Kerala could not use Article 356 as its predecessors had used it, and the era of routine dismissal of state governments effectively closed during these years, to be codified later by the Supreme Court's judgment in S.R. Bommai in March 1994. The Inter-State Council, recommended by the Sarkaria Commission in its report of October 1987 and promised in the Common Programme, was constituted in May 1990, a small institutional brick in the federal wall the Front had pledged to build. The Prasar Bharati legislation of 1990 wrote broadcast autonomy into statute. The government's economic management, under Finance Minister Madhu Dandavate, held the fiscal line of an economy approaching the payments crisis that would break over his successors in 1991, and its budget of March 1990 raised the income-tax exemption threshold and the rhetoric of social expenditure in the socialist idiom its finance minister had carried since the Janata years. None of these entries is dramatic. Their collective significance is that a government without a majority, sustained by opponents of each other, nonetheless legislated, appointed, negotiated and budgeted for eleven months, refuting by performance the Congress campaign's prediction that a negotiated government would be a paralysed one.

The anti-Mandal agitation of August to October 1990 belongs to the government's record as well as to its fall. The reservation order of August 1990 applied to central government services and public-sector undertakings, and the movement against it, led by upper-caste students in the northern universities and amplified by a press largely hostile to the decision, produced the season's defining images: the attempt at self-immolation by Rajeev Goswami at a Delhi bus stop on 19 September 1990, and the dozens of similar attempts by young men and women that followed across the northern states. The movement's social composition was never a secret: it defended a distribution of state employment in which the upper castes, a small fraction of the population, held a share of senior posts that the Mandal Commission's tables had documented at many times their proportion. Politicians of the Congress and, in quieter registers, of the BJP's urban wings sympathised with the agitators; the government's OBC base read the agitation as confirmation of the announcement's necessity. Indian politics had acquired, in the space of one autumn, two mass mobilisations organised around the two great questions, caste and temple, that the Congress System had spent forty years keeping inside its own committee rooms. That both questions escaped the committee rooms in the same season is the final evidence that the absorptive order the verdict had pronounced dead was dead in fact.

## Nine Months of Government, and the Two Decisions That Ended It

The National Front government held office from 2 December 1989 to 7 November 1990, when it lost the confidence of the Lok Sabha by 142 votes to 346. Its tenure divides into a first phase of consolidation, in which the coalition's contradictions were managed, and a second phase, from the summer of 1990, in which two decisions, one by the Prime Minister and one against him, destroyed the arrangement's foundations. The first decision was V.P. Singh's dismissal of Devi Lal from the deputy prime ministership, announced at the start of August 1990 after a season of public warfare between the two men, fought through rival rallies and through Devi Lal's interviews attacking the Prime Minister's leadership. The dismissal was meant to establish that the coalition had a single centre of authority. It established instead that authority inside the Janata Dal was divisible, and it freed the government's most formidable organiser to work against it from outside the cabinet.

The second decision followed within the week. On 7 August 1990, V.P. Singh announced in Parliament the government's acceptance of the Mandal Commission's principal recommendation: 27 per cent reservation for the Other Backward Classes in central government employment, with the report's broader architecture to follow. The announcement's timing has been debated since the day it was made. The sympathetic reading, given by V.P. Singh's associates and by Jaffrelot's account of the silent revolution, holds that the commitment was genuine and long-held, rooted in the Janata Dal manifesto and in the Prime Minister's reading of the social direction of north Indian politics. The hostile reading, given at the time by Congress speakers and by Chandra Shekhar's camp inside the Janata Dal, holds that the announcement was a defensive strike in the leadership war, designed to consolidate the OBC electorate behind the Prime Minister against Devi Lal's challenge and to bury the internal quarrel under a social earthquake. The documentary record cannot fully settle the motive, and this article marks the question as disputed. What the record settles is the announcement's effect: it reordered the political field around caste at the precise moment when the BJP was preparing to reorder it around religion, and the collision of the two reorderings is the story of the government's fall.

The temple movement had been gathering since the shilanyas, the foundation-laying for a Ram temple at Ayodhya, in November 1989, days before the general election. On 25 September 1990, the BJP president L.K. Advani began a rath yatra from Somnath in Gujarat toward Ayodhya, travelling in a vehicle styled as a chariot, drawing crowds that dwarfed the anti-Mandal protests in their organisation and menace. The yatra's destination and date were chosen to force the question of the temple onto the government's agenda in the last week of October 1990. The Bihar government of Lalu Prasad Yadav, a Janata Dal chief minister, arrested Advani at Samastipur on 23 October 1990. The BJP withdrew its outside support from the National Front government the same day. The Left parties, confronted with a government they still preferred to any alternative, stayed; the arithmetic, which the support arrangement had suspended for eleven months, resumed its operation. On 7 November 1990 the Lok Sabha refused the government its confidence, 142 votes to 346, and V.P. Singh resigned. Chandra Shekhar, who had split the Janata Dal with a bloc of MPs counted at the time at about sixty-four, formed a government on 10 November 1990 with Congress support from outside, completing the era's second demonstration that office in the new order belonged to whoever could assemble a negotiation, however narrow its base.

The Ayodhya mobilisation that broke the government belongs to a later chapter of this series and is treated there in full; here it stands as a forward reference, the point at which the verdict's creation collided with the next structural force gathering in Indian politics. What matters for the adjudication that follows is narrower. The National Front government was destroyed by the interaction of its own composition with the two great mobilisations of 1990, caste and temple. Its destruction changed nothing about the method by which it had been made. The method, negotiation among parties under a hung arithmetic, survived the government, survived its successor, and survived the century.

## Mandal Would Have Had Another Vehicle

The verdict-priority claim stands or falls on the counterfactual at its centre, so the counterfactual should be stated with precision and then tested against the record. The claim is not that the August 1990 implementation was unimportant. It is that the implementation was over-determined: the social and electoral pressures behind OBC reservation at the centre had accumulated through so many independent channels, over so many years, that some Union government, within a small number of years of 1990, would have carried a version of it. The verdict, by contrast, was under-determined: it depended on a chain of contingent acts inside a thirty-month window, and its failure at any of several points would have left the Congress System's obituary unwritten for another electoral cycle, perhaps for a generation.

Consider the channels through which Mandal's substance was already moving before V.P. Singh announced it. The Commission itself was a creation of the Janata Party government: Morarji Desai's cabinet appointed the Second Backward Classes Commission under B.P. Mandal in January 1979, and the Commission reported on 31 December 1980, estimating the Other Backward Classes at 52 per cent of the population and recommending 27 per cent reservation in Union government posts and public-sector undertakings. The report then sat with the Congress governments of Indira Gandhi and Rajiv Gandhi for a decade, unimplemented and also unrepudiated, a loaded instrument on the shelf of every subsequent government. At the state level, the substance had been law for years: Bihar's 1978 order under Karpoori Thakur reserved 26 per cent of state posts for backward classes, Karnataka operated an extensive quota regime built through the 1970s, and Tamil Nadu's reservation total had stood above 60 per cent for decades under governments of both Dravidian parties. In the political marketplace of the north, the socialist tradition descending from Ram Manohar Lohia had carried caste-based reservation as a central plank since the 1960s, and the parties that would dominate the 1990s in Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, the Samajwadi Party and the Janata Dal's successor formations, were assembling the OBC electorate into their founding constituency whether Delhi acted or not.

Jaffrelot's India's Silent Revolution is, on this point, the verdict-priority reader's best witness, although Jaffrelot's own emphasis differs. His demonstration that the lower-caste political awakening proceeded state by state, election by election, through the 1970s and 1980s, driven by the spread of education, the green revolution's new rural proprietors and the organisational work of caste associations, establishes that the demand for reservation at the centre was the surface expression of a deep social process. A process of that depth does not require a particular prime minister. It requires an occasion, and occasions multiply: a Congress government seeking a new social coalition after 1991, a United Front government dependent on OBC-led state parties after 1996, a coalition of any colour arithmetically unable to refuse its own constituents. The strongest evidence for the counterfactual is what happened when the implementation did come under legal attack: the Supreme Court's judgment in Indra Sawhney, delivered on 16 November 1992, upheld the 27 per cent reservation with the creamy-layer exclusion, and no Union government of any composition thereafter proposed its repeal. By 1992 the substance of Mandal had become part of the settlement every party accepted. Settlements of that kind are not the work of irreplaceable men. They are the record of pressures no government could finally refuse.

None of this requires diminishing what the August 1990 announcement cost or what it took. V.P. Singh spent his government's remaining capital on the announcement, faced the anti-reservation movement in the streets of the northern cities, and never recovered his authority inside his own coalition afterward. The verdict-priority claim honours that record by placing it correctly: the announcement was an act of political courage performed inside a structure that the verdict, eleven months earlier, had created. A prime minister heading a single-party majority government in 1990, had the Congress won the 1989 election, would have faced the Mandal report with the same reluctance his party had shown since 1980, and the pressure would have built through the states until some later arithmetic forced the question. The man was replaceable in the Mandal story. In the verdict story, the sequence of April 1987 to December 1989 runs through his biography at every joint, and no substitute biography was on offer.

## The Historians' Quarrel: Three Readings of One Election

The disagreement this article adjudicates has a scholarly literature, and the literature's divisions are worth mapping because each school reads the same Election Commission tables and finds a different event inside them. The first reading is the biographical school, whose exemplary figure is Inder Malhotra. Malhotra, who covered the period as a senior editor and whose Indira Gandhi biography of 1989 set the frame for the succession struggles, treated 1989 as a chapter in the decline of a dynasty and the rise of an unlikely challenger. In the biographical frame, elections are things that happen to leaders; the verdict is the sum of Rajiv Gandhi's errors and V.P. Singh's virtues, and its consequences are measured in the careers it made and unmade. The frame explains the campaign superbly and the structure poorly, because it cannot say why the same electorate that forgave the Congress its errors for forty years stopped forgiving them in 1989, or why no leader of the old type has reassembled the old majority since.

The second reading is the social school, whose exemplary figure is Christophe Jaffrelot. India's Silent Revolution reads 1989 as one station in the long transfer of power from the upper castes to the lower, a transfer accomplished through the ballot box over decades and across the northern states at uneven speed. In the social frame, the Mandal implementation is the moment the transfer reached the Union government's own hiring hall, and it naturally outranks the verdict that merely seated the transfer's temporary agent. The frame explains the content of post-1989 politics, the caste parties, the reservation settlements, the social profile of legislatures, better than any rival. Its weakness is timing. The silent revolution had been proceeding since the 1960s through state after state without breaking the Congress System at the centre; Bihar's backward politics under Karpoori Thakur in 1978 coexisted with a Congress majority in Delhi. A social tide explains why the sea rose. It does not explain why the dam broke in November 1989 rather than in 1984 or 1996, and the breaking of the dam is the event under adjudication here.

The third reading is the institutional school. Pradeep Chhibber's Democracy without Associations located the fragmentation of the party system in the changing economy of the Indian state, whose expanding reach made its capture worth narrower and narrower mobilisations as the encompassing parties decayed. Yogendra Yadav, writing on the elections of the 1990s, described a third electoral system succeeding the Congress System and the 1977 to 1989 interregnum, a system whose rules were coalition, regional weight and social bloc voting. The institutional frame explains the durability of the post-1989 order, its survival of governments of every colour, better than either rival. Its weakness is agency: structures in the institutional account tend to change by accumulation, and the account can understate how much of November 1989 depended on named persons doing specific, risky things in sequence, a Swedish radio broadcast, a finance minister's resignation, a by-election in Allahabad, an expulsion that its authors intended as a burial and that functioned as a launch. The verdict-priority claim defended in this article is the institutional reading completed by the biographical evidence: the structure was ripe, the tide was rising, and the event still had to be made, by a man whose biography happened to fit the making. Adjudicating among the schools, the article finds the institutional reading correct about what changed, the social reading correct about what the change contained, and the biographical reading correct about how the change was triggered, and it ranks the verdict first because the trigger and the structural break coincide in it, while the Mandal implementation belongs wholly to the content the break released.

## The Adjudication: Verdict First

The named disagreement can be judged on the evidence assembled. The Mandal-priority reading, in its strongest form, runs as follows. Elections are frequent and their results decay; the 1989 verdict produced a government that lasted eleven months and a party that split within the year. The Mandal implementation, by contrast, altered the composition of the Indian state itself, redirected the life chances of hundreds of millions of citizens, survived judicial review in Indra Sawhney, and reorganised north Indian politics around caste for the remainder of the century. On any measure of consequences, the reading concludes, August 1990 outweighs November 1989, and V.P. Singh's place in history is the place of the man who signed the reservation order.

The verdict-priority reading answers on three grounds, each anchored in the primary record rather than in rhetoric. The first ground is replaceability, established in the preceding section: the Mandal substance had multiple vehicles, a decade of state-level operation and a judicial settlement waiting to happen, while the verdict had one chain of contingent acts and no understudy. The second ground is scope. The Mandal implementation changed policy within the political system; the verdict changed the system within which policy was thereafter made, inverting all seven indicators in the comparison table, from single-party dominance to the pricing of regional support. A change in the rules of government formation conditions every subsequent policy, including reservation policy itself, since the governments that consolidated Mandal after 1992 were coalition governments working the verdict's grammar. The third ground is durability under reversal. The verdict's creations were tested by the fall of two governments within eighteen months, by the Congress restoration of 1991 and by the BJP's growth through the 1990s, and the method the verdict installed survived every test: no Union government between 1989 and 2014 was formed by any other method than inter-party negotiation, because no party in that span won the majority that would have made negotiation optional.

The adjudication therefore finds for the verdict, with one concession recorded in full. The Mandal-priority reading is correct about the depth of August 1990 in the lives of citizens, and a history of Indian society, as distinct from a history of Indian government, may reasonably keep Mandal first. The question this article set out to answer is a question about Indian politics and its structure: what did the 1989 verdict do, and why does V.P. Singh's role in producing it outweigh his subsequent implementation? To that question the answer is the namable claim with which this article began and which the evidence sustains. V.P. Singh's lasting consequence was the 1989 verdict; Mandal would have come from another vehicle if not from him, but the 1989 verdict was irreplaceable.

## What the Verdict Made Permanent

Permanence, in politics, is a claim about what successors could not undo, and the successors of 1989 supply the test. The Congress, returned to office in 1991 under P.V. Narasimha Rao, governed as a minority dependent on abstentions, and its managers spoke the language of floor management that the Congress System's prime ministers had never needed to learn. The elections of 1996, 1998 and 1999 produced governments assembled by public negotiation among parties that had campaigned against one another, and the United Progressive Alliance of 2004 reproduced, under Congress leadership, the exact architecture of December 1989: a core party in office, outside support from the Left, and a common minimum programme negotiated among sovereign constituents. The BJP's rise, the era's other great fact, also proceeded inside the verdict's grammar: the party of 85 seats in 1989 learned to assemble allies, moderate its programme in coalition documents and price regional support, and its governments of 1998 to 2004 were coalition constructions from first to last. Even the era's closing election of 2014, which returned a single-party majority for the first time in thirty years, was won by a party that had spent the intervening decades as a coalition animal and fought the election inside a pre-poll alliance. The verdict's deepest legacy may be the simplest to state. After November 1989, no serious participant in Indian politics expected power to arrive by inheritance inside a single organisation. Power arrived by negotiation, and everyone, including the heirs of the Congress System, reorganised their ambitions around that expectation.

The immediate sequel underlined the point with a second minority construction. Chandra Shekhar's government, formed on 10 November 1990 from a Janata Dal splinter of about sixty-four MPs and sustained by the Congress from outside, lasted until March 1991, when the Congress withdrew its support over the surveillance of Rajiv Gandhi's residence by Haryana police personnel and the country returned to the polls. The mid-term election of May and June 1991, conducted in the shadow of Rajiv Gandhi's assassination at Sriperumbudur on 21 May 1991, returned the Congress as the largest party with 244 seats, still short of a majority, and P.V. Narasimha Rao formed the minority government that governed for a full five-year term. Three governments in nineteen months, none resting on a single-party majority, each assembled by a distinct negotiation: the sequence reads, in retrospect, as the new system's training course, and every participant in it, including the Congress of Narasimha Rao with its celebrated floor management, graduated.

The grammar reached the presidency itself. Venkataraman's consultation method of November 1989, documented in his memoir, became the standing template for his successors: Presidents Sharma, Narayanan and Kalam each faced hung verdicts or fractured mandates and each worked through the same sequence of inviting, testing and counting that Rashtrapati Bhavan had improvised in 1989. K.R. Narayanan's insistence, during the government formations of 1998 and 1999, on written letters of support from alliance partners carried the 1989 lesson into constitutional practice: a claim to office had to be proved by arithmetic produced in advance, not asserted by the largest party's presumption. The Election Commission's conduct of the era's elections, and the anti-defection jurisprudence that the coalition decades generated, belong to the same settlement. Institutions learn from founding episodes, and December 1989 was the founding episode of the coalition republic's working constitution, the set of practices by which power changed hands for twenty-five years without a single disputed succession.

Vishwanath Pratap Singh died on 27 November 2008 in New Delhi, twenty-one years after the resignation that set the sequence in motion. His later decades were spent largely outside office, in painting, in illness and in intermittent returns to the causes of his prime ministership. The obituaries, predictably, led with Mandal. The argument of this article is that they led with the lesser of his two achievements, and that the greater one was hiding in the arithmetic of a November election: 197 seats for the party that had held 414, 143 for a party thirteen months old, 85 for a party that had held two, and a government assembled from the fragments by negotiation. The Congress System had survived a split, an Emergency and an assassination. It did not survive that arithmetic, and the man who produced the arithmetic, whatever else history asks of him, is the man who ended the system that Kothari had named.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was V.P. Singh?

Vishwanath Pratap Singh, born on 25 June 1931 in Allahabad and adopted heir to the Raja of Manda, was an Indian politician who served as Prime Minister from 2 December 1989 to 10 November 1990. He rose through the Congress organisation in Uttar Pradesh, became the state's chief minister in June 1980 and resigned that office in July 1982 accepting moral responsibility for his government's failure against dacoity. As finance minister from December 1984 he directed enforcement raids on major industrial houses and engaged the Fairfax Group to trace illegal Indian funds abroad; transferred to the defence ministry in January 1987, he ordered an inquiry into the HDW submarine contract and resigned from the cabinet in April 1987. Expelled from the Congress in July 1988, he founded the Jan Morcha in October 1987 and became president of the Janata Dal at its founding in October 1988, leading the National Front to power after the 1989 election. His government implemented the Mandal Commission's reservation recommendations in August 1990 and fell that November. He died in New Delhi on 27 November 2008.

### Q: What happened in the 1989 Indian general election?

The ninth general election, held on 22 and 26 November 1989, produced the first hung Parliament of the republic in which no successor party held a majority of its own. The Election Commission of India's Statistical Report records the Congress falling from 414 seats in 1984 to 197 seats on 39.53 per cent of the valid vote, the Janata Dal winning 143 seats on 17.79 per cent in its first election, and the Bharatiya Janata Party rising from two seats to 85 on 11.36 per cent, with the Communist Party of India (Marxist) at 33 seats and the Communist Party of India at 12. President R. Venkataraman first invited Rajiv Gandhi, leader of the largest party, who declined to attempt a government. The National Front, a pre-poll alliance formed in August 1988 around the Janata Dal and regional parties including the Telugu Desam Party, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam and the Asom Gana Parishad, then formed the government under V.P. Singh, sworn in on 2 December 1989, with outside support from both the BJP and the Left Front.

### Q: What was the Bofors scandal?

The Bofors scandal concerned the Indian Army's contract of March 1986 with the Swedish manufacturer Bofors for 410 FH-77B 155-millimetre howitzers, valued at roughly 1,437 crore rupees. On 16 April 1987 Swedish Radio disclosed that Bofors had paid commissions through Swiss accounts to intermediaries, in breach of the Rajiv Gandhi government's declared policy of admitting no middlemen in defence purchases. The Swedish National Audit Bureau confirmed in June 1988 that payments of at least 319 million Swedish kronor had been made. The Hindu's 1988 investigation, led by N. Ram and Chitra Subramaniam, published documents and diary notations that kept the recipients' identities at the centre of national debate, and the Comptroller and Auditor General's report of July 1989 criticised the purchase's evaluation and haste. A Joint Parliamentary Committee under B. Shankaranand reported in April 1988 that no evidence established payments to Indian politicians, a conclusion its opposition members refused to endorse in dissent notes. The scandal supplied the opposition's common vocabulary in the 1989 campaign and destroyed the Congress government's reputation for clean administration.

### Q: What was the National Front government?

The National Front government was the Union government headed by V.P. Singh from 2 December 1989 to November 1990, formed by the National Front alliance after the hung verdict of the 1989 general election. The National Front, constituted in August 1988 with N.T. Rama Rao as chairman and V.P. Singh as convener, joined the Janata Dal with regional parties including the Telugu Desam Party, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, the Asom Gana Parishad and the Indian Congress (Socialist). Its parliamentary strength fell well short of a majority, and the government survived on the outside support of the Bharatiya Janata Party, with 85 seats, and the Left Front, with roughly 52 seats, two blocs ideologically opposed to each other that agreed only on excluding the Congress. The cabinet was drawn from the Front alone, with Devi Lal as Deputy Prime Minister. The government adopted a Common Programme promising a Lok Pal, electoral reform, Sarkaria Commission federalism and broadcast autonomy, passed the Prasar Bharati Act, implemented the Mandal Commission's reservation recommendations in August 1990, and lost the confidence of the Lok Sabha on 7 November 1990 by 142 votes to 346.

### Q: Why did the V.P. Singh government fall?

The government fell because the two outside props and the internal unity that sustained it gave way in the same season. Inside the Janata Dal, the rivalry between V.P. Singh and Deputy Prime Minister Devi Lal broke into open warfare through the summer of 1990, ending in Devi Lal's dismissal at the start of August 1990 and the alienation of the party's strongest state organiser. On 7 August 1990 the Prime Minister announced the implementation of the Mandal Commission's reservation recommendations, which consolidated his OBC support and simultaneously set the government's remaining authority against the anti-reservation movement in the northern cities. The decisive blow came from the temple movement: L.K. Advani's rath yatra, begun from Somnath on 25 September 1990 toward Ayodhya, was stopped by his arrest at Samastipur in Bihar on 23 October 1990, and the Bharatiya Janata Party withdrew its outside support the same day. Deprived of the BJP's 85 votes, the government faced arithmetic it could not survive, and on 7 November 1990 the Lok Sabha refused it confidence by 142 votes to 346, whereupon V.P. Singh resigned.

### Q: How did the 1989 verdict change Indian politics?

The 1989 verdict ended the Congress System, the order in which a single dominant party absorbed dissent inside its own organisation and opposition parties acted as pressure groups without a path to office. Its seat arithmetic, Congress 197, Janata Dal 143, BJP 85, Left parties about 52 in the Election Commission's record, made government formation an act of negotiation among sovereign parties, and the National Front government, sustained simultaneously by the BJP and the Left from outside, proved that such negotiation could produce a working Union government. The verdict rewarded the dissident's exit over the factionalist's patience, since V.P. Singh moved from expulsion from the Congress in July 1988 to the prime ministership in seventeen months. It converted regional parties into shareholders of Union power. Every Union government from 1989 to 2014 was thereafter assembled by inter-party negotiation, because no party in that span won a Lok Sabha majority, and the seven indicators of the old system, dominance, absorption, opposition viability, coalition ease, exit costs, regional leverage and organisational discipline, all inverted at the same election.

### Q: What is the relationship between V.P. Singh and Mandal?

V.P. Singh implemented the Mandal Commission's central recommendation, and the implementation became so identified with his name that the association has obscured his larger consequence. The Second Backward Classes Commission, chaired by B.P. Mandal, was appointed by the Janata Party government in January 1979 and reported on 31 December 1980, estimating the Other Backward Classes at 52 per cent of the population and recommending 27 per cent reservation in central government posts. The report waited unimplemented through the Congress governments of the 1980s, while states including Bihar, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu operated backward-class reservations of their own. As Prime Minister, V.P. Singh announced the implementation in Parliament on 7 August 1990, accepting 27 per cent reservation in Union government employment. The announcement triggered the anti-reservation agitation, contributed to his government's fall, and was upheld with a creamy-layer exclusion by the Supreme Court in Indra Sawhney on 16 November 1992. The relationship is therefore double: Mandal made V.P. Singh's reputation, while his defenders argue the reservation would have been implemented by some later government in any case, and that his irreplaceable act was producing the 1989 verdict itself.

### Q: When was the Janata Dal founded, and which parties merged to form it?

The Janata Dal was founded on 11 October 1988, thirteen months before it headed the Union government, by the merger of three formations: the Jan Morcha, the forum V.P. Singh and his Congress dissident associates had built since October 1987; the Janata Party, the surviving organisation of the 1977 experiment, whose president Chandra Shekhar brought the socialist tradition and a parliamentary remnant; and the Lok Dal, the party of the northern peasant politics descended from Charan Singh, whose strength lay in Haryana, Uttar Pradesh and Rajasthan. V.P. Singh became the merged party's president. The merger followed the Allahabad by-election of June 1988, in which V.P. Singh had defeated the Congress candidate Sunil Shastri, and it converted a dissident platform into a legislative party with state units, recognised symbols and the standing to negotiate the seat adjustments of 1989. The Janata Dal contested about two hundred and forty seats in the general election and won 143 on 17.79 per cent of the valid vote in the Election Commission's count, a strike rate built on the opposition's division of the field against the Congress.

### Q: Why did V.P. Singh resign from Rajiv Gandhi's cabinet in April 1987?

V.P. Singh resigned from the Union cabinet in April 1987 after a sequence of confrontations over corruption investigations that had made his position inside the government untenable. As finance minister from December 1984 he had authorised Enforcement Directorate action against leading industrial houses and engaged the Fairfax Group to investigate Indian funds held illegally abroad, inquiries that embarrassed interests close to the political executive. In January 1987 Rajiv Gandhi transferred him from finance to the defence ministry. As defence minister he ordered an inquiry into allegations of kickbacks in the 1981 contract for HDW submarines from a West German yard. His resignation followed within weeks, days before Swedish Radio disclosed the Bofors payments on 16 April 1987. His public explanation, maintained consistently afterward, was that the government would not permit the investigations to proceed where the evidence led. The Congress leadership's counter-account held that he had resigned in resentment at losing the finance portfolio and then built a crusade on the grievance. The chronology is not in dispute; the motive remains contested between the two accounts.

### Q: Who chaired the Joint Parliamentary Committee on Bofors, and what did its report conclude?

The Joint Parliamentary Committee on the Bofors contract was appointed in August 1987 and chaired by B. Shankaranand, a Congress MP and former minister. The committee examined the March 1986 howitzer contract, the government's no-middlemen policy and the Swedish disclosures, hearing officials of the defence and finance ministries and reviewing the documentary record available to Parliament. Its report, tabled in April 1988, concluded that the evidence did not establish that payments by Bofors had reached Indian politicians or public officials, and it treated the commissions as a matter between the manufacturer and its agents abroad. The opposition parties refused to accept the finding: their members appended dissent notes that rejected the majority's reasoning and charged the committee with declining to pursue the identities behind the codenamed accounts, and the dissenting parties had earlier walked out of the proceedings in protest at its scope. The report therefore settled nothing. Its majority conclusion became a Congress campaign document, while its dissent notes became the opposition's charge-sheet, rehearsing in official language the argument about disabled investigation that the Janata Dal carried into the 1989 election.

### Q: From which constituency was V.P. Singh elected to the Lok Sabha in 1989?

V.P. Singh was elected in 1989 from Fatehpur in Uttar Pradesh, the constituency he represented in the ninth Lok Sabha through his prime ministership. His parliamentary route to the 1989 election had been prepared the previous year in the neighbouring political terrain of Allahabad, where he won the June 1988 by-election caused by Amitabh Bachchan's resignation, defeating the Congress candidate Sunil Shastri, son of the former Prime Minister Lal Bahadur Shastri. The Allahabad victory was the first electoral proof that an expelled Congress dissident campaigning on the Bofors record could defeat the ruling party's machine in its own heartland, and it converted V.P. Singh from a resigning minister into a plausible alternative prime minister in the eyes of the opposition parties then negotiating the Janata Dal merger. In the 1989 general election he chose Fatehpur, a seat within the same eastern Uttar Pradesh region where his Manda estate and his family's political base lay, and he held it while leading the National Front's national campaign. His election from a heartland seat mattered symbolically: the verdict that ended Congress dominance in the north was fronted by a man the north had itself returned.

### Q: What did Chandra Shekhar do after the National Front government fell in November 1990?

Chandra Shekhar, the senior socialist leader who had been passed over for the National Front leadership in December 1989 and had dissented from V.P. Singh's government from inside the Janata Dal, split the party when the government fell. He carried a bloc of MPs, counted at the time at about sixty-four, into a separate Janata Dal (Socialist) formation, and on 10 November 1990 he was sworn in as Prime Minister at the head of a minority government sustained by the Congress's outside support. The arrangement inverted the geometry of 1989: the Congress, excluded from office by the verdict, returned as the arbiter of a government's survival without joining it. Chandra Shekhar's administration lasted until March 1991, when the Congress withdrew its support over the presence of Haryana police personnel near Rajiv Gandhi's residence, and the country proceeded to the mid-term election of May and June 1991. His four months in office completed the demonstration the 1989 verdict had begun, that in the new order the prime ministership itself had become a negotiable office, available to a faction leader with sixty-four MPs and a patron, rather than the property of a majority party.

### Q: What was the result of the confidence vote that ended the V.P. Singh government?

The National Front government lost the confidence of the Lok Sabha on 7 November 1990 by 142 votes to 346, one of the heaviest defeats of a government on a confidence question in the republic's parliamentary history. The division followed the Bharatiya Janata Party's withdrawal of outside support on 23 October 1990, after L.K. Advani's arrest at Samastipur during the rath yatra, which removed the 85 BJP votes on which the government's survival had depended. The Left parties voted with the government, as did the Janata Dal's remaining members, but the combined strength of the treasury's supporters reached only 142 against a combined opposition of Congress, BJP and the Chandra Shekhar dissidents voting together to bring the government down. V.P. Singh resigned the same evening, having held office for eleven months. The size of the defeat is itself evidence about the 1989 arrangement: a government assembled by negotiation among parties that shared only an exclusion could be disassembled by the same method, and the confidence division of November 1990 was the first time the Lok Sabha's arithmetic, rather than a party's internal decision, terminated a Union government mid-term.

### Q: Why did V.P. Singh dismiss Devi Lal as Deputy Prime Minister in August 1990?

The dismissal, announced at the start of August 1990, closed a leadership war that had run inside the Janata Dal since the government's formation. Devi Lal, the Haryana chief minister who had become Deputy Prime Minister as the price of his support for V.P. Singh's leadership in December 1989, commanded the party's strongest state machine and regarded himself as the coalition's senior figure by organisation and seniority. Through the first half of 1990 the rivalry was conducted in public: Devi Lal gave interviews attacking the Prime Minister's competence and intentions, addressed rallies that functioned as demonstrations of his independent strength, and resisted the authority of the prime ministerial office over party and government appointments. V.P. Singh's dismissal of his deputy on grounds of indiscipline was intended to establish a single centre of authority in the government. Its effect was the opposite: Devi Lal carried his grievance and his machine outside the cabinet, aligned himself with the government's internal and external critics, and left the Prime Minister dependent on a narrower base in the weeks before the Mandal announcement of 7 August 1990 and the autumn's converging crises.

### Q: What role did N.T. Rama Rao play in the National Front?

N.T. Rama Rao, the Telugu film star who had founded the Telugu Desam Party in 1982 and served as chief minister of Andhra Pradesh, was the National Front's chairman from its constitution in August 1988, with V.P. Singh as its convener. The division of labour expressed the Front's federal design: Rama Rao, the most senior regional leader in the alliance and a former leader of the united opposition conclaves of the mid-1980s, lent the Front his convening authority and his standing among the state parties, while V.P. Singh conducted the negotiations and emerged as its prime ministerial candidate. Rama Rao's chairmanship reassured the regional parties that the Front would not become a Delhi party with provincial branches, the form they associated with the Congress System. His authority survived the Telugu Desam's own collapse in the 1989 Lok Sabha election, in which his party fell from thirty seats in 1984 to two as the Congress recovered Andhra Pradesh, because the Front priced his convening role above his parliamentary contingent. He remained the alliance's presiding figure through the government's tenure, the elder statesman of the coalition method.

### Q: How did the Sixty-First Amendment change the electorate for the 1989 election?

The Constitution (Sixty-First Amendment) Act, passed in 1988 and brought into force in March 1989, amended Article 326 to lower the voting age for elections to the Lok Sabha and the state assemblies from twenty-one years to eighteen years. The 1989 general election of November 1989 was the first national election conducted on the enlarged franchise, and the Election Commission's rolls for that election carried tens of millions of voters aged eighteen to twenty-one who had never before held a vote. The amendment belonged to Rajiv Gandhi's programme of youth-oriented reform and was carried by his government's majority, an irony the opposition did not spare him, since the new voters had no adult memory of any government before his own and were disproportionately exposed to the Bofors campaign through the press and the campuses. The size of the youth effect on the verdict remains debated, and the Election Commission's aggregate data cannot isolate it. The 1989 electorate was the largest and youngest in the republic's history to that date, and that the verdict ending Congress dominance was delivered by the first electorate in which a substantial bloc owed the franchise itself to the defeated incumbent's reform.

### Q: What was the Allahabad by-election of June 1988, and why did it matter?

The Allahabad by-election of June 1988 was caused by the resignation of the sitting MP, the film actor Amitabh Bachchan, who had won the seat for the Congress in 1984 and left Parliament amid the gathering Bofors controversy. V.P. Singh, then in the process of building the Jan Morcha after his expulsion from the Congress was taking shape, contested the seat as the opposition's candidate and defeated Sunil Shastri, the Congress nominee and son of Lal Bahadur Shastri, in a constituency saturated with Congress history and organisation. The result mattered out of proportion to a single seat. It demonstrated, eighteen months before the general election, that the Bofors issue could defeat the Congress machine in the Hindi heartland when the opposition united behind one challenger, and it demonstrated that V.P. Singh personally, rather than any party apparatus, was the issue's credible carrier. The by-election accelerated every negotiation that followed: the Janata Dal merger of October 1988, the National Front's consolidation and the seat adjustments of 1989 were all priced by politicians who had read the Allahabad figures and understood that exit from the Congress, organised nationally, had become a paying proposition.

### Q: What is the difference between a coalition government and a government with outside support?

A coalition government distributes cabinet office among the parties whose combined strength makes the majority, so that each supporting party holds ministries, shares responsibility for decisions and can bring the government down by withdrawing its ministers and its votes. A government with outside support keeps the cabinet within one party or alliance while other parties, remaining outside the executive, pledge their votes on confidence and supply. The National Front government of December 1989 was the second kind in its purest form: the cabinet came from the National Front alone, while the Bharatiya Janata Party and the Left Front, holding between them about 137 seats, supported the government from outside without portfolios, without a common programme with it and without any obligation beyond the confidence pledge. The arrangement let ideologically opposed parties sustain a government they did not join and could not direct, and it left the government exposed to termination by a single withdrawal, as the BJP's exit in October 1990 proved. The distinction became a standard instrument of the coalition era, used in variants by the United Front after 1996 and the United Progressive Alliance after 2004.

### Q: What happened to the Janata Dal after V.P. Singh left office?

The Janata Dal fragmented along the regional and social lines that its federal design had accommodated rather than dissolved. Chandra Shekhar's split of November 1990, which carried about sixty-four MPs into the Janata Dal (Socialist), was the first fracture; through the 1990s the party's state units separated into successor formations, including the Rashtriya Janata Dal in Bihar under Lalu Prasad Yadav from 1997, the Biju Janata Dal in Odisha under Naveen Patnaik from 1997, the Janata Dal (United) in Bihar and the Janata Dal (Secular) in Karnataka under H.D. Deve Gowda, who himself became Prime Minister of the United Front government in June 1996. The parent party's national organisation withered as its state leaderships built parties of their own. The fragmentation confirmed the logic of the 1989 settlement from the inside: the Janata Dal had been assembled as a negotiation among regional and social forces, and when the negotiation's central prize disappeared, the forces retained their followings and re-formed as sovereign parties. The Congress System's parties had absorbed their splits; the coalition era's parties reproduced by splitting.

### Q: Did the Congress try to form a government after the 1989 election?

No. President R. Venkataraman followed the convention of approaching the leader of the single largest party first, and Rajiv Gandhi, whose Congress held 197 seats in a House where 272 made a majority, declined to attempt forming a government. The decision, recorded in Venkataraman's memoir My Presidential Years of 1994 and debated within the Congress for years afterward, rested on the absence of any party willing to sustain a Congress minority: the Janata Dal had fought the election to remove him, the BJP and the Left had both committed against Congress rule, and the regional parties of the National Front were bound to the Front. A Congress attempt would therefore have faced a confidence vote it could expect to lose, and Rajiv Gandhi judged a spell in opposition preferable to a humiliating fortnight in office. Critics inside his party later argued that the attempt should have been made and the opposition forced to combine publicly against the largest party. The refusal cleared the way for the National Front's invitation, and with it for the first Union government in the republic's history formed entirely by post-election negotiation among parties.
