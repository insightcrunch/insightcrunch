For two hundred and sixty seven years the richest state in the Islamic world was ruled by men who had been bought as children. Not by their descendants, which would be an ordinary story of a founding conqueror, but by fresh purchases in every generation, since a Mamluk sultan's own sons were disqualified from the caste their father had risen through.

The claim this article defends is the slaves who became sultans thesis: that the Mamluk system converted bought slave boys into the ruling caste of a great state, that it renewed itself from the slave market rather than through inheritance, and that this arrangement produced the longest lasting and most formidable regime in medieval Egyptian history.

![The Mamluk Sultanate explained, showing how bought and trained slave soldiers ruled Egypt from 1250 to 1517 - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-38.webp)

The assumption this corrects is that rule by slaves implies weakness or instability. This regime stopped the Mongol advance that had destroyed every state in its path from China to Baghdad, eliminated the crusader states that four centuries of Muslim powers had failed to remove, and governed Egypt for longer than the Fatimids, the Ayyubids and the Tulunids combined.

This article is the hub for the Mamluk cluster and it deliberately routes rather than covers. The system itself, its recruitment and its training belong to the article on [who the Mamluks were](/2012/08/01/who-were-the-mamluks/), the confrontation with the Mongols to the article on [the battle of Ayn Jalut](/2012/09/01/battle-of-ayn-jalut-mongols/), and the question of how such an arrangement could work at all to the article on [how a slave army ruled Egypt](/2013/03/01/how-a-slave-army-ruled-egypt/).

What this article owns is the orientation: the dates, the two dynasties, the capital, the scale of the power and what followed. The regime it replaced belongs to the article on [Ayyubid Egypt](/2011/09/01/ayyubid-egypt-age-of-saladin/), and the regime that replaced it to the article on [Ottoman Egypt](/2013/05/01/ottoman-egypt-complete-guide/).

## The Founding

How the regime began is the subject of the article on Ayyubid Egypt from the other side, and the hub statement belongs here.

The corps in question had been purchased, trained and quartered by the last effective Ayyubid sultan, and the article on Ayyubid Egypt describes it as the unit that decided the crusader invasion of 1249 to 1250 and then decided the succession.

### When did the Mamluk Sultanate begin?

In 1250, when the corps killed the Ayyubid heir during a crusader invasion and took power in its own name. The transition took several years to settle, with a former sultan's widow briefly holding authority and several arrangements tried, so the founding is a process across the early 1250s rather than a single dated act.

The sequence was that the sultan died mid campaign, his widow concealed the death while the fighting continued, the heir arrived from a distant province, alienated the corps that had won the battles, and was killed.

The novelty was not that soldiers took power, since the article on the Tulunid dynasty and the article on Fatimid Egypt both describe military establishments destroying or capturing regimes. The novelty was that they ruled in their own name rather than installing a figurehead.

The settling took several years, with a widow of the previous sultan holding authority briefly, several commanders rising and falling, and the arrangement only stabilizing after the Mongol crisis of 1260 gave it a founding achievement.

The legitimacy problem was acute and the article's later section describes how it was solved, since a regime of former slaves with no descent claim and no religious authority had less to build on than any predecessor.

The general observation is that the Mamluk sultanate was not founded so much as it emerged, and the article on Ayyubid Egypt supplies the mechanism: a professional corps whose loyalty ran to its purchaser had nowhere for that loyalty to go when he died.

## The Sultans Who Mattered

A hub article should name the reigns that shaped the period, while routing the detail to the specialists who own them.

The founding figures of the 1250s are several rather than one, since the transition took years to settle and no single commander established the regime alone.

Baybars is the towering figure of the first period, consolidating the sultanate after the Mongol confrontation, conducting much of the campaign against the crusader states, installing the Abbasid caliph in Cairo and establishing institutional structures that lasted, and the specialist article treats his reign.

The long reign of the early fourteenth century is the second, a sultan who held power across several decades in three separate periods, under whom the sultanate reached its territorial and economic height and whose building programme was substantial.

The plague era sultans faced a situation none of their predecessors had, and the article on the Black Death describes conditions in which competent government was scarcely possible.

The great builders of the fifteenth century are the third group, and the article on Mamluk architecture describes the foundations of a period whose architectural quality rose as its economic position fell.

The last sultans faced the Ottoman confrontation, and the article on the Ottoman conquest treats them.

The general pattern is that a handful of long reigns account for a disproportionate share of the campaigns, the building and the institutional development, while a great many short ones account for the impression of chaos.

The general observation is that a hub article naming fifty rulers would be useless and one naming none would be evasive, and the honest middle is to name the reigns that changed things and to route the reader to the specialists for the rest.

## The Citadel Under the Mamluks

The seat of government belongs to the article on the Citadel of Cairo, and the hub statement of what this regime did with it belongs here.

The inheritance was the Ayyubid fortress begun around 1176, and the article on [the Citadel of Cairo](/2012/01/01/citadel-of-cairo-history/) describes a defensible complex holding the ruler, the garrison, the treasury and the administration.

The Mamluk transformation converted it from a fortress into a palace and government complex, with residences, audience halls, mosques, treasuries, offices and enormous stables built across the period.

The stables deserve emphasis, since a regime resting on cavalry required facilities on a scale a modern reader underestimates, and the horses were as much part of the system as the men.

The audience hall was the ceremonial centre, used for receptions, investitures and the sessions at which the sultan heard petitions directly, and it became the most celebrated structure in the complex.

The division into a northern military zone and a southern residential and governmental one is described in the article on the Citadel of Cairo and structured how the government physically worked.

The politics conducted there were the factional struggles the article's own section describes, and control of specific gates within the complex acquired real significance during transitions.

The loss is almost total, since the article on the Citadel of Cairo describes the nineteenth century clearances removing most of the Mamluk palace architecture, which is why a period so well documented is so poorly represented on the ground at its own centre.

The general observation is that the best documented and least visible phase of the Citadel's history is the Mamluk one, and the contrast between the abundance of the written record and the near absence of the buildings is a useful reminder that survival and significance are unrelated.

## The Pilgrimage and the Holy Cities

The protectorship of Mecca and Medina was among the sultanate's most valuable assets and deserves its own treatment.

The relationship was indirect, exercised through the local rulers of the holy cities rather than by direct administration, which is the arrangement the article on Ayyubid Egypt describes for the earlier period.

The prestige value was enormous, since protecting the pilgrimage and the holy places is among the strongest claims to legitimacy available to any Muslim sovereign, and a regime of freedmen needed every claim it could obtain.

The practical obligation was the annual caravan, which required organization, provisioning, escort, water along the route and security across country the state did not directly govern.

The infrastructure investment followed, with wells, cisterns and staging posts maintained along the pilgrimage roads, which the article on the Ayyubid economy describes as serving commerce and pilgrimage together.

The ceremonial dimension was the annual departure of the caravan from Cairo with the ceremonial covering for the sanctuary, which became one of the great public occasions of the city and a display of the sultanate's standing.

The competitive dimension mattered too, since rival powers sought the same protectorship, and the Ottoman acquisition of it after 1517 was among the conquest's most significant transfers.

The commercial connection is the one the article on the Ayyubid economy makes, since pilgrims carry goods, buy supplies and frequently trade, which makes the annual movement an economic event as well as a religious one.

The general observation is that a regime with a legitimacy deficit will invest disproportionately in the assets that answer it, and the Mamluk attention to the pilgrimage is the clearest instance in this period of a policy driven by what the rulers were not rather than by what they wanted.

## The Fiscal Machine Under Pressure

How the revenue system behaved across the period's long decline deserves a section, since it is where the strain became visible.

The starting position was the inherited machinery the article on taxation describes, operating through the flood, the survey and the district assessment, with the Ayyubid revenue assignment system laid over it.

The demographic shock changed the arithmetic permanently, since a system assessing land in relation to available labour faces a shrinking taxable base when the labour disappears.

The response was intensification, with rates raised, collection tightened and new levies introduced, which is the standard response and the counterproductive one.

The consequence for the cultivator was the cascade effect the article on taxation describes, in which pressure on those who remain drives flight, which shrinks the base further and increases the pressure on the rest.

The revenue assignment system compounded it, since a soldier collecting his own revenue from a shrinking base has an immediate incentive to extract harder and no interest in the district's long term productivity.

The commercial substitute was the drift toward monopolies, which the article on the Ayyubid economy traces and which sought from trade the revenue that agriculture could no longer supply.

The monetary disorder followed from the same pressure, since a state short of revenue debases, and the article on Fatimid trade explains what a reliable currency is worth to a transit economy.

The general observation is that the Mamluk fiscal decline is a textbook sequence and the article on taxation predicts every stage of it: a shock reduces the base, the state intensifies, intensification accelerates the decline, and the alternative revenue source is squeezed until it also moves away.

## What the Sultanate Could Not Do

Naming the limits is as useful as listing the achievements, and several of them determined how the period ended.

It could not produce a stable succession, and the article's section on the factional system describes the price of a mechanism that reliably produced adults and never produced peace at a transition.

It could not integrate with the population it governed, since the caste was renewed from outside by design and no Egyptian could enter it, which is a more absolute separation than any dynastic arrangement imposes.

It could not adapt its military system quickly, and the article's section on the army explains why: a force whose excellence rests on a decade of cavalry training cannot absorb firearms without dismantling the training pipeline that makes it what it is.

It could not control the trade routes it depended on, since the article on the Ayyubid economy establishes that the traffic depended on producers, shippers and buyers in places no Egyptian ruler could influence.

It could not resist the plague, and no state anywhere could, which is worth stating because the demographic collapse is frequently discussed as though policy were relevant to it.

It could not restrain its own fiscal extraction, and the drift toward monopolies described in the article's section on the economy is the standard failure of a transit state seeking a larger share.

It could not prevent the ocean route, and the article on Fatimid trade names the general principle: a chokepoint is only a chokepoint until somebody finds a way around it.

The pattern in these limits is that most of them are conditions of ruling Egypt with this kind of army rather than Mamluk failures, and the one that is genuinely theirs, the succession, is also the thing that made everything else possible.

## The Legacy

What the period left behind deserves a closing hub statement, since a regime lasting two and a half centuries leaves a great deal.

The city is the largest item, and the medieval Cairo a visitor walks through is substantially a Mamluk creation, with several hundred surviving buildings from the period.

The religious institutions are the second, and the article on al-Azhar describes an institution the Mamluks restored and endowed into the foremost school of the Sunni world, a position it has held ever since.

The written record is the third, and the article's section on it explains that much of what can be said about earlier Egyptian centuries comes through Mamluk period compilations.

The military class is the fourth and the strangest, since the households survived the conquest of 1517 and dominated Egypt for nearly three further centuries.

The territorial settlement is the fifth, since Egypt with Syria and the Hijaz is the shape the Ottoman province inherited.

The negative legacy is the sixth and it deserves stating: an extractive fiscal system applied to a shrinking population, a commercial position squeezed by monopolies, and an agricultural base that had deteriorated across the final century.

The comparative legacy is the seventh, since the succession solution the article's own sections describe is a genuine political innovation and one that no other medieval state attempted at this scale.

The general observation is that the Mamluk period is the last era in which Egypt was an independent power of the first rank, and everything after 1517 in this series is the history of a province rather than of a state, which is a change of category rather than of degree.

## The Word Mamluk

A terminological section is worth including, since the word carries assumptions that mislead in several directions at once.

The Arabic term means owned or possessed, and it was applied specifically to military slaves rather than to slaves generally, which is a distinction the language itself marks and English does not.

The distinction matters because the position of a military slave was entirely unlike that of a domestic or agricultural one, involving intensive training, high status, expectation of manumission and a career leading to command.

The manumission is the element most often missed, since the men who ruled were freedmen rather than slaves, having been manumitted on completing their training, and the caste consisted of former slaves throughout.

The paradox in the terminology is therefore real, and a sultan described as a Mamluk was legally free, wealthy, powerful and had been purchased as a child, all at once.

The institution predates this regime by four centuries, and the article on the Tulunid dynasty describes caliphal armies rebuilt around military slaves from Central Asia in the ninth century, with the article on Fatimid Egypt describing four such imported groups.

The novelty of 1250 was not the institution but its assumption of sovereignty, which the article's founding section describes.

The English usage varies, with Mamluk used for the individuals, for the caste, for the sultanate and for the period, and a reader should watch which is meant.

The general observation is that a word carrying a technical meaning in one language and a general meaning in another will produce confusion whenever it is translated, and the gap between the Arabic term and the English word slave is responsible for most misunderstandings of this entire subject.

## How Historians Have Read the Period

The historiography deserves a section, since the assessment of these centuries has shifted considerably and a reader encountering older writing should know why.

The older view treated the period as decadent, an interlude of foreign military rule between the crusading era and the Ottoman conquest, judged largely by its final century and by the eventual defeat.

The reasons for that view included the emphasis on political narrative, in which factional violence and rapid turnover look like chaos, and a general assumption that alien military castes govern badly.

The revision has been substantial and it rests on the sources, since the article's section on the written record describes an evidentiary abundance that supports institutional and social history rather than only political narrative.

The administrative dimension has been the largest gain, and the chancery manuals, endowment deeds and biographical dictionaries permit a picture of a sophisticated governing apparatus that the chronicles alone do not.

The architectural assessment has risen correspondingly, and the article on Mamluk architecture treats a body of building that few periods anywhere can match in quality or quantity.

The economic history has become more granular, and the article on Mamluk Egypt and world trade describes both the peak and the decline with attention to mechanism rather than to moralizing.

The Egyptian national perspective has its own complications with the period, since a foreign military caste ruling for two and a half centuries fits awkwardly into any narrative of national self assertion, whatever it achieved.

The general observation is that the reassessment of the Mamluk period is a good example of how source availability drives historical judgment, since the same events look different when read through endowment deeds and administrative manuals rather than through chronicles of factional violence.

## The Chronology in Sequence

Setting the period out in order is worth doing, since the article treats themes and a reader needs the sequence in one place.

In 1250 the military corps killed the Ayyubid heir during a crusader invasion of the Delta and took power, with several years of unsettled arrangements following.

In 1258 the Mongols destroyed Baghdad and killed the Abbasid caliph, removing an institution that had existed for five centuries.

In 1260 a Mamluk army marched north and checked the Mongol advance in Palestine, which is the founding achievement the regime rested on thereafter.

Through the 1260s and 1270s the regime consolidated under Baybars, whose reign the specialist article treats and who established much of the institutional structure.

Across the following decades the crusader states were reduced fortress by fortress, with the last significant coastal holding taken in 1291 and the coastline systematically dismantled to prevent reoccupation.

Through the early fourteenth century the sultanate reached its height, with Syria held, the trade at its peak and the great building programme underway.

In 1347 the plague arrived and returned in waves across the following century and a half, permanently altering the demographic and fiscal position.

In 1382 the recruitment shift produced the second period, conventionally named for the Circassian origin of its soldiers.

Across the fifteenth century the building reached its greatest quality while the economic position deteriorated under plague, monetary disorder and the drift toward monopolies.

At the end of the fifteenth century the sea route around Africa opened, removing the necessity of the Egyptian corridor and undermining the commercial foundation.

In 1516 and 1517 the Ottomans defeated the sultanate in Syria and then in Egypt, ending it as a sovereign state.

Through the following three centuries the Mamluk households survived under Ottoman authority, recovering much of the real power, until 1811.

## Cairo as a City to Live In

Beyond the monuments, what the greatest city of its world was like deserves a section, since a hub article that describes only buildings describes only the surface.

The scale is the first fact and it is hard to convey. Cairo in the fourteenth century was among the largest cities anywhere outside China, several times the size of any European city, in a period when most people lived in villages.

The quarters were the organizing structure, with residential districts having their own gates, markets, mosques and internal life, which the article on life in Fatimid Cairo describes for the previous period and which persisted.

The commercial geography ran along the principal street inherited from the Fatimid enclosure, and the article on how the Fatimids founded Cairo notes that street surviving because property boundaries do.

The markets specialized by trade, with quarters for particular crafts and goods, and the great commercial buildings combined warehousing, lodging for merchants and retail in a single structure.

The water supply came from the river through carriers, wells and the aqueduct the article on the Citadel of Cairo describes, and the logistics of watering a city that size without pumps is among the underappreciated achievements of the period.

The food supply came from the countryside through the river, and a city of this scale requires a grain trade of considerable sophistication and is correspondingly vulnerable to a bad flood.

The public buildings the ruling caste endowed served the population directly, since a drinking fountain, a hospital or a college is infrastructure as well as commemoration, and the article on Mamluk architecture describes these functions.

The general observation is that the Mamluk achievement most visible to an ordinary person was not a battle or a dynasty but a city that worked, and the fact that a settlement of that size functioned at all with the technology available is the period's least celebrated accomplishment.

## Why This Period Is Worth Studying

A closing argument for the attention this hub and its cluster give to two and a half centuries.

The first reason is the system itself, since a self renewing slave elite governing a major state successfully for that long is genuinely unusual in world history and poses questions that ordinary dynastic history does not.

The second is the demonstration about succession, which this series has traced across four regimes and which the Mamluk case resolves by abandoning the assumption that rule passes through families.

The third is the achievements, since stopping the Mongol advance and eliminating the crusader states are events with consequences far beyond Egypt.

The fourth is Cairo, since the medieval city a visitor walks through is substantially a Mamluk creation and the article on Mamluk architecture describes several hundred surviving buildings from a single period.

The fifth is the documentation, since this is the point where medieval Egyptian history becomes abundantly evidenced and where the sources for the earlier periods in this series were compiled.

The sixth is the economic story, since the article on Mamluk Egypt and world trade describes the medieval Egyptian commercial position at its peak followed by the technological change that removed it.

The seventh is the moral question, which the article's own section states plainly and which a reader should carry rather than set aside: this was a ruling class assembled by purchasing children.

The general recommendation is that the Mamluk period is the best available medieval case for asking how a political system reproduces itself, and that the question is more interesting and more transferable than any account of who won which battle.

## The Abbasid Caliph in Cairo

The arrangement by which a shadow caliphate was maintained in Egypt deserves its own treatment, since it is among the period's stranger institutions.

The origin was the Mongol destruction of Baghdad in 1258, which the article on the Fatimid and Abbasid rivalry describes as ending a caliphate that had existed since 750.

The Mamluk response was to bring a surviving member of the family to Cairo and install him as caliph, which supplied the formal legitimating apparatus that a regime of freedmen conspicuously lacked.

The function was investiture, since a sultan receiving a diploma from a caliph holds delegated authority rather than seized power, which the article on the Fatimid and Abbasid rivalry describes as an endorsement costing the grantor nothing.

The reality was that the caliph in Cairo had no independent power whatever, lived on a stipend, and served entirely at the sultanate's convenience.

The irony is the one the article on how Saladin ended the Fatimids sets up, since the city was founded to house a rival caliphate opposing the Abbasids and ended by housing a puppet Abbasid maintained as an instrument.

The wider recognition was limited, since rulers elsewhere in the Islamic world did not generally accept the Cairo caliph as the legitimate successor to the Baghdad line, which reduces the arrangement's external value.

The end came with the Ottoman conquest, and the later claim that the caliphate was transferred at that point to the Ottoman sultan is a considerably later construction rather than a documented event of 1517.

The general observation is that a legitimating institution with no power is worth maintaining if it costs little and answers a question the regime cannot otherwise answer, which is exactly the calculation the article on the Fatimid and Abbasid rivalry describes from the other end.

## What Would Change This Picture

Naming the evidence that would revise the account is the fairest test of a period overview, even one built on abundant sources.

Fiscal series across the period would be transformative, since the article's sections on the economy and the strain rest on structural reasoning and on qualitative indicators rather than on measurement.

Demographic evidence beyond estimate would settle the plague question, and the article on the Black Death describes figures that are inferences from indirect indicators rather than counts.

Systematic study of the endowment deeds in aggregate, rather than individually, would quantify the scale of elite patronage and its share of the total economy.

Archaeological work on the rural settlement pattern would establish what the demographic collapse did outside the cities, which is presently inferred from revenue rather than observed.

Recruitment records, if any survive, would document the scale and the sources of the slave purchases on which the whole system depended.

Provincial administrative material from Syria would balance an account written largely from Cairo.

None of these is impossible and several are active areas of work, which distinguishes this period from the earlier ones in this series.

The boundary is therefore clearer than usual. The dates, the structure, the succession mechanism, the major campaigns, the building programme and the written record are secure. The economic scale, the demographic magnitude and the rural experience are reconstructions, and this article has tried to mark which is which.

## Women in the Mamluk Period

An element that a military caste narrative tends to omit entirely deserves a section, since the documentary record permits more than usually be said.

The legal position was the inherited one, with women holding property in their own right, inheriting, contracting marriage on terms and appearing in court, which the article on life in Fatimid Cairo describes for the previous period from the merchant letters.

The property dimension is the substantial one, since women of the elite held revenue assignments, urban property and commercial investments, and endowment deeds record women as founders of mosques, colleges and charitable institutions in significant numbers.

The building record is therefore partly theirs, and the article on Mamluk architecture describes foundations established by wives, daughters and freedwomen of the ruling caste.

The political role appears at moments of transition, and the article on Ayyubid Egypt notes the widow who concealed a sultan's death during the crusader invasion of 1249 and briefly held authority afterward, which is the founding moment of this whole regime.

The household position of women within the Mamluk system is a peculiar one, since a caste renewing itself by purchase produced households in which the wives and mothers were frequently themselves purchased, freed and married.

The consequence for the sons is the structural point the article's section on succession makes, since a Mamluk's children were born free and Muslim and therefore outside the caste, which made the family a place of exclusion from power rather than of transmission.

The documentary evidence is better here than for earlier periods, and the article's section on the written record explains why: endowment deeds name founders and biographical dictionaries record lives.

The general observation is that a period described entirely through its soldiers omits half its population and most of its property holders, and the Mamluk endowment record is an unusually good corrective to that omission.

## Science, Medicine and Learning

The intellectual production of the period deserves more than the passing mention that accounts focused on cavalry and factions usually give it.

The medical tradition was strong and institutionally supported, with hospitals endowed on a substantial scale, and the article on the Tulunid dynasty traces the Egyptian hospital tradition back to the ninth century.

The astronomical work was of a high order, connected to the practical requirements of timekeeping for prayer and the determination of the direction of prayer, and specialists attached to the great mosques produced instruments and tables.

The historical writing is the period's outstanding achievement in this area, and the article's section on the written record describes chronicles, biographical dictionaries and topographical compilations of a quality and quantity nothing earlier matches.

The legal scholarship flourished on the endowment base the article's section on religion describes, with the four schools taught in colleges across the country and the establishment enjoying real independence.

The encyclopedic tradition produced administrative manuals and compilations covering geography, chancery practice and the sciences, written by officials who knew the machinery from inside.

The transmission function is the underrated one, since the Mamluk period preserved, copied and taught the accumulated learning of the preceding centuries at a moment when the eastern centres had been destroyed.

The Baghdad comparison makes the point, since the article on the Fatimid and Abbasid rivalry describes the great translation movement and its inheritance, and after 1258 the continuation of much of that tradition ran through Cairo.

The general observation is that the intellectual centre of the Islamic world moved to Cairo in this period by default as much as by achievement, and that the Mamluk endowment system was what allowed it to stay there.

## Diplomacy and the Wider World

The sultanate's external relations extended considerably further than its campaigns, and the range is worth setting out.

The Mongol successor state in Persia was the principal rival for a century, and relations combined warfare, truces and diplomatic exchange in the ordinary manner of neighbouring powers.

The alliance with the Mongol power north of the Black Sea is the elegant piece of diplomacy of the period, since that state was itself Muslim, hostile to the Persian branch, and situated exactly where the Mamluk slave markets were, which made the relationship simultaneously strategic and commercial.

The Italian merchant republics maintained the treaty arrangements the article on the Ayyubid economy describes, renewed and renegotiated across the centuries, with quarters, warehouses and agreed rates in Egyptian ports.

The Byzantine relationship mattered for the Black Sea passage, since the recruitment route ran through the straits, which gave a declining empire a lever it used.

The European powers dealt with the sultanate over pilgrimage access, over the Christian holy places and over trade, and the correspondence survives in some quantity on their side.

The Indian Ocean connections ran through Yemen and the Red Sea, and the article on the Ayyubid economy describes the corridor these relations were meant to protect.

The African relations ran south through Nubia and along the Red Sea coast, involving both campaigning and commerce.

The general observation is that a state at the junction of three continents conducts diplomacy in every direction at once, and the Mamluk correspondence is one of the better documented medieval examples of a chancery managing that.

## Reading a Long Regime

The methodological problem a period of this length presents deserves stating, since two and a half centuries cannot be described the way a reign can.

The first move is to divide it, and the article's section on the two periods describes the conventional division while noting that the labels are later scholarly conventions rather than contemporary categories.

The second is to distinguish the founding conditions from the operating conditions, since the crises of 1250 to 1291 shaped the regime's self image permanently and had little to do with how it functioned for the following two centuries.

The third is to separate the system from the individuals, since a regime with fifty rulers cannot be described through biography and the structure is what persists.

The fourth is to identify what did not change, which for Egypt is the fiscal machinery, the flood and the cultivators, as the article on how the Arab conquest reshaped Egypt establishes as the deepest layer.

The fifth is to date the decline honestly, since the article's section on the strain describes a century and a half of accumulating pressure rather than a sudden collapse, and treating the whole period through its ending is as distorting as treating it through its founding.

The sixth is to route the detail, which is what a hub article does and what this one has attempted throughout.

The seventh is to state the source situation, and the article's section on the written record notes that this period is the first in the series where evidence is abundant rather than scarce.

The general observation is that long regimes are described badly by narrative and well by structure, and a reader who wants to understand two and a half centuries should ask how the system worked rather than what happened in sequence.

## The Moral Question

An article about a regime constituted from purchased children owes its reader a direct statement rather than a euphemism.

The practice was the purchase of children, mostly boys, from regions outside the Islamic world, transported across long distances, sold, converted and trained for a life they had not chosen.

The scale was continuous rather than occasional, since the system required fresh purchase in every generation and a caste of some thousands renewing itself is a substantial and permanent demand on the slave markets.

The routes are the ones the article on the Ayyubid economy describes as part of the same commercial networks that carried pepper and linen, which is a connection that article states explicitly and this one repeats.

The outcomes for the individuals varied enormously, from the highest positions in a great state to death in training or on campaign, and the existence of the first does not mitigate the second.

The comparison that is sometimes offered, that these slaves became rulers and were therefore not really slaves, does not survive examination. They were bought as children who could not consent, and what some of them later achieved is a fact about the system's structure rather than about its acquisition of them.

The contemporary view differed and this article states that too, since slavery was universal across the societies involved and the specific institution of military slavery was regarded as normal and even as a route to advancement.

The historian's position is to describe the system accurately, to note what its participants thought about it, and to decline to let either the achievements or the normality serve as a defence.

The general observation is that a complete account of a political system includes how it acquired its personnel, and the Mamluk sultanate acquired its rulers by buying children, which is the first thing to say about it and the thing most accounts say last.

## The Ottoman Conquest

How the sultanate fell is treated by the articles on the Ottoman conquest, and the hub statement belongs here.

The opponent had grown across the preceding century, taking Constantinople in 1453 and expanding across Anatolia and the Balkans into the dominant power of the eastern Mediterranean.

The frontier friction ran through southern Anatolia, where both powers had interests among the Turkmen principalities, and the confrontation developed from that rather than arriving from nowhere.

The campaigns came in 1516 in northern Syria and in 1517 in Egypt itself, and the article on the Ottoman conquest of Mamluk Egypt treats both.

The military explanation usually offered is firearms, with Ottoman artillery and infantry musketry against a cavalry tradition that had incorporated them slowly, and the article on the Mamluk war machine explains why a system built on a decade of cavalry training adapts poorly.

The qualification is that the outcome was not foreordained, since Mamluk cavalry remained formidable and the campaigns turned on more than technology.

The internal condition contributed, and the article's section on the strain describes a regime under a century and a half of demographic, fiscal and commercial pressure.

The aftermath is the surprising part and the article's own section on survival describes it: the households continued, recruited, held land and progressively recovered real power in an Ottoman province.

The general observation is that the sultanate ended as a sovereign state in 1517 and as a system in 1811, and the gap between those two dates is nearly as long as the sultanate itself.

## Four Egyptian Regimes Compared

Setting the sultanate beside the three this series has already treated identifies what was general about Egyptian rule and what was particular to this arrangement.

The Tulunids of the 870s, treated in their own article, were a governor's family who captured the revenue, built an army of imported soldiers, founded a capital quarter and lasted thirty seven years before their own troops and a recovered caliphal centre destroyed them.

The Fatimids of 969 were a rival caliphate arriving from outside, building a walled palace city, importing four military groups, holding Egypt for two centuries and ending in a sentence because their doctrine had acquired no constituency.

The Ayyubids of 1171 were a military family taking the state from inside, building a citadel, expanding through relatives, endowing a religious establishment, holding Egypt for seventy nine years and being removed by the corps they had built.

The Mamluks of 1250 were that corps ruling in its own name, and they lasted longer than the other three combined.

The constants across all four are striking and this series has traced them repeatedly. Each was founded by outsiders with no Egyptian constituency. Each inherited the same fiscal machinery unchanged. Each built in the capital as a legitimacy programme. Each depended on imported professional soldiers.

The variable that predicts duration is succession, and the article on Ayyubid Egypt states the argument: three consecutive regimes were destroyed by the same mechanism and the fourth survived by abandoning family rule entirely.

The building record confirms it, and the article on the Citadel of Cairo notes that three purpose built palace complexes were demolished while a fortress useful to everyone survived, with the Mamluks adding endowed foundations that outlasted them too.

The general observation is that reading four regimes together identifies a structural problem that reading any one would attribute to circumstance, and the Mamluk solution is the answer to a question the previous three centuries had been asking.

## Why the System Lasted

The analytical question belongs to the article on how a slave army ruled Egypt, and a hub statement of the argument belongs here.

The first element is that the succession produced adults. Every sultan had been purchased as a boy, trained for a decade, manumitted, served in a household, risen to command and then won a contest among his peers, which selects for competence in a way inheritance does not.

The second is that the caste renewed itself from outside, so it never became a hereditary nobility with local entanglements, local land in perpetuity and local loyalties competing with its own.

The third is that the exclusion of sons is the mechanism enforcing the second, since permitting inheritance would have converted the caste into an ordinary aristocracy within two generations.

The fourth is that the household structure aligned political and military power, so that a man's standing and his fighting strength were the same thing, which prevents the mismatch between formal authority and real power that destroyed the three previous regimes.

The fifth is that the administration was left to a professional Arabic speaking class, so the state functioned regardless of what the ruling caste was doing.

The sixth is that the endowment system converted elite wealth into permanent institutions, which the article on al-Azhar describes as the mechanism of survival and which gave the regime a religious establishment invested in its continuation.

The cost of the whole arrangement was instability at the top and the continuous purchase of children, which is the moral fact underneath everything else in this article.

The general observation is that the Mamluk system worked because it was internally consistent, with each element supporting the others, and that the article on how a slave army ruled Egypt develops the argument in full.

## The Factional System

The internal politics of the ruling caste is what most accounts of the period find chaotic, and understanding its logic makes it considerably less so.

The unit was the household, since each senior commander purchased, trained and manumitted his own soldiers, who owed him personal loyalty and formed his political base.

The bond within a household was strong and lasted for life, running between a master and his men and horizontally between men trained together, which the article on who the Mamluks were describes as the system's central social fact.

The consequence is that political weight and military strength were the same quantity, since a commander's standing was measured in the men he had bought and could call on.

The competition was therefore structural rather than a symptom of disorder, since a system in which the sultanate goes to whoever can command sufficient support guarantees continuous manoeuvring among those who might.

The violence was episodic and generally contained, with fighting in the streets of the capital at moments of transition and periods of stability in between, and the article on the Citadel of Cairo notes that struggles were frequently fought over control of the complex itself.

The moderating factors were real. A commander needed the administration to function, the revenue to arrive and the army to be paid, so a faction that destroyed the state's capacity destroyed its own prize.

The comparison with dynastic instability is the useful one, since the article on Ayyubid Egypt describes a family confederation fragmenting at every death and the article on the Fatimid caliphs describes six child accessions in eleven reigns, neither of which is more orderly than the Mamluk arrangement.

The general observation is that the Mamluk system traded predictable succession for competent succession, and the factional violence is the visible cost of a bargain that also produced two and a half centuries of adult rulers who had earned their positions.

## The Provinces and Syria

The territories beyond Egypt deserve a hub statement, since the sultanate governed a larger area than the country it was based in.

The Syrian provinces were the largest addition, held after the Mongol confrontation and the removal of the crusader states, and administered by Mamluk governors appointed from Cairo.

The governorships were significant commands and significant risks, since a governor of Damascus or Aleppo held troops, revenue and distance from the capital, and revolts by provincial governors recur across the period.

The management technique was rotation and the balancing of appointments, which is the standard imperial answer to a governor who might accumulate too much.

The Egyptian provinces were administered through the district system the article on taxation describes, unchanged in its essentials, with Mamluk officers holding revenue assignments across them.

The Hijaz relationship was protectorship rather than administration, exercised through local rulers, and it carried the prestige asset the article's section on legitimacy describes.

The southern frontier ran toward Nubia and involved periodic campaigning, with the region beyond the first cataract a zone of influence rather than of administration.

The northern frontier was the dangerous one across the later period, facing the Turkmen powers of Anatolia and eventually the Ottomans, and the article on the Ottoman conquest treats what came of it.

The general observation is that Egypt and Syria together are the combination the article on Ayyubid Egypt identifies as decisive, and the Mamluks held it for two and a half centuries where the Ayyubids managed it intermittently for eighty.

## The Architecture

The physical legacy is the most visible thing the sultanate left and the article on Mamluk architecture treats it, so the hub statement belongs here.

The scale is the first fact. Several hundred Mamluk buildings survive in Cairo, which is a density of medieval monuments from a single period that few cities anywhere can match.

The types include congregational mosques, endowed colleges, mausoleums attached to both, hospitals, drinking fountains, commercial buildings and residences, frequently combined into single foundations serving several functions.

The mechanism producing them is the endowment, and the article on al-Azhar explains why a founder builds: property assigned to a purpose creates a permanent institution carrying the founder's name and prayers.

The competitive dimension is the reason for the quantity, since a ruling caste of commanders each seeking standing, and each holding revenue assignments, produced a building competition running for two and a half centuries.

The style is distinctive and recognizable, with striped masonry, elaborate stone carving, muqarnas vaulting, minarets of characteristic profile and inlaid marble, and the article on Mamluk architecture treats the vocabulary.

The mausoleum practice is the peculiarly Mamluk element, since men who could not pass position to their sons could at least be buried under a dome bearing their name and endowed to pray for them.

The survival is unusually good because these were endowed religious foundations, which the article on al-Azhar identifies as the category that survives regime change, unlike the palaces the article on how the Fatimids founded Cairo describes vanishing entirely.

The general observation is that a caste whose members could not found dynasties founded buildings instead, and the density of Mamluk architecture in Cairo is the physical trace of a succession system that denied its members any other form of permanence.

## The Written Record

The documentary and historiographical output of the period deserves emphasis, since this series depends on it throughout.

The scale is the first point. The Mamluk centuries produced an enormous body of writing: chronicles year by year, biographical dictionaries covering thousands of individuals, administrative manuals, legal collections, geographical and topographical works and scientific treatises.

The topographical compilation of the fifteenth century is the single work this series cites most, and its author described the buildings, quarters, institutions and history of Cairo systematically at a moment when the Mamluk city was intact.

The biographical dictionaries are the distinctive Mamluk genre, recording lives of scholars, officials, commanders and merchants in numbers that permit something close to prosopography for a medieval society.

The endowment deeds survive in quantity and are documents of a different order, since they specify property, purposes, salaries and procedures with legal precision and were produced to do work rather than to persuade.

The chancery manuals describe the administrative machinery from the inside, which is the kind of institutional evidence earlier periods in this series conspicuously lack.

The reason for the abundance is partly the endowment system, which required documentation, and partly the scholarly class the patronage supported, and partly simply that later material survives better.

The consequence for the earlier articles in this series is direct, since much of what can be said about Fatimid and Ayyubid Cairo comes through Mamluk period compilations drawing on sources now lost.

The general observation is that the Mamluk period is the point where Egyptian medieval history becomes richly documented, and a reader who has followed this series from the pharaohs is arriving, for the first time, at a period whose problem is the quantity of evidence rather than its scarcity.

## The Population They Ruled

The Egyptians beneath the ruling caste deserve attention, since a period defined by its elite risks describing a country nobody lived in.

The separation between rulers and ruled was sharper here than in any previous Egyptian regime, since the Mamluks were foreign by origin, foreign by language, renewed from outside in every generation and legally distinct as a caste.

The consequence is that no Egyptian could join the ruling class, which is a more absolute exclusion than any dynastic arrangement imposes, since a dynasty at least recruits ministers and commanders locally.

The compensating opening was the administration and the religious establishment, both of which were Egyptian and Arabic speaking and both of which offered careers of real standing.

The commercial class was the third avenue and the article on the Ayyubid economy describes the great merchants as financiers to rulers, which is a form of influence that requires no office.

The peasantry continued as the article on taxation describes, with the flood setting the year, the district assessment setting the burden and the village headman standing between the two.

The revenue assignment system placed a soldier in direct contact with the cultivator, which the article on the Ayyubid economy identifies as the change that reached the village, and the Mamluk continuation of it is the arrangement most Egyptians experienced.

The urban population of Cairo lived in the greatest city of its world, with the employment, the food supply and the crowding that implies, and the article on Mamluk architecture describes the physical setting.

The general observation is that the most striking fact about Mamluk Egypt is the completeness of the separation between the people who governed and the people who were governed, and that the arrangement nonetheless lasted longer than any regime in which the two overlapped.

## The Christians and the Jews

The non Muslim communities under Mamluk rule deserve a hub statement, since the period marks a change from what came before.

The framework was the inherited one, and the article on Copts under early Islamic rule describes it as protected but pressured, with worship and property guaranteed alongside fiscal and social disadvantage.

The Ayyubid operation of it is treated in the article on religion and tolerance in Ayyubid Egypt, which describes orthodoxy combined with substantial practical tolerance.

The Mamluk period was harder, and the honest statement is that pressure increased. Restrictions that earlier regimes had enforced episodically were applied more consistently, episodes of demolition and dismissal recur, and the general position deteriorated.

The reasons proposed include the crusading context, the plague years and the search for scapegoats during them, popular pressure that rulers found convenient to accommodate, and the fiscal squeeze of the later period.

The demographic consequence is significant, and the article on conquest and conversion places the Christian proportion of the Egyptian population declining substantially across exactly these centuries.

The administrative continuity persisted regardless, since the article on Copts establishes that Christians staffed the fiscal bureaucracy across every regime and that recurring dismissal decrees prove recurring reinstatement.

The Jewish community continued through the period with its own institutions, and the article on Fatimid trade notes that the merchant archive documenting the earlier centuries thins considerably by this point.

The general observation is that the Mamluk centuries were harder for Egypt's non Muslim communities than the Fatimid or Ayyubid ones, which is a genuine change and one that a hub article should state rather than smooth over.

## The Plague

The demographic catastrophe of the fourteenth century is the largest event of the period after the founding, and the article on the Black Death in Mamluk Egypt treats it fully.

The arrival was in 1347, reaching Egypt through the trade routes the article on the Ayyubid economy describes, which is the standing risk of a corridor economy that nobody could have anticipated.

The recurrence is the element that distinguishes the medieval plague from a single catastrophe, since waves returned repeatedly across the following century and a half, preventing recovery between them.

The demographic effect was severe and the article on the Black Death treats the estimates, which are estimates rather than counts and which indicate a very large proportion of the population.

The fiscal consequence followed directly, since the article on taxation establishes that Egyptian revenue is a function of cultivated area and cultivated area is a function of available labour.

The urban effect was equally severe, and a city the size of Cairo concentrated mortality in a way the countryside did not.

The military effect reached the ruling caste itself, since Mamluk households were urban and the plague did not distinguish, so commanders and their soldiers died alongside everybody else.

The recruitment effect is the interesting secondary consequence, since a system depending on continuous purchase from distant markets faced disruption in those markets and rising costs.

The long consequence was that the sultanate's second period operated under permanent demographic pressure that its first had not known, which is a substantial part of what the article's section on the strain describes.

The general observation is that the largest events in a period are frequently not political at all, and a regime that stopped the Mongols and removed the crusader states was reduced by an organism nobody could see.

## Religion and the Establishment

The relationship between the ruling caste and the religious institutions is central to how the regime governed and deserves a hub statement.

The starting position was a legitimacy deficit larger than any predecessor's, since former slaves have no descent, no local base and no religious authority of their own.

The response was patronage on an unprecedented scale, and the article on Mamluk architecture describes mosques, colleges, hospitals and charitable foundations endowed in numbers that dwarfed anything the Ayyubids or Fatimids had managed.

The al-Azhar restoration is the emblematic case, and the article on al-Azhar describes an institution suppressed by the Ayyubids as a Fatimid foundation, then restored and richly endowed by the Mamluks until it became the foremost school of the Sunni world.

The mechanism is the one that article explains at length: endowed property assigned irrevocably to a purpose creates institutions with revenue independent of any ruler, so a patronage programme outlasts the regime that funds it.

The four schools were all supported, continuing the Ayyubid policy the article on religion and tolerance in Ayyubid Egypt describes as a deliberate breadth calculated to secure the widest possible establishment backing.

The caliphal arrangement supplied the formal covering, with a member of the Abbasid family maintained in Cairo after 1258 granting investiture.

The scholars' position was correspondingly strong, since a class with endowed revenue and a ruler who needs its endorsement has real independence, and the Mamluk period produced a religious establishment of considerable standing.

The general observation is that the deepest Mamluk legacy is institutional rather than military, since the endowed foundations of these centuries shaped Egyptian religious life for far longer than the sultanate itself lasted.

## How the Country Was Actually Governed

The administration beneath the military caste deserves attention, since a cavalry elite of a few thousand men did not personally run a country of millions.

The machinery was the inherited one, and the article on taxation in early Islamic Egypt establishes the pattern this series has traced from the pharaohs: districts, registers, assessment keyed to the flood, collection through village headmen, operated by secretarial families whose expertise passed down through generations.

The Mamluk relationship to it was that of employer to a professional class, since the ruling caste spoke Turkic or Circassian among themselves and the administration functioned in Arabic.

The division of labour was therefore sharp and stable. The military caste held the sword, the revenue assignments and the sultanate; the Arabic speaking bureaucracy held the registers, the accounts and the day to day operation of the state.

The judiciary was a third element, with judges of all four Sunni legal schools appointed, which the article on religion and tolerance in Ayyubid Egypt describes as a deliberate Ayyubid policy that the Mamluks continued and extended.

The religious establishment was a fourth, funded by the endowments described in the article's own section on legitimacy and operating with the independence that endowed property confers.

The provincial structure covered Egypt in districts and Syria in provinces under Mamluk governors, with the article's later section on the provinces treating the arrangement.

The consequence of this division is that Egypt was governed competently through periods when the sultanate itself was in factional chaos, since the machinery did not depend on who held the Citadel.

The general observation is the one this series makes repeatedly and the Mamluk case demonstrates most clearly: the visible layer of Egyptian history is the ruling caste and the durable layer is the administration, and the second continued through every disturbance in the first.

## The Economy

The commercial and fiscal basis is treated fully in the article on Mamluk Egypt and world trade, and the hub statement belongs here.

The inheritance was the position the article on the Ayyubid economy describes: the only practical route by which eastern goods reached Europe, taxed at several points and supplemented by an agricultural base renewed annually by the flood.

The peak came in this period, and the article on Fatimid trade notes that the great spice merchants reached their highest wealth and influence in the later medieval centuries rather than in the Fatimid or Ayyubid ones.

The state's role shifted decisively toward participation and then toward monopoly, continuing the drift the article on the Ayyubid economy traces and taking it considerably further.

The consequence of the monopolies is the standing risk of a transit economy, which the article on Fatimid trade names: a merchant with a choice of routes compares total costs, so a state that raises its share too far watches its traffic look elsewhere.

The agricultural base suffered across the period from the demographic collapse the article on the Black Death in Mamluk Egypt describes, since fewer cultivators means less land under cultivation and less revenue.

The monetary difficulties compounded everything, and the article on the Ayyubid economy describes what a reliable currency does for a transit economy, which indicates the cost of losing one.

The building programme was funded from all of it, and the article on Mamluk architecture describes the endowed foundations that absorbed a very large share of elite wealth.

The general observation is that the Mamluk economy was the medieval Egyptian position at its peak followed by its longest sustained decline, and that both halves occupied the same regime, which is why accounts of the period disagree so sharply about whether it was prosperous.

## Removing the Crusader States

The elimination of a presence that four centuries of Muslim powers had failed to remove deserves its own treatment, since it is the achievement the Ayyubid cluster sets up.

The position inherited in 1250 was a coastal strip with fortified cities, supplied by sea from Europe, which the article on Saladin and the crusades identifies as the standing obstacle: a land power cannot reduce a sea supplied enemy without command of the sea.

The Mamluk solution was systematic rather than clever. Fortress by fortress and city by city across four decades, taking each place and then destroying its fortifications and harbour so it could not be reoccupied.

The demolition policy is the distinctive element and it is the answer to the naval problem. A port that has been dismantled cannot receive a fleet, so a coastline systematically ruined stops being a landing ground.

The cost of that policy was economic, since the destroyed ports had been commercial assets, and it is a striking measure of how seriously the threat was taken.

The sequence ran across the reigns of several sultans, and the article on Baybars treats the ruler who conducted much of it and who established the pattern.

The final significant coastal holding fell in 1291, which is the conventional end date though smaller positions persisted a little longer.

The comparison with the Ayyubid record is the one the article on Ayyubid Egypt draws and it is unflattering to the earlier regime: Saladin recovered Jerusalem and left the coast, while the Mamluks removed the presence entirely.

The reason for the difference is the succession, and the article on Ayyubid Egypt argues that a family confederation fragmenting at every death cannot sustain a forty year campaign while a caste selecting adult sultans from within itself can.

The general observation is that long campaigns are won by institutional continuity rather than by generalship, and the Mamluk elimination of the crusader states is among the clearest demonstrations available.

## The Army

The military institution that defined the regime deserves a hub statement, with the article on the Mamluk war machine carrying the detail.

The core was heavy cavalry, trained from childhood in horsemanship, archery from the saddle, lance and sword, at a level of skill that required a decade of practice to reach.

The training regime is what distinguishes this force from the imported soldiers of earlier Egyptian regimes, and the article on who the Mamluks were describes a systematic programme rather than the purchase of ready made troops.

The scale was modest by modern standards and large for the period, with the sultan's own corps supplemented by the households of the senior commanders, each of whom maintained his own purchased and trained soldiers.

The household structure is the organizational key and it explains the politics, since a commander's power was measured in the men he had bought and trained, which made military strength and political weight the same quantity.

The auxiliary forces included Bedouin contingents, Syrian troops, infantry and specialist units, and the article on the Mamluk war machine describes the composition.

The financing was the revenue assignment system inherited from the Ayyubids, and the article on the Ayyubid economy describes soldiers holding assignments of land revenue and collecting directly.

The weakness that eventually mattered was adaptation, since a system built around a decade of cavalry training is slow to absorb firearms, and the article on the Ottoman conquest treats the consequence.

The general observation is that an institution optimized for one form of warfare is difficult to reform precisely because its excellence depends on a long training pipeline, and the thing that made the Mamluk army formidable for two centuries is the same thing that made it obsolete in one.

## The World in 1250

Placing the founding in its context explains why a regime of this kind emerged at this moment and why its first decade was so consequential.

The Islamic world in 1250 was in the worst condition of its history to that point. The Abbasid caliphate at Baghdad was a symbolic institution with eight years left to live, Syria was fragmented among Ayyubid relatives, and no power in the region could concentrate resources on anything.

The Mongol advance had been running for four decades, destroying the states of Central Asia and Persia in sequence, and every polity that had faced it had been broken. Nothing in the historical record available to contemporaries suggested it could be stopped.

The crusader states had held the Syrian coast for a hundred and fifty years and had just mounted a major invasion of Egypt itself, which the article on crusader invasions of Egypt describes ending in the Delta in 1250.

The Ayyubid confederation was in its final condition, and the article on Ayyubid Egypt describes a family arrangement that had fragmented and reassembled twice and was doing neither successfully by this point.

Egypt itself was intact, wealthy and defensible, and the article on the Ayyubid economy sets out the transit trade and the agricultural base that made it the strongest position in the region.

The military corps was the newest element, purchased and trained by the last effective Ayyubid sultan, quartered on a river island, and freshly victorious against a crusader invasion.

The general observation is that the Mamluk sultanate was founded at the moment of maximum danger and maximum opportunity, with an intact revenue base, a victorious professional army, no functioning central authority above it, and two existential threats approaching from opposite directions.

## The Mongol Confrontation

The threat that defined the regime's first decade deserves a hub statement, with the article on the battle of Ayn Jalut carrying the detail.

The scale of the Mongol achievement by 1258 is difficult to overstate. Empires across Central Asia and Persia had been destroyed, and in that year Baghdad was taken and the Abbasid caliph killed, ending an institution that had existed since 750.

The article on the Fatimid and Abbasid rivalry treats that event from the other side and notes that it was felt as a rupture across the entire Sunni world, since a caliphate with a genuine constituency had been removed by force.

The advance continued into Syria, taking Aleppo and Damascus, which put Mongol forces on the approaches to Egypt and made the confrontation unavoidable.

The Egyptian response was to march north rather than to await attack, which is a considerable decision for a regime less than a decade old with an unsettled succession.

The engagement in Palestine in 1260 is the subject of the article on the battle of Ayn Jalut, which treats the circumstances, the conduct and the significance, including the important qualification that the force engaged was not the main Mongol army.

The consequences were immediate and long. The advance was checked, Syria was recovered, and the sultanate acquired the founding achievement that its legitimacy rested on thereafter.

The confrontation continued for decades afterward with further campaigns against the Mongol successor state in Persia, so 1260 was a beginning rather than a conclusion.

The general observation is that a regime's founding achievement shapes everything it says about itself afterward, and a caste of former slaves that had stopped the force which destroyed the caliphate had an answer to the legitimacy question that no amount of genealogy could match.

## The Two Periods

The internal division of the sultanate is the first structural fact to hold, and it is a matter of recruitment region rather than of dynasty in the ordinary sense.

### What were the two Mamluk dynasties?

The Turkic period from 1250 to 1382 and the Circassian period from 1382 to 1517. The names are conventional rather than dynastic, since neither was a family line: one is named for the river island barracks of the founding corps and the other for the Caucasus region its recruits came from. Recruitment changed, the system did not.

The first period drew its recruits chiefly from the Turkic peoples of the steppe north of the Black Sea, which is the same recruitment pool the article on the Tulunid dynasty describes for the ninth century.

The second period drew chiefly from the Circassian population of the Caucasus, a shift produced by changing availability in the slave markets rather than by any policy decision.

### How long did the Mamluks rule Egypt?

Two hundred and sixty seven years, from 1250 to 1517. That is longer than the Fatimids, the Ayyubids and the Tulunids put together, and it makes the Mamluk sultanate by a considerable margin the most durable regime in medieval Egyptian history.

The difference between the periods is real and is one of emphasis rather than of kind. The first was the era of the great military achievements; the second was the era of the greatest building and of the deepest economic difficulty.

The continuity across both is the system itself, which the article on who the Mamluks were describes and which operated on the same principles throughout.

The labels themselves are later scholarly conventions, and contemporaries did not divide their own history this way, which is worth knowing before treating the division as more than a useful shorthand.

The general observation is that a regime lasting nearly three centuries will change substantially inside itself, and the two period division is the standard way of marking a shift that was gradual and real.

## The Great Victories

The military achievements are what the sultanate is remembered for and they belong to specialists, so the hub statement is what belongs here.

The Mongol confrontation came first and the article on the battle of Ayn Jalut treats it fully. A force that had destroyed the Abbasid caliphate at Baghdad in 1258, and every other state in its path across two continents, was defeated in Palestine in 1260 by a Mamluk army from Egypt.

The significance is that it was the first serious check the Mongol advance had received, and the article on the Fatimid and Abbasid rivalry describes what had happened at Baghdad two years earlier to indicate what was at stake.

The crusader elimination came next and ran across four decades, with fortress after fortress and city after city taken, ending with the last significant coastal holding in 1291.

The comparison with the Ayyubid record is the one the article on Ayyubid Egypt draws. Saladin recovered Jerusalem and left the coast in enemy hands; the Mamluks removed the crusader states entirely, and the difference is a regime with the succession problem solved.

The Syrian consolidation followed, giving the sultanate control of the whole eastern Mediterranean coast and its hinterland.

The later campaigns were less spectacular and more defensive, against Mongol successor states, against Turkmen powers in Anatolia and eventually against the Ottomans.

The general observation is that a military caste organized around cavalry produced military results, which is not surprising, and that the interesting question is how such a caste governed a complex agricultural and commercial state for nearly three centuries between the campaigns.

## The Sultans

Who actually held the throne and how they got there is worth setting out at hub level, since the pattern differs from every other regime in this series.

### Who were the Mamluk sultans?

Former slave soldiers who had risen through the military hierarchy, selected by their peers rather than inheriting. Roughly fifty men held the office across the period, some for decades and many for months. The most celebrated is Baybars, who consolidated the regime after Ayn Jalut and whose reign the specialist article treats.

### How did a Mamluk become sultan?

By rising through the military hierarchy and then commanding enough support among the senior commanders to take and hold the position. Purchase, training, manumission, service in a household, promotion to command and the accumulation of a personal following were the stages, and the final step was agreement or force among the men who mattered.

### Were Mamluk sultans hereditary rulers?

Not in principle and sometimes in practice. Sons of sultans were frequently installed and rarely lasted, because a Mamluk's son was born free and Muslim and therefore could not enter the caste his father had risen through. Such sons served as short lived placeholders while the commanders settled the succession among themselves.

### How did Mamluk succession actually work?

By a contest among the senior commanders, resolved by negotiation, alliance and sometimes force. A sultan's son might be installed briefly to hold the position while the real decision was made. The system had no rule determining the outcome and it reliably produced a sultan, which is more than the dynastic regimes in this series managed.

The turnover was consequently high, and the article's own count indicates roughly fifty sultans across two hundred and sixty seven years, which averages just over five years each.

The long reigns are the exceptions that shaped the period, since several sultans held power for two decades or more and those reigns account for a disproportionate share of the building, the campaigns and the institutional development.

The comparison with the dynastic regimes is the analytical point. The article on the Fatimid caliphs describes six child accessions in eleven reigns because designation takes no account of competence; the Mamluk arrangement produced adults who had survived a competitive hierarchy, at the cost of frequent instability.

The general observation is that the Mamluk system solved the succession problem that the article on Ayyubid Egypt identifies as destroying three consecutive Egyptian regimes, and it solved it by abandoning the assumption that rule passes through families, which is a considerable thing for any political system to give up.

## The Capital

Cairo under the Mamluks became something it had not been before, and the transformation deserves a hub statement.

### What was the capital of the Mamluk Sultanate?

Cairo, governed from the Citadel on the hills to the east. Across the Mamluk centuries it grew into the greatest city of the Islamic world by population, wealth and building, and the density of surviving Mamluk architecture in the medieval quarter is unmatched anywhere.

The Citadel was inherited from the Ayyubids, and the article on the Citadel of Cairo describes the Mamluks converting a fortress into a palace and government complex with residences, audience halls, mosques and enormous stables.

The city grew around it, and the article on the founding of the garrison city describes the older commercial settlement and the walled Fatimid enclosure merging through growth, with the space between them and the hill becoming the most desirable quarter.

The building programme is the physical legacy and the article on Mamluk architecture treats it, with mosques, colleges, hospitals, mausoleums and commercial buildings raised in numbers and quality that define the medieval Cairo a visitor sees.

The population made it the largest city west of China by most estimates, and the article on Fatimid trade explains the commercial position that supported it.

The comparison with the alternatives is the striking part. Baghdad had been sacked in 1258, Damascus was a provincial centre under Cairo's authority, and the European cities were far smaller, which left Cairo without a rival in its world.

The general observation is that a city becomes great when the wealth of a large territory concentrates in it and something induces the wealthy to build, and Mamluk Cairo had both conditions in a degree nothing in the region matched.

## The Scale of the Power

Assessing what the sultanate actually commanded requires the same discipline this series applies to every claim of wealth and strength.

### How powerful was Mamluk Egypt?

The leading power of the Islamic world for most of the period. It defeated the Mongols, eliminated the crusader states, held Egypt, Syria and the Hijaz, controlled the eastern transit trade at its peak, and maintained a cavalry army no regional rival matched. Regional rather than global, and dominant in its own world.

### What territories did the Mamluk Sultanate control?

Egypt from the Mediterranean to Nubia, Syria and Palestine including the whole eastern Mediterranean coast, and the holy cities of the Hijaz through protectorship rather than direct rule. Influence extended into southern Anatolia and along the Red Sea, and the effective frontier with rival powers shifted repeatedly across the period.

The military capacity was the foundation and the article on the Mamluk war machine treats it, describing a professional cavalry force trained from childhood in a system the article on who the Mamluks were sets out.

The economic capacity is the subject of the article on Mamluk Egypt and world trade, and the article on Fatimid trade supplies the geography: the only practical route by which eastern goods reached Europe.

The prestige asset was the protectorship of the holy cities, which the article on Ayyubid Egypt describes as among the strongest claims a Muslim sovereign can make.

The diplomatic reach extended to the Mongol successor states, the Italian merchant republics, the Byzantines and eventually the Ottomans, with a documented correspondence.

The symbolic asset was the Abbasid caliph maintained in Cairo after 1258, and the article on the Fatimid and Abbasid rivalry notes the irony: the city built to house a rival caliphate ended by housing a puppet version of the one it had opposed.

The general observation is that power at this level is regional rather than global, and the sultanate was the strongest state in a world running from Morocco to Persia, which is the correct frame for judging it.

## Legitimacy

How a regime of former slaves justified its rule is the most interesting political question the period poses, and it deserves a hub treatment.

### How did the Mamluks legitimize their rule?

Through service rather than descent. They presented themselves as the defenders of Islam who stopped the Mongols and removed the crusaders, as the protectors of the pilgrimage and the holy cities, and as the patrons of religious institutions on an enormous scale. They also maintained an Abbasid caliph in Cairo whose formal investiture supplied a legal covering.

The achievement claim was the strongest and it was genuine, since a regime that had done what four centuries of Muslim powers had failed to do could point at results rather than at ancestry.

The caliphal covering was the formal element, and after the Mongols destroyed Baghdad in 1258 a member of the Abbasid family was brought to Cairo and maintained there, granting investiture to sultans who needed the legal form.

The value of that arrangement is exactly what the article on the Fatimid and Abbasid rivalry describes: an endorsement costing the grantor nothing that gives a ruler standing with his own Sunni population.

The religious patronage was the third and largest element, and the article on Mamluk architecture describes mosques, colleges and charitable foundations endowed on a scale that dwarfed anything previous Egyptian regimes had managed.

The al-Azhar restoration belongs here, and the article on al-Azhar describes the Mamluks restoring and richly endowing an institution the Ayyubids had suppressed, which turned it into the foremost Sunni school in the world.

The pilgrimage protection completed the picture, since organizing and securing the annual caravan is a visible service to the whole Muslim community.

The general observation is that a regime without descent must buy legitimacy with performance and patronage, and the Mamluk investment in both was proportionate to the size of the deficit they started with.

## The Golden Age

The claim that this period was a high point deserves examining rather than asserting, since golden ages are usually constructed retrospectively.

### Why is the Mamluk period called a golden age?

Because of the concentration of building, wealth and cultural production in Cairo across the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. The city was the largest and richest in the Islamic world, its architecture was of the first order, and its scholarship was prolific. The label describes Cairo far better than Egypt as a whole.

The architecture is the strongest element of the claim, and the article on Mamluk architecture describes the buildings that define the medieval city.

The scholarship is the second, since the Mamluk period produced an enormous body of historical, biographical, legal and scientific writing, and this series relies on the fifteenth century Egyptian topographical compilation throughout.

The commercial position is the third, and the article on Fatimid trade notes that the Karimi merchants reached their greatest wealth and influence in exactly these centuries.

The al-Azhar restoration is the fourth, and the article on that institution treats the Mamluk period as its real formative era rather than its Fatimid foundation.

The qualification is substantial and this article states it. The same centuries contained repeated plague, monetary disorder, agricultural decline in some regions and heavy fiscal pressure, and the article on the Black Death in Mamluk Egypt treats the demographic catastrophe.

The distribution qualification is equally important, since a golden age of building in Cairo funded by extraction from the countryside is not obviously golden for the people doing the paying.

The general observation is that a golden age is usually a statement about elite production rather than about general welfare, and the Mamluk case is a particularly clear instance of the two coming apart.

## The Strain

The difficulties of the later period deserve treatment, since a hub that presents only achievement misrepresents nearly two centuries.

### What weakened the Mamluk Sultanate before 1517?

Repeated plague from 1347 onward reducing population and revenue, monetary disorder, heavy fiscal extraction that damaged the agricultural base, the drift toward commercial monopolies that gave merchants reasons to seek other routes, factional violence among the military households, and finally the opening of the sea route around Africa which removed the necessity of the Egyptian corridor.

The plague is the largest single factor and the article on the Black Death in Mamluk Egypt treats it, describing recurring waves rather than a single event.

The demographic consequence reached everything, since fewer cultivators means less revenue, less revenue means fiscal pressure, and fiscal pressure applied to a shrinking population accelerates the decline.

The monetary disorder followed, and the article on Fatimid trade describes what a reliable currency does for a transit economy, which indicates what its loss costs.

The commercial monopolies are the policy failure, and the article on the Ayyubid economy traces the drift from taxing toward participating toward monopolizing, with the competitive discipline eventually asserting itself.

The factional violence was chronic throughout and intensified, since a system that selects sultans by contest among commanders produces armed disputes whenever the contest is close.

The ocean route is the external shock, and the article on Fatimid trade names it as the technology that removed a thousand year advantage without anything in Egypt changing.

The general observation is that the sultanate's decline was cumulative rather than sudden, running across a century and a half, and that a regime surviving plague, monetary collapse and the loss of its principal revenue source for that long was more resilient than the eventual defeat suggests.

## The Ending

How the sultanate fell belongs to the article on the Ottoman conquest of Mamluk Egypt, and the hub statement belongs here.

The opponent was the Ottoman empire, which had taken Constantinople in 1453 and expanded across Anatolia and the Balkans, and which by the early sixteenth century was the dominant power of the eastern Mediterranean.

The confrontation came in 1516 in northern Syria and in 1517 in Egypt itself, and the article on the Ottoman conquest of Egypt in 1517 treats the campaigns.

The military asymmetry is the standard explanation and it needs qualifying, since the Ottoman use of firearms and artillery was more developed while the Mamluk cavalry tradition was formidable and the outcome was not a foregone conclusion.

### What came after the Mamluks?

Ottoman rule. Egypt became a province of the Ottoman empire in 1517 and remained one in form for nearly three centuries, governed by appointed officials with a garrison of Ottoman corps. The article on Ottoman Egypt treats the period, which is the subject of the next cluster in this series.

### Did the Mamluks survive after 1517?

As a governing class, yes, for nearly three centuries. Mamluk households continued to recruit, train and buy soldiers, held land and offices under Ottoman authority, and progressively recovered much of the real power in Egypt. Their political existence ended only in 1811, and the article on the Citadel of Cairo describes the event.

The survival is the most surprising fact about the ending, since a defeated ruling caste that continues recruiting and eventually dominates its conquerors' province is an unusual outcome.

The consequence for Egyptian history is that the Mamluk system shaped the country for over five and a half centuries in total rather than for two and a half.

The comparison with the Fatimid ending is instructive and the article on how Saladin ended the Fatimids supplies it: a dynasty with no constituency vanished at a word, while a military caste with land, households and recruitment networks survived conquest by an empire.

The general observation is that regimes end at different depths, and a change of sovereign is a shallower event than the destruction of a governing class, which is why 1517 changed less in Egypt than the date suggests.

## Myths Worth Correcting

Six claims about this period circulate and each misleads.

The first is that Mamluk means the same as slave throughout. These were military slaves purchased as children, converted, trained, manumitted and then serving as free men, and the sultans were freedmen rather than slaves.

The second is that the sultanate was two dynasties. Neither period was a family line, and the labels describe recruitment regions and barracks locations rather than descent.

The third is that rule by slave soldiers meant instability and weakness. Turnover at the top was high and the regime lasted longer than any other in medieval Egypt and defeated every opponent it faced until 1516.

The fourth is that sons never succeeded. Sons were frequently installed and rarely lasted, serving as placeholders while the commanders settled the real succession among themselves.

The fifth is that Ayn Jalut destroyed the Mongol threat permanently. It stopped an advance and further confrontations continued for decades, which the article on that battle treats.

The sixth is that 1517 ended Mamluk power in Egypt altogether. The households survived the conquest, continued to recruit and train, held land and offices under Ottoman authority, and progressively came to dominate the province until 1811, which is nearly three further centuries.

A seventh belief is a proportion error rather than a myth, which is treating the whole period as an epilogue to the crusades. The crusader states were eliminated within the first forty years, and the sultanate then continued for another two and a quarter centuries doing entirely different things, which is the great majority of its existence.

## The Verdict

The argument this article has defended is that the Mamluk system produced the most durable and the most formidable regime in medieval Egyptian history precisely because it abandoned the one assumption every predecessor had shared, which is that rule passes through families and that a ruler's task includes securing it for his sons.

The problem it solved is the one this series has traced three separate times across four centuries. The article on the Tulunid dynasty describes an imported military establishment destroying its own regime within a few years of the founder's death. The article on Fatimid Egypt describes four imported groups fighting each other in the capital and reducing caliphs to figureheads for a century. And the article on Ayyubid Egypt describes a professional corps dispensing with the dynasty that had built it, in 1250, which is where this article begins.

The solution was to stop pretending otherwise. If the corps holds the power, then let the corps rule openly, recruit its own successors by purchase in every generation, and select its sultans from among its own senior members, which removes at a stroke the mismatch between formal authority and actual power that had destroyed three consecutive predecessors.

The cost was chronic instability at the top, with roughly fifty sultans across two hundred and sixty seven years and factional violence at nearly every transition, which is the unavoidable price of a system containing no succession rule at all. The second cost was moral and this article has stated it plainly in its own section: the continuous purchase of children.

The benefit was that every sultan was an adult who had been trained for a decade, had commanded troops and had survived a competitive hierarchy before reaching the throne. The article on the Fatimid caliphs supplies the contrast, describing six child accessions in eleven Egyptian reigns produced by a doctrine of designation that took no account whatever of age or capacity.

The results were the ones this article has set out across its sections: the Mongol advance stopped in 1260 after it had destroyed every state in its path including the caliphate at Baghdad, the crusader states eliminated entirely by 1291 after four centuries of Muslim powers failing to remove them, Syria and the Hijaz held for two and a half centuries, the eastern transit trade brought to its medieval peak, and Cairo made the greatest city of the Islamic world.

The qualifications are those stated throughout and each is substantial. Revenue figures are unusable, as everywhere in this series. Population estimates for medieval cities are inferences rather than counts. The golden age label describes elite production in one city rather than general welfare across a country. The decline ran across a century and a half and the relative weight of its causes is genuinely debated. The rural experience of the whole period is reconstructed from revenue rather than observed. And the specialists in this cluster carry the detail that this hub deliberately routes to them.

What remains is the observation this article opened with. For two hundred and sixty seven years the richest state in the Islamic world was governed by men who had been bought as children in slave markets a thousand kilometres and more from Cairo, converted, trained for a decade, freed, and then promoted through a hierarchy in which none of them could pass his position to a son. The arrangement stopped the Mongols, removed the crusader states, built the greatest city of its world, and outlasted every dynasty that had ever tried to rule Egypt by blood. It is among the stranger political systems any society has devised and among the more successful.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the Mamluk Sultanate known for?

Four things above all. It stopped the Mongol advance at Ayn Jalut in 1260, which was the first serious check that force had received after destroying every state in its path including the Abbasid caliphate at Baghdad. It eliminated the crusader states entirely by 1291, completing what the Ayyubids had begun and could not finish. It made Cairo the greatest city of the Islamic world in population, wealth and building. And it was governed throughout by a caste of former slave soldiers who renewed themselves from the slave market rather than through inheritance.

### Q: How long did the Mamluks rule Egypt?

Two hundred and sixty seven years, from 1250 to 1517. That is longer than the Fatimids, the Ayyubids and the Tulunids combined, and it makes the Mamluk sultanate by a considerable margin the most durable regime in medieval Egyptian history. The Mamluk households then survived the Ottoman conquest as a governing class, continuing to recruit, hold land and exercise real power in Egypt for nearly three further centuries until 1811, so the system shaped the country for well over five hundred years in total.

### Q: Who were the Mamluk sultans?

Former slave soldiers who had been purchased as boys, converted, trained intensively from childhood, manumitted and then risen through the military hierarchy to command. Roughly fifty men held the office across the period, some for two decades or more and many for only months. They were selected by contest and agreement among the senior commanders rather than by inheritance. The most celebrated is Baybars, who consolidated the regime after the Mongol victory and whose reign the specialist article in this cluster treats in full.

### Q: When did the Mamluk Sultanate begin?

In 1250, when the military slave corps built by the last effective Ayyubid sultan killed his heir and took power in its own name during a crusader invasion of the Delta. The transition took several years to settle, with a former sultan's widow briefly holding authority and several arrangements tried and abandoned, so the founding is a process across the early 1250s rather than a single dated act. The regime stabilized properly only after the Mongol crisis of 1260 gave it a founding achievement to point at.

### Q: What were the two Mamluk dynasties?

The Turkic period from 1250 to 1382 and the Circassian period from 1382 to 1517. Neither was a dynasty in any ordinary sense, since neither was a family line: the first is named for the river island barracks where the founding corps was quartered and the second for the Caucasus region from which its recruits were increasingly drawn. The change was in the source of recruitment rather than in the system, it happened gradually rather than at a stroke, and the labels themselves are later scholarly conventions.

### Q: What was the capital of the Mamluk Sultanate?

Cairo, governed from the Citadel on the Muqattam hills to the east of the city, which the Mamluks inherited from the Ayyubids and converted from a fortress into a palace and government complex. Across the Mamluk centuries the city grew into the greatest in the Islamic world by population, wealth and building, and the density of surviving Mamluk architecture in the medieval quarter is unmatched anywhere. With Baghdad sacked in 1258 and Damascus a provincial centre under Cairo's own authority, it had no regional rival.

### Q: How powerful was Mamluk Egypt?

The leading power of the Islamic world for most of the period. It defeated the Mongols, eliminated the crusader states, held Egypt, Syria and Palestine directly and the holy cities of the Hijaz through protectorship, controlled the eastern transit trade at its medieval peak, and maintained a professional cavalry army no regional rival could match until the Ottomans. It was not a world power in any modern sense and it was the most formidable state in a world running from Morocco to Persia for two and a half centuries.

### Q: What came after the Mamluks?

Ottoman rule. Egypt became a province of the Ottoman empire after the campaigns of 1516 and 1517 and remained one in form for nearly three centuries, governed by appointed officials with garrisons of Ottoman corps quartered in the Citadel above Cairo. The reality on the ground was considerably more complicated than that formal description suggests, since the Mamluk households survived the conquest, continued recruiting and training exactly as before, and progressively recovered much of the real power in the province. The Ottoman period is the subject of the next cluster in this series.

### Q: How did a Mamluk become sultan?

By rising through the military hierarchy and then commanding enough support among the senior commanders to take the position and hold it against rivals. The stages were purchase as a boy from outside the Islamic world, conversion, intensive training from childhood, manumission on completion, service in a commander's household, promotion to command, and the accumulation of a personal following of one's own purchased soldiers. The final step was agreement or force among the men who mattered, and both were used regularly. Nothing in the system determined the outcome in advance.

### Q: Were Mamluk sultans hereditary rulers?

Not in principle and only sometimes in practice. Sons of sultans were frequently installed and rarely lasted long, and the structural reason is elegant: a Mamluk's son was born free and Muslim, which disqualified him from the caste his father had risen through, since the whole system rested on purchase and training from outside. Such sons therefore functioned as short lived placeholders holding the throne while the senior commanders settled the real succession among themselves by negotiation or by force.

### Q: How did Mamluk succession actually work?

By a contest among the senior commanders, resolved through alliance, negotiation and sometimes armed force in the streets of the capital. There was no rule determining who should succeed and no mechanism for designating an heir who would be accepted after a sultan's death. What the system reliably produced was an adult sultan who had survived a competitive military hierarchy, which is considerably more than the dynastic regimes preceding it managed. The price was frequent instability, with roughly fifty sultans across two hundred and sixty seven years.

### Q: What territories did the Mamluk Sultanate control?

Egypt from the Mediterranean south toward Nubia, Syria and Palestine including the entire eastern Mediterranean coast once the crusader states had been removed, and the holy cities of the Hijaz through a protectorship exercised via local rulers rather than by direct administration. Influence extended into southern Anatolia and down the Red Sea toward Yemen, varying with circumstance. The effective frontiers with the Mongol successor states, with the Turkmen powers of Anatolia and eventually with the Ottomans shifted repeatedly across the two and a half centuries.

### Q: How did the Mamluks legitimize their rule?

Through service and patronage rather than descent, since they had no descent claim available. They presented themselves as the defenders of Islam who had stopped the Mongols and removed the crusaders, as the protectors of the pilgrimage and the holy cities, and as patrons of religious institutions on a scale that dwarfed every previous Egyptian regime. They also maintained a member of the Abbasid family in Cairo after 1258 as a caliph whose formal investiture supplied a legal covering that cost them nothing.

### Q: What language did the Mamluks speak?

Turkic languages among themselves in the first period and Circassian in the second, since the ruling caste was recruited from those regions and retained its own speech as a mark of caste identity that distinguished it from the population. Arabic was the language of administration, law, scholarship, religion and the people they governed, and Mamluk sultans commissioned Arabic literature, endowed Arabic teaching and issued all their documents in Arabic. Some rulers were fluent in it and some were not. The multilingual arrangement was entirely normal for the period.

### Q: Why is the Mamluk period called a golden age?

Because of the concentration of building, wealth and cultural production in Cairo across the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. The city was the largest and richest in the Islamic world, its architecture was of the first order and survives in remarkable quantity, its religious institutions were preeminent, and the period produced an enormous body of historical, biographical and scientific writing that this series relies on throughout. The label describes elite production in one city considerably better than it describes general welfare in Egypt.

### Q: What weakened the Mamluk Sultanate before 1517?

Several pressures accumulating across a century and a half rather than any single blow. Recurring plague from 1347 onward reduced the population and therefore the revenue. Monetary disorder undermined the currency a transit economy depends on. Heavy fiscal extraction applied to a shrinking population damaged the agricultural base further. The drift toward state monopolies on key commodities gave merchants reasons to seek alternative routes. Factional violence among the military households intensified. And the opening of the sea route around Africa eventually removed the necessity of the Egyptian corridor altogether.

### Q: Did the Mamluks survive after 1517?

As a governing class, remarkably, for nearly three further centuries after the conquest. Mamluk households continued to purchase, train and manumit soldiers exactly as they always had, held land and offices under Ottoman authority, and progressively recovered much of the real power in the province while the appointed governors' authority became increasingly formal and increasingly contested. Their political existence in Egypt ended only in 1811, in the event at the Citadel that the article on that complex describes, which is nearly six centuries after the system first arrived in the country.

### Q: How is the Mamluk period viewed by historians?

Far more favourably than it once was. Older writing tended to treat it as a decadent interlude between the crusading era and the Ottoman conquest, judging it by the decline of its final century. Current work emphasizes the durability of the system, the sophistication of its administration, the quality and quantity of its cultural production, the richness of its documentary record, and the sheer improbability of a self renewing slave elite governing a major state successfully for two and a half centuries.

### Q: What sources describe the Mamluk Sultanate?

An exceptionally rich body of material, considerably better than for any earlier Egyptian period covered in this series. The Mamluk centuries produced an enormous historiographical tradition of chronicles, biographical dictionaries and administrative manuals, including the fifteenth century Egyptian topographical compilation that these articles rely on throughout. Endowment deeds survive in quantity and document individual institutions with legal precision. Dated inscriptions cover hundreds of surviving buildings. European commercial and diplomatic records describe the same trade from the buying end, which supplies an independent check on the Egyptian accounts.

### Q: Why do the Mamluks matter to Egyptian history?

Because they governed Egypt for longer than any other medieval regime, shaped the physical city that a visitor to Cairo still walks through, held the country's commercial position at its peak, and produced the documentary record from which much of the earlier history in this series is reconstructed. They also solved the succession problem that had destroyed three consecutive Egyptian dynasties, by abandoning the assumption that rule passes through families, which is among the more radical political innovations of the medieval world.
