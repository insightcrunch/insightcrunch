Compositor analysis is the bibliographical discipline that reads a printed book as the record of the hands that made it, and Romeo and Juliet needs that discipline more than most plays of its period. Every early text of the tragedy, the two quartos and the First Folio, passed letter by letter through the fingers of workmen whose names are lost, whose spelling habits differed from the playwright's and from each other's, and whose errors stand in the line of descent between the lost manuscript and every modern edition. The verdict argued through this study is plain: compositor analysis cannot hand back the author's manuscript, and its boldest claims deserve the scepticism they have drawn, but it remains the only method that can separate, page by page and habit by habit, what the playwright wrote from what the shop imposed. Without it, an editor mistakes a typesetter's favourite spelling for an author's, a tired apprentice's guess for a reading, and a press correction for an intention. With it, used at its proper strength, the early pages become evidence twice over: for the words on them, and for the workshop that put the words there.

![Compositor analysis and the printing house - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-59.webp)

The stakes are easy to miss because modern editions hide them. A school text of the balcony scene prints "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks" (2.2.2) in clean modern spelling, and nothing on the page admits that the line reached print through a quarto set in Thomas Creede's shop in 1599 from a manuscript whose own spellings the workmen partly followed and partly overrode, then through a Folio set a generation later in William Jaggard's shop from a printed quarto, where a single apprentice set nearly the whole play. Compositor analysis is the set of techniques, spelling tests, type evidence, press-variant study, and shop history, by which scholars reconstruct that hidden labour. Its findings decide practical questions an editor faces on every page: whether a strange word is more likely the author's oddity or a setter's misreading, whether a passage printed as prose was prose in the copy or verse crushed by a man short of space, and whether a whole stretch of a book deserves less trust than the stretches around it. This article explains the method, follows it into the two printing houses that matter for the play, weighs the great Folio investigation of Charlton Hinman against its sharpest critics, and reaches a graded verdict on what the evidence can and cannot prove.

## What Is Compositor Analysis, and Why Does This Play Need It?

A compositor was the workman who built a page of type. Standing at a wooden frame holding two shallow cases of loose metal sorts, one for capitals and one for lower case, he read a line of his copy, held the words in memory, and picked the letters one by one into a composing stick held in his left hand, justifying each line with spaces of varying thickness so that every line filled the measure exactly. When a page was complete he tied it up, and pages were assembled into formes, the locked chases of type that printed one side of a sheet. The compositor was therefore the last craftsman to touch every word of a book before the press crews, the correctors, and the readers did, and he touched the words under pressures that had nothing to do with literature: piece rates, deadlines, a case running short of a common letter, a colleague waiting for the same sorts. His spelling, his spacing, his tolerance for cramped lines, and his skill at deciphering handwriting all stand between a modern reader and the manuscript an author delivered.

### Q: What exactly does a compositor do?

A compositor turns manuscript copy into printable type by hand. He reads a short stretch of copy, memorises it, and selects individual metal letters from a type case into a composing stick, spacing each line to an even measure. Completed pages are locked into formes for the press, and the type is then distributed back into the cases for reuse.

Compositor analysis begins from a simple observation about that workflow: the work was repetitive enough, and personal enough, that a man's habits became systematic. A compositor who preferred to spell a common word one way would do so hundreds of times in a stint, because reaching for the same sorts in the same order is faster than deliberating. A compositor whose case was laid out for speed would justify lines in characteristic ways. A compositor who misread secretary hand would misread it in characteristic places, stumbling over the same letterforms. Because the habits are systematic, they are detectable in aggregate even when no single instance proves anything. The analyst tabulates the habits page by page, watches them cluster and change, and draws boundaries where one workman's stint ends and another's begins. The play needs this because its early books were set by several distinguishable men under two very different regimes: a small quarto shop in 1599 working from manuscript, and a large syndicate shop in 1622 and 1623 working from printed copy under the commercial pressure of a collected volume. Each regime left its own kind of damage, and only the analysis can apportion the damage correctly.

The distinction that organises everything in this field is the one W. W. Greg drew between substantives and accidentals. Substantives are the words themselves and their order, the readings that carry meaning. Accidentals are the dress the words wear in a given printing: spelling, punctuation, capitalisation, line division. In Greg's rationale of copy-text, an editor takes the accidentals of the earliest authoritative edition, on the ground that they stand closest to the author's own, and exercises judgement on substantives case by case. Compositor analysis complicates that bargain in a fruitful way. It shows that the accidentals of any edition are a blend, the copy's forms filtered through the spelling preferences of the men who set them, so that even the accidentals of the chosen copy-text are partly the shop's. It also shows that some stretches of a book are substantively shop-made: a compositor who could not decipher his copy guessed, a compositor who ran short of space compressed, and an apprentice who set verse as prose rewrote the shape of a speech without changing most of its words. Knowing where those stretches are does not decide any single reading, but it grades the evidence, and grading evidence is the editor's whole trade.

The economics of the playbook trade explain why the grading is so often needed. A quarto playbook was a cheap, fast commodity: a few sheets of paper, a cover price within reach of the readership the title pages courted, and a production schedule measured in weeks rather than months, because the publisher's money was locked up in paper from the day the shop began. Speed was the trade's first commandment, and it was enforced by the piece-rate structure of the work itself, since compositors were paid by the amount of copy they set and pressmen by the tokens they pulled. Under that regime, the workman's attention was a rationed resource, spent where the copy rewarded it and withheld where the copy was routine, illegible, or late. Nothing in the system selected for fidelity to an author's intentions; the system selected for throughput, and fidelity survived as the by-product of habits formed under supervision. When compositor analysis measures a stint's error rate, it is measuring the local balance of that economy in one shop in one season, and the play's three editions, a divided small book in 1597, a trade quarto in 1599, and a prestige folio a generation later, sample three different balances. The method's findings differ across the three because the economies differed, which is why no single story about printers and poets survives contact with the evidence.

Three features of this play's transmission make the grading indispensable. First, the copy behind the second quarto is lost, so every claim about that manuscript (that it was the author's working draft, that it carried his spellings, that certain oddities are his) must be argued from the printed page, where the shop's filter is thickest. Second, the Folio text descends from print, not manuscript, so its departures from the quartos are shop events by default unless they can be shown to come from the annotated copy the Folio men used. Third, the play was reprinted repeatedly in the author's lifetime and after, each reprint resetting the accidentals wholesale while copying the substantives with varying fidelity, so the family tree of the texts is also a history of workshops. A reader who wants to know what the playwright wrote is therefore always, whether aware of it or not, asking a question about compositors.

## Inside the Printing House: How a Play Was Set in Type

A London printing house of the period was a small factory with a strict division of labour. The master printer ran the business, bought paper, and contracted with publishers. Under him, compositors set type, pressmen inked the formes and pulled the sheets, a corrector read proofs against copy and marked errors, and apprentices fetched, cleaned, and, in their later years, set type themselves under supervision that varied with the shop's workload. The establishment that produced a Shakespeare quarto was modest: Thomas Creede's house, which set the second quarto of the play in 1599 for the publisher Cuthbert Burby, was a working trade shop that printed sermons, pamphlets, and playbooks side by side, including many of the period's most read drama. The establishment that produced the First Folio was an industrial undertaking by comparison: the Jaggard shop in Barbican, taken on by Isaac Jaggard after his father William's death midway through the project, set some nine hundred pages of double-column folio for a syndicate of publishers. Both shops worked under the same physical constraints, and those constraints are what compositor analysis reads.

The first constraint was the economics of type. A shop owned a finite stock of each letter, and type locked in a forme could not be reused until the sheet was printed and the sorts distributed back into the cases. For a large book this forced a celebrated expedient: the compositors set the pages out of order, by formes, so that printing could proceed sheet by sheet while type was continuously recycled. Setting by formes required the copy to be cast off in advance, meaning a senior man estimated how much manuscript would fill each printed page and marked the copy into page-sized portions, so that a compositor could set page eight before page five existed in type without leaving gaps or overrunning. Where the casting-off was accurate, the pages meet cleanly. Where it failed, the seams show: a page ends cramped, with verse squeezed into prose and abbreviations multiplied, or a page runs loose, with spaced-out lines and a stretched speech prefix, and those seams are among the most legible evidence the analyst has. The Folio was set by formes throughout, against cast-off copy, in two columns a page. Quarto playbooks, small and cheap, were more often set seriatim, in reading order, page after page, though even a quarto might be shared between two compositors working from different ends of the copy, or between two shops when a deadline pressed.

The second constraint was the copy itself, which reached the compositor as a stack of manuscript leaves or, for reprints, as a marked-up copy of an earlier edition. Early modern handwriting, especially the secretary hand in which plays were drafted and copied, was a genuine obstacle course: minim strokes that make u, n, m, and i ambiguous, a forest of similar forms for e, h, and the long s, abbreviations everywhere, and, in an author's draft, interlineations, deletions, and marginal alternatives. The compositor was not paid to interpret; he was paid to set, and his responses to difficulty, expansion, guesswork, faithful nonsense, or quiet normalisation, are stratified through every early play text. The third constraint was correction. Proofing was real but partial: a corrector read proofs of at least some formes, and corrections were made by stopping the press, unlocking the forme, and replacing the offending sorts, which is why surviving copies of the same edition differ from one another in scattered readings, the phenomenon called press variation. Much error escaped anyway, to be found by readers, editors, and compositors' modern analysts.

The presswork itself added a final stratum of accident. Two pressmen worked each press, one inking the forme with leather balls, the other laying the dampened sheet on the tympan, folding the frisket over it, running the carriage under the platen, and pulling the bar; a good crew sustained a rate of roughly two hundred and fifty impressions an hour, and the thousand or more sheets of an edition's forme were a day's heavy labour. Sheets came off with type battered progressively by the press's force, with letters working loose and rising to be inked on their sides, with frisket bites and slurs where the sheet shifted, and the men mending the forme between pulls introduced their own small changes. None of this is compositor work, and the analyst must subtract it before charging a reading to the case, but it completes the picture of the page as a manufactured object: copy, composition, correction, machining, each stage depositing its characteristic noise, and the noise of each stage legible to the method trained to read it.

Division of labour inside the shop is the fact that makes stints exist at all. A book was rarely one man's continuous work. The master assigned gatherings to whichever compositors were free, a compositor might set a few pages, be pulled away to a jobbing pamphlet, and return days later, and an apprentice's stint often interleaves with a journeyman's because the two shared a pair of cases. The physical evidence of this sharing survives in the finished book as abrupt changes in spelling texture, in the recurrence of the same damaged pieces of type, and in the running titles and skeleton formes that moved from gathering to gathering. Charlton Hinman's great Folio study reconstructed the whole choreography for Jaggard's shop, establishing the order in which formes were machined, the points at which work stopped and restarted, and the shares of the several workmen, from precisely these traces. The method's promise is that a book remembers its making. The sections that follow test how much of that memory can be trusted, and where memory ends and the analyst's invention begins.

## How to Identify a Compositor: Spelling Habits, Spacing, and Damaged Type

The oldest and still the most productive identification tool is the spelling test. Early modern English had no fixed orthography, and for dozens of common words two or more spellings circulated freely: do and doe, go and goe, here and heere, young and yong, dear and deere, shew and show. An author writing copy used his own mixture, a scribe used his, and a compositor, setting type at speed, reached by reflex for the forms his hand knew. The analyst chooses test words whose variants are common enough to give a usable count on every page, tabulates them across a whole book, and looks for the cliffs in the data, the pages where doe abruptly gives way to do and stays changed for a run of pages before changing back. Each cliff marks a boundary between stints, and each run of consistent preference constitutes a fingerprint. Alice Walker's pioneering work on the Folio in the 1950s, and Hinman's refinement of it in the following decade, rested on exactly such pairs: Hinman's clustering of Folio pages turned on the spellings of words like do, go, and dear, where one setter of the volume preferred doe, goe, and deere while his colleagues preferred the shorter forms. The tests work because a spelling preference is invisible to the man who holds it. He betrays himself precisely where he is not attending.

Spelling never stands alone in a mature identification. Compositors differ in smaller mechanics that accumulate into a second layer of evidence: the width and frequency of spaces after punctuation, the habit of spacing before a colon or parenthesis, the treatment of speech headings and stage directions (whether italic or roman, capitalised or lower case, abbreviated or full), the use of swash capitals, and the willingness to turn a letter when the right sort ran short, printing a u for an n or reaching for the wrong font's k. They differ in justification, the fine spacing that fills a line, and in their handling of verse that will not fit, whether by running lines together, splitting them, or setting them as prose. Above all, they differ measurably in accuracy, and the errors themselves take characteristic forms: the misreading of particular minim sequences, the omission of short phrases where the eye skipped to a repeated word, the transposition of adjacent letters, the substitution of a common word for a rare one. An analyst reading a page can often feel the difference in competence before the counts are made, the way a proofreader feels the difference between a careful and a hurried keyboard. The counts exist to discipline that feeling, and the discipline matters, because feeling alone has led the field astray more than once.

### Q: Why do spelling habits betray a typesetter?

Spelling habits betray a typesetter because they are automatic. Setting type for hours at piece rates, a compositor reaches for the sorts his hand knows and spells common words the same way hundreds of times without deciding. When his stint ends and a colleague with different reflexes takes the copy, the page's texture changes, mapping the boundary between them.

The third layer of evidence is physical and, where it is available, the most objective: the type itself. Every sort in a case was an individual piece of metal with its own minute injuries, a nicked serif, a bent foot, a face worn at one corner. When a compositor set a page, the damaged types already in his case went into it; after printing, those same sorts were distributed and, in due course, set again, so a distinctive damaged letter recurs across the pages a shop produced, and its pattern of recurrence lets the analyst sequence formes and sometimes assign pages to the case, and hence to the man, who used them. Hinman exploited this systematically for the Folio, tracking individually recognisable types through the volume to establish the order of machining and to identify which pages had been set from the same case at the same period. Running titles offer a fourth witness: the headline at the top of each page was not reset for every sheet but transferred within a skeleton forme, and the skeletons' movements chart the progress of work through a gathering. Press figures, copy marks, and the changing stock of ornaments and initials add corroboration. No competent modern attribution rests on one class of evidence. The identification earns belief when spelling cliffs, mechanical habits, error profiles, and type recurrence converge on the same boundaries, and it is treated as provisional when they diverge, a distinction the field's later controversies, examined below, made impossible to forget.

Running titles and skeleton formes deserve a fuller word, because for the Folio they are the evidence that converts page clusters into a chronology. A folio page carried a headline, the running title, set not in the page's type but in a separate skeleton forme that travelled from sheet to sheet through a gathering and beyond it, acquiring damage and small rearrangements as it went. By tracking a skeleton's career, its damaged letters, the progressive battering of its rules, the analyst can establish the order in which formes were machined, which formes were on the press in the same week, and where the work stopped and restarted. Hinman's command of the skeletons let him show that the Folio's campaigns interrupted one another, that gatherings of different plays were machined in interleaved sequence, and that the compositors' stints must be read against a schedule of formes, not of pages. For the play, the skeletons corroborate the spelling evidence at exactly the contested point: the long apprentice run crosses gathering boundaries without a break in the skeleton sequence, confirming that the stint's continuity is a fact of the pressroom and not an artefact of the counting. Evidence that knows nothing of spelling, agreeing with evidence that knows nothing of presswork, is the strongest currency the discipline mints, and the play's Folio setting is rich in it.

One further principle governs the whole method and deserves statement before the case studies: compositor analysis identifies stints first and men second. What the evidence directly yields is a partition of a book into blocks of homogeneous manufacture. Turning blocks into persons requires the further assumption that each block's habits belong to one workman rather than to a case, a copy shift, or a week of unusual haste, and turning persons into judgements requires the still further step of rating each man's fidelity. The strongest work in the field keeps the three steps separate and states the confidence proper to each. The weakest work, which its critics have not spared, compresses them into a story: a named villain apprentice here, a careless journeyman there, and readings condemned or saved by the story rather than by the evidence. The play offers the supreme test case for keeping the steps apart, because in its Folio text the partition is unusually clear, the temptation to moralise it is unusually strong, and the editorial consequences of yielding to that temptation are unusually large.

A worked miniature shows the logic better than any definition. Lay the Folio's pages out in setting order and count, for each page, the test spellings: doe against do, goe against go, heere against here. For long runs the proportions hold nearly steady, the signature of one man's reflexes, then at a single page boundary they invert and hold inverted for the next run. The runs are the stints; the inversions are the boundaries; and the pattern is checkable by any sceptic with a facsimile and patience, which is the point. Later refinements added subtler tests to the same procedure: Gary Taylor's work on the volume drew on a compositor's preference for ending words in -ie rather than -y, a habit distributed unevenly enough to separate men whose do and doe counts ran too close to call. End-of-line practice supplies another test family, since a setter dividing a word across lines, or choosing between a full form and an abbreviation under space pressure, reveals preferences as steady as his spellings, and the treatment of stage directions, italic or roman, full or curtailed, clusters with the rest. What the miniature also shows is the method's built-in honesty condition: a boundary is believed when several independent test families invert at the same page, and doubted, however elegant the story it would complete, when only one family moves.

## Alice Walker, Charlton Hinman, and the Invention of Compositor Study

Compositor analysis has a prehistory in nineteenth-century proofreading and an austere debut in the New Bibliography, but its modern form begins with Alice Walker. Working on the First Folio in the early 1950s, and publishing her findings in Textual Problems of the First Folio in 1953, Walker showed that the volume's pages could be sorted into groups by the spellings their setters preferred, and that the groups shifted in ways that matched the physical structure of the book. Her insight was methodological rather than theatrical: she treated spelling not as noise to be normalised away but as a signal carrying information about manufacture, and she was candid that a compositor's preferences were tendencies, not laws, so that identification had to rest on bundles of spellings across many pages rather than on any single form. The restraint of that founding statement is worth remembering, because the field's later troubles came when its practitioners forgot it.

Charlton Hinman took Walker's beginnings and built from them the largest single structure in analytical bibliography. Hinman's instrument was his collating machine, the device he developed to compare copies of the Folio optically, blinking two copies of the same page against each other so that every press variant stood out; with it, and with the Folger Shakespeare Library's unmatched run of Folio copies, he collated the volume exhaustively and published the result in 1963 as The Printing and Proof-Reading of the First Folio of Shakespeare. The book did several things at once. It established the order in which the Folio's formes were printed and the points where work was interrupted and the copy changed hands. It distributed the pages among five compositors, labelled A, B, C, D, and E, charting each man's stints through the three sections of the volume. And it profiled the men: B the most experienced and most employed setter in the shop, on whom the syndicate's schedule most depended; E a young apprentice whose stints are short in most plays, whose spelling is erratic, and whose errors multiply as a stint lengthens, as though his supervision thinned and his attention failed together. Hinman's portrait of the shop at work, men sharing cases, formes waiting for copy, proofing done at speed and sometimes not at all, converted the Folio from a monument into a workplace, and no editor since has been able to treat its text responsibly without his map in hand.

The schedule Hinman reconstructed has a bearing on this play that the stint map alone does not show. The Folio's printing did not proceed in the volume's reading order: the comedies were set first, the histories followed, and the tragedies were delayed, reshuffled, and in places set under evident difficulty in obtaining copy, so that the campaign in the Barbican shop stretched across two years with interruptions for other work. The tragedy reached the compositors late in that campaign, in a shop whose routines had been worn smooth by hundreds of pages and whose senior men were the syndicate's scarcest resource. Its copy, a printed quarto needing annotation but no heroic casting off, was exactly the assignment a master gives to the workforce's junior member while the veterans take the manuscripts. Hinman's proof-reading evidence sharpens the picture: he showed which formes were corrected and which were not, and the play's formes sit at the neglected end of that distribution too. Schedule, assignment, and correction are three faces of one industrial fact, and compositor analysis is the discipline that recovers industrial facts from literary objects. The recovery is what lets a modern editor say, with evidence rather than temperament, that this play's Folio text was made under the volume's least favourable conditions.

The generation after Hinman refined the map without discarding it. T. H. Howard-Hill reworked several of the Folio identifications in the 1970s, adding spelling tests and questioning particular stint boundaries. Gary Taylor, editing for the Oxford Shakespeare, adjusted attributions in the histories and tragedies to the needs of his copy-text arguments, defending a revised distribution in which the identity of the setter bore directly on the authority of disputed passages. When Peter W. M. Blayney compiled the standard tabulation of the whole field's attributions in the 1990s, the five men of Hinman's account had become eight, as finer analysis split some stints and reassigned others, notably in the comedies, where a further setter, designated F, was carved out of what Hinman had given to A. The multiplication of persons is itself a datum. Either the early tests lumped distinct hands together, in which case their error rate was high exactly where the counts were thinnest, or the refinements invented men to tidy recalcitrant data. The controversy that follows from that fork is treated in its own section below. What matters here is the direction of travel: from Walker's cautious clusters to Hinman's confident workforce to a modern position in which attributions are printed as probability-weighted ranges, the field has spent seventy years learning how much its own method can bear. A study of this play should adopt the modern discipline, not the mid-century confidence: stints where the evidence converges, probabilities where it merely suggests, and silence where it runs out.

The method's export trade mattered as much as its home market. Fredson Bowers, who founded the journal Studies in Bibliography and edited the dramatic works of Thomas Dekker by analytical methods, carried compositor and press study across the whole field of early modern drama, training a generation of editors to ask of any quarto the questions Hinman had asked of the Folio: how many men set it, from what copy, in what order, with what correction. Under that dispensation the play's quartos received the same scrutiny as its Folio, at the hands of scholars of the quarto texts from the mid-century onward, and the smaller books yielded their smaller harvests: shared setting, divided shops, reprint patches, corrector's states. The export also spread the method's characteristic risk, since a technique calibrated on the Folio's mountain of evidence was now applied to books a twentieth of its size, where the same confidence could not be earned, and the field's later self-corrections, Blayney's among them, began in the quarto literature before they reached the Folio's. The play sits at the crossing of both currents, its big book the method's foundation and its little books among its proving grounds.

The play figured in the method's development in a particular way, because its Folio text turned out to be an extreme case. When Hinman charted the tragedies, the pages of Romeo and Juliet resolved into the longest single run of work by the shop's weakest man. The opening page of the play in the Folio was set by Compositor B, and the closing page also fell to B, but the three thousand lines between them were set by Compositor E. For most Folio plays the apprentice's share is a gathering here and there; for this play he was, in effect, the printer. That distribution made the tragedy a laboratory: it let Hinman watch one workman's habits deepen and decay across an unusually long stint, and it handed editors a standing explanation for the Folio version's uneven quality in this play, its mixture of respectable transmission and startling blunder. The laboratory and the explanation both need scrutiny, and both get it below. First, the method must be carried back a generation, to the quarto shop where the play's authoritative text was made.

## Two Shops, One Small Book: Danter, Allde, and the First Quarto's Divided Setting

The first quarto of 1597 gives the method a case of a different shape: a book divided not merely between workmen but between firms. The edition was begun in the printing house of John Danter and finished in the house of Edward Allde, the change falling partway through the book. The evidence for the division is the analyst's familiar kit, visible to any reader who knows where to look: the stock of type changes, the ornaments and initials change, the headline practices change, and the spelling texture of the pages changes with them, as though the book had been picked up, carried across the trade's geography, and set down in front of different cases and different hands. Why the copy changed shops in mid-course the record does not settle: Danter's career was turbulent and his relations with the Stationers' Company strained, subcontracting between houses was a routine expedient when deadlines pressed, and the book's publisher had a commercial interest in speed. Compositor analysis cannot choose among the motives. What it establishes is the fact and the place of the division, and for textual purposes the fact is the valuable part.

A divided book is a natural experiment, and the first quarto's division runs the experiment on the play's most difficult copy. The copy behind the edition, on the account its modern students have given, derived from performance, assembled from what the company's playing had left in memory and on paper; the studies of that derivation belong to sibling articles, and the compositor's share in the book is the narrower matter here. Two shops set that copy in succession, each with its own cases, its own spelling habits, its own standards of correction, and the printed book therefore carries two layers of shop filter over the same troubled exemplar. Where the texture changes at the division, variants cluster in ways that track manufacture rather than performance: the second shop's men normalised differently, misread differently, and punctuated differently from the first shop's. An editor weighing a first-quarto reading, a reading on which a staging reconstruction or a textual argument might be built, needs to know which side of the division it stands on, because the two sides have different error profiles and the same reading is worth different amounts in each. This is compositor analysis at its least glamorous and most useful: not a gallery of identified men, but a surveyed boundary inside a small book, with the evidence repriced on either side of it.

The Danter and Allde division also teaches the method's humility in the right place. The two shops' compositors have resisted the lettered identification the Folio men received, and for structural reasons: the book is short, the copy's spellings are themselves unstable (a text reconstructed from performance brings no consistent orthography to mask or reveal), and the type evidence, while it proves the change of shop, does not resolve the work within each shop into persons. The analyst reports a division, a set of contrasting textures, and a repricing of readings, and stops. A field that can stop there, with the evidence short of biography, is a field whose stronger claims elsewhere deserve their hearing.

## Thomas Creede's Shop in 1599: Setting the Second Quarto

The second quarto of 1599 is the edition on which every modern text of the play is founded, and it was a product of Thomas Creede's printing house, produced for Cuthbert Burby, who sold it at his shop in Paul's Churchyard. Creede was among the busiest playbook printers of the decade, and his house set a large share of the drama that survives from the 1590s. The title page of the quarto announced a text "newly corrected, augmented, and amended," and the book delivered on the claim: it printed the play at full length, roughly three thousand lines, in a text whose copy, on the consensus of its modern editors, was a manuscript close to the author's own working papers. The companion study of [the second quarto, the 'good quarto' of 1599](/2026/04/03/second-quarto-1599-good-quarto/) examines the edition whole; the concern here is what its pages disclose about the men who set it, and what those disclosures do to the edition's authority.

A quarto playbook was a smaller industrial problem than a folio, and Creede's shop attacked it in the trade's usual way. The book runs to eleven gatherings of quarto sheets. Copy reached the compositors as manuscript, cast off or set seriatim in reading order; the balance of the evidence for this quarto favours setting largely in sequence, with the work shared. Sharing is visible in the texture. The spelling of the book is not uniform across its length: runs of pages show measurably different preferences in the variable spellings of common words, and the changes of texture fall where a fresh stint would be expected, at gathering boundaries and partway through long gatherings where a second man took the copy from the first. Analytical work on the quarto's spellings, including linguistic tabulations of its whole vocabulary, finds a text that is regular enough to suggest disciplined workmen and divided enough to deny a single hand. The prudent conclusion, and the one this study adopts, is that at least two compositors set the book, working from the same copy in the same shop, with the division of their shares recoverable only within margins the evidence will not narrow. That reticence is not failure. It is the quarto situation exactly: small books, thin counts, habits partly swamped by the spellings of the copy itself, since a setter following manuscript spellings at close quarters betrays his own preferences far less often than a setter working from print.

One episode in the book's manufacture is legible in detail, and compositor evidence is what makes it legible. For a short stretch of the text, the end of the scene in Capulet's orchard through the opening of the Nurse's scene with Juliet (1.2.55-1.3.37), the second quarto abandons its manuscript copy and reprints, nearly verbatim, the corresponding pages of [the first quarto, the 'bad quarto' of 1597](/2026/03/27/first-quarto-1597-bad-quarto/). The Folger's editors state the fact plainly: for that stretch the good quarto is no more than a reprint of the bad one. The likeliest reconstruction is a shop event, not an authorial one. A portion of the manuscript copy was missing, mislaid, or illegible at the moment the compositor reached it, and the shop, which had the earlier edition to hand, set from the printed pages rather than stop work. Compositor analysis earns its keep in exactly such a case. The spellings and readings of the stretch belong to the transmission of the other edition; an editor who treats them as manuscript readings imports the reported text's errors into the copy-text; and the episode proves, from inside the book, that Creede's men worked with printed copy on the bench beside the manuscript, a bench arrangement whose influence need not have stopped at one patch. The same bench logic explains the quarto's most famous compositorial puzzle, the passage in which four lines about the grey-eyed morning stand twice in succession, once closing Romeo's parting speech and once opening the Friar's, with small differences between the two versions: the detailed treatment of [the Friar's duplicated lines and the crux they create](/2026/05/22/friar-duplicated-lines-crux/) belongs to its own study, but the compositor's part in the puzzle can be stated here. A compositor setting from a draft in which the author had written an alternative and cancelled neither version would print both if the cancellation was faint or ambiguous; the small differences between the versions rule out the compositor's eye skipping back, and the standing doublet is therefore evidence about the copy, its unfinished state, rather than about the man, whose fidelity in the episode is arguably the scandal and the virtue at once.

### Q: How many men set the second quarto?

The honest answer is a range, not a census. Changes in the spelling texture across the book's eleven gatherings indicate at least two compositors sharing the work, at plausible stint divisions, but the counts are thin, the copy's spellings mask the setters' habits, and scholarship has not converged on a lettered map of the quarto comparable with the Folio's.

Whole-text tabulation has since given the shop's competence a measurable form. Rita Queiroz de Barros, in a linguistic study of spelling standardisation across the second quarto and the Folio text, counted the variants for the quarto's whole vocabulary and found a degree of orthographic regularity higher than the period's reputation for chaos predicts: most words appear in a single spelling, and variation concentrates in a minority of forms. Read with a compositor's eye, that regularity is the signature of disciplined setting, workmen whose habits were steady and whose copy was followed closely enough for its own consistencies to show through. The finding cuts two ways, and both ways serve the analysis. It confirms that Creede's shop was no den of carelessness, so the quarto's real oddities cannot be waved away as general shop noise; and it confirms that the irregular residue, the places where spellings and readings do break pattern, is exactly where investigation should dig, because in a regular book the irregularities are events, not weather.

The errors of the second quarto, where they occur, are of the kinds the trade produced everywhere: single letters transposed or taken from the wrong box, short words dropped where the eye travelled, speech headings occasionally misassigned where the copy's designations were functional rather than nominal (the manuscript's "Mother" or "Wife" for Lady Capulet standing uncorrected in the printed headings, a feature editors read as a fingerprint of the draft behind the book). What compositor analysis adds to this familiar list is distribution. Errors and oddities cluster; a crux like the Nurse's daughter Susan, whose surname the quarto expands into the puzzling "Grindstone," is tested against the habits of the stretch in which it stands, and the question becomes whether the oddity is the kind that stretch's setter produced elsewhere. Such tests rarely convict or acquit alone, but they convert an editor's hunch into a weighed probability, and they warn against the opposite blunder, crediting the author with flourishes that pattern exactly like a known man's known mistakes. Creede's shop in 1599 was no boutique operation, and the quarto's manufacture should be imagined at trade speed. The house turned out a heavy list of pamphlets, sermons, and plays in those years, and a playbook of eleven gatherings was a routine job between larger commissions, its sheets machined and its type redistributed within weeks so the sorts could earn their keep elsewhere. That tempo is the context for the book's bench practices: the recourse to the first quarto when copy failed was not a scholarly decision but a production decision, taken at the speed the list demanded, and the comparative cleanliness of the result is the more creditable for it. Burby, the publisher, staked the edition's marketing on its superiority to the text of 1597, and the shop, whatever its shortcuts at one patch, delivered a book good enough to carry the claim for four centuries.

The second quarto's authority survives the analysis essentially intact, and in one respect enhanced: the shop that made it was competent, its copy was authorial, and its compositorial noise is light enough, and localised enough, to be filtered. The same cannot be said, with the same confidence, for the play's next great edition.

## Jaggard's Shop and the First Folio: Hinman's Five Workmen

The First Folio of 1623 was the largest publishing venture the English trade had attempted for a single author, and its printing was a marathon conducted in William Jaggard's Barbican shop across 1622 and 1623, interrupted by other commissions and completed under Isaac Jaggard after his father's death. The volume was set by formes from cast-off copy, in double columns, and the copy differed by play: unpublished plays arrived as manuscripts of several kinds, while plays already in print were set from quartos, sometimes annotated. For this tragedy the copy was a copy of the third quarto of 1609, itself a reprint of the second quarto, marked with annotations whose nature editors still debate, since the Folio text departs from its quarto ancestor in a scatter of readings too purposeful, and occasionally too well informed, to be charged wholly to the typesetters. The companion study of the Folio text treats the edition whole; here the question is the workforce.

Hinman's charting of the volume's setting remains the foundation. He distributed the tragedies, histories, and comedies among five compositors and reconstructed the schedule of work: the Folio was not printed straight through but in campaigns, with the tragedies delayed and rearranged when copy proved hard to obtain, and the setting of this play fell in the volume's later stages. Hinman's men have held their letters for sixty years. Compositor A, a senior workman, set much of the comedies and favoured the longer spellings, doe and goe and heere. Compositor B, the shop's workhorse and its most experienced setter, set more of the Folio than any other man and preferred the shorter forms. Compositors C and D, journeymen of middling profile, took steady shares chiefly in the comedies and histories. Compositor E, the apprentice, appears across the volume in shorter stints, his spelling unsettled, his type-setting marked by the errors of inexperience, and his employment in the shop evidently brief. The portraits are behavioural, not biographical: with one partial exception the men have never been securely matched to names in the shop's records, and E's identity, often guessed at, remains unproved. The letters are labels for clusters of habit, and everything sound in the method follows from remembering that.

### Q: What is a stint, and how is one mapped?

A stint is the continuous run of pages one compositor sets. It is mapped by tabulating his habits, preferred spellings, spacing, and error patterns, page by page until the texture changes at a boundary where a colleague took the copy. Forme structure and casting-off fix where stints can begin and end, and recurring damaged types corroborate the divisions.

The structure of the Folio's manufacture explains the strange geography of its stints, and the geography matters for the play. Because pages were set by formes, a compositor's stint is not a run of consecutive pages in reading order; he set the pages of one forme, which leapfrog across the finished gathering, and the next forme might be another man's. Because copy was cast off, the compositors worked to a page budget, and where the budget misjudged the copy the page shows the strain, in this play most memorably where verse is marshalled into prose-like blocks to save room or spreads thin to fill it. Because two compositors often shared a gathering, the analyst's boundaries can fall mid-scene, and do. When Hinman's chart for the tragedies is read against the play, the shape of its setting emerges: the play begins partway through a gathering shared with Titus Andronicus, its first page falls in a forme set by B, and then the copy passes to E for the long haul. Nothing in the text's literary character predicts that distribution; it is pure shop scheduling, the apprentice given a long, late, comparatively unhurried stretch of a reprinted play while the senior men were reserved for copy the syndicate valued harder to cast off. The accident of scheduling became, for this play, a fact of its textual condition, because the man who took the copy was the man least able to be trusted with it.

Proofing in Jaggard's shop, as Hinman reconstructed it, was genuine but uneven, and its unevenness maps onto the workforce. Corrected formes show that proofs were read and the press stopped for amendment, but the correction rate varies by section, and stretches of the apprentice's work carry error densities that suggest his pages received less, or less effective, correction than the senior men's. The Folio also preserves evidence that some correction happened against a second authority, since a handful of its departures from the third quarto restore readings found in earlier printings, the first quarto among them, which is one ground for believing the copy was annotated by someone with access to better texts, or to a playhouse manuscript, a possibility the Folio's editors have weighed ever since. Compositor analysis disciplines that weighing. A departure that patterns like an E error, transposed words, a banal substitution, a dropped phrase, is weighed as an E error however attractive it looks; a departure that agrees with independent early testimony is weighed as annotation. The Folio's text of the play is thus a two-source object, print filtered through annotation and then through an apprentice, and the analysis is what keeps the sources sorted.

## Compositor E: The Apprentice Who Set Nearly All of Romeo and Juliet

The compiled attributions of the Folio, from Hinman's chart as refined by his successors, give this play's setting a shape unlike any other in the volume. Compositor B set the play's first Folio page, through line 96 of the through-line numbering, and its last page, from through line 3108 to the end. Everything between, from through line 97 to through line 3107, across the gatherings signed ee, ff, and gg, was set by Compositor E. In practical terms the Folio Romeo and Juliet is an apprentice's book with a senior man's bookends. Hinman noticed the anomaly and made the play his chief exhibit for the apprentice's character, and the exhibit repays attention, because the play's Folio text shows, in magnified form, every virtue and every failing the method claims to detect.

The geography of the stint deserves a paragraph, because it explains how one man came to hold a whole play. The Folio's gatherings are signed in sequence, and the play begins partway through the gathering signed ee, whose earlier leaves finish Titus Andronicus: the two tragedies share a gathering because the press, not the reading order, governed the sheet. Compositor B set the play's first page in that shared gathering; then the copy passed to E, who set the rest of ee, the whole of the gathering signed ff, and the whole of gg, before B resumed for the final page in the gathering signed Gg. Because setting proceeded by formes, E did not set those pages in the order a reader meets them: he set the forme-mates as the schedule demanded, leapfrogging through each gathering, so his stint is a lattice across the play rather than a walk through it. Hinman's reconstruction of the skeleton formes, the recycled headline frames whose movements track the machining order, is what turns that lattice from conjecture into chronology. The chronology matters to judgement, because E's deterioration can be plotted against it: the pages he set late in his engagement with the play, which are not the pages a reader meets last, carry the densest errors, and an editor armed with the setting order can find the tired pages where a reader, innocent of the shop, would never think to look for them.

E's profile begins in his spellings, which are the least settled in the shop. Where the senior men hold to their preferences with machine-like consistency, E wavers, his proportions of doe and do, goe and go, shifting within a stint in ways that suggest a young setter still acquiring reflexes and partly copying the spellings of the printed copy in front of him. The wavering is diagnostic gold for the analyst, since it confirms the stint from a second direction: a block of pages whose spelling texture is not merely different but unstable belongs to the man whose habits were not yet formed. His spacing and justification tell the same story of a worker under instruction, competent at the mechanics, slower at the judgement calls. His handling of verse is the point at which inexperience becomes textual damage. Faced with copy whose line lengths strained his page budget, E resorted to the apprentice's expedients, crowding verse, misdividing lines, and letting the column's discipline override the copy's, so that passages of the Folio play stand lineated in ways every editor since has had to unpick. Article-length treatments of the play's lineation belong to a sibling study; the compositor's share in it is the inexperience itself.

The coordinate system that makes the stint describable at all is the through line numbering, the continuous count of the Folio's lines by which scholars cite the volume, and its numbers for this play tell the story in shorthand: B to line 96, E from 97 to 3107, B from 3108 to the final line 3185. An editor's note that a reading stands at through line 2400 is, in compositor terms, a note that the reading passed through the apprentice deep in his engagement with the book, and the apparatus of the modern editions silently encodes the map whenever it prefers a quarto reading in E's territory or pauses before adopting a Folio one. The numbering itself descends from Hinman's facsimile of the Folio, so the very grid by which the text is cited is a product of the investigation that exposed its manufacture.

His substantive errors follow the classic apprentice pattern, and they matter because the Folio is one of the three witnesses every editor collates. E misread his copy where misreading was easy, substituting familiar words for unfamiliar ones and flattening rare forms into common ones. He dropped short phrases, the kind of omission a tired setter makes when his eye returns to the copy at the wrong place. He transposed words and, at his worst, produced the garblings that editors mark as Folio nonsense and emend from the quartos without a qualm. The pattern worsens with distance into the stint, exactly as Hinman's character sketch predicts: the apprentice's early pages are respectable, his later pages in the play's fourth and fifth acts are the least reliable in the Folio text of the tragedy. The tomb scene, the play's most pressured passage, with its heaped properties, its comings and goings, and its long final speeches, reached print through the apprentice at his most extended, and the Folio's version of the scene bears the marks, in muddled directions and degraded readings, that its editors have catalogued ever since.

Proportion matters in judgement here. E also transmitted, at a rate his senior colleagues would not have shamed, the great bulk of a three-thousand-line play, and the Folio text of the tragedy, for all its blemishes, collates honourably against its quarto copy across long stretches. The apprentice was not a vandal; he was a learner set a task beyond his seasoning, in a shop that then failed to proof his work with the care it gave to harder copy. The editorial moral is distributive, not dramatic. The Folio's authority in this play is not uniform, and compositor analysis says where its credit runs out: trust the Folio's departures from the quartos least where they occur singly in E's later pages, and most where they agree with early testimony or correct the quarto's manifest nonsense. That gradient of trust, defensible page by page, is a thing no other method supplies, and it is the strongest single argument for the discipline's existence.

## Compositor B: The Veteran Who Set Only the Play's First and Last Pages

Compositor B was the Jaggard shop's senior setter, the man Hinman found everywhere in the volume's most demanding work, and the standard against which the apprentice's performance is measured. B's habits are the crispest in the shop: he preferred the shorter spellings, do and go and young against doe and goe and yong, held them steadily across hundreds of pages, spaced and justified with an economy that betrays long practice, and misread copy rarely, his errors tending to the mechanical (a turned letter, a foul-case sort) rather than the interpretive. When Hinman needed to show what professional Folio setting looked like, B's pages were the evidence. He set the volume's first play and much of its most prestigious copy, and his stint boundaries are the ones about which the whole field agrees most readily, because his fingerprint is the strongest signal in the book.

In this play his share is an irony of scheduling: the first page and the last. The play's opening page in the Folio, through line 96, carries the Prologue and the start of the street brawl, set by B with his usual competence before the copy passed to the apprentice; the closing page, from through line 3108, carries the end of the tomb scene and the Prince's closing couplet, "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3.309-310), set by B after the apprentice's long stint ended. Between those competent bookends the apprentice held the play. Editors have sometimes moralised the distribution, as though the syndicate cared for the play's facade and neglected its interior, but the sober reading is industrial: pages were assigned as formes fell due and men fell free, B was wanted for copy still arriving and harder to cast off, and the reprinted tragedy, whose copy needed no casting-off heroics because its length in print was already known, was the natural assignment for the shop's learner. The lesson generalises across the Folio and beyond it. A text's condition in an early edition reflects the shop's labour market at the moment of setting, not the shop's estimate of the work's worth, and any argument that infers esteem from accuracy, or neglect from error, without the compositor evidence is building on air.

Across the volume, B's habits repay the closest study the field has given any workman, because his steadiness makes him the instrument's calibration standard. His spelling proportions barely wander across hundreds of pages set months apart; his pages are the ones where Hinman's type evidence and spelling evidence agree most completely; and his characteristic lapses, when they come, are a professional's lapses, the foul-case sort pulled from the wrong box, the turned letter, the rare misreading of an unfamiliar proper name. Even B was not proof against the shop's conditions: where his copy was at its most difficult, in the late copy of the tragedies, his error rate rises measurably, a reminder that the profile an analyst draws is of a man in a workplace, not of a machine, and that stint attributions must always be read against the difficulty of what the stint contained. The syndicate's dependence on him is written in the schedule Hinman recovered: B's stints cluster at the beginnings of sections and at the points of resumption after interruptions, the places where a master restarts a stalled campaign with the hand he trusts most. That the play drew him only for its frame is therefore doubly informative. The frame was where the gathering's forme structure put the first and last pages, and the campaign's needs took the trusted hand elsewhere for everything between.

B's two pages also serve the analyst as a control. Because B set the play's frame and E its body, the play offers a same-copy comparison rare in the volume: identical copy, two setters, measurable difference in error density and spelling discipline. The comparison validates the method's premise at the level where validation is possible. The spellings change at the boundary in the predicted direction, B's shorter forms giving way to E's mixture, and the character of the errors changes with them. Control cases like this one are the field's answer, in advance, to the charge that its identifications are circular stories. Where the story can be checked against a boundary the story did not create, it survives the check, and Romeo and Juliet, precisely because its Folio manufacture was so lopsided, supplies one of the cleanest checks in the book.

## What Compositor Evidence Can Prove About the Copy Behind an Edition

The most consequential use of compositor analysis is not the naming of workmen but the reconstruction of copy. A compositor's behaviour is a seismograph of the document in front of him: where copy was clean print, his errors are visual; where copy was difficult manuscript, his errors are interpretive, and the distribution of the two kinds across a book testifies to the copy's nature in stretches no other evidence reaches. Greg's rationale of copy-text turned on judging which edition stood closest to the author's papers, and compositor study supplied the instruments for the judgement. For the second quarto, the converging signs, the draft-like speech headings left uncorrected, the standing doublet of an uncancelled alternative, the occasional expansion of a word beyond what print-to-print transmission produces, support the editorial consensus that Creede's men set from a manuscript with the author's draft character still on it. The compositor evidence does not prove the consensus, but it is the kind of evidence the consensus is made of, and its discipline keeps the claim honest: the argument runs from the men's behaviour to the copy's faults, not from a theory of the play to the page.

For the Folio the seismograph reads differently, and the difference is the whole point. The apprentice's errors in this play are preponderantly the errors of a man setting from print, visual confusions and mechanical slips against a legible exemplar, punctuated by departures that no visual confusion explains and that agree with earlier texts the quarto copy did not contain. Hinman's press study supplied the framework, and the Folger editors' judgement that the copy "must have been annotated by someone" states the inference the evidence supports: a marked third quarto, annotated from an authority beyond the print, set by workmen whose own contribution was noise rather than signal. Compositor analysis thus sorts the Folio's departures into the shop's and the annotator's, and an editor deciding whether a Folio reading deserves adoption is, in practice, deciding which pile it belongs to. The piles are statistical, and single readings can sit in either, which is why the analysis grades evidence rather than issuing verdicts, and why editors who use it well speak in its language of likelihood.

The annotator behind the Folio's copy deserves a closer look, because the figure tests the sorting discipline at its most delicate. The Folio text of the play departs from the third quarto, its exemplar, in a body of readings that pattern in two incompatible ways. Most departures are compositorial in character: visual slips, banal substitutions, the apprentice's fingerprints. A minority depart in the opposite direction, restoring sense where the quarto line had decayed, agreeing with the first quarto against the Folio's own ancestry, or supplying directions fuller than the quarto's, and those departures cannot be the apprentice's work, since his profile runs the other way and the knowledge they imply was not on his bench. Barbara A. Mowat and Paul Werstine, summarising the evidence for the Folger edition, conclude that the printer's copy was annotated by someone whose corrections exceed what typesetters supply. Who the annotator was, a playhouse hand, a syndicate editor, a reader with a better quarto, the compositor evidence cannot say; what it says is where the annotation stops and the setting begins, because the two populations of variants separate under the profile tests. The separation is the method's mature product: not a story about a lost manuscript, but a sorted apparatus in which each Folio reading carries the mark of the process that most plausibly produced it.

Casting-off supplies the third kind of copy evidence, the most mechanical and the most secure. The fit between cast-off copy and printed page is measurable: where a compositor was given too much copy for his page, the compression strategies appear (crowded lines, prose marshalled from verse, abbreviations thickened), and where he was given too little, the stretching appears (generous spacing, expanded headings, a speech allowed air). In the Folio, setting by formes against cast-off copy, the misfits reconstruct the copy's physical extent with a precision that sometimes settles scholarly questions, how much copy a scene occupied, whether material was added or removed between copy and print, whether a suspiciously short passage is short in the copy or short by the shop's compression. In the quartos, set closer to reading order, the misfits are subtler but the same logic applies. None of this depends on identifying any workman by name or letter. It depends only on pages remembering the dimensions of what filled them, and it is the part of the discipline that even its sternest critics have never seriously impugned.

The first quarto reads on the same instrument from the other direction. Its compositors in two shops set from a written exemplar, and their errors are the visual kind, misread letters, dropped words, transposed phrases, which tells against any account in which the book was taken down from dictation at the case; the performance element in its ancestry had already been committed to paper before the shops received it, and what the paper was, memorial, theatrical, or mixed, the error profile cannot distinguish, since all written copy produces the same compositor behaviour. That negative precision is itself a contribution: compositor evidence confines the first quarto's mystery to the pre-print stage, where the sibling studies of its status conduct the argument, and it clears the two shops of the book's famous deformities, which arrived with the copy and were merely, faithfully, and sometimes carelessly transmitted. An instrument that can acquit is worth as much as an instrument that can convict, and the acquittal of Danter's and Allde's men is among the analysis's quieter services to the play.

A word belongs here to the boundary this study was set: the pointing of the texts. Punctuation in the early editions is compositorial as much as authorial, set or altered at the case under the same pressures as spelling, and the analysis of pointing habits across stints is one of the tools by which stints themselves have been corroborated. The play's punctuation as a system of meaning, its rhetorical pointing and its modern replacement, is a sibling study's owned subject and is left to it; what belongs here is only the shop fact, that a comma in the second quarto or the Folio is a workman's deposit before it is an author's, and Greg's accidentals were shop-filtered on every page.

## Press Correction: The Book That Changes While It Prints

No account of the shops is complete without the corrector, the workman whose interventions make every early edition a population rather than an object. Proofreading in the period's houses worked forme by forme: a proof of a forme was pulled, read against copy by the corrector, and marked; the press was then stopped at some point in the run, the forme unlocked, the marked sorts replaced, and printing resumed. Sheets printed before the stop carry the uncorrected state of the forme, sheets printed after it carry the corrected state, and since the printed sheets were gathered indiscriminately into copies, two copies of the same edition, bound side by side, differ in scattered readings. Hinman's collating machine was built to find exactly those differences across the Folger's dozens of Folio copies, and his study remains the great demonstration of the phenomenon: the Folio's formes carry press variants in profusion, the correction rate varies by section and campaign, and stretches of the volume, including stretches of the apprentice's work in this play, show markedly less correction than the senior men's pages, whether because proofs of his formes were read cursorily or because the schedule had moved on.

The editorial consequence is subtle and compositor-shaped. A corrected state is not automatically the true reading, because the corrector worked from the same copy the compositor had, under the same time pressure, and his intervention could repair a blunder, introduce a sophistication, or regularise a deliberate oddity into a commonplace. What the states give the editor is a sequence: the uncorrected state shows what the compositor set, the corrected state shows what the shop, on reflection, preferred, and the preference of the shop is evidence about the copy only where the corrector plainly read the copy again rather than his own sense of the sentence. For this play the Folio's press variants matter most as calibration. They let the analyst watch the shop's quality control operating on the apprentice's stint in real time, page by page, and the pattern they show, correction attempted, correction partial, correction abandoned where the run was long, is the institutional counterpart of the apprentice's own profile. The quartos carry the same phenomenon at quarto scale: surviving copies of the second quarto differ from one another in a handful of readings, the residue of Creede's corrector at work, and an editor citing the edition cites, strictly, a family of states. Compositor analysis began with the habits of the men at the case; press-variant study completes it with the habits of the man at the proof, and a book's authority is the product of both.

## The Sceptics' Case: Blayney, McKenzie, Werstine, and the Limits of Identification

Compositor analysis has been doubted from inside by its best practitioners, and the doubts are part of the method's maturity. Peter W. M. Blayney delivered the most instructive blow. In his great study of the King Lear texts, published in 1982, Blayney re-examined compositor attributions he had himself relied on in earlier work, found the spelling evidence beneath them thinner and more ambiguous than the field's practice assumed, and set aside conclusions built on those attributions. The episode is famous because the correction was self-inflicted: the scholar with the most to lose from the concession made it anyway, in print, at length. Its lesson is not that identification is impossible, Blayney's own later compilation of Folio attributions runs to eight compositors and embodies decades of careful attribution, but that spelling tests measure tendencies entangled with the copy's spellings, the case's supply of sorts, and the analyst's choice of test words, so that an identification is a probability with error bars, not a signature. Any study that prints a stint boundary without its confidence has already overclaimed.

D. F. McKenzie's challenge came from the archive. In "Printers of the Mind," published in 1969, McKenzie took the surviving records of a later Cambridge printing house and showed how violently the actual division of labour could diverge from the tidy model bibliographers brought to the evidence: workmen shared tasks mid-forme, apprentices and journeymen interleaved in patterns no spelling test would resolve, and the same man might set, correct, and impose within a single sheet. The moral was not nihilism but anthropology. A printing house is a workplace before it is a text-producing machine, and the analyst who imagines its routines from the printed page alone, without the documentary record of how such shops actually ran, will find in the page exactly the orderliness the imagining put there. McKenzie's own remedy, the sociology of texts, widened the discipline's frame rather than closing it: compositors stayed at the centre of study, but as workers in a documented economy, not as letters in a puzzle.

### Q: Can a compositor study ever be proved wrong?

Yes, and the field's honour rests on the cases where it was. Identifications fall when fresh evidence, a newly tabulated test word, a damaged-type recurrence, a press record, contradicts the pattern an earlier analyst drew, and the history of Folio study is a sequence of such corrections, from revised stint boundaries to an eight-man workforce replacing a five-man one.

Paul Werstine pressed the critique at its editorial joint. In a series of studies from the 1980s onward, Werstine argued that the narratives bibliography told about copy, foul papers, promptbooks, and the compositors who supposedly betrayed them, often functioned as scenery for editorial decisions reached on other grounds: the story of the author's draft ratified the copy-text the editor already preferred, and the apprentice's villainy condemned the readings the editor already disliked. His charge, fairly heard, is about inference hygiene. Compositor evidence is real, but it is evidence about manufacture; the step from manufacture to authority, from "E set this page" to "therefore this Folio reading is corrupt," requires a premise about how E treated the kind of copy he had, and that premise is itself built from the readings it is then used to judge. Werstine's scepticism does not abolish the step; it forbids taking the step unconsciously. The defence of the method, properly stated, is that revision is its normal state. T. H. Howard-Hill's reworkings of the Folio attributions in the 1970s did not rescue Hinman by loyalty; they redrew boundaries where fresh spelling tests and type evidence demanded it, and the eight-man workforce of the modern tabulation is Hinman's structure as corrected, not Hinman's structure abandoned, which is how a measurement tradition is supposed to behave. Computational work has since supplied an outside check the founders could not have imagined: an unsupervised attribution model published in 2017, trained on the same classes of evidence the bibliographers used, orthographic variation and the visual features of the page, sorted Folio pages into clusters agreeing with the manual attributions at a rate of eighty-seven percent. A method whose clusters can be recovered by a machine that knows none of its stories is not merely telling stories. The agreement also calibrates the residue honestly: roughly one page in eight resists the classification the scholars themselves reached, a standing measure of the uncertainty the sceptics insisted on.

The adjudication this study reaches among the three critics and Hinman is therefore a division of the estate. On manufacture, Hinman wins: the Folio's setting is recoverable in the large, its schedule, shares, and workmen's profiles have survived sixty years of hostile scrutiny with revisions at the margins rather than collapse. On inference, the sceptics win: an attribution is a probability, a page's authority is a gradient, and no single reading was ever condemned or saved by a compositor's letter alone. Editors who work inside that division use the method at its full legitimate strength; editors who exceed it write fiction with footnotes.

## Compositor Analysis at the Editor's Desk: Gibbons, Evans, and Levenson

The proof of a bibliographical method is editorial practice, and the play's three standard modern editions show the method at work in proportion. Brian Gibbons, editing for the Arden Shakespeare in 1980, founded his text on the second quarto and treated the Folio as a derivative witness whose readings earn adoption only on their merits, case by case. The compositor evidence underwrites that architecture: since the Folio descends from the third quarto, since its play was set almost wholly by the shop's least reliable man, and since its departures pattern as shop noise except where annotation intervenes, Gibbons's presumption against Folio singular readings is the method's presumption, stated in an introduction and applied in a collation. Where he adopts a Folio reading, it is typically where the quarto errs manifestly and the Folio's correction is of the kind either the annotator or an attentive setter could supply. G. Blakemore Evans, editing the play for the New Cambridge Shakespeare, worked to the same architecture with the same instruments, his textual notes repeatedly testing disputed readings against the setting history of the page that carries them, asking whether the oddity in question behaves like its setter's other work. Jill L. Levenson, editing for the Oxford Shakespeare in 2000, gave the printing-house dimension its fullest modern statement for this play, her account of the texts treating the quartos' and Folio's manufacture as part of the play's meaning for its first readers, and her edition's decisions showing the same gradient of trust, steepest against the apprentice's stretches, that the analysis prescribes. The convergence of three independent editorial traditions on one practice is the strongest pragmatic evidence the method has.

The working tests an editor actually runs are worth stating plainly, because they are compositor analysis in daily dress. A disputed word is first asked what it is: if it matches the known error profile of the man who set the page (the apprentice's banal substitutions, a Creede setter's minim misreadings), it is demoted; if it matches no shop profile and fits the author's habits elsewhere, it stands. A spelling in the copy-text is asked whose it is before it is modernised or glossed: the study of [the spelling of the lovers' names in early print](/2026/07/31/spelling-lovers-names-early-print/) pursues one family of such cases, where the forms of the names themselves, Montague and Montagew, Capulet and Capilet, carry evidence about copy that only the shop context can weigh. A stage direction is asked which witness's habits it matches, since the Folio's directions for this play passed through the apprentice while the first quarto's came from a shop reporting performance. And a whole passage is asked the casting-off question: does its printed shape reflect its copy, or the room its setter had? Seminar presentation of such page-by-page evidence, stint charts against facsimile pages, is a recognised way of teaching it; a slide deck assembled for the purpose can be carried through the [ReportMedic PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) while the facsimiles themselves stay open beside it. The method ends, in short, not in a monograph but at the desk, in the margin of a proof, where it has decided real readings in every serious edition of the play for half a century.

### Q: Why does it matter which compositor set a page?

A page's authority is not uniform: an edition contains careful work and careless work, and a disputed reading inherits the credit of the hands that set it. A reading from a disciplined stint starts trusted; one from the apprentice's tired pages starts suspect. Editing without the distinction treats the book as one witness when the shop made it several.

One more layer of the desk practice deserves record, because it shows how fine the grain of the evidence can go. Compositors betray themselves in their word division, in the choices they make when a word must break across a line end: which syllable boundary a man favoured, whether he reached for an abbreviation or divided, whether he tolerated a turned letter rather than borrow sorts from the neighbouring case. Editors collating the play's quartos have used such minutiae to test whether a suspect passage was set by the same hands as its surroundings, and the tests have their cleanest application in the second quarto's reprinted patch, where the question is precisely whether the bench behaviour changes when the copy changes. The patch answers yes, and the yes is the method in miniature: copy changed, behaviour changed, and the edition's authority repriced in one bounded place, with no claim made about any other page. That discipline of bounded claims, repeated across thousands of apparatus entries, is what separates compositor analysis as editors practise it from compositor analysis as its caricaturists describe it.

## The Verdict: What the Printing House Finally Explains

The argued verdict of this study can be stated in three propositions, in descending order of certainty. First, and beyond serious dispute: the early texts of the play are manufactured objects whose pages preserve the labour of identifiable workmen, and the map of that labour is recoverable in its outlines for the Folio and in its tendencies for the quartos. Second, and strong: the map changes the weight of the evidence at identifiable places. The second quarto's authority stands, its shop noise light and local; the Folio text of this play, set almost entire by an apprentice from an annotated reprint, inherits a presumption of corruption in its singular readings that no account of the play's texts may responsibly omit; the first quarto's eccentricities belong to other shops and other copy and must be weighed in their own terms. Third, and probabilistic: in the grading of individual readings, compositor profiles supply likelihoods that an editor ignores at the price of naivety and obeys slavishly at the price of circularity. The discipline's founder generation sometimes claimed the third proposition at the strength of the first; its critics sometimes denied the first at the strength of their case against the third. The evidence supports the graded middle, and the graded middle is enough to make compositor analysis indispensable: it is the difference between reading a page and reading a page knowing whose hands, and what pressures, stand between the reader and the author.

The series thesis finds its sharpest case here. The cliché of textual study imagines a pure authorial text corrupted by transmission, a poem in a foul world. The printing house dissolves the cliché. There was never a moment when this play existed as print without workmen inside it: Creede's setters filtering a draft's spellings and reprinting a patch from the rival edition when the copy failed; Jaggard's apprentice turning a shop's schedule into a text's condition. To restore the compositors to the story is not to demote the author but to locate him, a working playwright whose papers entered a trade that did its skilled, hurried, fallible best with them. Editors, students, and readers inherit the trade's work along with the author's, and compositor analysis is simply the honesty of saying so with evidence.

One boundary of the verdict should be drawn in words, since the article has enforced it throughout. Nothing argued here touches the play's standing, its poetry, or its author's genius; the printing house explains the condition of the texts, not the quality of the work the texts transmit, and the apprentice's blurred pages still carry the balcony scene entire. What the analysis changes is the posture of everyone who handles the evidence: the editor, from judge of readings to auditor of witnesses; the student, from consumer of a settled text to examiner of a manufactured one; the reader, from guest in the author's study to visitor in his printer's workshop, where the work was handled by many hands and bears all their marks. That posture, once taken, cannot be untaken, and it is the discipline's permanent contribution to the play's long afterlife in print.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch compositor explainer, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch compositor explainer is the table in this article that sets each trace a compositor leaves (spelling preferences, spacing, damaged types, error profiles, casting-off fit, press variants) beside the method scholars use to read it and its bearing on this play. The pattern it exposes is that every kind of evidence grades readings rather than deciding them: the traces converge to map the workforce reliably, and diverge often enough at single readings to forbid certainty. The play's three early editions all fit the pattern, their pages remembering their shops in proportion to the evidence each shop left.

### Q: What is compositor analysis in plain terms?

Compositor analysis is the study of who set the type for an early printed book and how their habits shaped its text. Because typesetters spelled common words by reflex, justified lines in personal ways, and made characteristic errors, their work clusters into recognisable stints that scholars can map across a book's pages. For this play the method maps the second quarto's shared setting in Thomas Creede's shop and, most famously, shows that the First Folio's text was set almost entirely by one apprentice. The point of the map is judgement: it tells editors which stretches of a page deserve trust and which deserve suspicion.

### Q: Who set the type for the First Folio text of this play?

Compositor B set the first and last pages, and Compositor E set everything between. In the compiled attributions descended from Charlton Hinman's study of the Folio's printing, the veteran B set the play's opening Folio page, through line 96 of the through-line numbering, and its closing page, from through line 3108. The apprentice E set the remaining three thousand lines and more, across the gatherings signed ee, ff, and gg. No other Folio play was left so completely to the shop's least experienced workman, which is why this play's Folio text carries its peculiar mixture of sound transmission and sudden blunder.

### Q: What made scholars call the Folio's busiest setter of this play an apprentice?

His habits form the profile of a learner. Compositor E's spellings are unsettled where the senior men's are fixed, his justification crowds and slackens unevenly, his errors are the classic novice kinds, familiar words substituted for rare ones, short phrases dropped, verse lines mishandled under pressure, and his accuracy decays as a stint lengthens, as though supervision thinned with distance. Hinman matched that profile to the shop's structure and concluded that E was an apprentice whose employment in Jaggard's house was brief. The identification is behavioural: no surviving record names the man, and guesses at his identity remain unproved.

### Q: What are cast-off copy and setting by formes?

Casting off is the advance measurement of copy into page-sized portions, so a book can be set out of reading order. Setting by formes means compositors set the pages that will be printed together on one side of a sheet, which in a folio gathering leapfrog across the finished pages. The practice let a shop recycle its limited type sheet by sheet instead of locking up the whole book. Its evidence survives where the measurement failed: pages crammed tight or stretched loose against their budgets. The First Folio was set this way throughout; the play's quarto, a smaller undertaking, was set largely in reading order.

### Q: How do damaged pieces of type help identify a compositor's pages?

Each piece of type was an individual object with its own injuries, a nicked serif, a worn corner, a bent foot. A compositor's case held a particular stock, so his pages carry his damaged sorts; after printing, the sorts were distributed and reset, and a recognisable damaged letter recurs in later pages from the same case. Tracking those recurrences lets scholars sequence a shop's formes and assign pages to the case, and thus usually to the man, who set them. Hinman used the method systematically on the Folio, corroborating his spelling-based stints with type evidence that no compositor could consciously control.

### Q: What spelling pairs did the Folio studies use as tests?

The classic test pairs are common words with two living spellings: do against doe, go against goe, here against heere, dear against deere, young against yong. Alice Walker's pioneering tabulations and Charlton Hinman's Folio charts turned on proportions of such forms across many pages, because a compositor's reflexes show in aggregate even though any single instance might copy the spelling in front of him. One of Hinman's setters favoured doe, goe, and deere; another preferred the shorter do, go, and dear. The cliffs in the counts, where a preference holds for a run of pages and then changes, mark the boundaries between stints.

### Q: Why are there two different counts of Folio compositors, five and eight?

Later analysts split what the first analysis joined. Hinman's 1963 study distributed the Folio among five compositors, A to E. Refinements by T. H. Howard-Hill, Gary Taylor, and others through the following decades subdivided several stints, and Peter W. M. Blayney's compiled tabulation of the whole field recognises eight workmen, carving, for instance, a further setter F out of pages Hinman had assigned to A in the comedies. The disagreement is about resolution, not about the existence of the workforce: finer tests detect more hands, at the price of thinner counts behind each attribution. Modern practice prints attributions with their confidence attached rather than pretending the higher resolution is certain.

### Q: What did Peter Blayney take back about his own compositor work?

He withdrew conclusions he had built on spelling-based attributions. In his study of the King Lear texts, published in 1982, Blayney re-examined the compositor identifications underlying earlier work, including his own arguments, and found the evidence thinner and more ambiguous than the field's practice had assumed, since a compositor's spellings are entangled with the copy's spellings and the supply of type. He set aside the conclusions those attributions had carried. The episode became the method's standard of honour: its most accomplished practitioner corrected himself in print, and his later compiled tabulation of the Folio's workforce embodies attribution practised with stated probabilities rather than signatures.

### Q: What did D. F. McKenzie find in the printing-house records?

He found a workplace that broke the tidy model. In "Printers of the Mind," published in 1969, D. F. McKenzie worked from the surviving records of a later Cambridge printing house and showed compositors sharing tasks within single formes, apprentices and journeymen interleaving, and the same men setting, correcting, and imposing in patterns no spelling test could disentangle. The finding did not abolish compositor study; it socialised it. McKenzie argued that a book's manufacture must be reconstructed as labour in a documented economy, with the archive correcting the imagination, rather than read off the printed page by an analyst who assumes the shop ran to a bibliographer's plan.

### Q: How does a compositor's stint help an editor choose between two readings?

It weights the evidence. When two early texts disagree, an editor asks which reading behaves like its setter's known work. A Folio reading in the apprentice E's late pages that matches his profile of banal substitution and dropped words is demoted; a reading that matches no shop profile and agrees with the author's habits elsewhere stands. Editors of this play, Brian Gibbons for the Arden Shakespeare, G. Blakemore Evans for Cambridge, and Jill L. Levenson for Oxford, all work with that gradient of trust, steepest against the stretches the shop history condemns. The stint never decides alone; it loads the scales before judgement speaks.

### Q: How are the compositors of the quartos harder to identify than the Folio's?

Three disadvantages compound. The quartos are short, so the spelling counts behind any attribution are thin. Their copy was manuscript, and a setter following manuscript spellings at close quarters betrays his own preferences far less often than a setter working from print, so the signal is masked by the copy. And the shops left no archive comparable with the Folio project's scale for the tests to be corroborated against. The result is that the second quarto's workforce is known as a texture, at least two men sharing the book, with boundaries stated as ranges, while the Folio's men hold letters that sixty years of scrutiny have largely confirmed.

### Q: Why does the second quarto reprint the first quarto for one short stretch?

The likeliest cause is a failure of copy in the shop. For the passage from the end of the orchard scene into the Nurse's scene with Juliet (1.2.55-1.3.37), the second quarto follows the first quarto nearly verbatim instead of its usual manuscript source, and the Folger's editors describe the stretch as no more than a reprint. The standing reconstruction is that the manuscript leaves for those lines were missing, damaged, or illegible when the compositor reached them, and Creede's shop, keeping the earlier edition on the bench, set from its printed pages rather than stop the press. Editors therefore weigh that stretch as reported-text evidence, not as manuscript testimony.

### Q: What kinds of error mark the apprentice's pages in this play?

The errors are the classic novice set, and they thicken toward the end of his long stint. Compositor E substitutes familiar words for unfamiliar ones, flattens rare forms into common ones, drops short phrases where his eye returned to the copy at the wrong place, transposes adjacent words, and mishandles verse lines when his page budget pressed, crowding or misdividing them. In the tomb scene, the most pressured passage he set, the Folio's stage directions and speeches carry this damage at its densest, and editors have emended its Folio readings from the quartos ever since. The errors matter because the Folio is collated for every edition; knowing their profile is how an editor discounts them.

### Q: Did the same workmen set the quartos and the Folio?

No: the books came from different shops a generation apart. The first quarto of 1597 was begun in John Danter's house and finished in Edward Allde's. The second quarto of 1599 was set in Thomas Creede's house for Cuthbert Burby. The First Folio of 1623 was set in the Jaggard shop by the men Hinman lettered A to E, working from a printed third quarto rather than from manuscript. A compositor of 1599 might in principle still have been at a case in 1623, but no evidence places any of the quarto workmen in Jaggard's shop, and the Folio's compositors are identified by habits internal to that volume alone.

### Q: How can compositor habits show whether copy was a manuscript or a printed book?

The errors change character with the copy. A compositor setting from print makes visual mistakes: confused letters, skipped lines where the same word recurs, transposed sorts. A compositor setting from manuscript makes interpretive mistakes: minim misreadings in secretary hand, wrong expansions of abbreviations, guesses at damaged words, and he preserves draft features a printed exemplar would not carry. The second quarto's texture, draft-like speech headings and an uncancelled alternative passage included, points to manuscript copy; the apprentice's Folio errors are preponderantly visual, pointing to the printed quarto before him. Distribution of error kinds, not any single blunder, is the testimony.

### Q: What can compositor analysis never prove?

It cannot prove the reading of a single word. The method yields probabilities about manufacture: that this run of pages came from one hand, that this hand erred in these ways, that this stretch was copied from print. Between those probabilities and the authority of one disputed word stands an inference the evidence cannot close, because authors write odd things too, and a compositor's worst day still transmitted most of his copy faithfully. Paul Werstine's criticism fixed the limit precisely: compositor stories have decorated editorial decisions reached on other grounds. Used inside its limit, the analysis grades evidence honestly; used outside it, the analysis writes fiction with footnotes.

### Q: Why does the Folio text of this play carry less authority than its quarto?

Its line of descent is longer and its workman weaker. The Folio text was set from a copy of the third quarto, itself a reprint of the second, so it stands two printings from the manuscript the second quarto used. Its departures from that ancestry are therefore shop events unless they can be credited to the annotator who marked the copy, and the setting of nearly the whole play fell to Compositor E, the apprentice whose errors are the densest in the Folio. Editors accordingly found their texts on the second quarto and admit Folio readings singly, on merit, most readily where the quarto errs and the Folio's correction fits the annotator's profile rather than the apprentice's.

### Q: How are compositors named when their real names are lost?

They are lettered in order of identification. Hinman designated the Folio's workmen A, B, C, D, and E as his analysis separated them; later scholars extended the sequence, adding F and others as finer tests split the early stints. The letters are labels for clusters of habit, not disguised biographies: with the partial exception of senior men whose careers touch the shop's records, the Folio compositors have never been securely matched to named individuals, and attempts to identify the apprentice E with a documented Jaggard apprentice remain conjecture. The discipline keeps the letters precisely to mark the difference between a profile the evidence supports and a person it cannot produce.
