In the middle decades of the twentieth century, a group of scholars in India set out to rewrite the history of their own country from the ground up, and the name they argued over longest was Indian feudalism, the question whether the land grant economy of the early medieval centuries amounted to a feudal order of an Indian kind. They were Marxists, and they worked with the instruments of historical materialism, yet the conclusions they reached unsettled two orthodoxies at once. Against the colonial account of India as a civilisation that had stood still for millennia, they demonstrated movement: changing property relations, expanding and contracting trade, states forming and dissolving, peasants resisting and adapting. Against a simple transfer of European periodisation, they insisted that every category they borrowed had to earn its place against Indian evidence. And against Karl Marx himself, whose picture of India had been assembled from British administrative writing, their findings stood as a refutation written by his own method.

![Land grant inscriptions, punch marked coins, revenue registers and a plotted map of early medieval settlements on a historian's desk, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

That triple achievement is the subject of this article. The Indian Marxist historians, working from the 1950s onward, applied historical materialism to Indian history and produced accounts of the transition from tribe to caste, of an Indian form of feudalism, and of Mughal agrarian relations that remain reference points for every later scholar, including the scholars who set out to demolish them. Their founding figure, Damodar Dharmananda Kosambi, was not a historian by training at all. He was a mathematician who also worked in numismatics and Sanskrit philology, who walked the hills around his home city collecting stone tools and interviewing villagers about their gods, and who defined his discipline as the study of how a society's productive means and relations develop, in sequence, across its whole past. His most consequential heirs disagreed with him about nearly everything except the premise that the Indian past was to be explained by evidence rather than venerated as heritage or dismissed as stagnation.

## A school the standard syllabus left out

The first fact to register is the school's sheer weight inside India. For two generations, the questions these historians posed set the agenda of the Indian History Congress, the large annual gathering where the country's historians present and contest their work. Their books anchored university syllabuses from Patna to Delhi to Aligarh. Their periodisations, their vocabulary of modes of production and social formations, their habit of asking who held the land and who took the surplus, became the default grammar in which Indian history was argued, so that even scholars who rejected Marxism outright found themselves answering questions the Marxists had framed. A historiography does not need to be believed to be unavoidable, and this one was unavoidable.

The second fact is how little of this travelled. Outside India, the school remained a rumour. International surveys of Marxist historiography, which gave generous space to British and French practitioners, rarely found a page for the Indians, although the Indian literature was larger, more archival, and arguably more original than much of what was celebrated elsewhere. Part of the explanation is geography of publishing: the school's books appeared with Indian presses, circulated through Indian universities, and were reviewed in Indian journals. Part is the provincialism of the metropolitan academy, which treated Indian scholarship as a local commentary on Indian material rather than as a contribution to method. And part is the school's own orientation: its members wrote for the argument at home, against the colonial history they had been taught and against the nationalist hagiography that threatened to replace it, and they did not package their work for export.

Its journals carried the same weight. The Indian Historical Review, founded in 1974 as the organ of the national council for historical research, published the school's monographic articles and its long review essays, in which new work was weighed with a severity that made the reviews a literature of their own. Social Scientist, founded in 1972, gave the wider left academy, historians among them, a monthly forum in which the school's questions were argued at shorter length and with an eye on the present, and its pages record the tradition thinking aloud between its books. A school is partly the sum of the venues that will print it, and the Indian school was fortunate in possessing venues of both kinds, the archival quarterly and the argumentative monthly, in which the slow work and the fast could proceed together. The debates this article recounts were conducted in those pages, issue by issue and rejoinder by rejoinder, and a reader who wants the sound of the school at work, rather than its settled conclusions, should spend an afternoon in the bound volumes, where the footnotes quarrel in public and the corrections arrive, as corrections should, in the next number but one.

That double character, canonical at home and nearly invisible abroad, creates the gap this article exists to close. The coverage available to a general reader tends to split into reverence and polemic. One literature treats the school as a founding pantheon and summarises conclusions without reconstructing arguments. Another treats it as an ideological occupation of the universities and summarises motives without engaging evidence. Both are useless to a reader who wants to know what the historians actually claimed, how they argued, where they were wrong, and why the argument mattered. The reconstruction offered here follows the evidence in the order the school itself produced it: a founding method, a central thesis about the early medieval centuries, a standard account of the Mughal agrarian order, and then the reckoning, the criticisms from inside the discipline, the breakaway of a younger school, and the strange culminating fact that the tradition's most durable international contribution was to correct the empirical foundations of Marx's own writings on India.

A word about what the school was not. It was not homogeneous, and treating it as a single voice is the first error to unlearn. Kosambi feuded with orthodoxies of every kind, including the orthodoxy of the communist parties, whose stage theories he regarded as a catechism rather than an analysis. Ram Sharan Sharma, who built the feudalism thesis into a system, worked as an academic institution builder and stayed closer to the organised left. Irfan Habib, at Aligarh, subjected his own tradition's inherited pieties to the same archival scepticism he applied to Mughal chroniclers. Around these three stood a wider company: epigraphists who doubted the feudalism thesis from the start, south Indian specialists who thought it a north Indian story dressed as a national one, and younger scholars who absorbed the method while discarding the conclusions. The school is best pictured as an argument sustained over fifty years by people who shared a premise, that social and economic relations explain the movement of history, and contested almost every application of it.

### How did a shared premise hold together scholars who agreed on little else?

The premise was methodological rather than doctrinal. Each held that the organisation of production, the ownership of land, and the extraction of surplus supplied the questions a historian should ask first, while the answers remained an empirical matter, to be settled from inscriptions, coins, texts, and field observation. Shared questions, open answers allowed fierce disagreement inside a recognisable school.

The school's institutional ecology deserves a paragraph, because ideas travel through institutions or not at all. Its centres were the great public universities founded or expanded after independence, and its characteristic figure was the professor who taught undergraduates in the morning, supervised research in the afternoon, and wrote for the History Congress in the vacation. Its publishing infrastructure was the university press, the scholarly journal, and the conference volume. Its adversaries were correspondingly institutional: the older dynastic narrative lodged in colonial textbooks, and the devotional nationalism that preferred the past as a gallery of heroes. Against both, the school offered an unusually austere pleasure: the pleasure of watching a coin hoard, a copper plate grant, or a revenue register overturn a story that had been repeated for a century because it flattered somebody.

## The founder: a mathematician among the archives

Damodar Dharmananda Kosambi was born in 1907 in Goa, then under Portuguese rule, into a family that had already produced one formidable scholar. His father, Dharmananda Kosambi, was a pioneering student of Pali and Buddhist literature who taught in India and in the United States, and the son's childhood was divided between the two worlds. The younger Kosambi was educated largely in America, in Massachusetts, where his father held academic posts, and he took his degree at Harvard in mathematics, with a distinction that marked him as a researcher of the first rank. He might have had an international career in pure mathematics. He chose instead to return to India, and the choice, made by a man in his early twenties with his training still fresh, determined the character of everything that followed: the Indian past would be studied by someone whose first discipline was the most demanding of the formal sciences, and who never lost the mathematician's impatience with unexamined categories.

His Indian career was spent mainly in two institutions. At Fergusson College in Pune he taught mathematics through the nineteen thirties and into the forties, building around his teaching a second life of historical research: walking the Deccan hills, surveying the routes of old trade paths, collecting microliths, copying inscriptions, and interrogating the cults of local goddesses whose shrines dotted the countryside he crossed. In 1946 he joined the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research in Bombay, where his colleagues were physicists and where he pursued statistical work of international standing, including contributions to the statistical analysis of coin weights and to the mathematics of function spaces, alongside the history that was consuming more and more of his energy. He died in 1966, leaving books that had already changed his country's historiography and a reputation for astringency that his admirers and detractors describe in identical terms.

### Why did a mathematician become the school's founder?

Because Indian history, as he found it, was accumulated description without a theory of its own movement, and his training taught him to distrust material that could not say what would count as an explanation. He brought the habits of a formal scientist: define the terms, state the mechanism, and let the evidence arbitrate between accounts.

The books came in a concentrated sequence. An Introduction to the Study of Indian History, published in 1956, announced the method and sketched the whole arc of the Indian past in its light. Exasperating Essays, a year later, applied the method to episodes and texts with deliberate provocation. Myth and Reality, in 1962, pursued the formation of Indian culture through cults, festivals, and survivals. The Culture and Civilisation of Ancient India in Historical Outline, in 1965, compressed the argument for students. Each book quarried the same seam: the Indian past as a succession of social formations, each arising from changes in how people produced their living, and each leaving deposits in religion, custom, and literature that a sufficiently careful reader could excavate.

Kosambi's relation to the organised Marxist movement was that of a near relation who refused to move into the family house. He was close to the left, moved in its circles, and took its central premise as his own, yet he quarrelled publicly with the habit of applying ready made stages to India by decree. The famous periods of the textbooks, he complained in effect, were being discovered in Indian evidence because they had been placed there in advance. His alternative was austere: let the sequence of formations be whatever the Indian material shows it to be, and if the material shows a development with no European parallel, then theory must widen to admit an Indian path rather than the path being trimmed to fit the theory. That insistence, that the evidence of one subcontinent could amend the general scheme, is the seed of everything this article later calls the correction from within.

His temperament shaped the school as surely as his method did. He was a polemicist of unusual gifts, contemptuous of cant, and his footnotes could be lethal. Younger scholars who met the printed Kosambi before the printed evidence sometimes imitated the contempt without the erudition, a mannerism the school's critics were quick to tax it with. Yet the deeper legacy was disciplinary: he made it impossible, for anyone who had read him, to write Indian history as a procession of kings and invasions while the questions of land, labour, and surplus went unasked. Even the historians who spent careers refuting his students began their refutations by conceding the questions.

## Kosambi's method: reading the past from the surviving present

Every school has a methodological signature, and this one's was formed in the founder's practice before it was stated in his books. Kosambi defined history as the presentation, in chronological order, of "successive developments in the means and relations of production", and the definition sounds conventional until one watches him work. For most practitioners of his generation, the sources of Indian history were texts in Sanskrit, Persian, and the regional languages, supplemented by dynastic chronicles and the reports of foreign travellers. Kosambi treated that archive as one stratum among several, and not necessarily the most honest one. Texts were composed by the literate few, usually in the service of courts and priesthoods, and they recorded what their patrons needed remembered. The ground itself, the coin in the hoard, the potsherd in the field, and the custom in the village kept a different and less supervised record.

His innovation was to combine these registers systematically. Philology gave him the texts, read critically and in sequence, alert to the layers within a work like the Mahabharata that had accumulated over centuries. Numismatics gave him something no chronicle could: a measurable series. By weighing punch marked coins in large numbers and analysing the statistical distribution of their weights and marks, he extracted evidence about the state of exchange and the authority that issued them, turning a cabinet curiosity into an instrument of economic history. Archaeology gave him the prehistoric and protohistoric groundwork, the microlithic sites and megalithic burials of the Deccan. And fieldwork gave him the fourth register, the one that marked him off from every contemporary: the living present, interrogated as a document.

The fieldwork followed a simple conviction. In a country where change had moved at very different speeds in different regions, communities on the margins of the market economy preserved, in their tools, their marriage rules, their clan organisation, and above all their religion, arrangements that belonged to much earlier stages of social development. A goddess worshipped with rites no priest would sanction, a festival whose calendar ignored the temple year, a clan that still traced descent through women: these were not curiosities for the anthropologist's notebook but survivals, evidence of the past that had not been written down because the people who lived it could not write. Kosambi tramped the hills on weekends with students and colleagues, mapping cult spots and stone tools, and came home to argue that the historian who ignored the surviving present was discarding half the archive.

### What did Kosambi mean by using the present to read the past?

He meant that practices persisting among communities at the edge of the market economy could be read as evidence for earlier social forms, much as a geologist reads strata. A clan cult, a mother goddess shrine, or a survival of group marriage preserved arrangements that literate records never described, and fieldwork could recover them where documents were silent.

The method carried an obvious risk, and he knew it. A survival is not a fossil sealed in rock; living communities have histories of their own, and a custom may be a recent invention wearing archaic dress. His defence was comparative discipline: a practice counted as evidence for an earlier stage only when it converged with independent testimony from archaeology, from the distribution of related cults, or from textual strata that could be dated by other means. Where the registers disagreed, the disagreement itself became the problem to work on. The method, in other words, was not a licence to project the present backward but a cross examination in which unlike witnesses were made to confront one another.

### How did coin evidence enter an argument about society?

Punch marked coins, weighed in bulk and classified by their symbols, yielded patterns no narrative source supplied: regular weights pointing to issuing authority, wear and distribution pointing to circulation, debasement or disappearance pointing to strain in exchange. The coin series was a statistical instrument, and his training made Kosambi the first Indian historian to use one systematically.

The method also implied a stance toward religion that distinguished the school from cruder versions of materialism. Kosambi refused to treat cults and myths as mere deception practised by priests on the credulous. A myth, in his handling, was a historical document of a special kind: a record, preserved in narrative amber, of the social arrangements among which it formed. The great epics recorded the collision and fusion of tribal peoples; the mother goddesses recorded the agricultural communities that worshipped fertility before the plough societies subordinated them; the rise of devotional religion recorded the feudalising centuries in which personal lordship, divine as well as human, became the form of dependence. Read so, the religious archive became one of the richest sources for the very history its custodians thought they were keeping out of the record.

## Tribe into caste: the founder's central problem

The method existed to answer a question, and the question was the one Kosambi judged central to Indian history: how did a society of tribes become a society of castes? The problem had been obscured by two inherited answers. The colonial answer treated caste as an eternal essence, the timeless peculiarity of a civilisation outside history. The traditional answer treated it as a divine ordinance described in sacred texts. Kosambi's answer was historical and material. Tribal peoples, organised by kinship and holding land in common, were progressively absorbed into an expanding agrarian order built on the plough, on surplus extraction, and on the village. Absorption did not erase them; it placed them. A tribe that entered the agrarian order as a whole could persist inside it as a caste, keeping its name, its god, its crafts or its stigma, ranked according to the function it performed and the ritual status the ranking priests assigned it.

This single mechanism explained, in his hands, a remarkable range of phenomena. It explained why caste was so stubbornly local, since each region had absorbed its own tribes in its own sequence. It explained the proliferation of castes, since every absorbed group carried its distinctness into the order that subordinated it. It explained why ritual rank and economic function so often failed to coincide, since the ranking had frozen the circumstances of an absorption completed centuries before. And it explained the peculiar duplex character of Indian culture, in which a Sanskritising high tradition and a dense undergrowth of local cults coexisted in every village, because the undergrowth was the absorbed population's own religion, carried into the caste order rather than extinguished by it.

The analysis of the Bhagavad Gita showed the method working on the most guarded text of all. Kosambi read the Gita as a document of its feudalising age, a work that reconciled the warrior's duty with a doctrine of devotion to a personal lord, and he treated its layered composition as evidence of the social pressures that had revised it. Whatever one thinks of the reading, its bearing on the school's self understanding was large: the sacred canon itself was admitted as historical evidence, and no text was exempt from the question of whose order it served. His critics, including scholars of religion who found the readings reductive, nonetheless conceded that after Kosambi the Gita had to be argued about historically, which is to say argued about in the open, rather than merely expounded.

### Why did the tribe to caste transition matter more than dynasties in this account?

Dynasties changed the personnel of extraction without changing its structure, while the absorption of tribes into caste society changed the structure itself: who worked, who took the surplus, and under what sanction. For a history defined by developments in production, the formation of the caste order was the main event of the ancient centuries.

One further founder's thesis belongs to the record, because his successors turned it into their central battleground. Kosambi sketched, in the Introduction of 1956, a two sided account of how feudal relations formed in India: feudalism from above, as kings made grants of villages and revenues to officials, soldiers, and temples, creating a class of intermediaries between the state and the cultivator; and feudalism from below, as local chiefs and strongmen consolidated power in the countryside and the state later ratified what it could not prevent. The sketch occupied a few pages. It proposed a category, hedged it with Indian specifics, and left the evidence largely unworked. A decade later, one of the most gifted of the younger historians took the sketch, filled it with epigraphic evidence, and built it into the thesis around which the school's internal argument would rage for a generation.

## The founder on faith, cities, and the uses of a creed

Kosambi's method faced its most delicate test on religious material, where the temptation to reduce belief to economics was strongest and where he, characteristically, produced some of his most durable work. His treatment of early Buddhism is the standard example. He asked why a doctrine of renunciation, preached by a prince of a tribal oligarchy, should have conquered the cities of the Ganges valley in the centuries when those cities were filling with merchants, artisans, and money. His answer located the teaching in the social transformation it addressed: a society moving from tribal kinship toward class division, private property, and the cash nexus needed an ethic that loosened the claims of clan and sacrifice, sanctified the accumulation of merit rather than of cattle, and offered the new men of property a discipline as portable as their goods. The monastic order itself, with its rules of admission, its assemblies, and its voting procedures, preserved in amber the constitution of the tribal republics from which its founder came, so that the religion carried the vanished polity inside itself as a fossil carries the shape of an extinct shell. The reading has been contested by scholars of Buddhism on textual grounds, and parts of it have been revised by later research, but its form governed the school's approach to every creed thereafter: treat the doctrine as an answer to a social question, identify the question from the structure of the society, and let the fit between the two stand or fall by the evidence.

The same approach governed his reading of the earliest cities. Where the older historiography had treated the Indus civilisation as a mystery sealed by its undeciphered script, Kosambi treated it as an economy: a bronze age order resting on riverine agriculture and organised trade, whose cities embodied a surplus and a priestly or mercantile authority that the surviving material could partially reconstruct. The decipherment he could not supply, and he said so with a mathematician's clarity about the difference between a problem unsolved and a problem insoluble. What he insisted on was that the Indus order belonged to history, connected by trade routes and technique to the world beyond the passes, and not to the realm of timeless Indian essence to which the colonial imagination had consigned whatever it could not date.

His statistical treatment of coinage belongs in this section too, since it shows the school's founding gesture in miniature. The punch marked coins of the early historic centuries bear clusters of symbols whose meaning had defeated generations of cataloguers content to describe and arrange. Kosambi weighed the coins in their thousands and subjected the weights to statistical analysis, showing that the distributions carried information: about the authorities that guaranteed the standard, about the erosion of that standard over time, and about the difference between coins struck under central control and the looser currencies of dissolving states. The symbols themselves he treated as the marks of issuers and examiners in a chain of guarantee, and while later numismatists have disputed his particular attributions, the principle he established, that a coin series is a statistical population to be analysed rather than a picture book to be admired, passed into the discipline's toolkit. It is difficult to think of another founder of a historical school who arrived with a technique of that kind in his luggage, and the school's enduring respect for quantitative series, in prices, in revenue assessments, in the counting of inscriptions, begins in those weighed trays of silver.

A biographical fact rounds out the portrait and explains a strain in the school's later development. Kosambi never held a chair of history, never directed a department of it, and trained no doctoral students in it. His influence ran through print and through the admiration of younger scholars who attached themselves to his questions without ever sitting in his classroom. The school that bears his imprint was therefore built by other hands, in universities he did not govern, and its institutional character, syllabus bound, examination driven, collective, is Sharma's and Habib's rather than his. The tension between the founder's freelance example and the school's academic reality recurs in its history: the method was his, the machinery theirs, and the friction between a method that invites heresy and a machinery that must examine students on settled answers was never wholly resolved.

## The founder's other life: theorems, genes, and the unity of his method

The school's founder is routinely described as a mathematician who turned to history, and the description understates the strangeness of the career, because the mathematics was never abandoned and its products still circulate under his name in fields that have never heard of his history. In genetics, the mapping function he published in 1944, which converts recombination frequencies into map distances along the chromosome, remains a standard instrument known to every student of the subject as Kosambi's function. In statistics, his work of the same years on the expansion of functions in series, developed independently in the same period by Karhunen and Loève, is cited as the Kosambi Karhunen Loève expansion and underlies techniques of signal analysis far from any archive. In differential geometry his papers on path spaces were the work of a young Harvard trained researcher expected to make his career in the purest of the pure sciences. The man who weighed punch marked coins in the evenings had spent his days at the frontier of three exact disciplines, and the school's later character, its respect for series, for measurement, for the refutation that arrives as a number, descends from that daytime life as surely as from any text of its doctrine.

The unity of the career was a unity of method, and Kosambi himself insisted on it with some asperity when admirers tried to separate the scientist from the historian. In every field he touched, his instinct was the same: refuse the classification that the material had inherited, and ask instead what process had generated it. The geneticist's crossover data, the statistician's noise, the numismatist's coin weights, and the historian's caste order were all, to that instinct, deposits of a process, legible if one found the operation that had laid them down. Historical materialism, when he came to it, did not give him the instinct; it gave the instinct a subject worthy of it, the largest process of all, the making of human society by its own labour. The school's students, most of whom had no mathematics, inherited the instinct in diluted form, as the habit of asking of every institution the founder's question: not what is it called, but what made it, and what is it in the middle of becoming? It is not the least of his legacies that a school of historians should have been founded by a man for whom a theorem and a temple inscription were the same kind of object, a surface under which a process ran, awaiting the reader patient enough to reconstruct it.

## The founder's Pune: a scholarly ecology

The founder's method was formed in a particular city's scholarly ecology, and the ecology deserves its paragraph because the school's habits of interdiscipline were Pune's habits before they were the school's. Pune in Kosambi's decades held, within a few miles, Fergusson College where he taught, the Deccan College with its linguists and archaeologists, and the Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute, where the critical edition of the Mahabharata was being assembled by the collation of manuscripts from across the subcontinent. The anthropologist Irawati Karve, whose studies of kinship organisation mapped the marriage systems of India with a fieldworker's patience, worked in the same small world, and her demonstration that kinship followed regional and caste logics rather than a single Indian pattern ran parallel to Kosambi's demonstration that society itself had been assembled from tribes. The air of the place favoured a particular intellectual posture: contempt for the armchair synthesis, respect for the specimen, whether manuscript, potsherd, or marriage rule, and a conviction that India's unity, where it existed, was a product to be explained rather than a premise to be assumed. Kosambi's weekend parties into the hills, students and colleagues trailing a professor who read the landscape like a palimpsest, locating a megalith here and a goddess shrine there and narrating the whole valley as a document, became the stuff of the city's academic legend, and the legend carried the method further than the books alone could have done: generations of Maharashtra's historians learned from the story, before they read a page, that history began in the field and that the library was where one went to check what the field had said.

The ecology also imposed the school's characteristic limitation, which its history would spend decades correcting. A method formed on the Deccan's dry hills, its tribal survivals, and its early charters travelled imperfectly to the wet deltas, the temple cities, and the Persianate courts, and the school's first syntheses bear the marks of their birthplace in a confidence about survivals and a relative thinness on the Islamic centuries' own categories. The correction, characteristically, came from the school's expansion: Aligarh supplied the Persian archive and the Mughal centuries, Delhi the epigraphic north, and the regional universities of the third generation supplied the rest, until the method that had been one man's way of walking his home district had become a national discipline's common practice, tested against every archive the subcontinent keeps.

## The goddess in the field: the founder's ethnography at work

Abstract statements of method persuade less than a worked case, and Kosambi's studies of the mother goddess supply the school's favourite worked case of the survival method in action. The Deccan countryside he walked is dense with goddess shrines: stones daubed with red lead beneath trees, hilltop sanctuaries reached by worn steps, village goddesses whose festivals ignore the Brahminical calendar and whose priests are often not Brahmins but men of the cultivating and shepherd castes. The Sanskrit texts know these goddesses dimly or not at all, and the orthodox tradition, where it acknowledges them, supplies them with borrowed genealogies marrying them into the great pantheon. Kosambi read the distribution of the cults as a map of the past. The goddess, in his reconstruction, belonged to the agricultural communities that first cleared and settled the land, for whom fertility, of field and of womb, was a single power presided over by a mother; her shrines marked the sites of early settlement and the boundaries of early fields; and her worship, with its animal offerings and its possession rites, preserved the ritual of a world before the temple, the priest, and the caste order had arranged religion into ranks. When the agrarian order absorbed those communities, the goddess was absorbed with them, demoted in the Sanskrit retelling to a consort or a local form of a great deity, but never extinguished, because the cultivators who needed her continued to feed her.

The case shows the method's three registers meshing. The field register supplied the distribution, shrine by shrine, across a countryside the historian had walked. The textual register supplied the demotions, the Sanskrit genealogies that papered over the goddess's origins and could therefore be read, in reverse, as evidence that an origin had needed papering. The archaeological register supplied the depth, the microliths and megaliths of the same hills, attesting communities of the right antiquity in the right places. No register alone proved the reconstruction; their convergence made it an argument, and its vulnerability was equally clear, since an opponent who could show the cults to be late importations, or the distributions to be accidents of the survey, could break the chain at its weakest link. Later ethnographers and historians of religion have revised many of Kosambi's particular identifications, sometimes severely, and have charged his reconstruction with the survival method's congenital vice, the temptation to treat a practice as a message from a single past when practices in fact accumulate meanings from every age they pass through. The school absorbed the criticism in its usual way, by tightening the standard: a survival must now be shown, not intuited, and the goddess in the later literature appears with her full stratigraphy of meanings, early and late, distinguished where the evidence allows and confessed indistinguishable where it does not.

For the portrait of the school, what matters is the direction of attention the case established. A historiography that sends its founder out on foot to catalogue the shrines of peasant goddesses has declared, in practice, that the religion of the labouring population is a primary source for the history of the whole society, and that the Sanskrit canon is one archive among several, privileged in preservation and biased in content. That declaration, more than any doctrine, is what the subaltern generation inherited from the founder even as it accused his school of elitism, and it is why the accusation, when the school's defenders answer it, always returns to the hills around Pune: whatever the state and revenue tradition became in the hands of the institution builders, the founder's own practice had begun at the shrine, with the cultivator's goddess, in the field.

## The founder's chronology: moving the furniture of the Indian past

A method that claims the right to redescribe society will soon claim the right to redate it, and Kosambi's chronological revisions were among his most unsettling bequests. The inherited calendar of ancient India had been arranged by literary and dynastic convenience: texts were dated by the traditions that transmitted them, and whole bodies of literature floated in a pious vagueness that permitted any antiquity a devotee desired. Kosambi insisted that texts be dated by their contents, by the society they presupposed, and his datings repeatedly dragged revered works down the centuries toward the conditions that explained them. The Bhagavad Gita, in his reading, belonged not to the heroic age it depicted but to the feudalising centuries that needed its doctrine of devoted service to a lord; the great codifications of law and custom were assigned to the periods whose property relations they regulated rather than to the sages whose names they bore. The procedure scandalised traditional scholarship and delighted the school's students, for it converted chronology from an act of deference into an act of analysis: to date a text was to state a theory about the society that produced it, and the theory stood open to refutation by anyone who could show a different society in the work.

His handling of the Buddha's century worked the same transformation in the opposite direction, pulling the teacher out of the realm of timeless wisdom and setting him in a datable transformation: the clearing of the Ganges forests, the rise of the new kingdoms and republics, the first coins and the first cities of the historic age. The Buddha's order, with its propertyless monks fed by a laity of merchants and householders, became in Kosambi's hands an institution of that transformation, unintelligible a thousand years earlier and unnecessary a thousand years later. Whether or not every detail of the reconstruction stands, the gesture defined the school: nothing in the Indian past, however sanctified, escaped the question of when, in the history of production, it belonged.

The chronological audacity had a cost that the school's critics itemised with care. A historian who redates by social content risks circularity: assign the text to the feudal age because its doctrine suits feudalism, then cite the text as evidence for the feudal age. Kosambi's defenders replied that the circle is broken wherever independent series, coins, inscriptions, archaeology, converge on the same placement, and that his best datings were triumphs of convergence rather than of intuition. The dispute is a permanent one in historical method, and the school's practice, at its most careful, institutionalised the defence: no redating was to rest on the content of a single text, and the master's own more venturesome placements were quietly allowed to lapse when the convergence failed to arrive. Here again the tradition corrected its founder from within, and here again the correction is the most convincing evidence that the school was a discipline and not a devotion.

## The founder's India, period by period

To grasp what the school inherited, it helps to walk the founder's own sketch of the whole Indian past, which the Introduction of 1956 laid out with a compression that still startles. For the earliest age he posited a long prehistory of food gathering communities, whose survivals he hunted in the hills, followed by the food producing revolution of the Indus cities, a bronze age order he treated as the subcontinent's first class society. After its decline came the age of the pastoral tribes, the Indo Aryan speakers of the Vedic texts, whose clan organisation, cattle wealth, and sacrificial religion he read against the surviving evidence of the peoples they displaced and absorbed. The tribal order dissolved, across the middle centuries of the first millennium before the common era, into the class divided society of the Ganges valley: kingdoms and oligarchies, cities and guilds, coined money and standing armies, and with them the great ideological ferment of the Buddha and the Jains, whose doctrines he interpreted as the spiritual register of a world being remade by the plough, the market, and the tax collector.

The Mauryan empire, which unified most of the subcontinent in the third century before the common era, occupied a special place in his scheme. Reading the administrative treatise attributed to Kautilya beside the Greek accounts and the edicts of Ashoka, Kosambi pictured a state of unprecedented reach, which owned mines and monopolies, ran crown farms with unfree labour, and supervised production and exchange with a bureaucracy of spies and superintendents. It was, in his judgment, the nearest approach in Indian history to a state that organised production directly, and its fiscal appetite explained both its extent and the strain that dissolved it. After the Mauryas his narrative darkened into the centuries of the guilds and the Roman trade, which he treated as a genuine commercial efflorescence resting on craft production and merchant capital, and then into the Gupta age, where his feudalism from above began: the grants multiplied, the state's salaried bureaucracy thinned into hereditary intermediaries, and the long early medieval transformation commenced. The sketch was audacious, uneven, and programmatic, and its function in the school's history was precisely that of a programme: it told two generations of researchers where the problems lay and what kind of evidence would settle them.

Two features of the sketch deserve emphasis, because they became the school's reflexes. The first is its treatment of religion throughout as a register of social change rather than as a self moving mystery: each great creed arrives on schedule with the formation that needed it, and none is permitted to float free of the plough and the market. The second is its handling of invasion. The older historiography explained Indian change by successive waves of conquerors, as if the subcontinent's own population were inert. Kosambi's invaders arrive, certainly, but they are absorbed into processes already under way, and the motor of change is consistently internal: the extension of cultivation, the deepening of class division, the growth and sclerosis of states. A reader who absorbs those two reflexes has absorbed the founder, whatever becomes of his individual theses.

## Merchants, guilds, and the urban centuries

Between the founder's iron age cities and Sharma's feudal countryside lies the school's account of the urban centuries themselves, the half millennium in which Indian craft production and long distance trade reached their ancient height. The school's historians, following the founder's lead, reconstructed this world from the guilds. The sreni, the corporate organisation of artisans and merchants, appears in the texts as banker, employer, lawgiver to its members, and donor to temples and monasteries, and the school read it as the institutional form of a mature commodity economy: production organised for markets, quality enforced by corporate discipline, and surplus accumulated by merchant houses whose donations endowed the cave temples of the western Deccan. The Roman trade, which carried Indian pepper, textiles, and steel to the Mediterranean and brought back the gold coins that still surface in southern hoards, was integrated into the same picture: India in the early centuries was a workshop of the ancient world economy, and its ports and caravan cities were nodes in a system that reached from the Red Sea to Central Asia.

This reconstruction did double duty in the school's argument. Backward, it buried the stagnant village of the colonial account under the evidence of a commercial civilisation. Forward, it set the terms of the feudalism debate, since a decline can only be measured against a height: the towns whose decay Sharma would chart had first to be shown flourishing, their mints busy and their guilds rich, or the early medieval contraction shrank to a change of fashion. Critics of the school sometimes charged it with romanticising the ancient market as a reproach to the feudal centuries, and the charge contains a half truth the school's own historians acknowledged: the urban centuries mattered to them partly because the contrast organised the whole story. Yet the evidence for the efflorescence, the coin hoards, the inscriptions of guild donations, the archaeology of the port sites, is independent of the use the story made of it, and it remains the standing refutation of any account in which Indian commerce begins with the European companies. The school's point was never that the ancient economy was capitalist before its time. It was that it was an economy, in motion, with classes and markets and crises of its own, and that the historian's first duty was to describe its motion rather than to admire its stillness.

The craft production of those centuries also supplied the school with one of its favourite correctives to textual snobbery. The Sanskrit sources were composed by men who regarded manual work with cultivated distaste, and a historiography that read only them inherited their blindness to the workshop. The school's archaeologists and economic historians countered with the material record: the beads of the Deccan sites, the iron and steel whose quality foreign buyers prized, the pottery sequences that chart the spread and retreat of settlement. In their hands the artisan, invisible in the literature of his betters, became a historical personage with a technology, an organisation, and a fate. The recovery exemplified the founder's fourth register applied to things rather than cults: the mute evidence of production, interrogated by a historian who believed that how a society makes its living is the first fact about it.

## Iron, slavery, and the ancient economy in the school's account

The school's treatment of the ancient economy turned on two connected questions, and its answers distinguish it from the Marxist historiographies of Europe. The first question was whether ancient India had rested on slavery, as the classical Mediterranean had. The inherited Marxist sequence expected a slave mode of production in the ancient world, and the older Indian scholarship, colonial and nationalist alike, had largely ignored the problem of labour altogether. Kosambi's answer, developed and refined by Sharma, was that India knew slaves, the dasas of the texts, but never rested production on them in the classical manner. The work of agriculture and craft was performed overwhelmingly by peasants, artisans, and hired labourers organised within the village, with slaves concentrated in domestic service and in the households of the rich. What India developed instead of the slave plantation was the subordination of whole communities: the Sudra varna supplied a hereditary labouring order whose compulsion was enforced by ritual rank and by the denial of property and learning, a system the school analysed as the local equivalent, in function though not in form, of the unfree labour on which other ancient civilisations had built. The distinction mattered because it freed the sequence of Indian formations from a Procrustean bed: India had its ancient world, with cities, trade, states, and class exploitation, without passing through the slave mode that the European timetable prescribed.

### Did ancient India rest on slavery like the classical Mediterranean?

No, on the school's account. Slaves existed and were bought, sold, and inherited, but production rested on peasants, artisans, and hired labourers, with the Sudra order supplying hereditary unfree labour under ritual sanction. The school therefore refused to force India into a slave mode of production, and treated the varna order as the subcontinent's own machinery of labour compulsion.

The second question concerned the engine of the ancient world's expansion: the spread of iron. In work gathered in his studies of material culture and social formations, Sharma argued that iron tools, axes to clear the heavy forests of the middle Ganges plain, ploughshares to turn its soils, gave the expanding agrarian order the technical means to colonise the river valleys, and that the famous second urbanisation of the sixth century before the common era, with its cities, its punch marked coinage, its guilds and its heterodox religions, stood on that material base. The argument gave Indian history a materialist account of its first great transformation that owed nothing to invasions or dynastic genius, and it connected the school's ancient history to its founder's Buddhism in a single arc: iron cleared the land, the cleared land fed the cities, the cities bred the merchants and monks, and the monks preached the ethics of a world remade by the plough.

The iron thesis attracted the archaeologist's version of the feudalism critique. Specialists in the material record objected that iron objects appear in the relevant strata too late, too thinly, or too unevenly to carry the causal weight assigned to them, and that the clearance of forest by fire and the axe of bronze or stone was older and more effective than the thesis allowed. The debate, like the feudalism debate, sorted itself into a strong form that few would defend entire and a structural residue that most absorbed: whatever the precise metallurgy, the early historic transformation was a transformation of production, agrarian, craft, and commercial at once, and any account of the age of the Buddha that omits the changing material base has omitted the condition of everything else. Here again the school's pattern repeated itself: a bold materialist construction, a decade of empirical battering, and a settlement in which the question survived the answer.

## Indian feudalism: the thesis and its evidence

Ram Sharan Sharma was in almost every outward respect Kosambi's opposite. Where the founder was a freelance of genius, suspicious of institutions, Sharma was an institution builder: a professor at Patna and then at Delhi, a trainer of research students in cohorts, and the first chairman of the Indian Council of Historical Research when that body was founded in 1972. Where Kosambi sketched, Sharma documented. His book Indian Feudalism, published in 1965, took the founder's provisional category and subjected it to the full weight of the epigraphic record, and the result was the most debated thesis in modern Indian historiography.

### What is the Indian feudalism thesis?

the argument that land grants from around the fourth century onward produced a decentralised agrarian order in India, with declining trade and urban life and a dependent peasantry, constituting a distinctively Indian feudalism rather than a copy of the European form. It remains contested.

The thesis can be decomposed into its working parts. From roughly the fourth century of the common era, and with gathering pace under the Gupta emperors and their successors, Indian kings began to make permanent grants of land and, crucially, of the revenues and jurisdiction of villages, to Brahmin communities, to temples, and increasingly to royal officials and military men. The copper plate charters that record these grants survive in large numbers, and Sharma read them not as acts of piety alone but as transfers of power. The donee received the right to collect the king's share, to levy dues the king had levied, to punish offences the king's officers had punished, and in many charters to command the labour of the village's cultivators. The state, in effect, was paying its servants and purchasing its legitimacy with slices of its own authority, and each grant inserted a lord between the cultivator and the crown.

Upon that institutional base, the thesis erected a connected account of the whole society. Political authority fragmented as grantees became local powers in their own right. The peasantry sank into dependence: charters transferred villages together with their inhabitants, cultivators were bound to render forced labour, known as vishti, and the freedom of movement that produces a labour market withered. Trade declined, on this account, under a double pressure: the decay of the urban markets that had flourished in the early centuries, and the growing self sufficiency of the granted village, which needed less from outside. Coinage thinned dramatically, a fact Sharma read from the numismatic record as the monetary signature of a retreating exchange economy. Towns shrank or were abandoned, their sites yielding the archaeological signature of decline. Society closed in on itself, region by region, and the resulting order, decentralised, agrarian, hierarchical, and resting on a subjected peasantry, was feudalism: not the European article transplanted, since India knew neither the manor nor vassalage in their classic forms, but a formation answering to the same structural description under Indian conditions.

### What evidence did the feudalism thesis rest on?

Four bodies of evidence carried it. Copper plate and stone inscriptions recorded the grants and their immunities in legal detail. The numismatic record showed coined money thinning markedly for several centuries. Urban archaeology showed occupation layers contracting or ending. And the normative texts showed cultivators increasingly bound, burdened, and forbidden to leave the villages assigned over them.

The thesis drew strength from the way its parts corroborated one another, and from the comparative frame it offered for facts that the older historiography had left unconnected. The paucity of coins between the Kushanas and the early medieval centuries had been a cabinet fact without an explanation. The silence of the record about flourishing cities in those same centuries had been another. The charters' multiplying immunities, forbidding royal officers to enter granted villages, had been a third. Sharma's construction made the three facts legible as one process. That unifying power, more than any single demonstration, explains why the thesis organised research for decades: it gave hundreds of scholars a question to test against every district's plates and every region's coin finds, and a research programme that can be tested district by district is one that generates knowledge even when it fails.

The thesis also carried a periodisation that redrew the map of Indian time. If the argument held, then the centuries between the Guptas and the Sultanate were not a dark interval between two ages of empire but a distinct social formation with its own logic: an early medieval order, feudal in character, within which the classical heritage was preserved by the very decentralisation that dissolved the classical state. Indian history acquired, in place of a sequence of dynasties, a sequence of formations: the urban, commercial, slave and wage assisted order of the early historic centuries; the feudal order that succeeded it; and the centralised revenue empires that eventually overcame it. However the details were contested, that reframing has never been reversed.

### What distinguishes feudalism from above from feudalism from below?

In the founder's sketch, feudalism from above was created by the state's own acts, as kings alienated villages, revenues, and jurisdiction to grantees and officials. Feudalism from below grew in the countryside itself, as chiefs, strongmen, and local lineages consolidated coercive power that kings later recognised. The distinction explained the formation by converging pressures rather than by decree.

Sharma's other works filled in the society around the thesis. His study of the Sudras traced the labouring order of ancient India and its transformation as the feudal centuries deepened the burdens of the lowest varna. His study of urban decay assembled the archaeological case site by site. His later work on material culture ranged across the whole ancient period with the same method: begin from production, and let political narrative take the place that remains. In each case the procedure was the school's procedure, applied with a patience the founder rarely permitted himself: assemble the evidence exhaustively, state the mechanism plainly, and invite the refutation that Kosambi's aphorisms had sometimes seemed to forbid.

## The feudalism debate: was there feudalism in Indian history?

A thesis that organises a field invites a challenger, and the sharpest challenge arrived in 1981, in the Journal of Peasant Studies, from Harbans Mukhia, a historian at Jawaharlal Nehru University whose credentials inside the tradition were impeccable. His essay asked its question in its title: was there feudalism in Indian history? The answer, argued with care and without polemic, was that there was not, and the argument cut at the thesis's foundations rather than its details.

Mukhia's case rested on the character of the Indian peasantry and the ecology that sustained it. In the classic European formation, he argued, feudalism arose where land was scarce relative to labour and lords could bind peasants to the soil because the peasants had nowhere else to go. India presented the inverse condition. Land was abundant and labour was scarce, and a cultivator oppressed beyond endurance could flee to the forest frontier and clear new fields, which meant that no class of lords could durably subject the peasantry in the European manner. The Indian peasant remained, by comparative standards, a relatively free producer, working family land with family labour, paying a share of the produce upward to a state that taxed him but did not own his person. What India had was a powerful revenue extracting state confronting a free peasantry, a configuration for which the vocabulary of feudalism, with its serfs, demesnes, and vassalage, was simply the wrong instrument. To import the term was not analysis but analogy, and analogy, he insisted, is what a historian reaches for when the evidence has stopped speaking.

### Why does the question of a free peasantry decide the debate?

Because the two sides agreed on most facts and divided on what they implied about the cultivator. If peasants were substantially bound, their surplus taken by lords holding jurisdiction over them, the feudal description gained content. If peasants remained mobile family producers taxed by a distant state, the charters recorded taxation rather than subjection.

Sharma replied, in the same journal in 1985, with a counter question: how feudal was Indian feudalism? The reply conceded the differences from Europe and argued that the concession was beside the point. Feudalism, on his restatement, named not a checklist of European institutions but a structure: a class of landholders interposed between state and cultivator, living off the surplus extracted by extra economic compulsion, under conditions of a relatively closed agrarian economy. By that structural test the Indian evidence qualified: the charters did transfer jurisdiction, the immunities did exclude royal officers, the forced labour dues were named in the documents, and the transfer of villages with their inhabitants was not a formula but a conveyance. Mukhia's free peasant, Sharma argued, was an abstraction drawn from the ecology of one region and one period, while the charters showed dependence deepening across centuries and regions. The debate, at this level, was a genuine scientific disagreement about what a category is for: a summary of a structure, to be judged by explanatory yield, or a proper name for one civilisation's experience, to be judged by fidelity to the original.

Others entered from different directions, and their entries changed the debate's shape. The epigraphist D. C. Sircar, whose command of the inscriptions few could match, held that the grants created something better described as landlordism than feudalism, a distinction turning on his reading of what rights the charters actually conveyed. B. D. Chattopadhyaya, in work culminating in The Making of Early Medieval India in 1994, challenged the empirical premise of decline itself: surveying the evidence region by region, he argued for agrarian expansion, for the spread of settlement into new areas, for state formation proceeding through the integration of local powers rather than through fragmentation, and for exchange networks that, while changed in form, had not collapsed. His early medieval India was not a closed and decaying world but an expansive one, and if he was right, the feudalism thesis had mistaken a transformation for a decline. John Deyell, studying the monetary history of the period, showed that the apparent absence of coinage was partly an artefact of looking for the wrong money: billon and base metal issues, and cowries, carried exchange through the very centuries in which Sharma had seen a moneyless natural economy. Burton Stein, working on the south, proposed the segmentary state as a better model for the Chola polity than either empire or feudal fragmentation, and south Indian evidence generally sat awkwardly inside a thesis built largely on northern plates.

### What exactly did the critics of Indian feudalism deny?

They denied different things, and the denials must be kept separate. Mukhia denied the category, arguing that a free peasantry and an abundant frontier made the European comparison misleading. Sircar denied the conveyance, reading the charters as creating landlords rather than feudatories. Chattopadhyaya denied the decline, presenting expansion where the thesis had presented contraction. Deyell denied the monetary evidence.

The debate's outcome resists a verdict, and the honest summary is a grading. The thesis in its strongest form, with generalised urban decay, thinned coinage, and a subjected peasantry across the subcontinent, did not survive the regional evidence intact; few of its author's own students would defend every clause of the 1965 book. The thesis in its structural form, that land grants created an enduring class of intermediaries with jurisdictional powers and claims on the cultivator's surplus and labour, and that the early medieval centuries are therefore a distinct formation requiring a distinct analysis, survived and became the common property even of many critics, who now argue about the formation under other names. A category can lose the argument about its name and win the argument about its object, and that, on a fair reading, is what happened. The school's defenders add a further point which the record supports: the debate itself, conducted over two decades in journals and conference volumes, with inscriptions re read and coin hoards re weighed, was the method working as intended. A research programme is vindicated not by the survival of its first formulation but by the knowledge its refutations are forced to produce.

## Inside a grant: the mechanics of the feudal conveyance

Abstract argument about formations comes alive, and becomes testable, at the level of a single document, and the school's seminar rooms taught the feudalism thesis by walking students through one copper plate charter clause by clause. Imagine the standard instrument of the fifth or sixth century, engraved on plates of copper, ringed and sealed, recording a king's gift of a village to a community of Brahmins. The charter opens with the king's genealogy and titles, conventional matter that the trained reader skims. Then come the operative clauses. The village is conveyed with its fields and waste, its trees and ponds, its pastures and its mines if any, together with the fines for offences committed within its bounds and the taxes on its produce, its ferry dues and its market dues where they exist. The inhabitants are addressed and instructed: they are to obey the donees, render to them the customary dues, and bring them the shares formerly brought to the king's officers. The royal officers, present and future, are warned off: no soldier, no tax collector, no policeman of the crown is to enter the village or disturb the donees' enjoyment of it. The grant is declared permanent, to last as long as the sun and moon, and imprecations in verse threaten with the sin of killing a cow, or a Brahmin, any future king who resumes it.

Every clause of that instrument does work in the thesis. The conveyance of fines and jurisdiction transfers a fragment of sovereignty, not merely an income. The instruction to the inhabitants establishes the donees as their effective rulers for the purposes that matter daily. The exclusion of royal officers creates the immunity, the walled garden of lordship, that feudal analysis requires. The permanence clause, and the theological artillery guarding it, show the king purchasing merit and legitimacy at the price of power he cannot recover. And the accumulation of such instruments, across centuries and kingdoms, in their thousands, charts the process by which a centralised early empire became a landscape of mediated lordships. The critics' counter readings fastened on the same clauses with equal care: the immunities, they argued, were formulaic, copied from charter to charter by scribes who reproduced the boilerplate of a chancery style; the dues conveyed were the king's fiscal share, not ownership of the soil; the inhabitants were instructed to pay, not surrendered as serfs. The debate at the level of the document was thus a debate about how legal instruments mean, whether a formula records a practice or a pretension, and it could be settled, where it could be settled at all, only by multiplying instances and comparing regions, which is precisely the labour the controversy provoked. A generation of epigraphists re edited the plates with the question in mind, and the Indian epigraphic corpus is permanently richer for a quarrel about feudalism. Few historiographical episodes anywhere offer a cleaner demonstration that a thesis earns its keep by the documents it forces its enemies to read.

The human content of the conveyance should not be lost in the diplomatics. Behind the clause that transfers a village with its inhabitants stands a cultivator who woke one morning to find that the dues he had carried to the king's granary were now owed to a settlement of Brahmins at the village's edge, that the disputes he had carried to the king's court would now be judged by his new lords, and that the officer who might have heard his complaint against them was forbidden by charter to cross the boundary. Multiply that morning by the thousands of villages the plates record, and the feudalism thesis acquires its moral weight: it is a claim about how ordinary people's rulers changed, and about how the change was written, in copper, to be unchangeable. Whether the change amounted to feudalism is the question the debate argued. That it amounted to a transformation in the government of the countryside, no party to the debate denied.

## The career of a word, and the school's ancient India completed

The feudalism thesis had a philological jewel at its centre, and it deserves display because it shows the school's method working at the scale of a single word. The Sanskrit term samanta appears in the early texts meaning a neighbouring king, the ruler next door, an equal whose territory adjoins one's own. Across the early medieval centuries the word's meaning travels: in the charters and court literature it comes to denote the subordinate lord, the feudatory who holds his position under an overlord, attends his court, supplies his troops, and receives his rank from the imperial hand. Sharma traced the word's career through the inscriptions as a seismograph of the political order: when neighbours become feudatories in the language of the documents, the structure of power has changed in fact. A critic might quarrel with any single citation, and the debate produced quarrels in plenty, but the procedure, semantic history as institutional history, became a model for the school's students, who learned to treat the vocabulary of the sources as evidence of the same order as their statistics. Words, like coins, are issued under authority and debased under pressure, and the historian who weighs either series must attend to whose hand is on the scale.

Sharma's own range extended beyond the feudalism thesis, and a fair portrait must follow him into the study of ancient political institutions, where his conclusions were in one respect surprising for a historian of his formation. Examining the gana sanghas, the non monarchical polities of the early centuries, the Licchavis and their fellows, he found assemblies, elected chiefs, and corporate decision making that sat awkwardly beside any picture of an immemorial oriental despotism, and he said so in print: the Indian past contained republican forms, oligarchic and in some respects genuinely deliberative, whose existence the colonial theory of Asiatic stagnation had no room for. The finding reinforced the school's central contention from an unexpected quarter. A civilisation that had experimented with republics before the common era, that had built cities and guilds and universities of monks, was not the timeless village of the administrative imagination, and the political evidence joined the economic in refuting it. The school's critics on the nationalist right sometimes accused it of disparaging the Indian past, and its ancient history is the sufficient answer: nobody who had read Sharma on the republics, or Kosambi on the cities of the Ganges, could believe the school thought India had no history worth honouring. What it disparaged was the claim that the past needed no explanation.

The Aryan question shows the school at work on the most inflammable material in Indian historiography, and its handling of it displays the method's discipline under maximum pressure. The question, in its scholarly form, asks how the Indo Aryan languages entered the subcontinent and how their speakers interacted with the populations and the great cities that preceded them. The school's synthesis, stated by Kosambi and restated a generation later in Sharma's book length examination of the Aryan evidence, held together linguistics and archaeology in a single account: pastoral groups speaking Indo Aryan tongues entered from the northwest in the second millennium before the common era, in movements spread over centuries rather than in a single invasion; they encountered the remnants of the Indus order and the farming peoples of the interior; and from the long interaction of their clan organisation, their cattle and horse wealth, and their sacrificial religion with the agriculture and cults of the absorbed populations, the society of the Vedic texts and eventually the caste order emerged. The older invasion story, with its Aryan conquerors massacring the dark city dwellers of the Indus, was rejected on the evidence: the skeletons at the famous sites did not show a massacre, and the cities had declined before the newcomers arrived in force. Race, in the school's treatment, dissolved into language, economy, and custom, and the Aryans became a historical process rather than a master people. That the question was also a live wire in the country's politics the school's historians knew perfectly well; their response was characteristic, to publish the evidence and the argument and to let the monograph carry the weight that a manifesto could not. The public controversy has been noted in these pages and is not entered here; what belongs to the record is that the school's account of the Aryans was an application of its method, tribe, production, and absorption, to the very material on which its founder's theory of caste had first been built.

## The Indian debate in the world's transition argument

The feudalism controversy belonged, whether its participants emphasised the fact or not, to a worldwide argument that Marxist historiography had been conducting since the nineteen forties: the argument about how one mode of production gives way to another. In Britain and America that argument had taken the form of the transition debate, opened by Maurice Dobb's studies of capitalist development and Paul Sweezy's challenge to them, over whether feudalism dissolved from within, by the differentiation of its own producers, or from without, by the solvent of trade. The Indian debate transposed the question into a prior key. Before India could argue about the transition out of feudalism, it had to establish whether the formation existed at all, and the establishment required the category to be rebuilt from Indian materials under the sceptical eye of scholars who knew exactly how the European original had been specified. The result was an unintended gift to the international discussion. The Indian controversy forced into the open the question that the European debate had left implicit: what, precisely, is a category of this size for? If feudalism named a checklist, serfdom, vassalage, the manor, the fief, then India failed the test and so, on inspection, did most of Europe outside a small and brief heartland. If it named a structure, lordship interposed between state and peasant, surplus extracted by political and legal compulsion, production organised in relatively closed local economies, then India presented a member of the family, with the family resemblance strong in the structure and the differences instructive in the detail. The school's own trajectory across the debate, from the confident checklist of the early formulations toward the structural restatements of the settlement, rehearsed in one generation the education the European discussion had taken three to acquire.

The dependency debates of the same decades gave the Indian argument a second international frame. When Habib wrote of the potentialities of capitalist development strangled by the Mughal fiscal structure, and when the school's colonial historians rebuilt the drain theory with the tools of the archive, they were addressing the question that the Latin American dependency school and its critics were addressing from the other side of the world: whether underdevelopment was an original condition or a manufactured one, and whether the blockage lay in the world market's demands or in the domestic structure that met them. The Indian school's answer was characteristically double. It documented the drain and the destruction with a thoroughness the dependency writers could envy, and it refused the consolation that the domestic order had been innocent: the fiscal machinery that squeezed the Mughal peasant, the caste compartments that divided him, and the intermediary classes that farmed him were India's own, and they had set the terms on which the foreigner found his purchase. A historiography that can hold its own society's structures and the empire's violence in a single account, assigning each its weight without acquitting either, has earned the right to be read wherever those questions are asked. That it was so little read abroad remains the standing reproach this article began with, and it is addressed not to the school but to its audience.

## The school's south: temples, assemblies, and the limits of a northern thesis

Every general thesis in Indian history is eventually taken south and tried against the Tamil country, and the feudalism thesis made the journey with instructive results. The south's early medieval record is dominated by an institution the northern charters know only in embryo: the great temple, which by the Chola centuries had become a landed corporation of the first rank, holding villages by royal and private gift, employing armies of servants and artisans, lending to village assemblies, commissioning bronze and stone on a scale that made it the region's largest single consumer of skilled labour, and redistributing its revenues and food through festivals that bound the countryside to its courts. Around the temple stood the assemblies, the sabha of the Brahmin settlements, the ur of the ordinary village, the nagaram of the merchants, whose inscriptional records, engraved on the temple walls themselves, document a local self government of remarkable formality: committees chosen by lot, qualifications for membership specified, accounts audited and defaulters disqualified. A historiography of decline and fragmentation confronted, in this record, an order that was dense, literate, corporate, and expanding, and the confrontation forced the debate's most productive refinement.

The southern evidence did not simply refute the thesis; it specified it. The land grant was as central in the south as in the north, since the temple's villages and the Brahmin settlements were created by the same conveyance, and the donees exercised the same fiscal and judicial rights the northern charters conveyed. What differed was the institutional ecology into which the grant was inserted: where the north's granted village faced a thinning state and a retreating market, the south's faced a temple economy that re circulated the surplus, assemblies that administered it, and maritime trade that connected the region to Southeast Asia and China. The same instrument, opposite outcomes: the comparison taught the school, and its critics taught it in return, that a conveyance is not a formation, and that the formation must be described at the level of the whole ecology of institutions through which the surplus moved. Burton Stein's segmentary state, proposed for the Chola polity, pushed the lesson further from another direction: the Chola realm, on his account, was not a centralised empire manqué nor a feudal fragmentation, but a polity in which ritual sovereignty radiated from a centre whose administrative grip faded with distance, a form for which neither the European feudal vocabulary nor the imperial one was made. The school's mature southernists absorbed what they could use, the temple as an economic institution, the locality as the real unit of power, while resisting the conclusion that economic relations were therefore beside the point. The general moral, which this article has now drawn from three regions, is the one the school's whole career teaches: the categories survive their journeys only by being rebuilt at each destination, and the building is the research programme itself.

## The debate grows a period: the early medieval settlement

Historiographical debates rarely end with a victor's proclamation. They end, when they end well, with a settlement: a shared vocabulary, a redistributed burden of proof, and a pile of new knowledge that neither side possessed when the argument began. The feudalism debate produced exactly such a settlement, and its monument is a period label. Where the textbooks had once passed from ancient to medieval across the boundary of a dynasty or a conquest, the discipline now habitually speaks of the early medieval centuries as a period in their own right, stretching roughly from the fourth century to the twelfth, defined not by who ruled but by a configuration: land grants as the characteristic instrument of statecraft, the expansion of agriculture into new regions, the proliferation of local and regional powers, the temple emerging as a landed institution, and the slow recomposition of society around hereditary occupation and rank. The label is used by historians who accept the feudalism thesis, by historians who reject it, and by historians who have never read the founding texts of either camp, which is the surest sign that the debate changed the discipline's furniture.

The participants' own later positions registered the settlement. Sharma, in the restatements of his maturity, accepted that trade and towns had not declined uniformly, that the chronology differed sharply by region, and that the formation he described had passed through phases, including a later phase in which exchange revived and the order began to transform from within. His restatement of the thesis in moderated form, carried forward by students such as D. N. Jha, who edited the debate's retrospective volumes and defended a qualified feudal formation against the category's abolitionists, kept the structural core, the interposed landholding class and the dependent cultivator, while surrendering the uniform darkness of the original picture. Chattopadhyaya's integrative model, which described the early medieval state as spreading by the incorporation of local chiefs, cults, and settlements into expanding agrarian polities, turned out to describe a process compatible with much of the grant evidence: the charters that Sharma had read as the state's dissolution could equally be read as the state's way of growing into country it had never administered. The two readings divide on emphasis and on the direction of the age's motion, and the discipline learned to hold both, deploying the vocabulary of integration for the expanding frontiers and the vocabulary of feudal relations for the settled cores where the intermediaries dug in.

The south supplied the settlement's other half. Historians of the Tamil country and the Deccan, working on temple inscriptions of extraordinary density, reconstructed an early medieval world of assemblies, temple economies, and long distance trade that neither darkness nor fragmentation described. The work of R. Champakalakshmi on south Indian trade and urbanisation demonstrated exchange networks of real sophistication in the very centuries of Sharma's decline, and compelled the thesis to regionalise itself or retire. It regionalised. The mature position, which a fair summary must attribute to the debate rather than to any participant, holds that the subcontinent's regions entered the early medieval configuration at different times, by different routes, with different mixtures of grant, temple, market, and chiefly power, and that the formation's unity lies in the questions it poses, about land, lordship, and the peasant's surplus, rather than in a single chronology imposed from Delhi or Patna.

### What survived of the feudalism thesis after the debate?

The object survived better than the name. The land grant as the period's master institution, the interposed class of intermediaries with claims on the cultivator, the early medieval centuries as a distinct formation: all passed into the discipline's common stock. What did not survive were the generalised decline of towns and the uniformly subjected peasantry.

There is a methodological moral the school itself drew, and it is worth stating because it governs the rest of this article. The feudalism thesis had been framed boldly enough to be tested: it made claims about coins that numismatists could check, claims about towns that archaeologists could check, claims about charters that epigraphists could check. Its partial refutation was therefore a productive event, yielding a generation of regional monographs that would not have existed without it. The school's defenders contrast this with theses so loosely framed that no evidence could embarrass them, and they draw the conclusion that a research programme earns its keep by the quality of its failures. On that standard, by the school's own account of itself and by the testimony of the shelf of books the debate produced, Indian feudalism was the most successful failure in modern Indian historiography.

## The predecessor in the archive: Moreland and the field the school inherited

The Aligarh argument, like the feudalism thesis, began by inheriting a field from a predecessor it would spend decades correcting, and the Mughal field's predecessor was not an Indian scholar but a colonial civil servant. W. H. Moreland, an officer of the Indian Civil Service who had administered land revenue in the United Provinces, retired to write the first modern economic history of Mughal India, in studies of the agrarian system and of the empire's economy from Akbar to Aurangzeb published in the nineteen twenties. Moreland was no crude apologist for empire, and the school's historians acknowledged the debt with a precision that did them credit: he had found the sources, mastered the Persian revenue terminology with a practitioner's advantage, and established that the Mughal state could be studied as an economic system rather than as a pageant of emperors. His compilations of the assessment data remained, for the Aligarh generation, the quarry from which their own work began.

The correction came at the level of the frame. Moreland had read the Mughal system with the eyes of a settlement officer, which is to say from the top of the revenue pyramid downward, and his India was accordingly a field administered, its peasants a factor in an imperial budget whose rationality he assessed as one administrator assessing another across three centuries. The Aligarh historians read the same registers from the bottom upward, asking what the assessment meant in the village, what the demand left in the cultivator's house, and what the assignment system did to the men who worked it, and from that inversion of the reading direction a different empire emerged: not Moreland's formidable but essentially static fiscal machine, but a system with a built in contradiction, whose demands generated the resistance that would eventually unmake it. The episode completes the school's pattern of inheritance and correction in a register this article has not yet sounded, because the predecessor here was the colonial scholarship itself, neither Marxist nor nationalist, and the correction was purely professional: a new generation, with better questions, rereading a great compiler's archive against his conclusions. When the school's critics charge it with having begun from doctrine rather than from evidence, the Moreland succession is the standing reply. It began from the best evidence the empire's own servants had assembled, and it beat them on their own ground.

## The Aligarh school and the Mughal agrarian order

If the feudalism thesis was the school's most debated construction, the Mughal work was its most solid one. Its centre was Aligarh Muslim University, where a tradition of medieval studies had been built by an earlier generation and was turned, from the nineteen fifties, toward the agrarian structure of the Mughal empire. The central figure was Irfan Habib, whose father, Mohammad Habib, had been among the university's most eminent historians, and whose own training combined Persian sources, Mughal administrative manuals, and the statistical patience that the material demanded. Around him worked colleagues and students, among them M. Athar Ali, whose study of the Mughal nobility reconstructed the empire's ruling class from its service records, and Satish Chandra, whose work on the politics of the Mughal court gave the agrarian argument its political counterpart. The Aligarh school, as the milieu came to be called, made the economic history of the empire a matter of documents counted and registers compared, and its collective monument was an account of how the greatest of India's early modern states took its living from the land, and why that manner of taking destroyed it.

Habib's The Agrarian System of Mughal India, 1556 to 1707, first published in 1963, remains the reference work. Its method deserves emphasis before its conclusions, because the method was the school's signature at its best. The Mughal state had been a paperwork empire: it assessed the harvest field by field, maintained manuals of rates and measurements, recorded the assignments of revenue to its officers, and preserved, in chronicles and documents, the arithmetic of its own extraction. Habib took the empire at its paperwork. He reconstructed the assessment system known as zabt, under which land was measured and the state's demand was fixed as a share of the produce, crop by crop and region by region. He reconstructed the prices and yields from which the demand's real weight could be judged. And he followed the money upward, from the village through the assignees and intermediaries to the imperial treasury, asking at each stage who was authorised to take, who actually took, and what the difference between the two did to the people at the bottom.

The conclusions formed a single connected argument. The state's demand was pitched extremely high: the assessment claimed for the sovereign a very large share of the peasant's produce, leaving the cultivating family, in the ordinary run of seasons, little beyond subsistence and the means of the next sowing. The demand was collected substantially in cash, which forced the peasant into the market, selling his crop at harvest when prices were lowest and borrowing from the moneylender to bridge the year, so that merchant and credit capital fed on the same surplus the state had skimmed. Between the state and the village stood the zamindars, a hereditary class of local chiefs and landholders who collected the revenue in their territories, kept their portion, and transmitted the rest, converting an imperial demand into a thousand local oppressions. And over the whole structure operated the assignment system, to which a section below is devoted, by which the empire paid its officers not in salaries but in temporary rights to the revenue of specified territories.

### What did the Mughal revenue system do to the peasantry?

It converted the state's fiscal demand into the organising fact of rural life. Assessment took an enormous share of the produce, cash collection forced the cultivator to sell at harvest and borrow through the year, and assignees and zamindars each added pressure of his own. The peasantry's surplus, and often its subsistence, flowed upward through the machinery.

Habib's agrarian history had a second movement, which gave the book its enduring influence: an account of the empire's agricultural technology and its limits. He catalogued the crops, including the new arrivals from the Americas that were transforming Indian fields in these very centuries, the implements, the irrigation works, and the organisation of village production, and he asked the question that distinguishes economic history from antiquarianism: why did a rich and sophisticated agrarian civilisation, with abundant skills and a vast internal market, not generate the transformation of production that its resources seemed to promise? His answer located the blockage in the structure of extraction itself. A system that drained the countryside's surplus into consumption by a military and administrative class, and that gave its assignees every reason to squeeze and none to invest, systematically prevented the accumulation from which a new mode of production might have grown. The essay in which he explored the potentialities of capitalist development in the Mughal economy, published at the end of the nineteen sixties, became one of the most argued counterfactuals in Indian historiography: the potentialities existed, in markets, in merchant capital, in craft skill, and were strangled by the fiscal structure that fed on them.

## The town in the school's Mughal world

The agrarian argument is sometimes misread as a claim that the Mughal world was a countryside with a court attached, and the school's own work on the towns corrects the misreading from within the tradition. The revenue demand was collected largely in cash, and a cash demand on that scale manufactures towns as surely as it manufactures markets: the peasant sold his crop to pay it, the assignee and the zamindar spent their retentions where goods and services were concentrated, the imperial camps and capitals consumed on a scale that drew artisans and merchants across continents, and the banking houses that moved the revenue's value between provinces kept their seats in the commercial cities. The school's urban history of the empire, built from the same registers as its agrarian history, reconstructed a network of towns whose size and specialisation impressed even the European travellers whose reports the school otherwise distrusted: manufacturing towns organised around the textile crafts, ports funnelling the export trade, administrative capitals swollen by the court and its service population, and a countryside of market villages in which the moneylender and the grain dealer performed the daily alchemy that turned harvests into revenue.

The point of the reconstruction was analytical, not picturesque. The towns were the fiscal system's other face: the surplus extracted from the village returned to circulation through the town, and the empire's celebrated manufactures, its muslins and brocades, its indigo and its saltpetre, were produced overwhelmingly by artisans working for merchants under arrangements, of advance and contract, that the school examined for signs of an emerging capitalist organisation. Its verdict was characteristically precise. The merchant's capital dominated the artisan without transforming his production: the weaver still worked at his own loom, in his own house, with his family's labour, bound to the merchant by debt and advance rather than gathered into a workshop under the merchant's eye. The school called this a blockage at the threshold, the point at which commercial capital, in the European sequence its historians knew, had begun to reorganise production itself. In India the reorganisation was postponed, and the school's explanation returned, as its explanations tended to, to the fiscal structure: the state's demand skimmed the countryside's surplus before it could accumulate in the countryside, while the court's demand pulled the towns' production toward luxury consumption rather than toward the cheap goods whose manufacture disciplines an industry into mechanisation. Whether the explanation is sufficient has been argued ever since by the historians of Indian industry, some of whom find the blockage less complete and the eighteenth century's workshops more dynamic than the Aligarh account allowed. The argument, however, set the question that the whole subsequent literature answers: not whether Mughal India had commerce, which nobody who had read the registers could doubt, but why its commerce did not become its transformation.

## Revenue, assignment, and the crisis of an empire

The assignment system, the jagir, was the empire's payroll and, on the Aligarh analysis, its built in catastrophe. A Mughal officer, a mansabdar, held rank from the emperor and was paid by assignment of the revenue of designated territory, his jagir, which he held for a few years before transfer to another. The arrangement spared the treasury the handling of cash salaries, and it aligned the officer's income with the empire's expansion, since new conquests created new revenue to assign. Its vices were the obverse of its conveniences. An assignee with a short and uncertain tenure had no interest in the long term fertility of his villages and every interest in extracting the maximum before his transfer arrived. The peasant thus faced not a settled lord, who might moderate his demands to protect his own inheritance, but a succession of temporary extractors racing against their own removal. Where the assessment exceeded what the land could bear, the cultivator's rational reply was flight, abandoning the village for the forest or for a less burdened territory, and the chronicles of the later seventeenth century fill with reports of deserted villages and peasants in arms.

The crisis, as Habib reconstructed it, closed like a vice. Expansion faltered at the empire's frontiers, so the pool of revenue available for assignment stopped growing, while the ranks of officers claiming assignment continued to swell, producing a shortage of jagirs and a lengthening queue of the unpaid and the disappointed. In the Deccan, conquered at enormous cost, the assessed revenue proved larger on paper than the war ruined countryside could yield, so assignees squeezed harder, peasants fled or revolted, and collection fell further behind assessment. The great peasant and zamindar revolts of the period, of Jats, of Satnamis, of Sikhs, and the Maratha insurgency that built a rival power in the western hills, appear in this account not as religious disturbances or dynastic accidents but as the agrarian system's contradictions arriving at their political form. The empire did not merely decline; it was pulled down, in part, by the classes its fiscal machinery had made desperate.

### Why did the assignment system generate a crisis of its own?

Because it separated the right to extract from any interest in the land's future. Assignees held their territories briefly and insecurely, so each squeezed the peasantry as hard as his tenure allowed, driving flight and revolt; meanwhile assignments depended on conquest, and when expansion stopped, the claims of a growing officer class outran the revenue available.

The Aligarh reconstruction was not unchallenged, and its critics made points the school absorbed. Historians of the eighteenth century objected that an account centred on collapse flattened a century in which regional economies and successor states showed real dynamism, and that trade, banking, and urban manufacture continued to develop within and beyond the empire's frame. Scholars working on prices and trade questioned elements of the extraction arithmetic and argued that the economy's commercial sinews were stronger than the agrarian crisis model allowed. Habib's replies, and the revised edition of the Agrarian System that he published in 1999, engaged the criticisms point by point, conceding detail while holding the structure: a fiscal demand of exceptional weight, an assignment system with perverse incentives, and a peasantry whose resistance set limits the empire finally could not cross. The debate continues, as the brief for serious work usually does, but it continues on terrain the Aligarh school surveyed, with its categories as the instruments. That is the ordinary form of victory in historiography.

## The zamindar and the village: the middle layer of the Mughal order

Between the imperial assessment and the cultivator's field stood the figure on whom the Aligarh reconstruction lavished some of its finest attention: the zamindar. The school's historians resisted both of the simplifications that had distorted the term. The zamindar was not a landlord in the English sense, owning the soil and letting it to tenants, and he was not a mere official of the empire, appointed and dismissed at pleasure. He was a hereditary local magnate, chief of a clan or master of a territory, whose rights over the land's revenue were older than the empire and recognised by it, who kept the peace or disturbed it with his own retainers, who collected the revenue in his jurisdiction and transmitted the imperial share after retaining his own perquisites, and whose cooperation the empire needed and whose defiance it feared. Habib's taxonomy of the class, from the great autonomous chiefs who were kings in all but tribute, through the intermediary zamindars who collected from the peasants of many villages, down to the primary zamindars who were themselves substantial peasants with superior rights in their own village, gave the field a vocabulary it still uses, and the taxonomy carried an argument: the Mughal state did not confront an undifferentiated peasantry but a layered society in which an armed, hereditary intermediary class stood permanently between the fisc and the field.

The argument mattered for the crisis thesis, because it located the empire's structural weakness in that middle layer. The imperial demand, however rationalised at the centre by measurement and schedule, reached the village through men whose interest was to enlarge their own retention and whose power was local, hereditary, and armed. When the centre weakened, it was the zamindars who converted fiscal rights into territorial lordships, who sheltered fleeing peasants on their own terms or led them in revolt, and who carved the successor principalities out of the empire's provinces in the eighteenth century. The Aligarh school's eighteenth century is therefore not a simple decline: it is the transfer of power downward to the class its analysis had identified, a reading that the subsequent historiography of the successor states absorbed even while disputing the crisis that supposedly preceded it. The village, at the bottom of the structure, appears in the school's account with its own internal order restored to it: the headman and the accountant, the hereditary artisans and servants remunerated by shares of the harvest, the dominant caste's panchayat managing the common lands and the water, the whole bound together by a customary division of the produce that the revenue demand had to penetrate before a single coin reached the treasury. To describe that order, the Aligarh historians used the same sources as their predecessors but asked a different question of them: not what the empire decreed, but what the decree became by the time it had passed through the hands that lived by intercepting it.

There is a historiographical point here that the school's critics and defenders can share. The Mughal village of the older literature, self sufficient and changeless, was in part a creation of the imperial archive's own surface: the manuals describe the assessment as if it were the reality, and a historiography that reads manuals takes the state's word for the countryside. The Aligarh method, reading the manuals against the chronicles of complaint, the records of revolt, and the evidence of what peasants actually paid and fled from, reconstructed the gap between the assessment and the exaction, and in that gap found the empire's real constitution. The lesson generalised beyond Mughal studies, and the school's students carried it to other archives: every state's paperwork describes the state it wished to be, and the historian's craft begins in the difference.

## The Sultanate antecedent: assignment before the Mughals

The Aligarh argument had a prehistory, and its historians were careful to supply it, since an institution explained without ancestors looks like an accident. The Mughal jagir descended, through the practices of the intervening centuries, from the iqta of the Delhi Sultanate: the assignment of a territory's revenue to an officer in return for military service, perfected under the Turkish sultans of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. The school's medievalists, building on the work of the university's earlier generation, traced the iqta through its transformations: granted first as a revocable delegation of the sultan's fiscal rights, hedged by transfer and audit, it showed a persistent tendency to harden into hereditary possession whenever the centre weakened, and each dynasty's reformers, most famously the Khalji and Tughluq sultans in their revenue measures, appear in the record attempting to reverse the hardening and restore the assignment to its revocable form. The pattern taught the school's students their characteristic lesson in institutional history: the assignment is a compromise between a state too poor to pay salaries and an officer class too strong to serve unpaid, and the compromise carries its dissolution inside it, since every assignee's rational ambition is to convert a temporary delegation into a permanent patrimony.

Reading the Sultanate through the assignment also let the Aligarh historians connect their Mughal work to the feudalism debate across the supposed wall of the Islamic centuries. The iqta holder was not a feudatory in Sharma's sense: he held no charter of immunity, owned no village, and remained in principle a servant of the court, transferable and audited. Yet the pressure of the institution, toward local entrenchment, toward the conversion of office into property, toward the peasant's subjection to the man on the spot, resembled the pressure the charters had documented for the earlier period, and the school's mature position treated the two assignments, the brahmin's granted village and the officer's granted revenue, as distinct instruments answering a common problem: how a pre modern Indian state, ruling an enormous agrarian population with a thin administrative cadre, could pay its servants out of anything except the land's surplus. The continuity mattered for the school's portrait of Indian history as a connected whole. Dynasties rose in Delhi and fell, creeds changed at court, languages of administration succeeded one another, and underneath, the fiscal problem persisted in recognisably the same shape, generating recognisably similar institutions and recognisably similar crises. That depth of continuity, established by documents rather than asserted by philosophy, is the school's implicit answer to the charge that its formations are imported abstractions: the abstractions were earned, reign by reign and register by register, from the Indian record itself.

## Counting an empire: prices, money, and technology after the founder of the Aligarh argument

The Aligarh argument did not rest with its founder's book. A school proves itself by the work it makes possible, and the Agrarian System opened a workshop in which two generations of scholars counted, compared, and mapped the Mughal economy with instruments the book had forged. The most characteristic product was Shireen Moosvi's statistical study of the empire's economy around 1595, built from the Ain i Akbari, the great administrative gazetteer compiled under Akbar. Where Habib had reconstructed the system, Moosvi measured its magnitudes: the extent of cultivation implied by the assessment statistics, the revenue demand set against the produce, the distribution of the imperial income among its recipients, the urban population that the tax base could support. Her figures, offered with a statistician's caveats about the sources, gave the debate something it had lacked: numbers that critics could attack only by producing better numbers, and several of her estimates, of the weight of the land revenue demand above all, hardened into the working assumptions of the field. The exercise matters to the school's portrait because it displays the Aligarh temperament at its purest: the conviction that a pre modern state's own statistics, criticised and cross checked, could support quantitative economic history of a rigour the narrative sources never allowed.

The school also pressed the question of technology, where the older picture of a stagnant Indian craft tradition lay waiting for the same treatment the village community had received. Habib's studies of technological change in the Mughal period traced the arrival and diffusion of devices that altered production: the spinning wheel, which multiplied the spinner's output and spread through the textile districts in the centuries before the empire; improved looms and the drawloom's ancestors; the geared Persian wheel that lifted irrigation water across the northern plains; the spread of sericulture; the new crops, tobacco, maize, the pineapple and the cashew among them, that crossed the oceans with the Portuguese and entered Indian fields with a speed that refutes, by itself, the stereotype of the immobile countryside. His conclusion was characteristically double edged. The Mughal centuries were not technologically frozen; devices arrived, spread, and raised productivity in the crafts. Yet the inventions were adopted into a structure that taxed away the surplus they created, and India produced few labour saving machines of its own in the sector where they mattered most, the spinning of yarn, so that the technological dynamism stopped short of the transformation that would have changed the mode of production itself. The argument gave the school's Mughal work its distinctive melancholy: an economy in motion, ingenious, commercialised, and going, on its own terms, nowhere that would save it.

The growth question divided the school's heirs from its revisionist critics in a debate that sharpened both. Against the crisis model, historians working on trade and on the seventeenth century economy emphasised expansion: the flood of American and Japanese silver into Indian mints, the deepening monetisation of the countryside, the growth of the great textile exports that clothed markets from Southeast Asia to Europe, the banking houses whose bills of exchange moved value across the empire more efficiently than its armies moved grain. On this evidence the Mughal economy was not strangling but growing, and its political collapse owed more to the accidents of succession, the overreach of Deccan conquest, and the ambitions of regional powers than to any agrarian contradiction. The Aligarh reply held that the two pictures describe different circuits of the same economy: commerce could flourish at the surface, in the ports and the banking towns, while the agrarian base that fed the fiscal system was being squeezed toward revolt, since the empire's income came overwhelmingly from the land revenue and not from the customs of its flourishing ports. Growth and crisis, in this reading, were contemporaries, and the question was which circuit set the terms for the state's survival. The debate remains open in the journals, conducted with a civility and a command of sources that reflect well on both parties, and it illustrates the school's late condition: its founding arguments no longer command assent, but they continue to supply the questions, the data, and the standards of proof.

### Was the Mughal economy growing or strangling?

Both, in different circuits, and the debate turns on which circuit decided the state's fate. Trade, monetisation, and textile exports expanded impressively, while the agrarian base from which the empire drew its income was squeezed toward flight and revolt. The school's heirs hold that the fiscal circuit was decisive: no flourishing port could rescue a countryside in arms.

One further Aligarh production deserves record because it changed how the empire is visualised. Habib's historical atlas of Mughal India, published in 1982, plotted the empire's geography from its own records: the boundaries of its provinces and revenue districts, the routes of its trade, the distribution of its crops and manufactures, the sites of its mints and its revolts. An atlas is an argument in cartographic form, and this one's argument was the school's: that the empire was an agrarian system before it was a court, that its splendours rested on the measured field and the assigned village, and that a reader who wanted to understand its fall should begin with the map of its extractions. Generations of students have learned the Mughal world from those plates, which is another way in which a school's influence outlives the assent its theses command.

## The refusal of the Asiatic mode

The school's relation to Marx's own Indian writings passed through a category that had to be disposed of first. In his journalism of 1853 and in his notebooks, Marx had sketched for Asia a distinct mode of production, later called the Asiatic mode, whose features were a self sufficient village community combining agriculture with domestic craft, the absence of private property in land, and a despotic state standing over the villages as the universal landlord and organiser of irrigation. The picture had ancestry: it descended from the traveller Bernier's report that in Mughal India the king owned all the land, from the philosophy of history of Hegel, and from the British administrative literature, the parliamentary reports and revenue officers' minutes in which the Indian village figured as an unchanging little republic, sufficient unto itself since before memory. Marx, who never visited Asia and read voraciously what the empire's servants wrote, took the picture over as the premise of his Indian analysis.

The Indian Marxist historians, with few exceptions, rejected the category as applied to their country, and the rejection was an empirical act before it was a theoretical one. Consider what the evidence showed. Inscriptions and documents recorded the sale, gift, and mortgage of land by individuals and families across the centuries, transactions unintelligible where private rights in land did not exist. Villages contained sharp internal differentiation: landholders and landless labourers, creditors and debtors, dominant castes and serving castes, a stratification the picture of the undifferentiated commune could not accommodate. Commodity production and market exchange, far from being marginal, ran through the economy at every period for which records survive, in grain, in textiles, in metalwork, in the produce of specialised villages supplying towns. And the supposedly changeless countryside had a history of technological and institutional movement, of new crops, new tenures, rising and dissolving states, that the school's own monographs were busy demonstrating. A category assembled from the reports of colonial officials, implying a society without property, without classes worth the name, and without internal motion, failed on every count against the archives of the society it claimed to describe.

Kosambi's position carried the nuance of a founder who had once found the category suggestive. His own work began from the village and the tribe and retained a sense of the Indian countryside's conservatism, yet his central demonstration, that tribes had been transformed into castes through a historical process driven by the spread of plough agriculture and surplus extraction, was itself a refutation of changelessness: a society that had undergone so profound a metamorphosis could not be exhibited as history's waiting room. Habib, a generation later, stated the rejection in its most systematic form, arguing that Marx's perception of India had been formed by sources, Bernier above all and the British official literature, that systematically misrepresented property and production in the Mughal and earlier orders, and that the corrective lay not in defending the master's Indian conclusions but in repeating his method on better evidence. The episode matters beyond its content. A school that can look at the founder's best known empirical claims about its own country, test them against the record, and discard them in print has answered, in the only convincing way, the charge that it practises a catechism.

### On what grounds did the school refuse the Asiatic category?

Because its defining features failed against the evidence. Land was bought, sold, and inherited; villages were stratified by property and caste rather than undifferentiated; markets and commodity production were pervasive; and the countryside had a documented history of change. A category assembled from colonial files could not survive the colonised society's own archives.

The rejection had a wider resonance in the international argument of the time. The Asiatic mode had become, in the nineteen sixties and seventies, a contested token in debates far from India: in Soviet and European theory, where reviving it threatened the official sequence of stages; in the controversy around hydraulic despotism, which had given the category a polemical second life as an instrument against Asian states; and in the early work of postcolonial critics, who took Marx's Indian journalism as Exhibit A in the case that even the great critic of empire had seen the East through the empire's eyes. The Indian school's contribution to that argument was distinctive because it was neither apologetic nor denunciatory. It did not say that Marx was a colonial ideologue, and it did not say that his Indian writings were an unfortunate slip. It said that his sources were the administrative literature of a conquering power, that the literature had interests, and that the remedy for bad sources is better history. The series treats the category itself in the study of the [mode of production explained](/2010/02/15/mode-of-production-explained/), and Marx's colonial journalism in its own right in the study of the [Marx on colonialism debate](/2017/03/01/marx-on-colonialism-debate/); what belongs here is the school's verdict, which was that the concept, as applied to India, described the archive it came from more accurately than the society it claimed to name.

## The correction from within

The school's largest claim on an international reader rests on a pattern this series has met before and will name here, because the extra questions of every examination and the deeper logic of the tradition both turn on it: the correction from within. The phrase describes what the Indian historians did to Marx's account of India. They did not defend it, and they did not merely denounce it. They repeated its method on evidence its author never saw, and the method, honestly repeated, overturned the author's conclusions. A tradition's own practitioners correcting the founder's empirical premises is stronger evidence for the framework's productivity than any defence of the founder's conclusions could be, because it shows the framework generating knowledge its originator did not possess and could not have predicted, including knowledge that embarrassed his reputation.

Set the two accounts side by side. Marx's India, in the journalism of 1853, was a society whose village communities had rested for ages on a basis of domestic industry and communal property, untouched in their inner organisation by the wars and dynasties that passed overhead, so that the country, in his arresting phrase of judgment, had no history in the proper sense, only the history of its successive invaders. British rule, in that account, was a vandal and a midwife at once: it destroyed the old society, cruelly and without creating anything in the act of destruction, yet it also laid, by railways, by law, by the shattering of the village's economic shell, the material premises from which a new society would eventually arise. The Indian historians accepted the seriousness of the question, what kind of society was pre colonial India and what did colonialism do to it, and answered it from the archives with a different society altogether: differentiated, commercialised in significant part, technologically and institutionally mobile, ruled by states whose fiscal systems reached deep into the village and changed its life, and inhabited by peasants who bought, sold, borrowed, fled, and rebelled. On their evidence, colonialism had not broken into a museum. It had conquered a going concern, and the conquest's effects had to be measured against the dynamism of what was conquered, not against a fiction of stillness.

### How can a school correct its own founder?

By treating the founder's method as binding and his information as replaceable. Marx's conclusions about India followed, they held, from the colonial sources available to him; working with inscriptions, coins, revenue records, and field evidence he never saw, they showed a society in motion. The correction is internal because it proceeds by the tradition's own instruments and questions.

The point deserves one more turn, because it reverses the usual direction of the colonial debate. In much international discussion, Marx's Indian writings are used as a fixed text, to be condemned or excused, while the historiography of India itself is treated as a local affair. The Indian school reversed the polarity. It treated Marx's journalism as a datable intervention resting on datable sources, and it treated the Indian evidence as the tribunal. Bernier could be checked against the Mughal documents. The Fifth Report's village could be checked against the inscriptions. The verdicts, delivered in monographs rather than manifestos, accumulated into something more damaging to the master's Indian reputation than any polemic, and more valuable to his tradition than any exoneration: a demonstration that historical materialism, applied to India by Indians with full command of the sources, produced a history in which India had a history. The wider argument with postcolonial criticism, which has its own account of what Marx saw and failed to see, is pursued in the comparison with [Marxism vs postcolonial theory](/2017/05/15/marxism-vs-postcolonial-theory/); the frame of the method itself is set out in the guide to [historical materialism complete guide](/2010/01/15/historical-materialism-complete-guide/).

## An argument across a curtain: the school and the Soviet Indologists

The Indian school's arguments about feudalism and the Asiatic mode had one audience that read them with a professional attention no Western academy matched: the Indologists of the Soviet Union, for whom the same categories were live questions of doctrine and of scholarship at once. Soviet Indology was a serious enterprise, with its own translations, its own expeditions of the mind into the Indian archives, and its own internal quarrels conducted under political constraints the Indian historians did not share. On the question of Indian feudalism, leading Soviet scholars had argued, from the nineteen fifties, for a feudal formation in early medieval India, and the convergence with Sharma's thesis was noted on both sides: two Marxist historiographies, working the same evidence under different regimes, had arrived at the same formation, a fact each side cited, in its own idiom, as corroboration. On the Asiatic mode the traffic ran the other way. The concept's revival in Soviet discussion was a delicate matter, shadowed by its implications for the doctrine of stages, and the Indian historians' archival demolition of the concept as applied to India supplied the Soviet sceptics with their best evidence and the Soviet defenders with their hardest problem. The Indian debate was followed, translated, and argued over in Moscow with an intensity that makes the indifference of the Western surveys the more striking: the categories were the same, the evidence was the same, and the side of the world that shared the school's theoretical premises took its conclusions seriously a generation before the side that did not.

The comparison throws the Indian school's freedom into useful relief. Its historians argued about the founder's categories, and about the founder, with a liberty that their Soviet counterparts could admire only from a distance: Habib's dissection of Marx's Indian sources, Mukhia's abolition of a category the textbooks required, the subaltern manifesto's indictment of the whole Marxist enterprise of Indian history, were published, debated, and taught in the country where they were written, without permission from any ministry of doctrine. The school had its establishments and its gatekeepers, as this article has recorded, but its arguments were settled by journals and conferences rather than by authority, and the record of its corrections, of founder, of thesis, of itself, is the documentary proof. When the school's achievement is graded internationally, this institutional fact deserves a line in the ledger beside the monographs: it demonstrated that a Marxist historiography could operate as an open research tradition, generating heresies faster than any orthodoxy could anathematise them, and that the demonstration was made not in the doctrine's European homeland but in the universities of a poor and newly independent republic at the other end of Asia.

## The Indian verdict in the world's argument about modes

The rejection of the Asiatic mode was argued in Indian journals through the nineteen sixties and seventies with an intensity that puzzled foreign observers, who had filed the concept away as a curiosity. The stakes, for the Indian participants, were not antiquarian. If the category held for India, then the subcontinent's past required no class analysis in the ordinary sense, its future could be theorised as an escape from an immemorial despotism, and the school's entire programme, of formations succeeding one another by internal contradiction, collapsed at its foundation. If the category failed, as the school held, then India took its place inside the general history of class societies, different in its combinations, ordinary in its intelligibility, and the colonial account of its peculiarity lost its last theoretical shelter. The debate therefore drew in everyone: the Aligarh historians with their registers, the Delhi seminar with its charters, economists arguing about the village community's survival into the census era, and political theorists who saw in the concept's hydraulic despotism a weapon in the cold war against Asian states. The Indian consensus that emerged, that the concept described a real archive (the colonial administrator's India) and an unreal society, was one of the school's most complete victories, ratified in time by the international literature itself, where the Asiatic mode survives in the literature chiefly as an object lesson in the dangers of theorising a civilisation from its conquerors' paperwork.

A parallel from a neighbouring historiography sharpened the Indian school's sense of its own achievement, and its historians noted the parallel themselves. In China, historians of the same decades debated the sprouts of capitalism: whether the Ming and Qing economies had generated, from within, the beginnings of a capitalist transformation that foreign invasion had interrupted. Habib's essay on the potentialities of capitalist development in Mughal India belongs to the same family of questions, and the comparison illuminates both. In each case a Marxist historiography, armed with the founder's method, refused the founder's geography of history, in which dynamism was Europe's property and Asia's part was to wait. In each case the refusal rested on archival demonstration of markets, merchant capital, and technical change. And in each case the counterfactual, what would have happened without the conquest, was left deliberately unprovable, a question posed to discipline the analysis of what did happen rather than to console anyone for it. The Indian school's version of the question was the more austere, since Habib's own analysis supplied the internal blockage, the fiscal structure, that made the potentiality fragile even before the foreigner arrived; the school did not need a villain from overseas to explain a transition that failed, having found the obstacles at home. That austerity is worth underlining, because it separates the school's colonial argument from the nationalist economics it is sometimes confused with. The drain was real, on its account, and the destruction was real, but the society drained and destroyed had carried its own contradictions at full stretch, and a different empire, or none, would not have waved them away.

The methodological yield of the whole episode can be stated as a rule the school bequeathed to its successors: distrust any category whose evidence base is a single archive with an interest. The Asiatic mode rested on the traveller and the administrator. The unchanging village rested on the revenue officer's need for a stable unit of assessment. The idolatrous, effeminate, or fanatical native of the older histories rested on the conqueror's need for a subject unfit to rule himself. Against each, the school's practice erected the same defence: multiply the archives, set the inscription against the chronicle and the coin against the memoir, and let the category that survives the confrontation earn its keep. The rule sounds elementary. Its consistent application, across a century of Indian evidence and against the pieties of three empires of interpretation, colonial, nationalist, and orthodox Marxist alike, is the school's working definition of itself.

## A second correction: the master's own second thoughts

The correction from within had a postscript that the Indian historians noted with satisfaction, because it closed the argumentative circle. Marx himself, in his last decade, had begun to revise the unilinear confidence of his earlier writing. In the ethnological notebooks he kept in his final years, excerpting the anthropologists of his day, and in the famous drafts of his letter to Vera Zasulich in 1881, he contemplated the possibility that a communal form, the Russian village commune, might pass to a higher order without traversing the full career of capitalist dissolution, provided the surrounding conditions favoured it. The passage is no repudiation of his method; it is the method correcting its own timetable, an admission that the sequence of formations was a finding to be established case by case and not a railway along which every society must travel in order. The Indian historians, whose whole practice embodied that finding, could fairly claim that their heresy had the master's late example behind it, and some of them said exactly that, with the relish of men who had been accused of betraying a doctrine they had in fact been obeying more literally than their accusers.

The second correction concerned the other half of the 1853 journalism: the prophecy that British rule, having destroyed the old society, would regenerate India by laying the material foundations of a new one. The school's verdict, delivered across its work on the colonial economy surveyed in the next section, was that the prophecy had failed in its own terms. The railways were built, but as a system for moving troops and raw materials to the ports rather than as the skeleton of a national market; the old industries were destroyed, but the new ones were throttled by policy whenever they threatened the metropolis's exports; political unity was imposed, but as the unity of a prison yard, administered for extraction. Destruction without regeneration: the formula inverted the master's double mission, and the inversion rested, once more, on the evidence the school's economists assembled rather than on any quarrel with his intentions. A reader who wishes to grade the correction from within as a whole should hold the two corrections together. On India's past, the school showed movement where he had seen stillness. On India's colonial present, it showed blockage where he had foreseen transformation. In both cases the corrective instrument was the same: the archive, worked by his method, against the conclusions he had drawn from the reports of the conquerors.

## The mode of production in the fields: the school's method tested on the living countryside

The school's categories faced their most technical examination not in the ancient centuries but in the present, when, in the nineteen seventies, the economists and historians of the Indian left turned the question of the mode of production upon the contemporary countryside. The debate, conducted over years in the pages of the Economic and Political Weekly and in the seminars of the universities, asked what kind of formation independent India's agriculture actually was. Was it capitalist, as the spread of wage labour, commodity production, and investment for profit suggested? Was it semi feudal, as the persistence of sharecropping, usury, attached labour, and landlord power in the eastern regions argued? Or was it something the old categories could not name, a colonial formation persisting into independence? The question was not scholastic. On its answer depended the strategy of every movement that proposed to change the countryside, and the debaters knew it, which accounts for the debate's unusual combination of statistical rigour and political heat.

Its methodological interest, for the purposes of this article, lies in the instruments the contenders forged. Utsa Patnaik proposed a criterion of classification drawn from the labour process itself: households were to be sorted by the ratio between the labour they hired in and the labour they hired out, so that the class structure of a village could be computed from its employment data rather than intuited from its landholdings, and landlord, rich peasant, middle peasant, poor peasant, and labourer emerged as positions in a measurable system. Ashok Rudra, surveying the same countryside, reported that the polar classes the theory expected, a capitalist farmer class employing free wage labour on the one side and a dispossessed proletariat on the other, could not be found in the expected purity, and drew the conclusion that the formation was not capitalist in the classical sense whatever its direction of travel. Others defended the semi feudal characterisation for the regions of the east, pointing to the interlocking of land, credit, and labour in the person of the landlord moneylender, which prevented the emergence of the free markets in land and labour that capitalism requires. The debate never closed with a victor's formula, and its literature is now read less for its verdicts than for its demonstration that the school's central category could be made operational, turned into survey schedules and arithmetic, and tested against the same countryside it purported to describe. The historians watched the economists' debate with proprietary interest, for it was their own feudalism argument conducted in the present tense, with living informants in place of inscriptions, and it taught the whole tradition a lesson the ancient historians had already learned from the charters: a category is an instrument for organising evidence, and it is graded by the evidence it organises, never by the loyalty it commands.

## The school's second harvest: colonialism and the movement for freedom

The school's demonstration that pre colonial India had been a society in motion carried an unavoidable corollary for the history of the colonial centuries. If the conquered economy had been differentiated, commercial, and changing, then colonialism could not be described, as the older imperial apologetics had described it, as the awakening of a sleeping world. It had to be described as an intervention in a going concern, whose costs and benefits could be assessed against the trajectory the concern was already following. The Indian Marxist historians took up that assessment as their second great subject, and their work on the colonial economy reconnected them, consciously, to the oldest tradition of Indian economic criticism, the drain theory of the early nationalists, rebuilt now with archival tools.

The drain, in the school's restatement, named the mechanism by which India's surplus was transferred to Britain without equivalent return: the home charges, the guaranteed profits of railway capital, the pensions and the manipulated terms of trade by which the colony's export surplus financed the metropolis's global position. Habib's essays on the colonial economy set the drain inside the school's framework: the same countryside whose surplus the Mughal fiscal system had consumed in India was now made to surrender a share abroad, while the colonial state, unlike its predecessors, blocked the responses, of protection, of state sponsored industry, of fiscal autonomy, by which other late developing economies had answered foreign competition. The deindustrialisation debate supplied the argument's hardest evidence. That India's handloom weavers had suffered grievously under the competition of machine made imports was the nationalists' oldest charge; the revisionist challenge, mounted most sharply by the American economic historian Morris D. Morris in the nineteen sixties, held that the decline had been exaggerated, that handloom production had adapted and survived in significant measure, and that the nationalist picture served politics rather than proof. The Indian reply, in work by Amiya Kumar Bagchi and others, met the challenge on the evidence of employment and output in the weaving districts, conceding adaptation at the margins while demonstrating contraction at the core: the spinning of yarn by hand, an occupation of millions of women, had been annihilated by imported twist, and no accounting that omitted it could describe the century honestly. The pattern is by now familiar to the reader of this article: a school's thesis, a serious external challenge, a decade of district level research, and a settlement in which the thesis returns reduced, documented, and harder to kill.

### What did the school add to the argument about colonialism?

It supplied the pre colonial baseline. By demonstrating a differentiated, commercial, and changing economy before the conquest, the school made colonialism measurable as an intervention: the drain and the destruction of hand spinning could be assessed against what the economy had been doing, rather than against an imagined stagnation that made any change an improvement.

The freedom movement itself received the school's treatment in the work of its third generation, of whom Sumit Sarkar, whose Modern India, 1885 to 1947 appeared in 1983, became the most widely read. Sarkar wrote the history of nationalism as social history: the movements from below, the peasants, workers, women, and lower castes whose energies the national leadership alternately summoned and restrained, stood at the centre of his account, and the great set pieces of the national epic were retold from the standpoint of the crowds who made them and were then asked to disperse. His work disputed, from the left, the interpretation associated with the Cambridge school of Indian historians, for whom nationalist politics was essentially the competition of indigenous elites for the spoils of the colonial state, factions pursuing patronage under ideological flags. Against that deflation Sarkar and his colleagues insisted that ideas, sacrifices, and genuine popular aspiration were historical forces, and that a historiography which dissolved the freedom struggle into elite manoeuvre had explained away the very thing that needed explaining. The quarrel was conducted between professionals across three continents, and it fixed the school's international reputation more firmly than any single monograph: the Indian Marxists were the party in the argument who took both the economy and the crowd seriously.

That generation also turned the method on the movement's shadows. The school's historians wrote on communalism as a historical formation rather than an eternal Indian failing, tracing the making of religious majorities and minorities to colonial enumeration, to separate electorates, and to the competition for the colonial state's favours, and they wrote on caste movements and peasant struggles that the national narrative had left at its margins. The effect, over two decades, was to change what counted as the history of modern India in the universities: the social history of the subordinated, which the subaltern collective would soon claim as its discovery, was already being written, in a different idiom and with a different theory of the subject, inside the school's own journals. The border between the school's social history and the subaltern break is therefore less clean than the manifestos of either side suggest, and the honest map shows a contested frontier rather than a wall.

## Property remade by law: the school's colonial countryside

The school's agrarian method, carried into the colonial centuries, produced its account of what British rule did to the Indian village at the level where the school always worked: the level of rights in land. The colonial state, in this account, did not merely tax the countryside more heavily or more regularly than its predecessors. It redefined what land was. The great settlements, the Permanent Settlement of Bengal in 1793, the ryotwari settlements of the south and west, the mahalwari arrangements of the north, each in its own idiom converted the layered, shared, and conditional rights of the old order, in which the king, the zamindar, the village body, and the cultivator all held claims of different kinds on the same field, into a single proprietary right, saleable, mortgageable, and answerable for a revenue demand fixed in cash and enforced by auction. The school's historians traced the consequences through the records the new order generated: the transfer of land by sale and foreclosure from cultivating communities to moneylenders and urban purchasers, the conversion of customary tenants into tenants at will, the rise of a litigating countryside in which the colonial court became an instrument of dispossession more efficient than any army. Guha's early study of the Permanent Settlement, written before his break with the school's framework, supplied the intellectual history of the transformation, the physiocratic dream of an improving landlord class that the Company imagined it was creating; the school's agrarian historians supplied the sequel, in which the created landlords rack rented their tenants and the improving agriculture failed to arrive.

This body of work gave the school's colonial argument its characteristic concreteness, and it distinguishes the school's account from the purely nationalist charge of plunder. The drain was a flow of wealth, measurable in principle and argued from the accounts; the remaking of property was a flow of power, recorded in the settlement reports and the sale deeds, and in the long run the school's historians judged it the deeper wound, because it restructured the village's internal order in ways that independence, by itself, did not reverse. The land question that the school's feudalism thesis had posed for the fifth century, who stands between the cultivator and the state, and on what terms does he take his share, proved to be the land question of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries as well, and the continuity of the question across the school's whole range is the surest evidence that it had identified something structural in Indian history rather than a succession of local accidents.

## The eighteenth century reclaimed

The school's two great constructions met, and were both put to the question, in its treatment of the eighteenth century, the hundred years between the Mughal empire's vigour and the colonial state's consolidation. The inherited account had treated the century as an anarchy, a dark interval of invasion, civil war, and economic collapse between two orders. The Aligarh analysis initially reinforced the gloom from its own side: if the agrarian system had entered crisis under Aurangzeb, the century that followed was the crisis working itself out in political fragmentation. Then the evidence began to accumulate against both versions of the darkness. Historians working on the successor states, on the regional economies of Bengal, Awadh, Maharashtra, and the south, and on the records of Indian and European trade, reconstructed a century of a different character: politically fragmented, certainly, and scarred by war, but commercially active, with banking networks of continental reach, textile production expanding for export, regional courts patronising new capitals, and agrarian frontiers advancing under the successor regimes. The revision posed a genuine problem for the school's constructions. If the empire's fall had not ruined the economy, how much of the crisis model survived? And if the regions were so vigorous in 1750, what exactly did the colonial conquest interrupt?

The school's response, developed across a generation of argument, is a model of its mature manner. The crisis was relocated: what had collapsed was not the economy as such but the imperial system of extraction and redistribution, the vast fiscal pump that had moved the surplus of the provinces to the centre and its armies. The regions inherited the pump's parts, the zamindars, the bankers, the revenue farmers, and ran them at provincial scale with provincial vigour, until the East India Company, capturing the pump in Bengal first, redirected its flow out of the country altogether. On this restatement, the eighteenth century's dynamism and the nineteenth century's drain are not rival stories but successive chapters: the dynamism is precisely what made the drain so productive for its beneficiaries and so costly for its victims, since a stagnant economy could not have sustained a century of tribute. The debate schooled the school's historians in a distinction their earlier work had blurred, between the fate of a state and the fate of an economy, and the distinction propagated backward into the Mughal work and forward into the colonial. It also illustrates, once more, the pattern this article has made its theme. The correction came from evidence assembled by scholars of several persuasions, working the regional archives the grand constructions had neglected; the school's contribution was to absorb the correction into a restated construction rather than to defend the original to the death. A tradition is not refuted by its own revisions. It is refuted only when it stops making them.

## The subaltern objection, and the answer from practice

The most consequential challenge to the school came neither from its epigraphic critics nor from outside the left, but from a breakaway within it. In 1982 a group of younger scholars, gathered around the historian Ranajit Guha and published as the Subaltern Studies collective, issued a manifesto accusing the existing historiography of colonial India, the Marxist school explicitly included, of a failure they named elitism. The charge deserves to be stated at the full strength its authors gave it, because this article's answer is worth nothing if the objection is first weakened for convenience.

The objection ran as follows. The historiography of Indian nationalism, Guha argued in the opening volume of Subaltern Studies in 1982, had been dominated by elitism in two varieties: the colonialist elitism of the imperial historians, for whom the making of India was the work of British rulers and institutions, and the bourgeois nationalist elitism of the national epic, for whom it was the work of the indigenous elite. Marxist historiography, for all its radicalism, had in the subaltern account reproduced the same structure from the other side: its narratives of class, mode of production, and state formation assigned the subordinated groups of Indian society the role of material on which processes worked. The peasant appeared in the school's pages as taxpayer, as rebel in the aggregate, as a quantity in an extraction equation, but rarely as a political subject with a consciousness and a politics of his own. Where the subaltern did act, in revolt, the school too often explained the action as the reflex of economic pressure or the product of elite mobilisation from outside, so that the insurgent's own understanding of his insurgency, his gods, his oaths, his reading of his enemies, dropped out of the explanation. Guha's study of peasant insurgency in colonial India, published in 1983, and his later essay on the prose of counter insurgency, pressed the point into method: the very documents from which the school worked, the reports of officials and the records of repression, were written in a code that converted the rebel's politics into crime and fanaticism, and a historiography that read those documents without decoding them inherited their contempt. The subaltern school proposed instead to recover the subaltern as the maker of his own history, in fragments, against the grain of the archive, with his consciousness treated as a domain of politics that neither landlords nor nationalists nor Marxist structures had authored for him.

### What did the subaltern historians ask that this school had not?

They asked where the subordinated appear as authors rather than as material. The school had asked who held the land, who took the surplus, and how the formation changed; the subaltern historians asked what the peasant insurgent understood his rising to mean, and treated the official record of his defeat as a hostile text requiring decryption.

Stated thus, the objection is the strongest the school ever faced, precisely because it came from within the left and shared the school's own premise that the subordinated are history's real subject. It did not accuse the Marxists of serving the empire or the nation. It accused them of failing, in practice, at the very recovery of the voiceless that their theory promised. And it had the immense tactical advantage of being, about an important part of the school's work, visibly accurate. The Aligarh reconstruction, for all its archival genius, sees the Mughal countryside overwhelmingly through the empire's paperwork: the peasant is measured, assessed, and squeezed in documents written by his extractors, and the inner world of the village, its own account of itself, enters the analysis at a distance. A historiography of structures can go a long way without noticing that its people have become silent, and the subaltern critics noticed.

The answer the school can give, and the one its record supports, operates at the level of practice rather than motive: not by asking what the historians intended, but by examining what each tradition's method actually does with a source. Begin with the founder. Kosambi's whole method was a refusal of the state archive's monopoly. His fieldwork took him to communities whose ancestors appear in no revenue register, and his treatment of their cults, myths, and customs as historical evidence was a deliberate elevation of non elite memory into the rank of source. When he read the epics as the record of tribal peoples colliding and fusing, he was doing, a generation before the manifesto, something the manifesto demanded: treating the traditions of the subordinated as evidence of their history rather than as decoration on the history of kings. His pages on the mother goddesses are pages about the religion of agricultural communities written from their shrines outward, not from the temple texts downward. Whatever the distance between his idiom and theirs, the founder's practice cannot be described as writing about the subaltern from above, because his archive was substantially built from below.

The agrarian wing supplies the second part of the answer. The school's social history of the countryside, from Sharma's work on the labouring orders to Habib's reconstruction of peasant resistance, treated peasants as economic and political actors whose choices, to flee, to withhold, to rise, set the limits within which states operated. The Mughal crisis, on the Aligarh account, is unintelligible without the agency of the peasantry whose flight and revolt broke the assignment system; the cultivator is not the empire's victim in the passive voice but one of the forces that brought the empire down. A later generation of the school's heirs carried the practice further into the history of tribal movements, of caste assertion, and of popular religion, and the boundary between the two schools blurred in the work of scholars who had been formed by both. The subaltern collective itself, in its later volumes, moved toward questions of community, gender, and the colonial state that overlapped with the older school's agenda, while members of the older tradition conceded ground in print and adjusted their practice.

The concession must be entered plainly, since the brief of honest assessment requires it and the evidence permits nothing else. The criticism identifies a real emphasis in the state and revenue tradition: a centre of gravity in the machinery of extraction, a reliance on the records of the powerful, and a corresponding thinness in the treatment of consciousness, of religion as lived conviction rather than as social deposit, and of gender, on which the founding generation had remarkably little to say. The school's women historians and its students of popular culture spent later decades repairing exactly those thinnesses, which is a way of saying the objection worked. A challenge that reforms the practice of the school it attacks has done more for that school than a decade of agreement, and the subaltern break, whatever its own later fortunes, stands in the record as the second great correction this tradition absorbed from critics who had learned their trade inside it.

## The rebel inside the house: Guha before the break

One fact about the subaltern break deepens its significance and is too often omitted from the polemics on both sides: its leader had been formed, and had distinguished himself, inside the very historiography he came to indict. Ranajit Guha's first major work, published in 1963, was a study of the Permanent Settlement of Bengal, the great land settlement of 1793 by which the East India Company had created a class of landlords on the English model in the hope of conjuring an improving agriculture out of a fiscal decree. The book, A Rule of Property for Bengal, was an essay in exactly the school's manner: an agrarian institution analysed through its intellectual origins in physiocratic and utilitarian doctrine and its consequences in the relations of the countryside, property, rent, and the state. It was admired then and is read still as a classic of the economic history of colonialism. The historian who wrote it spent the following two decades moving through the school's questions toward the one the school had not asked: his studies of peasant revolts, undertaken for the insurgency book of 1983, confronted him with documents in which the rebels appeared only as their enemies described them, and the confrontation converted a practitioner of agrarian history into the prosecutor of its archive.

The biography matters because it repeats, at the level of a career, the pattern this article has traced at the level of a tradition. The subaltern critique was not an invasion from outside the school's world; it was the school's own method, the suspicion of the official document, the insistence that the countryside's people are history's subject, turned upon the school's practice by a scholar the school had trained. Guha's charge that the Marxist historians read the prose of counter insurgency without decoding it is the workshop habit of asking what a document does, radicalised: the plate charter's formula and the magistrate's report on a rising are both instruments, and the historian who has learned to strip the boilerplate from the one has been given every tool needed to strip it from the other. That the school's heirs and its rebels should share a formation explains the peculiar intimacy of the quarrel, its mixture of genuine methodological advance and family bitterness, and it explains why the quarrel, unlike the public controversy noted elsewhere in this article, has been so fertile: it was conducted by people who could check one another's evidence, in journals both sides read, under standards both sides acknowledged. The tradition's capacity to generate its own prosecutors, and to be improved by the prosecution, is the quality that separates a research school from a church, and the Indian Marxist historians, whatever verdict is passed on their theses, pass that test in two successive generations: they corrected their founder, and they bred the critics who corrected them.

## The debate continues: the subaltern turn and its discontents

The argument between the school and its subaltern critics did not freeze at the moment of the break, and its later course belongs to the record, since it judges both parties. The Subaltern Studies collective, across its successive volumes, moved from the recovery of peasant insurgency toward the critique of colonial discourse and the categories of the nation state itself, drawing increasingly on the postcolonial theory that was rising in the same decades. The movement produced celebrated work on community, on the fragment as a form of historical writing, and on the colonial state's modes of knowledge. It also attracted, from within the older social history it had broken from, a critique that mirrored its own founding gesture. Sumit Sarkar, whose social history of nationalism had been written from inside the school's tradition, argued in an essay of 1997 that the subaltern project had declined as it rose: that the recovery of the subaltern as an autonomous subject had given way, in the later volumes, to a preoccupation with discourse and representation in which the material subaltern, the labouring poor of town and country, largely disappeared, and that a historiography which had begun by accusing the Marxists of elitism had ended by writing about texts for an audience of metropolitan academics. The charge was contested, sharply, by the collective's defenders, and the controversy renewed, at a higher level, the question the manifesto of 1982 had posed: what does a historiography owe the subordinated, an account of their conditions, an account of their consciousness, or an account of the languages in which both are imprisoned?

The school's historians could watch this quarrel with a certain grim satisfaction, since it reproduced, inside the camp of their critics, the same tension between structure and consciousness that had divided their own house. But the satisfaction would be misplaced if it disguised the permanent gains the break had forced. After Subaltern Studies, no Indian historian of any school could write the peasant back into the passive voice without hearing the objection in the seminar room. The questions of agency, of the archive's bias, of the official document as an interested artefact, entered the common practice of the discipline, and the school's younger practitioners absorbed them as a matter of course, writing agrarian history in which the cultivator's calculations, beliefs, and refusals appear alongside the assessment that pressed on him. Traditions renew themselves by metabolising their critics or they die unmetabolised, and the Indian Marxist school, which had corrected its founder, proved capable of being corrected by its own rebellious students. That capacity, more than any thesis, is the through line of this article, and it is the standard by which the school's achievement will finally be graded: not by the survival of the feudalism thesis or the agrarian crisis model, but by whether the practice of asking material questions of the Indian past, and accepting the archive's answers against one's own side, survived the schools and counter schools that carried it.

## Methodological criticisms inside the discipline

Set aside for a moment the great set pieces, the feudalism debate and the subaltern break, and a quieter catalogue of criticisms remains, made by working historians against the school's habits of mind. These matter because they mark the points at which the method, as practised, required correction by the ordinary standards of the discipline itself.

The first concerns the weight given to economic structure in explanation. Critics within Indian historiography charged that the school, in its weaker practitioners, practised a reflex determinism: assign the period its mode of production, and the politics, religion, and culture of the period could be read off as consequences, with the evidence consulted afterward for illustration. The charge does not fairly describe the founders, whose work is dense with counter instances examined for their own sake, but it describes a real deformation in the school's textbook afterlife, where periodisation hardened into a grid and the grid did the thinking. The repair, undertaken by the school's own best younger scholars, was to restore the founders' actual practice, in which the economic question opened the inquiry rather than closing it.

The second concerns periodisation itself. A scheme of formations imported from European history, however carefully tested, carries a timetable with it, and the critics asked whether the succession ancient, feudal, colonial did not silently set the Indian evidence to a European clock. The feudalism debate was partly this argument conducted under another name. The school's considered answer, visible in its mature practice, was that the formations named durable configurations of property and extraction rather than dates on a universal calendar, and that India would exhibit them in its own sequence and its own combinations, or not at all. Whether the answer fully disposes of the worry is a question on which historians of good faith still divide, and the division is itself a sign that the criticism touched something real.

The third concerns religion and culture as autonomous forces. A historiography that reads myth as social deposit can underrate the degree to which belief moves people on its own account, and the school's treatments of bhakti, of Buddhism, and of popular devotion were taxed, by historians of religion, with translating conviction into function until the conviction disappeared. Kosambi himself, whose readings of the mother goddesses were acts of recovery, was less guilty here than his imitators; the criticism stands against the school's centre of gravity, and the later work on caste and gender, much of it by scholars formed within the tradition, redressed the balance by treating ritual and kinship as structures with their own causal weight.

### Where does the method show its limits?

At the points where its favourite sources run out. The revenue register cannot report what a festival meant; the inscription cannot weigh a belief; the coin cannot testify about kinship. Its critics inside the discipline have been most persuasive when they work at exactly those silences, with sources and methods the founders used too little.

A fourth criticism concerns the institutional consequences of success. A school that trains the syllabus makers and staffs the research councils risks becoming the establishment against which it once rebelled, and younger historians complained, in the manner of younger historians everywhere, that the gatekeeping of appointments, journals, and conference programmes had acquired a doctrinal edge. The complaint is partly the eternal grievance of the rising generation, and partly true in the way such complaints usually are. Its effect, in any case, was healthy: it pushed critics to build alternative venues and pushed the school's heirs to defend their positions by evidence rather than by inheritance. No assessment confined to method can weigh the institutional charge further without sliding into the politics of the academy, which this article has undertaken to keep at the boundary marked out below.

## The school's tribes: the other India in its pages

Kosambi had begun with the tribes, and the school never entirely surrendered them to the anthropologists. The tribal peoples of the hills and forests, a substantial fraction of the subcontinent's population, stood in the school's scheme at the living edge of its founder's great transition: communities still organised by clan and custom, still holding land in forms the caste order had not digested, and therefore both an archive of the past and a population with a present of its own. The school's historians of the tribal world, of whom Kumar Suresh Singh became the most prolific, wrote the history of the tribal movements with the school's characteristic double vision. A rising like that of the Santals in 1855, or of Birsa Munda among the Mundas at the century's end, was read simultaneously as a defence of a communal order against the triple invasion of landlord, moneylender, and colonial official, and as a political creation in its own right, with its prophet's theology and its followers' programme taken seriously as thought. Singh's biography of Birsa, published in 1983, treated the young prophet's claim to divine mission neither as madness nor as disguise but as the idiom in which a dispossessed people formulated its refusal, an idiom whose content, the restoration of the community's land and the expulsion of the outsiders who had taken it, could be stated in the language of political economy without being exhausted by it.

The tribal work mattered to the school's development because it pressed, from a new direction, the question the subaltern manifesto was about to pose. The archive of a tribal rising consists largely of the records of its suppression, the magistrate's reports and the missionaries' letters, and the historians who worked it learned early that the document's contempt had to be read as part of the evidence. They also learned the limits of the founder's survival method from the inside: a community encountered as a survival of the past may resent the description, and its members' own account of their history, as a history of resistance and adaptation rather than of arrested development, corrects the evolutionism embedded in the school's founding metaphor. The school's tribal historiography at its best holds both truths: the clan organisation of a Munda village genuinely does preserve arrangements that illuminate the deep past, and the village that preserves them is a contemporary of the historian's, with claims on the present that no method may subordinate to its value as evidence. The tension was never dissolved, and it stands in the record as the founder's method discovering, in the field, the ethics of its own practice.

## Caste and class: the school's widening agenda

No subject tested the school's categories more persistently than caste, and its work on the caste and class relation forms the bridge to the companion study in this series on the caste debate. Kosambi had set the programme: caste as the historical deposit of tribal absorption, a system of production relations wearing ritual dress. The generations after him complicated the programme in both directions. Suvira Jaiswal, working on the origins and development of social groups, traced how varna had hardened into the caste order as the agrarian economy deepened, and how religious movements, including early Buddhism and the devotional cults, registered the friction of that hardening. Vivekanand Jha pursued the earliest strata of the untouchable communities, reconstructing from texts and inscriptions the process by which groups at the margin of the agrarian order were fixed in hereditary degradation. Habib, in essays on caste in the Mughal and earlier periods, proposed that caste functioned as a division of labour enforced by birth, segmenting the producing population into hereditary compartments whose separation weakened every collective resistance the producers might have mounted, and he held that any account of Indian production which treated caste as a decorative survival had misunderstood the machinery it was describing.

The sharpest internal development came from the scholars who charged the school with having no adequate place for gender. Uma Chakravarti's essays of the nineteen eighties, of which her study of the Vedic dasi, the female slave of the early texts, became the most cited, demonstrated that the control of women's labour and reproduction was not a by product of the caste and class order but one of its load bearing members: caste reproduced itself through the policing of marriage and sexuality, so that a history of production which ignored the household had omitted the site where the labour force itself was made. The criticism was delivered from inside the tradition, by a scholar who described her own work as extending its method to the evidence it had neglected, and the school's practice shifted accordingly: the social history of the following decades, on kinship, on domestic labour, on the gendered division of the village, is unthinkable without the correction. The episode belongs in this article because it repeats, in miniature, the tradition's characteristic motion: a framework strong enough to be turned on its own blind spots, and practitioners willing to do the turning.

## The household enters the account: the gender correction in depth

The gender criticism noted among the school's widening agenda deserves a fuller hearing, because it shows the correction from within operating on the school's most intimate blind spot. The founding generations had written the history of production as if the labour force arrived in the fields and workshops ready made, its reproduction, in the household, by women, costing nothing and deciding nothing. The critics who arose inside the tradition, women scholars trained in its seminars and armed with its questions, demonstrated that the omission was not a gap in coverage but a defect in the model. Caste, the school's own master institution, is reproduced through the control of women's marriages: the endogamy that keeps a caste a caste is enforced on daughters, the purity that ranks it is policed on wives, and the labour that sustains its hereditary occupations is organised in households where women's work is simultaneously indispensable and uncounted. A history that explained caste by production while ignoring the marriage market had explained the institution by everything except its mechanism of survival.

The evidence for the correction was found, characteristically, in the school's own archives read with new questions. The inscriptions record women as donors in numbers the older readings had passed over: queens and merchants' wives endowing temples, nuns and laywomen funding monasteries in the Buddhist centuries, the gifts small and large marking a real, if constrained, command over property. The normative texts, read for their anxieties rather than their rules, reveal the tightening of control as the agrarian order deepened, the lowering of the marriage age, the hardening of widowhood's penalties, the conversion of the wife's labour and fertility into the household's assets under the husband's lineage. Kumkum Roy's work on the early Indian household and monarchy, and Chakravarti's on the dasi and on the making of Brahmanical patriarchy, rebuilt the early centuries with the household restored to its place as a site of production and power, and the rebuilt account changed the meaning of the school's older findings: the tribe absorbed into caste was absorbed through its women as much as through its fields, and the goddess the founder had chased through the Deccan hills was the cult of communities whose descent, in many cases, still ran through the mother. The founder had glimpsed that connection, in his treatment of mother right among the survivals, and the feminist historians of the school were recovering, with better evidence and a harder theory, a road he had marked and his institution building successors had neglected to travel. The episode completes the pattern this article has followed from its opening pages. The school's framework, in the hands of practitioners it had trained, corrected the school's own practice on the school's own evidence, and the correction is now part of what the tradition means by its method.

## The Aligarh argument in the world's great divergence

The international repair this article has called for began, in the event, through a debate the school did not start. At the turn of the twenty first century, economic historians of the California school, Kenneth Pomeranz most prominently, reopened the question of the great divergence: why industrialisation had occurred first in northwestern Europe rather than in the comparably commercialised regions of Asia. The debate ran on evidence, and the evidence for India ran, to a degree the participants freely acknowledged, through the school's workshops. The Mughal economy's magnitudes, its revenue demand, its urbanisation, the condition of its textile industry and the structure of its agrarian relations, entered the global argument in the form the Aligarh historians and their critics had established: Habib's agrarian system and his potentialities essay, Moosvi's statistics, the revisionists' work on trade and silver, the whole contested shelf. A Californian argument about the Yangzi delta and Lancashire thus rested in part on registers read at Aligarh, and the school's categories, extraction, assignment, the blocked transition, were suddenly load bearing in the most discussed controversy in world economic history. The episode did not make the school's members into Californians or its theses into the debate's conclusions; the divergence literature used the Indian evidence for its own purposes and quarrelled with the Aligarh crisis model where it needed growth instead. But it demonstrated, in the most public forum the discipline possesses, that the Indian school's work had become part of the world's stock of established inquiry, citable by scholars who shared neither its doctrine nor its country. The correction from within had completed its circuit: the tradition that began by correcting Marx's picture of India with India's archives ended by supplying the archives from which the world corrected its picture of itself.

## The school among the world's historiographies

Place the Indian school beside its contemporaries abroad and its distinctiveness sharpens. The British Marxist historians, the generation of Christopher Hill, Eric Hobsbawm, and E. P. Thompson, worked on a national past whose archives were centralised, whose languages were their own, and whose great questions, the making of capitalism and of the working class, were posed by a society that had industrialised first. The Indian historians worked on a past recorded in a dozen scripts and languages, in inscriptions on stone and copper, in the paperwork of Persianate states and the oral memory of villages, and their great question was posed in reverse: not how capitalism had been made, but why a civilisation of comparable sophistication had not made it, and what had been done to the attempt by conquest. Where the British school produced the classic literatures of class formation and popular protest, the Indian school produced the classic literatures of land, revenue, and the state, because those were the levers by which Indian society had actually been moved. The comparison is not a competition. It is a demonstration that historical materialism, transplanted, grew into the shape of the soil that received it, which is what the founder's insistence on Indian evidence over imported stages had always promised.

The parallels with the French Annales tradition are equally instructive, and equally a matter of independent convergence more than of influence. Kosambi's programme of the nineteen fifties, with its attention to geography, to material life, to the slow time of custom and technique, resembles the Annales agenda enough to have struck every comparativist who has set them side by side, yet it was formed from Indian materials and Indian polemics, from the need to answer a colonial historiography that the French never faced. The Indian school's advantage over both its European cousins was its fieldworker founder's bequest: no British or French Marxist of the founding generations made the living village, its gods and its marriage rules, into a primary archive of the distant past. Its corresponding disadvantage was insularity: publishing at home, examining at home, and arguing at home, the school let the international conversation proceed for decades as if India had produced no Marxist historiography worth the name, an omission the world's survey literature has only partially repaired. The repair, when it comes, will not be an act of charity. A historiography that corrected Marx on the society he wrote about most confidently, that tested feudalism on a second civilisation, and that counted an empire's economy from its own registers, belongs to the international history of the method itself, and the survey that omits it has omitted one of the method's few unambiguous triumphs of evidence over inheritance.

### How did the Indian school differ from its British counterpart?

In its archives and therefore in its questions. The British historians worked from centralised records in their own language on a society industrialising first, and asked how classes were made. The Indian historians worked from inscriptions, coins, Persian registers, and fieldwork on a society conquered before it could industrialise, and asked how land, revenue, and the state moved it.

## The survey and the classroom: how the school reached its readers

A school's influence is measured not only by its monographs but by its genres, and the Indian school commanded two genres of unusual reach. The first was the survey: the single volume history of India, or of its ancient or medieval periods, written for students and general readers, in which the school's findings were compressed into a narrative that millions actually read. Romila Thapar's history of India for the Penguin series, published in 1966, carried the early period for two generations of readers in a prose that showed how the new questions could be asked without the apparatus of the monograph, and Sharma's textbooks performed the same office for the universities and schools of the republic, his Ancient India becoming, in its various editions and translations, the account of the early past from which the country's undergraduates learned what the questions were. The survey is an unforgiving form: it permits no footnoted evasions, and every periodisation must be committed to in plain sentences. The school's willingness to write surveys, and its success in them, explains the depth of its penetration: the undergraduate who never read the Agrarian System nonetheless learned the Mughal centuries as an agrarian system, from textbooks whose authors had.

The second genre was the presidential address and the programmatic essay, in which the school's seniors periodically stated where the discipline stood and where it should go. Delivered at the History Congress or published in the journals, these addresses functioned as the school's public philosophy of itself, and they record its changing preoccupations with unusual fidelity: the early confidence that the economic reading would dissolve the old narratives, the middle period's turn to region and to evidence, the later reckonings with the critics. A reader who wants the school's autobiography should read its addresses in sequence, for they show a tradition thinking about itself in public, a habit its critics, of every persuasion, have rarely matched.

## Potsherds and poems: the epic at the excavator's trench

The school's discipline about evidence can be watched at its most exposed in the controversy over the epics and the spade. The Mahabharata names places that exist, and the excavator B. B. Lal, digging at Hastinapura in the nineteen fifties, identified the Painted Grey Ware culture of the early iron age with the world of the epic, proposing that archaeology had found the material remains of the poem's civilisation. The identification delighted the public and entered the textbooks of popular belief. The school's response was characteristic and, to its critics, characteristically cold: a pottery style is not a poem, a stratum is not a war, and the correlation of a ceramic horizon with a text whose composition spanned centuries is a hypothesis about both that must be argued from independent evidence rather than assumed from the glamour of the match. Kosambi's own treatment of the epics, reading them as stratified records of tribal society in transformation, offered the alternative the school preferred: the poem as a document of the long process by which clan became kingdom, its layers separable by internal evidence, its gods and heroes carrying the marks of the peoples absorbed into its making. The quarrel was never only about pottery. It was about what kind of satisfaction history is permitted to give: the satisfaction of recognition, in which the past confirms the stories a civilisation tells itself, or the satisfaction of explanation, in which the stories themselves become the things to be explained. The school chose explanation, at the price of a popularity it never regained in the quarters where recognition was demanded, and the choice, made early and held consistently, is the deepest source both of its authority among professionals and of the public suspicion in which it is held in some quarters. This article, confined to the discipline's internal assessment, records the choice and its grounds without entering the larger quarrel.

## The archive the empire catalogued: epigraphy and the turning of the record

The school's evidence had a history of its own, and the history contains an irony the school's historians savoured. The inscriptions on which the feudalism thesis and the ancient history were built had been collected, copied, and catalogued, in large part, by the scholarly apparatus of the colonial state: the archaeological survey founded in the nineteenth century, the epigraphists who toured the districts taking estampages of stones in temple walls and village tanks, the great series of Epigraphia Indica in which the texts were published with translations. The empire catalogued the record in order to know its possession, and filed the charters of a thousand forgotten kings in the offices of its survey. Two generations later, the school took down the files and used them to write the history that demolished the empire's own account of India. The copper plate that a district officer's epigraphist had copied as a curiosity became, in Sharma's hands, the conveyance that proved a formation; the coin cabinet assembled by the survey's collectors became, in Kosambi's, the statistical series that charted the rise and retreat of exchange. There is a lesson in the succession that the school's method generalises: an archive does not dictate its interpretation, and the same documents, read under new questions, will support a history their collectors could not have imagined and would not have welcomed.

The survival of the record shaped the school's questions in ways its practitioners learned to state openly. India keeps its early documents disproportionately in durable media and religious custody: what was carved on stone or copper, and what temples and monasteries preserved, survived, while the perishable archive of palm leaf and paper, the merchant's ledger and the village accountant's roll, largely rotted in the climate that preserved the charters. The school's history is therefore richest exactly where the durable record is richest, in grants, in temples, in the fiscal acts of states, and thinnest where the record perished, in the daily economy of the household and the market, a bias the school's critics exploited and its honest practitioners confessed. Habib's Mughal work enjoyed the opposite fortune: an empire of paper, whose registers survived in quantities that let the historian count, and the contrast between the two evidentiary worlds, stone for the ancient centuries, paper for the Mughal, explains much about why the school's ancient history is a history of institutions inferred from conveyances while its Mughal history is a history of magnitudes computed from accounts. Method follows the archive, and the school's sophistication consisted in knowing it: in refusing to mistake the record's silences for the past's, and in designing, for each period, the questions its surviving evidence could actually answer. The founder's fieldwork was, in this light, an attempt to manufacture an archive where none had survived, to make the living countryside testify about the centuries whose paper had rotted, and the risks of that testimony, itemised in this article's account of the survival method, are the price of asking the early centuries questions worthy of the later ones.

## Many languages, one past: the school's apprenticeship

The school's method imposed an apprenticeship of a severity that deserves record, because it explains both the school's slowness to produce its classics and the authority they carried when they came. The historian of ancient India in this tradition was expected to command Sanskrit well enough to read the charters and the normative texts against the grain, to handle the epigraphic scripts in their regional and chronological variety, and to know the material record, coins, pottery, settlement patterns, as something more than illustration. The historian of the medieval centuries added Persian, the language of the Mughal archive, and the specialised vocabularies of its revenue manuals, whose technical terms had to be reconstructed from usage before the registers that used them could be trusted. Kosambi, who had begun the whole enterprise, added fieldwork in the regional language and a walking knowledge of the countryside's cults and clans. No single recruit commanded the whole armoury, and the school's characteristic unit of production was consequently not the solitary genius but the corridor: the department in which the epigraphist, the numismatist, the reader of Persian, and the fieldworker argued across one another's drafts until the formation they were describing had been tested from every side the evidence allowed. Aligarh's medieval seminar and Delhi's ancient history department, in their strong decades, were such corridors, and the festschriften and conference volumes in which their members published preserve the sound of the argument.

The apprenticeship also explains the school's distinctive relationship to translation. Its classics appeared in English, the republic's scholarly lingua franca, but their evidence moved constantly among languages, and a generation of its students learned their history in classrooms where a single afternoon might pass from a Sanskrit charter to a Persian register to a Maratha account book. The multilingual archive was the school's quiet refutation of every simple story about the Indian past, including some of its own: a civilisation that had kept its records in a dozen tongues, under Hindu, Muslim, and colonial sovereignties, in genres from the temple inscription to the tax manual, could not be the possession of any single community's memory, and the historian trained to read across the archive belonged, by professional deformation, to all of it and to none. Later polemics, of the kind this article notes without entering, have sometimes described the school as the custodian of a partisan past. The apprenticeship tells a different story about its formation: it was a machine for producing historians who owed their first loyalty to documents that no party had written for their benefit.

## The workshop: inscriptions, coins, and the historian's craft

A school's method is finally a set of workshop habits, and the Indian school's habits repay description because they explain both its findings and its confidence. Its first habit was epigraphic discipline. The copper plate charter, the school's characteristic document, is a legal instrument in a formulaic language, full of conventional phrases that a careless reader mistakes for facts and a careful one learns to strip away, until the operative clauses stand out: the boundaries of the granted village, the dues remitted, the immunities conceded, the witnesses named. Generations of the school's students learned their trade by editing such plates, and the habit of asking what a document does, as distinct from what it says, passed from the epigraphy seminar into the reading of chronicles, of court poetry, and eventually of the colonial archive itself, where the subaltern critics would later wield it with such effect. Its second habit was serial thinking: the conviction that a single coin, charter, or price proves nothing, while a series, counted, weighed, and plotted across centuries, can overturn a library of impressions. The school's characteristic graphs and tables, of coin weights, of assessment rates, of inscription counts by century, were not decorations on its arguments but the arguments themselves in their most honest form. Its third habit was the seminar's adversarial courtesy: the History Congress paper read to a hall of specialists who had handled the same evidence, the journal controversy pursued across a decade of issues, the festschrift in which a scholar's students honoured him by refuting his favourite thesis in his presence. Institutions of that kind are the unglamorous machinery by which a school corrects itself, and their strength in India, for the two generations this article covers, is the deepest reason the corrections described here came so often from within.

## A public controversy, noted and not entered

Beyond the discipline, in Indian public argument, there exists a politically motivated critique of this historiography which characterises the school as ideologically driven and its account of the national past as a distortion produced by commitment rather than by evidence. The critique circulates in public controversy, is attached in the public mind to the school's institutional eminence, and forms part of the environment in which its books are read and its syllabi contested. In accordance with the discipline this article has set itself, the existence and general character of that critique are noted here, and nothing further: no organisation, publication, or person associated with it is named, no position in the controversy is taken, and the assessment offered in these pages is confined, from first to last, to criticisms of method made within the discipline of history itself. The separation is not a courtesy. A methodological criticism can be answered from the archive, by producing the inscription or reweighing the coins; a political charge is answered from a platform, and the school's record, whatever else is said of it, was built in the archive. Readers who wish to grade the school are invited to do what its own critics at their best have done: take down the monographs and check the evidence.

## The school's teachers: institutions, lineages, and the making of historians

Behind the books stands a fact the books rarely state: the school was built by teachers. Its method reached India through lecture rooms and supervision as much as through print, and its lineage runs from one generation's seminar to the next with a regularity that the publication record alone does not explain. Romila Thapar was formed in the India of the nineteen fifties, in the orbit of the older nationalist scholarship and of the ancient history being written in London; Irfan Habib was formed at Aligarh, in a department his own family had helped to build, and completed a training at Oxford that joined Persian documentation to the European historiographical debates. The two formations, different in every detail, converged on the same demand: that Indian history be argued from its records, in the company of the world's scholarship and against the pieties of every camp.

The institutional setting mattered to the method. A university department is a machine for continuity: it appoints successors, sets papers, examines theses, and keeps a school's questions in circulation long after its founders have left. Aligarh performed that office for the Mughal centuries, building the documentary base through generations of students under teachers who edited the texts they studied. Delhi and, later, the Centre for Historical Studies at Jawaharlal Nehru University performed it for the ancient and medieval centuries, where Thapar's seminars and Jha's teaching carried the questions of formation and evidence into cohorts that had no personal memory of the founders. The school's survival across the century is partly an intellectual story and partly this institutional one: its questions were examined, literally, every year.

Teaching also disciplined the prose. The school's characteristic genres, the survey, the textbook, the examination text, were written for students who would test them in argument, and the compression they imposed is visible throughout the tradition: periodisation stated plainly, evidence cited economically, counterarguments acknowledged in a sentence before being answered. A historiography that must be taught becomes a historiography that can be learned, and the school's famous accessibility, its conquest of the undergraduate syllabus across the country, was not an accident of marketing but the direct product of its classroom life. The founders wrote as men and women who expected to be argued with by their own students the following morning.

The table also performs a service for the reader meeting these names for the first time: it shows that the school's internal critics, Mukhia above all, belong inside the tradition they criticised, trained in its questions and armed with its evidence. A school is healthiest when its sharpest examination is set by its own graduates, and by that test the Indian school, for two generations, was very healthy indeed.

## The school in the vernacular: translations and the regional seminar

The school's books were written in English, but its influence travelled in the republic's many languages, and the journey changed both the books and the destinations. Sharma's surveys were translated into Hindi and the major regional languages by the national textbook and translation programmes, carrying the feudalism thesis and the agrarian reading of the Mughals into classrooms whose students would never open the English monographs. Kosambi was read in Marathi in his own state with the pride reserved for a local genius, and his essays circulated in the little magazines of the Marathi left in versions their author, a perfectionist in two languages, supervised with characteristic ferocity. In Bengal, a historiographical culture with its own long Marxist tradition absorbed the Delhi and Aligarh arguments into a Bengali literature of agrarian and labour history that answered them from the delta's very different evidence. The vernacular career matters to the assessment because it explains the school's peculiar public position in India: its categories became the common sense of the educated lay reader, absorbed through textbooks, translations, and the examination system, so that the school's India, of land grants and revenue systems and formations succeeding one another, is the India that generations of civil servants, journalists, and teachers carry in their heads, including many who would describe their own views as anything but Marxist. A historiography that has formed the mental furniture of a governing class has achieved an influence no citation count records, and the achievement cuts both ways, since common sense is where research programmes go to stop being questioned; the school's later internal critics, the gender historians and the regional revisionists, were obliged to argue not only with the monographs but with the textbook certainties the monographs had hardened into, a labour their elders had performed, in their turn, against the colonial classroom.

## Against the golden age: periodisation as the school's quiet revolution

The school's most pervasive victory was won in the table of contents. The periodisation it inherited divided the Indian past by the religion of its rulers: a Hindu period, a Muslim period, and a British period, the scheme that James Mill had imposed in his History of British India at the start of the nineteenth century and that the colonial classroom had taught ever since. The scheme carried its judgment inside itself: the first age golden, the second a long despotism, the third a rescue, and it taught every schoolchild to read a thousand years of agrarian and artistic life as the epilogue to a religious war. The Marxist school abolished the scheme in its own practice and offered the discipline another: periods named for their social formations and their material orders, ancient, early medieval, medieval, colonial, in which a Sultanate and a Rajput kingdom of the same centuries belong to one period because they shared a configuration of land and extraction, while two dynasties of the same faith may fall on opposite sides of a boundary because their worlds differed. The replacement is so complete that young historians now find the older scheme barely intelligible, and it is worth pausing over, because periodisation is the point at which a historiography's theory becomes invisible common sense. The school's formations may be argued with, and this article has recorded the arguments at length; the proposition that Indian time should be organised by its social and economic configurations rather than by its kings' religions is no longer argued with at all.

The golden age received the same treatment at the school's hands. The Gupta centuries had been gilded by the older nationalist historiography into a classical perfection of art, literature, and mild government, a Hindu renaissance against which all later ages were measured and found wanting. The school's ancient historians, Sharma above all, did not deny the age its Kalidasa or its sculpture. They asked the school's question of it: on what agrarian base did the golden age rest, and who paid for the gold? The answer, assembled from the charters of the age itself, was the beginning of the feudal conveyance, the first great multiplication of land grants, the first immunities, the first transfers of villages with their people, so that the classical efflorescence and the subjection of the peasantry advanced, in the school's reading, as two faces of one process: the surplus that endowed the temples and rewarded the poets was being organised, in the same decades, by a machinery that bound the cultivator more tightly to the soil and to his new lords. The reading scandalised the devotees of the golden age and delighted the school's students, and its methodological point survives every quarrel about its details: a civilisation's cultural height is a fact about its surplus, and the historian who admires the height without auditing the extraction has written the appreciation of an art collection rather than the history of a society.

### Why did the school refuse to divide the past by the religion of its rulers?

Because the division explained nothing it claimed to explain. Dynasties of different faiths ran recognisably similar agrarian and fiscal orders, while dynasties of one faith presided over formations as different as the early historic cities and the feudal countryside. Periods named for formations grouped the evidence by the relations that moved it, not by a court's creed.

## The Maratha test: a polity read through its revenue

The school's method faced a convenient examination in the Maratha polity, the power that rose in the western Deccan in the seventeenth century and contested the subcontinent with the Mughals and then with the British. The older historiography had read the Marathas through the lens of national and religious awakening: a people's king, a holy war, a nation's first bid for freedom. The school's historians read the same polity through its revenue papers, and the polity that emerged from the papers was stranger and more interesting than the epic version. The Maratha state at its height drew its income from a double system: the settled territory administered through hereditary local officers and revenue assignments not unlike the Sultanate's and the Mughals', and the great fiscal expedients of chauth and sardeshmukhi, the quarter and the tenth levied on the revenues of territories the Marathas did not administer but could raid, protection money systematised into a constitution. The school asked its usual questions of this arrangement. Who bore the cost of the Maratha military machine, and the answer, from the records of the Deccan villages, was the peasantry of three empires at once, assessed by its own rulers, tithed by the Maratha claim, and plundered when the claims conflicted. How did the polity hold together, and the answer lay in the assignment of its revenues to its commanders and the hereditary grip of its local officer families, a saranjam order whose resemblance to the feudal conveyances of a millennium earlier the school's historians noted with the satisfaction of a theory finding its echo. What did the polity's ideology do, and here the school conceded the epic its force while denying it explanatory priority: the language of Hindu kingship and righteous resistance mobilised the Deccan's castes and clans, and the mobilisation was real, but the machine it powered was a fiscal and military one whose behaviour, in expansion and in collapse, followed the logic of its revenues rather than of its slogans.

The Maratha case mattered to the school beyond its intrinsic fascination, because it lay on the border between the school's two empires of evidence, the medieval and the colonial, and because the national epic had made it the property of a rival historiography. To reread the bakhars, the Maratha chronicles, against the revenue records, to treat the hero's court as a court, with accountants, was to demonstrate that the method feared no subject, not even the one wrapped in the strongest patriotic sentiment the country possessed. The demonstration was not received with universal gratitude, and the school's Maratha writings were taxed, in the quarters where the epic reigned, with the coldness this article has met before, in the case of the epics and the spade. The pattern of the reception is itself instructive. The school's offences were rarely errors of fact that its critics could correct; they were offences of temperature, the refusal to warm the past to the temperature the present demanded. A historiography is entitled to choose its temperature, and the school's choice, sustained across gods, epics, golden ages, and national heroes, is the single thread on which all its controversies hang.

## The census and the reinvention of caste

The school's historical account of caste had a colonial chapter that its later students developed into one of its most influential arguments. If caste was a historical formation, made by the absorption of tribes and the organisation of production, then it could also be remade, and the colonial state, the school argued, had remade it in the act of counting it. The decennial census, with its demand that every Indian state a single caste and its early attempts to rank the castes in order of precedence, converted a fluid and locally variable order of occupations and statuses into an enumerated grid of fixed identities, certified by the state and armed with administrative consequences. Communities petitioned the census commissioners for higher rank in their thousands, armed with new genealogies and reformed customs, so that the counting itself became an engine of the very caste consciousness it claimed merely to record. The school's historians did not invent this argument unaided, and its later elaboration, in the work of historians of colonial knowledge such as Bernard Cohn and Nicholas Dirks, carried it further than the school's own treatments had done. But the premise was the school's: caste had a history, the colonial state was part of that history, and the apparently timeless grid of the ethnographic surveys was, like the unchanging village, a product of the archive that described it. The argument completed the school's long campaign against the timeless India, by showing that even the institution most often exhibited as timelessness itself had been visibly re manufactured within living memory, under the eyes of the enumerators.

## The regional turn: the school's grandchildren rewrite the map

The school's third generation inherited a national narrative and proceeded, in the manner of the tradition, to correct it from the evidence of the parts. The grand constructions had been built disproportionately on the records of the north: the Ganges valley's inscriptions, the Mughal heartland's registers, the Bengal delta's colonial archive. From the nineteen eighties, historians trained in the school's methods but posted to the universities of the regions turned the instruments on their own pasts, and the map of Indian history changed under their hands. The Deccan acquired an agrarian history of its dry districts and its irrigation tanks that owed nothing to the Gangetic model of village and grant. The far south's early history was rewritten around the tinai landscapes and the assembly inscriptions by scholars who treated the Sanskritising narrative as one strand in a Tamil story rather than as the story itself. The northeast, the tribal belt, the coastal trading worlds of the west and the east, each received histories in which the national formations appeared as pressures arriving from outside a local logic that had to be reconstructed first. The cumulative effect was not the dissolution of the school's framework but its federalisation: the formations survived as questions, while the answers multiplied by region, and the all India synthesis, the genre the founders had loved, became correspondingly harder to write with a clear conscience.

This development deserves the last word in the article's survey of the school's career, because it represents the tradition's method outliving the tradition's conclusions in the most complete form. The regional historians who complicated the national story were not, for the most part, the school's enemies. They were its grandchildren, formed in its seminars, citing its classics, and applying its founding instruction, that the evidence of one's own ground outranks the scheme inherited from the metropolis, to the scheme's own Indian metropolis in Delhi and Aligarh. Kosambi had told the Indian historians not to trim the Indian evidence to fit a European theory. His intellectual grandchildren declined, in the same spirit, to trim the evidence of Karnataka or Assam to fit a theory built on the Ganges. The school's history thus ends, provisionally, where its founder would have wished: not with a canon defended, but with a method escaped from its canon, walking the districts with a notebook, asking after the holders of land, the takers of surplus, and what the goddess at the field's edge remembers of both.

## The unfinished question: the transition that did not happen

Running beneath every construction this article has surveyed is a single question the school never finished answering, and an honest assessment should end by naming it, because it organised the school's work from the founder's first sketch to the mode of production debate of the nineteen seventies. The question is why India, with its ancient markets, its skilled artisans, its merchant capital, and its sophisticated states, did not generate from within itself the transition to a capitalist organisation of production that its resources appeared to promise. Each of the school's classics is a partial answer addressed to one period's version of the blockage. Kosambi's feudalism from above named the early medieval diversion of the surplus into a hierarchy of intermediaries. Sharma's thesis described the closing of the agrarian order around the dependent village. Habib's potentialities essay indicted the Mughal fiscal structure, which consumed the surplus in military and courtly expenditure and gave its assignees no reason to invest. The colonial historians added the drain and the policy of the conquering state, which blocked the responses, of protection and public investment, by which other societies had forced the transition. And the participants in the agricultural debate of the seventies inherited the question in its contemporary form, asking whether the transition was at last under way in the independent republic's countryside or whether the old blockages had reassembled themselves in new institutional dress.

The question's unfinished state is not a scandal, and the school's own methodological conscience explains why it must remain partly open. A transition that did not happen has no archive of its own; its evidence is the pattern of near misses, the merchant capital that stopped at the workshop door, the fiscal pump that consumed what industry required, the market that flourished without reorganising production, and each generation's reconstruction of the pattern depends on the evidence its period has yielded and the comparisons its moment makes available. What the school bequeathed was not an answer but the question in its disciplined form, together with the demonstration that the question could be asked of every period with the same instruments and answered, for each period, from that period's documents. Foreign readers who meet the school through this article should understand that its classics are stations on that single inquiry, and that the inquiry's persistence, across ancient charters, Mughal registers, colonial sale deeds, and the survey schedules of the nineteen seventies, is the deepest unity beneath the school's famous disagreements. The historians quarrelled about formations because they agreed about the question, and the question, what does a society do with its surplus, and what does the doing make impossible, is as good a definition as this article can offer of the tradition they built.

## A balance sheet: what the school established and what it did not

The assessment this article has been building can now be drawn together, in the manner of the school's own ledgers, with the credits and the debits entered in the same hand. On the credit side stand five achievements that no subsequent historiography has reversed. The school established that the Indian past is a history of change in production and property, recoverable from evidence, and thereby abolished the unchanging India of the colonial account as a scholarly option. It established the land grant and the revenue assignment as the master institutions of the pre colonial state, and gave the discipline the documentary techniques to study them. It established the standard reading of the Mughal agrarian system, against which all later work, including the work that revises it, defines itself. It established, internationally, the standing counterexample to the founder's own geography of history: a Marxist historiography that used the method to correct the master's picture of the East. And it established a professional ethic, of evidence multiplied across archives and conclusions held open to the archive's reply, that survived the fate of its individual theses and formed the conscience of the discipline's next two generations, including the generation that rebelled against it.

One further credit belongs to the manner rather than the matter. The school wrote, at its best, a prose that a general reader could follow into technical country: Kosambi's aphorisms, Sharma's patient accumulations, Habib's registers unfolding into narrative, taught two generations of Indian students that economic history need not be a refuge from literature. The readability was not decoration. A historiography that aspired to displace the national epic had to be readable where the epic was read, in the classroom and the general library, and the school's surveys and textbooks conquered that ground precisely because their authors wrote as if the past were interesting, which, in their hands, it was. Later academic fashions, in India as elsewhere, retreated into a technical idiom that surrendered the general reader to the very mythologies the school had fought, and the loss is measurable in the public controversies this article has declined to enter: a discipline that will not narrate its findings accessibly leaves the narration of the past to those who will.

The debit side must be entered with equal plainness. The school's strong constructions repeatedly outran their regional evidence, and its central theses, feudal decline, agrarian crisis, stagnation averted only by conquest, each required the succeeding decades to cut them down to the size the documents would bear. Its centre of gravity in the state's records left consciousness, gender, and the inner life of religion underweight in its accounts until its own students and rebels forced the correction. Its periodisations carried a European timetable inside them even when its practitioners were most vigilant, and the vigilance itself consumed energy that a scheme grown from Indian evidence alone might have spent elsewhere. And its institutional success carried the ordinary corruptions of success: gatekeeping, syllabus ossification in its weaker classrooms, and a confidence, in its polemical moods, that the argument had been settled when it had only been well begun. A balance sheet of this shape, large credits honestly come by, large debits honestly recorded, is not the profile of a school that failed. It is the profile of a school that worked, in the only sense the discipline recognises: it changed what could be said, what had to be proved, and what counted as a question, and its errors were of the fertile kind that organise the labour of its correctors.

## Where to start with these historians

Two cautions belong with the route. The first is that the school's books reward being read in their controversies rather than in isolation: Sharma without Mukhia is a conclusion without its cross examination, and Habib without his eighteenth century critics is a system without its stress test, so the newcomer should budget for the rejoinders as part of the text and not as an optional sequel. The second is that the school's translations and textbook versions, through which most Indian readers meet it, compress the arguments and sometimes harden them; the student who finds a thesis stated in a survey with a confidence the monograph does not share has found the difference between the school's research frontier and its classroom sediment, and should follow the footnote back to the monograph before quoting either. A third resource deserves mention for the reader whose interest is the method itself: the school's programmatic addresses and review essays, scattered through the journals and the History Congress proceedings, in which its seniors periodically audited the discipline's progress and named its next problems. Read in sequence, they form the school's intellectual autobiography, and they teach the lesson this article has pressed throughout, that the tradition's health lay in the publicity of its self correction. The reader who has worked through the monographs, the controversies, and the addresses possesses the school as its own members possessed it: not as a set of answers to be believed, but as a standing invitation to take a category into the archive and see whether it comes back alive.

The reading order that serves a newcomer follows the argument's own order. Begin with the founder's short outline of ancient India, where the method appears in its most compressed and readable form, and pass from it to the larger Introduction for the method stated at full stretch, footnotes and all. For the feudalism thesis, read the 1965 book beside its challenger: the 1981 essay that asked whether feudalism existed in India at all, and the rejoinder of 1985, make a triptych that teaches more about historical method than any summary, including this one. For the Mughal order, the Agrarian System of 1963 remains the door, heavy but unavoidable, with the Atlas as its visual companion. The student of examinations will find the field gathered and placed in the series guide to [Marxism in India: the complete guide](/2017/12/01/marxism-in-india-complete-guide/), and its assessment form treated in the [Marxism in India exam answers](/2018/04/15/marxism-in-india-exam-answers/), while the school's own self reference stands at [Indian Marxist historians](/2018/03/01/indian-marxist-historians/) for the comparative historiography of the tradition. What the newcomer should not do is begin with the polemics, friendly or hostile. The polemics are about the school; the monographs are the school, and they repay the slower road.

## For students and exam candidates

For the student, this school is not an optional ornament on the syllabus but one of its load bearing topics, examined wherever Indian history and Indian political thought are assessed. Prepare it as an argument rather than as a list of names. ## For teachers and seminar leaders

The richest teaching asset in this article is the founder's method, which offers the classroom a complete example of interdisciplinary history in a single career. Set students the Kosambi problem directly: give them a custom, a coin series, and a text, and require an account of a society that uses all three, with an explicit statement of what would count as evidence against the account. The feudalism debate then teaches the second lesson, the life of a category: distribute the thesis, the 1981 challenge, and the 1985 rejoinder across a seminar, and let the group discover that the participants share the evidence and divide on what a category is for. Close with the correction from within, and ask the question that transfers beyond this topic: what would it take for a tradition you belong to to correct its founder, and does it possess the machinery, of journals, seminars, and standards of proof, that the correction requires? Teachers who want the comparative frame should pair this school with the British Marxist historians, whose different archive produced a different Marxism, and let the comparison carry the lesson that methods grow in the shape of the evidence they are raised on.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

For the researcher, the open files are identifiable and attractive. The feudalism settlement awaits its regional completions: district level studies, on the Chattopadhyaya model, that test the grant economy against local archives still unexploited in the record offices. The Aligarh data invite reworking with the methods of the digital archive, as the inscriptional corpora and the coin series become machine readable and the school's serial arguments can be rerun at a scale its founders could not attempt. The correction to Marx deserves a fuller documentary treatment than it has received, setting the school's monographs beside the colonial sources, Bernier, the parliamentary reports, the settlement literature, from which the master's India was built; the companion study of [Marx's colonial writings and their critics](/2017/03/01/marx-on-colonialism-debate/) states the textual case, and the methodological quarrel with the postcolonial reading is pursued in [the comparison with postcolonial theory](/2017/05/15/marxism-vs-postcolonial-theory/). Journalists covering the public controversy over textbooks and syllabuses will serve their readers best by holding the distinction this article has maintained: the methodological criticisms, which are checkable in the archive, are the story with a professional ending; the political charge belongs to a different genre of report, and confusing the two misinforms whichever side the reader is on.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who are the Indian Marxist historians?

They are the scholars who, from the nineteen fifties onward, applied historical materialism to the Indian past: D. D. Kosambi as founder, R. S. Sharma on the ancient and early medieval centuries, Irfan Habib and the Aligarh school on the Mughal order, and the students, rivals, and internal critics those three produced. What unites them is not a set of conclusions but a discipline of questioning: begin from how production is organised, how land is held, and how the surplus is taken, and settle every answer from the inscriptions, coins, registers, and field evidence. They disagreed about feudalism, about slavery, about the weight of trade, and about one another, and the disagreement, conducted in the Indian History Congress and the journals, is part of what the name denotes.

### Q: Who was D.D. Kosambi?

Damodar Dharmananda Kosambi, who lived from 1907 to 1966, was a mathematician by training, educated at Harvard, who taught at Fergusson College in Pune and later worked at the Tata research institute in Bombay, while building a second career that founded a school of Indian history. His Introduction of 1956 defined history through the development, in succession, of the productive means and the relations built upon them, and his method combined Sanskrit philology, archaeology, the statistical study of coins, and fieldwork among communities whose customs he read as survivals of earlier social forms. His central problem was the transformation of tribal society into caste society. He also left named results in genetics and statistics, an origin that explains the school's respect for measurement and for categories tested against evidence.

### Q: What is the Indian feudalism thesis?

It is the argument, built into a system by R. S. Sharma in his book of 1965, that the grants of villages and revenues which Indian kings made to Brahmins, temples, and officials from the Gupta centuries onward dissolved the centralised state into a hierarchy of landed intermediaries. On this account the grantees took over the king's fiscal and judicial rights, the cultivators sank into dependence marked by forced labour and transfer with the soil, exchange and towns withered as the granted village closed in on itself, and the resulting order, though lacking Europe's vassalage and manor, deserves the name feudalism in an Indian form. The thesis organised research for decades, and its strongest claims were cut down by the debate it provoked, while its structural core, the interposed landholding class, passed into the discipline's common stock.

### Q: What did Irfan Habib argue about Mughal India?

In The Agrarian System of Mughal India, first published in 1963 and still the reference work, Habib reconstructed the empire from its own administrative records and argued that its land revenue demand was pitched to take an exceptionally large share of what the peasant grew. The demand was realised through the measured assessment system and paid out to the officer class through jagirs, temporary assignments of revenue whose holders, facing transfer within a few years, had every incentive to squeeze and none to protect the countryside. Between state and village stood the hereditary zamindars, who collected, retained, and resisted. The system's contradictions, an officer class growing faster than the revenue available for its payment, peasants fleeing and rising, the great revolts of the later seventeenth century, produced on his account the agrarian crisis that helped bring the empire down.

### Q: Did Indian historians reject the Asiatic mode of production?

Most of them did, as a description of India, and they rejected it on evidence rather than on loyalty. The category, which Marx had assembled from travellers' reports and British administrative writing, required a countryside without private rights in land, without internal class division worth the name, and without historical motion. The Indian record shows land bought, sold, gifted, and mortgaged across the centuries, villages stratified by property and caste, markets and commodity production at every documented period, and states rising and dissolving over an economy in evident movement. Kosambi's position was the most nuanced, since his own early work had found the concept suggestive, but his central finding, that caste itself was the outcome of tribes absorbed across a long historical process, denied changelessness in itself, and Habib's later critique of Marx's sources stated the school's verdict in its systematic form.

### Q: What did this historiography contribute internationally?

Three things. It supplied the standing demonstration that Marx's own account of a colonised society could be corrected by his method worked on better evidence, since the school's India, differentiated, commercial, and in motion, refutes the stagnant village society of the 1853 journalism. It conducted, in the feudalism debate, the most rigorous test any historiography has made of whether a European category can travel, and its settlement, structure yes, checklist no, is a permanent contribution to comparative method. And its Mughal reconstructions, of the agrarian system, the statistics of the imperial economy, and the debate over growth and crisis, entered the world's economic history as working data, feeding the international argument over the great divergence. A school canonical at home, it earned abroad the rarer status of being indispensable to arguments its authors never joined.

### Q: What are the criticisms of Indian Marxist historiography?

Within the discipline, the charges are specific and checkable. The feudalism thesis overstated the decline of trade, towns, and coinage, and generalised the peasantry's subjection from regional evidence, as Mukhia, Deyell, and Chattopadhyaya showed from different sides. The school's weaker practitioners practised a reflex determinism, reading culture and politics off a mode of production assigned in advance. Its periodisation smuggled a European timetable into Indian evidence. Its centre of gravity in the records of states and revenue left consciousness, lived religion, and gender underweight until its own later scholars, and the subaltern critics, forced the correction. And its institutional eminence bred the ordinary gatekeeping of an establishment. This article confines itself to such methodological criticisms, which the archive can answer; a separate political charge against the school circulates in public controversy, is noted here without being entered, and is distinguished from the criticisms above in the final question of this set.

### Q: How did Subaltern Studies break with this school?

The collective gathered around Ranajit Guha announced the break in 1982 with a charge of elitism: the historiography of India, Marxist work included, had written the nation and its classes from above, treating subordinated groups as the material of processes rather than as political subjects with a consciousness of their own. Guha's study of peasant insurgency argued that the rebel's politics had been systematically mistranslated by the records of his suppression, the prose of counter insurgency, and that mainstream historians who used those records without decoding them inherited the mistranslation. The break was therefore methodological before it was doctrinal: a demand to read the archive against its grain and to restore the subaltern as the author of his own history. It came from within the left, and from a scholar whose own first classic, on the Permanent Settlement, had been written inside the school's agrarian tradition.

### Q: Why does this school matter for the colonial debate?

Because it changed the baseline against which colonialism is measured. The older imperial account, and the picture Marx took from it, supposed a stagnant society that foreign rule disturbed; against that baseline, almost any change could be entered as an improvement and conquest could pose as a rude form of progress. The school's demonstrations, of pre colonial markets, towns, statecraft, and technical change, replaced the baseline with a society in motion, against which the drain of wealth, the ruin of hand spinning, and the frustration of an indigenous industrial path appear as measurable injuries to a going concern. Its historians were correspondingly severe on the prophecy that destruction would be redeemed by regeneration, since their colonial research found the destruction systematic and the regeneration largely withheld. The textual argument over Marx's own journalism belongs to the companion study of the debate; the school's contribution was to supply the evidence by which the argument is settled.

### Q: Where should you start with these historians?

Begin with the founder in his most accessible form, the short outline of ancient Indian civilisation he published in 1965, and proceed to the Introduction of 1956 for the method at full stretch. Then read the feudalism controversy as a set piece: Sharma's book of 1965, Mukhia's essay of 1981 asking whether feudalism existed in India at all, and Sharma's rejoinder of 1985, which together teach both the subject and the standards of the discipline. For the Mughal order, Habib's Agrarian System is unavoidable and repays slow reading, with his historical atlas open beside it. New readers should postpone the polemics, friendly and hostile alike, until the monographs have been met on their own terms; the polemics are about the school, and are legible only to a reader who can check them against the work they describe.

### Q: How does the correction from within grade the school's achievement?

It grades it higher than fidelity would. A tradition whose practitioners merely repeat the founder's conclusions demonstrates only that the conclusions were memorable; a tradition whose practitioners, using the founder's method on evidence he never saw, overturn his best known empirical claims demonstrates that the method generates knowledge independently of the man. The Indian historians corrected Marx on the society he had described most confidently, showing movement where he had recorded stillness and blockage where he had prophesied regeneration, and they did so in his name and by his instruments. By that test the school's achievement ranks as the strongest available evidence for the productivity of the framework it inherited, stronger than any defence of the founder's Indian writings could have been, since a successful defence would have proved only that the writings were defensible, while the correction proved that the method worked.

### Q: What should a student separate between rejecting a category and testing whether it fits?

The two operations look similar and are opposites. To reject a category, as the school rejected the Asiatic mode for India, is to find that the evidence contradicts the category's defining features: the property relations, the stagnation, the undifferentiated village are not there, and no restatement can save the term because its content has failed. To test a category, as the school tested feudalism, is to hold the term against the evidence district by district, discovering which of its elements the material supports, the interposed lord, the dependent cultivator, and which it refuses, the manor, the vassal's oath, until the term survives amended or is honourably retired. Rejection is a verdict on a description. Testing is a research programme. The student who confuses them will either defend every inherited term as a loyalty or discard every borrowed term as an imposition, and the school's whole career is an argument for the harder middle discipline.

### Q: Why did the school reject the Asiatic mode for India?

Because its premises were false of the society it claimed to describe, and because its provenance explained why. The concept had been assembled from the writings of travellers and of the colonial administration, Bernier's report of universal royal ownership, the parliamentary papers' self sufficient village, and it served the administrator's need for a legible, timeless subject population. Against it the school set the society's own records: inscriptions conveying land between private parties, a village order stratified by caste and property, commodity production woven through the economy, and a political history of states forming, taxing, and dissolving across the centuries. A mode of production defined by the absence of property, classes, and change cannot survive an archive full of all three. The school therefore treated the category as evidence about the colonial archive rather than about India, and returned to the founder's method for concepts the evidence could support.

### Q: How do the historians and their questions order the tradition?

The table in this article arranges the tradition as a sequence of questions rather than as a roll of conclusions, and the arrangement is the argument in miniature. The founder asks how tribes became castes and invents a method to answer. Sharma asks what the land grants did to the early medieval order, and the question organises a generation of epigraphy, including the epigraphy of his refuters. Habib asks how the Mughal state took its living and why the taking destroyed it, and the question builds a workshop that counts an empire. Each row's final column, the principal criticism, belongs to the ordering as much as the thesis does, since in this tradition a figure's question is not finished until its sharpest critic has been heard. Read down the table and the school's history becomes legible as an agenda being sharpened by use, which is how the school understood itself on its best days.

### Q: What separates a methodological criticism from a political one in this controversy?

A methodological criticism names evidence and invites an answer from the documents. When Mukhia argues that the Indian peasantry was too free for feudalism, or Deyell that the coinage did not thin as the thesis required, any historian can take down the charters and the hoards and check; the criticism stands or falls by what the documents show, and the school's own practice treated such criticisms as contributions, answering them in the journals over decades. A political criticism attributes motive: it charges that the school's history was written to serve a cause, and it is characteristically indifferent to the district level evidence on which the school's claims rest. Such a charge exists in Indian public argument against this historiography; this article notes its existence and its general character, names none of the organisations, publications, or persons associated with it, and takes no position in it, because a controversy about motives cannot be settled by the instruments, of inscription, coin, and register, with which the school's work is properly judged.
