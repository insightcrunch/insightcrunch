Medicare Parts A and B are the two original components of the federal health insurance program for the aged and disabled, and this article is a reference to their operative text rather than a summary of their marketing. Brochures, fact sheets, and plan-comparison tools all flatten the program into slogans about hospital coverage and doctor coverage, and those slogans fail at exactly the moments when precision matters: when a hospital stay is coded as observation rather than inpatient, when a skilled nursing facility demands payment after a discharge, when a home health episode is denied as not medically reasonable. In each of those disputes the deciding document is not the brochure. It is Title XVIII of the Social Security Act, codified at 42 U.S.C. 1395 and the sections that follow, and the specific subsections that grant, limit, and condition each benefit.

That commitment to the statutory text is the thesis of this article. Medicare Parts A and B are best understood as two distinct statutory programs that happen to share a title of the Social Security Act, each with its own entitlement rules, its own benefit definitions, and its own financing mechanism. Treating them as two halves of one insurance product invites exactly the errors that cost beneficiaries money: assuming that enrollment in one automatically means enrollment in the other, assuming that a hospital billing department's description of a stay controls coverage, or assuming that medical necessity alone entitles anyone to payment. The statute answers each of these questions, and it answers them in language that rewards close reading.

![Medicare Parts A and B statutory structure showing hospital insurance and medical insurance - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

This section establishes the statutory identity shared by both parts and then carries out the full Part A deep dive. Part A, the hospital insurance program, is where the entitlement machinery is most mechanical and most misunderstood, because entitlement arises from a person's Social Security insured status rather than from any application the person files. Later sections of this article carry the same discipline into Part B, the supplementary medical insurance program, with its voluntary enrollment structure and its general-revenue financing. Reading the two programs side by side, with the statutory sections in front of you, is the only reliable way to see where the brochure version ends and the enforceable version begins.

A note on vocabulary before the analysis starts: the statute uses ordinary words in technical senses, and confusion about those senses is the root of most coverage disputes. "Entitlement" means the statute itself confers a legal right to have payment made; it is not a synonym for applying, enrolling, or qualifying under agency guidance. A "beneficiary" is an individual entitled to benefits under one of the parts. A "provider of services" is the statutory term for institutions such as hospitals and skilled nursing facilities that have entered agreements with the Secretary. And "reasonable and necessary" is a term of art defined by the exclusion provisions at section 1862 of the Act, 42 U.S.C. 1395y, not a general invitation to argue about medical judgment. With that vocabulary in place, the structure of the statute becomes legible.

## Medicare Parts A and B: The Statutory Identity

Medicare Parts A and B both live in Title XVIII of the Social Security Act, which Congress codified at 42 U.S.C. 1395 and the sections that follow it. Title XVIII is organized as a subchapter of chapter 7 of title 42 of the United States Code, and its parts map directly onto sections of the Social Security Act numbered in the 1800 series. Part A, hospital insurance benefits for the aged and disabled, begins at section 1811 of the Act, 42 U.S.C. 1395c. Part B, supplementary medical insurance benefits for the aged and disabled, begins at section 1831 of the Act, 42 U.S.C. 1395j. The architecture is deliberate: a single title establishes the program, and each part then carries its own description of benefits, its own conditions of payment, its own financing provisions, and its own trust fund. Anyone who wants to resolve a coverage question begins by identifying which part governs the service, because the answer determines which benefit section, which conditions section, and which exclusion analysis applies.

The 1965 origin of this structure deserves a paragraph of its own, because the design choices made at enactment still govern how the two parts operate. Title XVIII was added to the Social Security Act by the Social Security Amendments of 1965, Public Law 89-97, signed on July 30, 1965. Congress built the hospital insurance program on the existing Social Security insured-status system, so Part A entitlement flows automatically from a lifetime of covered work; it built the supplementary medical insurance program as a voluntary, premium-financed supplement, so Part B requires affirmative enrollment. The legislative story, the political compromises, and the original committee design are told in full in the companion pillar article on the Social Security Amendments of 1965 (/2010/11/01/social-security-amendments-1965-medicare/), and readers who want the enactment history should start there. This article takes that history as its foundation and proceeds from the text that Congress enacted, because the statute as amended is what decides disputes.

The internal organization of Title XVIII rewards a reader who learns the numbering. Section 1811 (42 U.S.C. 1395c) describes the Part A program in general terms. Section 1812 (42 U.S.C. 1395d) defines the scope of Part A benefits, listing the categories of covered services. Section 1814 (42 U.S.C. 1395f) sets the conditions that must be satisfied before payment can be made for those services, including physician certification and, for certain services, prior hospitalization. Section 1861 (42 U.S.C. 1395x) supplies the definitions that control the entire title, from "inpatient hospital services" to "posthospital extended care services" to "home health services," and it is the section to which every coverage argument eventually returns. Section 1862 (42 U.S.C. 1395y) states the exclusions from coverage, most importantly the exclusion of items and services that are not reasonable and necessary for the diagnosis or treatment of illness or injury. The entitlement bridge between the Social Security system and Part A sits just outside Title XVIII, at section 226 of the Act (42 U.S.C. 426), which confers entitlement to hospital insurance benefits on individuals who have attained the qualifying age and are entitled to monthly insurance benefits under Title II, or who satisfy the insured-status requirements through other statutory pathways. The point of learning this map is practical rather than academic: a coverage dispute is won or lost by citing the right section, and the right section is almost always more specific than the reader first expects.

## Part A: Hospital Insurance Under 42 U.S.C. 1395c

Part A is the hospital insurance program, and its most consequential feature is also its least intuitive. Entitlement to Part A does not arise from an application for health insurance, a health examination, or any determination by an insurance official that the individual needs or deserves coverage. It arises from insured status under the Social Security system. Section 226 of the Act provides that an individual who has attained age sixty-five and is entitled to monthly benefits under Title II of the Social Security Act, the old-age, survivors, and disability insurance program, is entitled to hospital insurance benefits under Part A. The statutory mechanism is automatic in the strong sense: the entitlement exists because the qualifying conditions are satisfied, not because anyone has granted it. An individual who has earned insured status through covered work and has reached the qualifying age holds Part A entitlement as a matter of law, whether or not that individual ever files a separate document asking for it.

Insured status itself is a term of art with a precise statutory meaning, and understanding it is essential to understanding why Part A works the way it does. Under Title II, an individual becomes insured for old-age and survivors purposes by accumulating quarters of coverage, which are earned through wages paid for covered employment or through net earnings from covered self-employment on which the applicable payroll taxes have been paid. The quarters accumulate over a working life, and once an individual has accumulated enough of them to be "fully insured" within the meaning of the statute, the insured status persists. Disability-insured status follows a parallel but distinct test tied to recency of work. The details of the quarter-of-coverage counting rules belong to Title II and to the regulations that implement it, but the qualitative point is what matters for Part A: insured status is a record of a lifetime of covered work, recorded in earnings records, and it is that record, not any health-related showing, that opens the door to hospital insurance. This design reflects the 1965 compromise directly. Congress wanted a hospital insurance program that rested on the contributory foundation of Social Security rather than on a new and separate administrative apparatus, so it borrowed the insured-status machinery wholesale and made it the key to Part A.

The automatic character of Part A entitlement has practical consequences that surprise even sophisticated readers. Because entitlement flows from insured status, there is no Part A application to be approved or denied in the ordinary case; the question for the agency is whether the statutory conditions are met, a question answered by earnings records and age. Because the entitlement is conferred by statute, it cannot be conditioned on health status, preexisting conditions, or the costliness of the individual's expected care. And because the entitlement attaches to the individual rather than to a policy, it travels with the individual across providers and across spells of illness, subject only to the benefit-period rules that the statute itself imposes. The contrast with Part B could not be sharper. Part B is a voluntary program that requires affirmative enrollment and the payment of a premium, and an individual who never enrolls simply has no Part B coverage. Part A has no such enrollment gate for the insured individual, because Congress deliberately made the hospital insurance benefit an incident of the Social Security system rather than a product anyone must elect.

### Why does Part A entitlement arise without an application?

Because Congress anchored Part A in the insured-status framework of the old-age, survivors, and disability insurance system instead of creating a separate health-insurance application process. Entitlement attaches by operation of law once the statutory conditions are satisfied, so the statute leaves no room for a determination that an insured individual does or does not deserve hospital insurance.

Entitlement, however, is only the threshold, and the statute is careful to separate the right to the program from the right to payment for any particular service. Section 1812 grants the benefit categories, but payment follows only when the conditions of section 1814 are satisfied and none of the exclusions of section 1862 apply. The statute also organizes Part A coverage into benefit periods, the technical concept defined at section 1861(a), 42 U.S.C. 1395x(a), that the older case law calls a spell of illness. A benefit period begins with a hospital admission and closes after a statutory interval during which the individual has received no inpatient hospital or skilled nursing care, after which the statute permits a new benefit period to begin. The mechanics of the benefit period matter because they govern how repeated hospitalizations are treated and because they set the framework within which the statute's duration limits operate. The practical lesson is the one this article keeps returning to: entitlement gets the individual through the door, but the text of sections 1812 through 1862 decides what happens inside the room.

The scope of Part A benefits is defined at section 1812 of the Act, 42 U.S.C. 1395d, and the list is brief enough to state in full. Part A covers inpatient hospital services, which are the core benefit and the one most readers have in mind when they think of Medicare. It covers post-hospital extended care services, the statutory term for care furnished in a skilled nursing facility following a qualifying hospital stay. It covers home health services furnished under a plan of care. And it covers hospice care for individuals who are terminally ill and who elect the hospice benefit in lieu of certain other benefits. Each of these categories is then defined, conditioned, and limited by the sections that follow, and no category can be understood from its name alone. The statute's definitions do the real work, and the definitions are where coverage disputes are decided.

Inpatient hospital services, defined with reference to section 1861(b), encompass the routine and ancillary services a participating hospital furnishes to its inpatients: bed and board in semiprivate accommodations, nursing services, the use of hospital facilities, and the diagnostic and therapeutic items and services the hospital provides in the course of inpatient care. The critical word in that sentence is "inpatient," because the statute conditions payment on the individual's status as an inpatient at the time the services are furnished. An individual who occupies a hospital bed but has been classified as an outpatient under observation status is not an inpatient within the meaning of the statute, no matter how indistinguishable the care may feel from the bedside. This is one of the places where the brochure version of Medicare, which speaks loosely of "hospital stays," collides with the statutory version, which speaks precisely of inpatient hospital services. The classification determines coverage, and the classification is governed by the statute and its implementing rules, not by the patient's reasonable impression of having been admitted.

Post-hospital extended care services carry the most litigated condition in Part A, and it deserves careful treatment because it is statutory rather than regulatory. Section 1861(i), codified at 42 U.S.C. 1395x(i), defines "posthospital extended care services" as extended care services furnished to an individual after transfer from a hospital in which the individual was an inpatient for not less than three consecutive days before discharge. The three-day qualifying inpatient stay is therefore a creature of the statute itself. It does not change from year to year, it cannot be waived by a claims administrator, and it does not bend for sympathetic facts. The same definition requires that the individual be admitted to the skilled nursing facility within the time limit the statute prescribes after hospital discharge, or within such time as is medically appropriate to begin an active course of treatment where the individual's condition makes prompt admission inappropriate. The policy logic is legible: Congress designed the skilled nursing benefit as a continuation of hospital-level care for patients who still need skilled services after an acute episode, not as a freestanding long-term-care benefit. But the logic does not soften the rule. A patient whose hospital time was coded as observation, or whose inpatient stay lasted only two days, does not satisfy the definition, and the resulting denial is a straightforward application of section 1861(i) rather than an exercise of administrative discretion.

Home health services under Part A occupy a narrower channel than many readers expect, because the statute conditions them tightly. Section 1861(m) defines the services, which include part-time or intermittent skilled nursing care, physical and speech therapy, and certain other services furnished under a plan of care established and periodically reviewed by a physician. Payment conditions require that the individual be confined to the home, that the services be furnished under a physician's plan of care, and that the need for skilled services be genuine rather than custodial. The statute draws a sharp line between skilled care, which Part A covers, and custodial or personal-comfort care, which the exclusions at section 1862 leave outside the program. Home health coverage turns on this line, and the disputes that reach adjudication almost always concern whether the services furnished were skilled within the meaning of the statutory definition or had crossed into custodial care that the statute excludes.

Hospice care entered Part A by later amendment as a distinct benefit with its own election mechanics, and it illustrates how carefully the statute manages the boundary between covered and non-covered care at the end of life. An individual who is certified as terminally ill may elect the hospice benefit, and the election carries consequences spelled out in the statute: for the duration of the election periods, the individual generally waives the right to have payment made under Part A for services related to the terminal condition other than through the hospice program, while retaining coverage for unrelated conditions. The benefit covers nursing care, medical social services, physician services, counseling, and other palliative services furnished by the hospice program, and it is oriented toward comfort rather than cure. The election-and-waiver structure is the point worth carrying forward. Congress did not simply add a new covered service; it created a defined alternative track with defined tradeoffs, and the text of the election provisions controls which track the individual is on.

Running beneath all four benefit categories are the conditions of payment at section 1814 (42 U.S.C. 1395f) and the exclusions at section 1862 (42 U.S.C. 1395y), and no account of Part A is complete without them. Section 1814 requires, among other things, that payment for inpatient hospital services be conditioned on physician certification of medical necessity and on the hospital's having the required agreement with the Secretary. Section 1862 then excludes from payment any items or services that are not reasonable and necessary for the diagnosis or treatment of illness or injury or to improve the functioning of a malformed body member, along with a catalog of specific exclusions that includes personal comfort items, custodial care, and most routine physical examinations. These two sections are the teeth of the statute. The benefit definitions at section 1812 say what Part A covers in principle; sections 1814 and 1862 say whether payment will actually be made in the particular case. A reader who stops at the benefit list has read only the promise. The conditions and exclusions are the terms, and they are where the government's payment obligation is finally measured.

When payment is denied, the statute provides its own path for contesting the decision, and that path reinforces this article's central claim. Determinations under Title XVIII are subject to administrative review culminating in a hearing before an administrative law judge and, beyond that, judicial review under section 1869 of the Act, 42 U.S.C. 1395ff. At every stage of that review, the controlling authority is the statute, followed by regulations the Secretary has issued through rulemaking; program manuals and guidance documents instruct the agency's contractors but do not carry the force of the text. This hierarchy is why coverage disputes are ultimately textual disputes. A denial letter that cites only a manual provision, without connecting it to a statutory condition or exclusion, has not finished its legal work. The reader who learns to trace every coverage question back to its section, its definition, and its conditions will read denial letters differently, and will understand exactly where the government's obligation begins and ends.

The financing of Part A completes the picture and explains why the program's politics differ so sharply from Part B's. Part A is financed principally through the hospital insurance payroll tax, a dedicated tax levied on wages paid for covered employment and on net earnings from covered self-employment, with employers and employees each bearing a share of the tax on wages. The proceeds are deposited in the Federal Hospital Insurance Trust Fund established at section 1817 of the Act, 42 U.S.C. 1395i, and the statute earmarks that fund for the payment of Part A benefits and related administrative costs. The trust fund structure matters for two reasons. First, it formalizes the contributory character of the program: the benefit is tied, through insured status, to a lifetime of taxed earnings, and the revenue is segregated from general government funds. Second, it means the program's long-term fiscal position is a function of the relationship between taxed payroll and benefit outlays, a relationship the statute's own trustees are required to report on. The qualitative contrast with Part B is instructive even before Part B is examined in detail. Part B draws its financing substantially from general revenues supplemented by enrollee premiums, which gives it the character of a subsidized insurance product. Part A, financed through a dedicated payroll tax and gated by insured status, carries the character of an earned benefit of the Social Security system. That difference is not an accident of drafting. It is the 1965 compromise rendered in fiscal architecture, and it is why the two parts, for all that they share a title of the Social Security Act, operate as distinct programs with distinct political economies. The statute is the map, and the map is the territory: read the sections, follow the conditions, and the coverage answer follows.


## What Part B Is: Voluntary Insurance Inside a Mandatory Program

Part B of title XVIII of the Social Security Act, codified beginning at 42 U.S.C. 1395j, establishes a voluntary insurance program of supplementary medical insurance. The word voluntary is doing decisive work in that sentence. Participation in Part A of Medicare attaches by operation of law once a person satisfies its conditions. Participation in Part B attaches only when the eligible individual takes a deliberate step. The statute calls for an affirmative act of enrollment, and without that act there is no Part B coverage, no premium obligation, and no protection against later enrollment consequences.

The design is fiscal as much as moral. Part B charges a monthly premium, and only people who choose the coverage owe it. Congress could have folded physician and outpatient services into the hospital insurance framework and financed them from payroll taxes, but that would have raised the payroll tax for a benefit not every beneficiary values equally. Instead, the statute created a second, separately financed program that eligible people may join or decline, and that choice shapes everything that follows: eligibility rules, enrollment architecture, penalties for late action, and the very nature of disputes between beneficiaries and the program.

Part B eligibility is broader than Part A in one dimension and narrower in another. Broader, because a person need not have an earnings record to enroll: those who reach age 65 and satisfy citizenship or residence conditions may elect coverage though they never paid hospital insurance payroll taxes. Narrower, because enrollment is never conferred by entitlement alone. An individual entitled to Part A at 65 may still decline Part B, and many do, often because they carry employer group coverage through current employment. The election is theirs, and the statute treats the choice with respect.

Enrollment is not a single event. The statute constructs a sequence of opportunities, and when a person uses them determines what the coverage costs. The initial enrollment period opens around the time eligibility first begins, and coverage effective dates depend on where inside the window the person enrolls. The general enrollment period gives a second chance each year, but coverage bought there takes effect later and arrives with a penalty attached. Special enrollment periods protect people who deferred Part B for a recognized reason, most prominently group health plan coverage through current employment. The program wants everyone eligible to have a path in, and it prices procrastination to encourage early enrollment.

The monthly premium is the hinge of the financing. The statute fixes the principle that each enrollee pays a premium each month, set on a periodic basis by formula rather than by appropriation. What matters is the relationship between the premium and the program's total cost. The premium covers only a share of the cost of Part B. The remaining and larger share comes from the general revenues of the Treasury, transferred into the Federal Supplementary Medical Insurance Trust Fund. Premiums flow into that trust fund, general revenue contributions are deposited into it, and benefit payments are drawn from it. Every Part B enrollee is therefore subsidized by the taxpayer at large, because the premium sits below the full average cost of the benefits. Part A draws on a dedicated payroll tax and stands or falls on its own earnings; Part B draws on the general fund, and its solvency depends on Congress continuing to appropriate the difference.

That financing design explains why the enrollment penalty exists and why it is permanent. A person who declines Part B at the first opportunity and enrolls years later has paid no premiums in the meantime yet arrives when need for medical services is typically higher than at age 65. The statute answers this adverse selection problem with a surcharge on the monthly premium for each full block of delayed enrollment. The surcharge is not a one time fine. It becomes part of the monthly premium for as long as the person remains enrolled, protecting the trust fund against people who game the timing of enrollment and signaling to every newly eligible person that the first enrollment decision has lasting financial consequences.

## What the Statute Covers: Physicians, Outpatient Care, Equipment, and Laboratories

Part B coverage is defined by an enumeration rather than by a general promise of medical care. Section 1832 describes the scope of benefits, and the operative definition of covered services lives in the statute's long catalog of medical and other health services. The catalog includes physicians' services, the services physicians perform in the practice of medicine and surgery, including services furnished incident to them. It includes outpatient hospital services, the diagnostic and therapeutic work hospitals perform for patients who are not admitted. It includes durable medical equipment, reusable items such as wheelchairs, hospital beds, and oxygen equipment that serve a medical purpose in the home and are not useful in the absence of illness or injury. It includes clinical diagnostic laboratory tests, the blood work and specimen analyses that guide diagnosis and treatment. And it includes a further list: home health services for those who do not qualify under Part A, medical supplies, ambulance services, prosthetic devices, certain preventive services, and outpatient physical, occupational, and speech therapy within statutory bounds.

The enumeration matters because anything not on the list, or excluded elsewhere, is not covered even when a physician considers it medically appropriate. Part B excludes most routine dental care, routine foot care, and cosmetic surgery, and it excludes services the statute or its rules classify as not reasonable and necessary for diagnosis or treatment of illness or injury. The reasonable and necessary standard is the gatekeeper of the entire benefit. A physician may order a test the statute lists as a covered category, but if it is not reasonable and necessary in the particular clinical circumstances, payment is denied. This is where the program's control over the practice of medicine shows its teeth, and it is why Section 1801 exists as a counterweight, a subject taken up below.

Beneficiaries also share in the cost of covered services beyond the monthly premium. The statute imposes an annual deductible the enrollee must satisfy before payment begins for most services, and cost sharing on many services after the deductible is met. The principle is what matters here. Part B is not a free good. The premium buys entry, the deductible and cost sharing ration use, and the combination is meant to keep enrollees sensitive to the cost of the care they consume. That sensitivity is imperfect, because insurance by design blunts the price signal, but the statutory architecture at least attempts it.

One further structural point deserves attention. Part B pays for services on a different footing than Part A pays for hospital stays. Hospital payment under Part A is largely bundled and prospective, tied to the diagnosis rather than to each service rendered. Part B payment for physician services is built on a fee schedule that assigns relative values to procedures and converts those values into payment amounts. The statute did not originally contain such a schedule. Congress added it when it concluded that paying physicians on the basis of customary and prevailing charges was inflating costs without bound. That history is a miniature of the whole Medicare story. A program founded on professional autonomy gradually acquired price setting power as costs rose, and each acquisition was justified as a necessary correction to the behavior the earlier generosity had induced.

The physician's relationship to the fee schedule is mediated by the assignment system, and the system deserves attention because it is Part B's price mechanism in its purest form. A physician may agree to accept assignment on all claims, taking the fee schedule amount as payment in full and collecting only the deductible and coinsurance from the patient. A physician may instead accept assignment claim by claim, or decline participation and bill the patient directly, with the statute capping how far above the schedule a nonparticipating physician's charge may go. The participation decision is thus a price negotiation conducted through statute rather than through an open market: the government sets the terms of the product it buys, providers decide whether the terms are worth accepting, and beneficiaries who see nonparticipating physicians absorb the difference within the statutory cap. Where Part A pays institutions under administered prices, Part B constructs a regulated market in physician services and lets the participants sort themselves by price, which is another expression of the program's insurance logic.


## Enrollment: Three Doors Into the Program

The enrollment architecture is best understood as three doors, each with its own timing, conditions, and consequences. The first door is the initial enrollment period, which opens before the month the individual first becomes eligible, spans that month, and continues for months afterward. Enrolling early within this window yields the earliest possible coverage start date; enrolling late pushes the start date back. The statute gives the newly eligible person a generous but bounded stretch of time, and the timing of the decision within that stretch is financially meaningful. A person who enrolls before eligibility begins receives coverage from the first month of eligibility. A person who waits until after eligibility began receives coverage months later. The difference is the administrative consequence of processing time, and it nudges the undecided toward deciding sooner.

The second door is the general enrollment period, which opens each year for a fixed stretch in the first quarter of the calendar year. Anyone eligible but not enrolled may enroll during this window, and coverage takes effect in the middle of that year. The delay between the enrollment window and the coverage start date discourages casual timing. A person who skips the initial enrollment period and enrolls in the next general enrollment period may wait months before coverage begins, and during that interval the person has no Part B protection. The general enrollment period is a safety net with a cost. It guarantees that no one is permanently locked out, but it does not pretend that missing the first door was free.

The third door is the special enrollment period, which protects people who had a good reason to decline Part B when first eligible. The principal good reason is coverage under a group health plan by reason of current employment, whether the employment is the individual's own or a spouse's. Such a person may enroll in Part B during a special enrollment period that begins when the employment ends or when the group health plan coverage ends, whichever comes first, and the late enrollment penalty does not apply. This is the statute's acknowledgment that it would be unfair to punish a person for choosing employer coverage over Medicare. It is also the provision that most often generates disputes, because its protection turns on precise questions: whether the employment was current rather than past, whether the group health plan was actually employment based, and whether the enrollment happened within the special window. The answers determine whether the penalty attaches, and the penalty is large enough to make those questions worth litigating.

Each of the three doors presumes that Part B coverage reflects the individual's choice, though the enrollment mechanics differ by population. Individuals who reach 65 while already receiving Social Security or Railroad Retirement cash benefits are deemed enrolled in Part B automatically, with a statutory opportunity to decline the coverage; the statute drafts them in and lets them opt out. Those not receiving cash benefits, including most people who keep working past 65 without drawing benefits, must affirmatively elect enrollment through one of the three doors. Either way, coverage can be refused, which is what keeps the program voluntary even for the automatically enrolled. Nobody is held in Part B against a stated refusal, and the deeming provision does not change the program's character: it remains a product the beneficiary may decline rather than an entitlement that attaches by status. That choice based character is the clearest expression of the voluntary principle, and it is why the enrollment provisions read less like benefit rules than like the rules of an insurance market the government happens to operate. It also explains the characteristic Part B casework. Beneficiaries miss the initial enrollment period because they misunderstood a notice, assumed retiree coverage counted as group coverage through current employment, or simply did not know the penalty existed. They enroll in a general enrollment period, discover the surcharge, and ask whether an exception applies. The statute's answer is narrow. Special enrollment protection attaches to defined circumstances, not to confusion. That narrowness is intentional, because a penalty riddled with exceptions would not deter strategic delay, and the program's financing depends on broad early enrollment to spread risk across the healthy and the sick alike.

## The Late-Enrollment Penalty: A Surcharge That Does Not Go Away

The late enrollment penalty is the statute's answer to a straightforward problem of insurance economics. The Part B premium does not vary with the enrollee's health. If enrollment were costless to delay, the healthy would wait until they needed care, enroll then, and pay the same premium as those who had been paying for years. The trust fund would collect premiums disproportionately from the sick while paying benefits disproportionately to them, and the general revenue contribution would have to grow to cover the gap. The penalty prevents that outcome by making delay expensive. For each full block of delayed enrollment, measured in twelve month periods beginning after the close of the initial enrollment period, the statute adds a surcharge to the monthly premium. The surcharge accumulates with each additional block of delay, so a person who waits several years pays a substantially higher monthly premium than a person who enrolled on time.

Three features of the penalty surprise beneficiaries. First, it is permanent. It is not a fine paid once and forgotten. It is added to the monthly premium for every month the person remains enrolled in Part B. A person who enrolled late at age seventy pays the surcharge at eighty and at ninety, for as long as the enrollment continues. Second, it compounds with the underlying premium. Because the surcharge is expressed as an addition to the premium rather than a fixed amount, it rises as the premium rises. Third, the penalty clock runs on enrollment opportunity, not on need. The surcharge accrues for periods in which the person was eligible to enroll and did not, regardless of whether the person had other unprotected coverage and regardless of whether the person knew the penalty existed. Ignorance of the provision is not a defense the statute recognizes.

The permanence of the penalty gives the initial enrollment decision its gravity. Most insurance penalties are temporary, designed to nudge behavior and then release. The Part B penalty is closer to a price adjustment reflecting the enrollee's true cost to the program. The late enrollee has paid fewer premiums over a lifetime of eligibility and is likely to claim more benefits per year of enrollment than the timely enrollee. The surcharge closes that gap, imperfectly but persistently, for the rest of the enrollee's life. Fairness, in the Part B design, means that those who carry the program's costs from the beginning are not subsidizing those who join only when the bills arrive.

The contrast with Part A is instructive. Part A late enrollment can also carry a premium surcharge for those who must buy in, because Part A too faces adverse selection among voluntary buyers. But the great mass of Part A beneficiaries never confront a penalty, because they never make an enrollment decision. Their entitlement arrives with their work history. Part B beneficiaries all make the decision, and the penalty is the price of deciding late. A program that requires affirmative enrollment must give the enrollment decision consequences, or voluntariness becomes a license to free ride.

## Section 1801 and the Limit on Federal Control

Section 1801 of the Social Security Act, codified at 42 U.S.C. 1395, is one of the most quoted provisions in the entire Medicare statute, and it is also one of the most misunderstood. Its operative sentence provides that nothing in the subchapter shall be construed to authorize any federal officer or employee to exercise supervision or control over the practice of medicine, over the manner in which medical services are provided, or over the selection, tenure, or compensation of the officers and employees of any institution, agency, or person furnishing health services, or to exercise supervision or control over the administration or operation of any such institution, agency, or person. In plain terms, the provision declares that Medicare is a payment program, not a nationalized medical service. The federal government pays the bills for covered services. It does not run the hospitals, employ the physicians, or dictate how medicine is practiced.

The provision was a political necessity at the statute's birth. The organized medical profession feared that a federal health insurance program would become a vehicle for federal control of clinical judgment, and the American Medical Association's opposition to Medicare was fierce. Section 1801 was the answer. It reassured physicians and hospitals that accepting Medicare payment would not make them agents of the federal government or subject their clinical decisions to bureaucratic second guessing. It drew a line between the government's legitimate interest in defining what it will pay for and its lack of authority to tell practitioners how to practice. A physician participating in Medicare remains a private professional treating a private patient. The government is the payer, not the employer and not the supervisor.

Practitioners cite Section 1801 because that line has eroded under fiscal pressure. Paying for whatever physicians ordered, at whatever charges physicians set, proved ruinously expensive, and Congress answered with payment reforms: cost based reimbursement gave way to prospective payment, customary charges gave way to fee schedules, and utilization review gave way to quality reporting. Each reform gave the program more influence over clinical behavior. Fee schedules set prices for procedures, which shapes which procedures physicians favor. Coverage determinations that a service is not reasonable and necessary are functionally a judgment that the physician's clinical decision was wrong, at least for payment purposes. Practitioners who chafe under these controls reach for Section 1801 and argue that the program has crossed the line the provision drew.

The provision's role as a limit on the program is real but bounded. Courts have read Section 1801 as a prohibition on direct federal control of medical practice, not on the conditions the government attaches to its money. The distinction is between regulating conduct and setting the terms of a voluntary payment arrangement. When Medicare declines to pay for a service it deems not reasonable and necessary, it is not ordering the physician to withhold the service. The physician remains free to furnish it, and the patient remains free to pay for it. The government has simply declined to buy it. That is the theory that has allowed the program's payment controls to coexist with Section 1801. But payment is power. When the government is the dominant payer in the market, a decision not to pay for a service is, for practical purposes, a decision to suppress it. The tension between the theory and the practice is permanent, and Section 1801 is the statutory text in which that tension is registered.

For Part B in particular, Section 1801 carries a special resonance. Part B pays physicians directly, and the fee schedule is the instrument through which the government exercises its greatest influence over the economics of medical practice. Every relative value assigned to a procedure is a federal judgment about the worth of medical work. Physicians who believe the schedule undervalues their services invoke Section 1801 as a reminder that the statute promised them professional independence. The program's administrators reply that setting payment rates is not supervising the practice of medicine. Both sides are reading the same sentence, and both are partly right, which is why the provision remains a live source of argument rather than a settled boundary.

The Part A contrast clarifies what Part B is. Where Part A confers coverage by insured status, Part B offers it by election, and every Part B rule about enrollment periods, premiums, and penalties follows from that choice-based design. The two parts share a title of the Social Security Act, but they run on different theories of obligation, and the statute's treatment of each follows its theory.

### Why is Part B voluntary when Part A is automatic?

Part B charges a monthly premium because Congress treated it as insurance the beneficiary chooses to buy, while Part A is the return on payroll taxes already paid. Making Part B automatic would impose a cost on people who never agreed to it; voluntariness lets each eligible person decide whether the price is worth the coverage.


## The Part C Alternative: Private Plans in Place of Original Medicare

Long before the Medicare Advantage name existed, Medicare had begun doing business with private managed-care plans. In the early 1980s Congress authorized the program to contract with private plans on a risk basis, and private health maintenance organizations started serving Medicare beneficiaries under those contracts. The Balanced Budget Act of 1997 (Public Law 105-33) gave the arrangement a formal statutory home by creating Medicare+Choice, codified as Part C of Title XVIII at 42 U.S.C. 1395w-21 and the sections that follow. The Medicare Prescription Drug, Improvement, and Modernization Act of 2003 (Public Law 108-173) then renamed the program Medicare Advantage and modified its payment and plan rules. The Social Security Administration's legislative history records both milestones together: established as Medicare+Choice in 1997, renamed and modified in 2003.

The 1980s risk contracts were the program's first experiment with the substitution model. Under the Tax Equity and Fiscal Responsibility Act of 1982, Medicare began paying private health maintenance organizations a fixed monthly amount per enrollee, set as a share of the local cost of traditional Medicare, and the plans assumed the insurance risk for their members' care. The arrangement attracted plans in some markets and repelled them in others, and disputes over the proper share of fee-for-service costs foreshadowed the neutrality debate that would later surround Part C. When Congress wrote Medicare+Choice in 1997, it preserved the core idea, a fixed government payment to a private plan that takes responsibility for the Medicare benefit, while building a more elaborate statutory structure around plan types, enrollment rules, and payment methods.

The core legal move of Part C is substitution rather than supplementation. A beneficiary who is entitled to Part A and enrolled in Part B may elect a Medicare Advantage plan instead of receiving benefits through original Medicare. When the election takes effect, the private plan becomes the channel through which the beneficiary receives Medicare coverage. Medicare stops paying hospitals, physicians, and other providers directly for that beneficiary under fee for service and instead pays the plan, which assumes responsibility for furnishing the covered benefits. The election is voluntary and reversible. Beneficiaries make their choices during defined election periods, may switch plans during the annual coordinated election period, and may return to original Medicare. The statute therefore treats the private plan not as an add-on to Medicare but as an alternative vehicle for the Medicare benefit itself.

Every Medicare Advantage plan operates under a contract with the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, and the contract is the statute's instrument of control. To offer a plan in a county, a sponsor must show that it can furnish the required benefits, maintain an adequate provider network where the plan model requires one, meet marketing and disclosure standards, and submit its bid for CMS review. The contract runs for a year and may be renewed, and CMS may decline renewal or terminate for failures of compliance. The private plan is therefore not a free agent operating at Medicare's expense; it is a regulated contractor performing a public benefit on federal terms, and the substitution of the plan for original Medicare happens inside that contractual frame.

The coverage floor is the statute's central protection for beneficiaries. Every Medicare Advantage plan must cover at least the items and services that Parts A and B cover. A plan may not strip away a hospital, physician, or other benefit that original Medicare would furnish and still qualify as a Medicare plan. Above that floor, sponsors design their own offerings, and the program accommodates several delivery models. Health maintenance organization plans organize care around defined provider networks. Preferred provider organization plans use broader networks and cover out-of-network care at higher cost sharing. Private fee-for-service plans pay providers for covered services without operating a managed network. The models differ in how they organize delivery, but the legal equivalence is constant: whatever the model, the plan must deliver at least the Parts A and B benefit package.

One feature of the election often surprises beneficiaries: joining a Medicare Advantage plan does not end the Part B premium obligation. The beneficiary continues to pay the Part B premium to Medicare, and any plan premium sits on top of it. The arrangement reflects the statute's logic. The Part B premium helps finance the government's payment to the plan, so the beneficiary's contribution to the program continues even as the delivery channel changes. A plan that bids below its benchmark may direct part of its rebate toward reducing the beneficiary's Part B premium, but the obligation itself continues unless the plan chooses to absorb it, a detail that shapes how beneficiaries compare the true cost of joining a plan.

Medicare pays the plans through a benchmark and bid system organized by county, and the mechanism deserves careful attention because the design is the source of the financing debate that follows. For each county, Medicare sets a benchmark reflecting what it would spend per beneficiary on Parts A and B services under traditional fee for service in that area. Each plan submits a bid stating what it would charge to furnish the Parts A and B benefit package to a typical beneficiary in the county. The bid is measured against the benchmark. When a plan bids below the benchmark, a share of the difference returns to enrollees in the form of supplemental benefits or reduced cost sharing and premiums, while the remainder is retained by Medicare. When a plan bids above the benchmark, the enrollee pays the excess as an additional premium. Payments are then risk adjusted: Medicare adjusts what it pays each plan to reflect the health status of its enrollees, as captured through recorded diagnoses, so that a plan enrolling sicker beneficiaries receives more and a plan enrolling healthier beneficiaries receives less. The design aims to make plans compete on efficiency rather than on avoiding the ill, and it ties every plan's revenue to the local cost of traditional Medicare.

The benchmark system also reveals a tension Congress wrote into the statute deliberately. In counties where traditional Medicare spending runs low, often rural counties with few providers, benchmarks were set above actual fee-for-service costs through statutory floors, because Congress wanted private plans to find those markets worth entering. The design thus pursues two goals at once: holding payments near the cost of traditional Medicare, and making plan participation attractive where traditional Medicare is cheap. Those goals pull in opposite directions, and the payment-adequacy debate is in large part an argument about which goal the statute should have prioritized.

That architecture raises the question the statute's drafters had to answer in legal form.

### How can a private plan substitute for Parts A and B?
The statute requires each Medicare Advantage plan to cover at least the items and services Parts A and B cover, so enrollment in a plan replaces original Medicare as the vehicle for those benefits. The beneficiary elects the plan, and Medicare directs payment to the plan instead of paying providers directly under fee for service.

## Financial Neutrality and the Payment-Adequacy Question

The payment machinery carries an implicit standard that analysts call financial neutrality: Medicare should pay a private plan no more than it would have spent covering the same beneficiary under traditional fee for service. The benchmark, anchored to county fee-for-service spending, is the device Congress chose to enforce that standard, and competitive bidding was meant to discipline plan costs beneath it. Whether the machinery achieves neutrality is one of the most contested questions in Medicare finance. The task here is not to resolve it but to state, factually, what the statute's design does and to attribute the contested positions to the named analyses that advanced them.

The Medicare Payment Advisory Commission, the independent congressional agency Congress created to advise it on Medicare payment policy, concluded in its March 2010 report to Congress that Medicare Advantage payments per enrollee would reach an estimated 113 percent of comparable fee-for-service spending in 2010 if then-scheduled cuts to physician payments took effect, and 109 percent if Congress blocked those cuts. The commission reported that for the fifth consecutive year it was advising Congress to equalize plan payments with fee-for-service costs, stating that it had consistently supported financial neutrality between payment rates for the fee-for-service program and private plans. The strongest version of the MedPAC position holds that the excess payments are not costless: they draw on the same trust funds and general revenues that finance traditional Medicare, and they raise Part B premiums for every beneficiary, including those who never enroll in a private plan. On this view, neutrality is the only defensible standard, and the statute's payment design was failing it through 2010.

The Congressional Budget Office approached the same question through the mechanics of bids and benchmarks in testimony before Congress on trends in the Medicare Advantage program. CBO found that plans' bids vary less from county to county than per capita fee-for-service spending does. In the counties with the highest fee-for-service costs, plans' bids ran about 10 percent below fee-for-service spending; in the lowest-cost counties, bids ran about 21 percent above it. The benchmarks against which those bids were judged also exceeded fee-for-service costs in many counties, ranging from about 4 percent above in high-cost areas to about 26 percent above in low-cost areas. The strongest version of the position drawn from CBO's analysis is that the statute's own benchmark construction, not only the behavior of plans, determines whether neutrality holds: benchmarks set above fee-for-service levels build excess payments into the system by design, even when plans bid efficiently. At the same time, CBO observed that private plans have the greatest opportunity to restrain costs precisely where fee-for-service utilization runs highest, which is where bids came in below fee-for-service spending. On this view, the bidding mechanism can produce genuine savings in expensive markets while the benchmark formula guarantees overpayment in inexpensive ones.

That tension explains why the adequacy question resists a simple answer. From the perspective of the plans and the beneficiaries who choose them, the payments above fee-for-service levels are what make supplemental benefits, reduced cost sharing, and plan participation in low-cost counties possible. The benchmark floors and the rebate mechanism were designed to produce exactly those results. From the perspective of the taxpayers and traditional Medicare beneficiaries who finance the system, the same payments are a subsidy for private delivery that buys no additional Medicare benefit beyond what the statute already requires plans to cover. MedPAC's neutrality standard and CBO's mechanical analysis are two ways of measuring the distance between those perspectives, and both keep returning to the same statutory features: benchmarks set above fee-for-service costs and a rebate system that converts the difference into enrollee benefits rather than government savings.

Both analyses use the same reference point: what Medicare would have spent on the same beneficiaries if they had remained in Parts A and B as administered directly. They differ in emphasis. MedPAC emphasizes the aggregate result, payments running above fee-for-service and the premium consequences for the entire program. CBO emphasizes the mechanism, benchmarks constructed above fee-for-service in many counties and bids that show plans can undercut fee-for-service where traditional Medicare is most expensive. The adequacy question is therefore narrower than whether plans are profitable or popular. It is whether the statutory payment machinery holds total plan payments to the fee-for-service reference point, and through 2010 the two agencies Congress relies on most were documenting reasons to doubt that it did.

## Part D: The Outpatient Drug Benefit Delivered Through Private Plans

For nearly four decades after Medicare's creation, the program did not cover most outpatient prescription drugs. Parts A and B covered drugs administered in hospitals and certain physician settings, but the prescription a beneficiary filled at a retail pharmacy generally fell outside the benefit. Congress closed that gap through the [Medicare Prescription Drug, Improvement, and Modernization Act of 2003](/2011/01/01/medicare-modernization-act-2003-part-d/), which added Part D to Title XVIII, codified at 42 U.S.C. 1395w-101 and the sections that follow. Coverage under prescription drug plans and MA-PD plans first took effect on January 1, 2006, exactly as the statute specifies.

Before the full drug benefit began, the same 2003 statute created a transitional program that previewed the private-delivery model. Beginning in mid-2004, Medicare offered prescription drug discount cards on a voluntary basis, with transitional financial assistance for low-income beneficiaries, and the card program phased out as the full Part D benefit took effect in 2006. The discount cards did not insure drug costs; they delivered negotiated lower prices through private sponsors. The episode matters because it shows Congress testing the private-sponsor mechanism before committing the program to a full insurance benefit delivered the same way.

The delivery design is the provision's defining feature. Part D operates exclusively through competing private prescription drug plans. There is no government-administered drug benefit. A beneficiary obtains coverage by enrolling in a stand-alone prescription drug plan or, if enrolled in Medicare Advantage, in a Medicare Advantage plan that includes drug coverage. The plans are offered by private sponsors that contract with the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services; the government subsidizes enrollment rather than purchasing drugs or setting their prices directly. The statute's wager is that competition among sponsors, rather than a government fee schedule, will discipline cost: plans compete for enrollees on premiums, formularies, and pharmacy networks, and beneficiaries choose among the competing offerings.

The statute defines a standard benefit structure that sets the floor every plan must meet, and the coverage gap known as the donut hole sits inside that structure. The benefit year begins with a deductible phase in which the beneficiary pays the cost of covered drugs. Once the deductible is satisfied, an initial coverage phase begins in which the beneficiary and the plan share costs. When total drug spending reaches the initial coverage limit, the coverage gap opens: during this phase the beneficiary pays the full cost of covered drugs, with the plan contributing nothing, until the beneficiary's own out-of-pocket spending reaches a defined threshold. Crossing that threshold opens the catastrophic coverage phase, in which the plan and the government cover the great majority of drug costs for the rest of the year. The donut hole is therefore not a quirk of any single plan's design. It is a phase written into the statute's standard benefit, placed between initial coverage and catastrophic protection as a deliberate feature of the benefit's architecture.

The gap reflected the statute's budget arithmetic. Covering every beneficiary's drugs from the first prescription would have cost more than Congress was prepared to spend, so the benefit concentrates its subsidy in the deductible, initial coverage, and catastrophic phases and leaves the middle stretch to the beneficiary. Whether that trade was fair to beneficiaries with high drug costs became one of the most discussed features of the new benefit, but the structure itself was a legislative choice about how far a fixed amount of federal money could be stretched.

Plans are not confined to the defined standard benefit. The statute permits sponsors to offer alternative coverage that is at least actuarially equivalent to the standard design, and enhanced plans may fill in portions of the standard benefit's cost sharing, including parts of the coverage gap, in exchange for higher premiums. Premiums are set through competitive bidding: each sponsor submits a bid reflecting its expected cost of providing the benefit, and the government's premium subsidy derives from those bids. Plans distinguish themselves through formularies, the lists of covered drugs organized into tiers with different cost sharing, and through the pharmacy networks they assemble. Beneficiaries with low incomes receive additional subsidies for premiums and cost sharing. The statute paid special attention to beneficiaries who qualify for both Medicare and Medicaid. Those beneficiaries had previously received drug coverage through state Medicaid programs; under Part D they were moved into the Medicare drug benefit with automatic enrollment in qualifying plans and with subsidies that sharply reduced what they pay at the pharmacy. The provision shifted a large drug benefit from the state-federal Medicaid program to the federal Medicare program in a single statutory move, and it made the low-income subsidy the mechanism by which the poorest beneficiaries experience Part D. Enrollment is voluntary, though the statute imposes a late-enrollment penalty on beneficiaries who forgo creditable drug coverage and sign up later, a penalty designed to keep beneficiaries from waiting until they need expensive drugs before enrolling.

The competition the statute envisions is renewed every year. Sponsors submit bids annually, CMS approves the plans that meet its standards, and beneficiaries choose among the offerings during an annual election period. A plan that prices itself too high loses enrollees; a plan that prices itself too low risks losses it must absorb. The government does not guarantee any sponsor's enrollment. The discipline is supposed to come from beneficiaries' choices, which is why the statute invests in the infrastructure of choice: standardized benefit information, comparative plan materials, and marketing rules that govern how sponsors may present their offerings to the public. The yearly cycle also means the benefit never settles into a single fixed form; premiums, formularies, and plan lineups shift from year to year as sponsors respond to one another and to the bidding rules.

The statute organizes the drug benefit by region. Sponsors offer their plans within defined prescription drug plan regions, and each beneficiary chooses among the plans available where they live. The regional structure was meant to ensure that competition reaches beneficiaries outside the largest metropolitan markets, the same geographic concern that shaped the benchmark floors in Part C.

Part D thus completed a transformation in how Medicare delivers benefits. Like Part C, it embodies a legislative choice: where Parts A and B administer benefits through government payment rules applied directly to providers, Part D administers its benefit entirely through regulated private competition. The benefit's phases, including the coverage gap, are the visible consequence of that choice, balancing the statute's fiscal constraints against the goal of extending drug coverage to every beneficiary who wants it. The statute made the private plan, not the government, the face of the benefit: the beneficiary's relationship runs to the sponsor that enrolled them, while the government's role recedes into subsidy payments, contract oversight, and the rules that structure the market.


## The Master Distinction: Entitlement Versus Enrollment

Medicare runs on two incompatible theories of obligation, and nearly every eligibility question, penalty question, and appeal question in the program is really a question about which theory applies. Part A is an entitlement. The beneficiary earned it through a work history, or through a spouse's work history, or through disability status, and the statute delivers it without asking the beneficiary to do anything. Part B is an insurance product the beneficiary buys. It requires an affirmative act of enrollment, it charges a premium, and it punishes late entry. The central claim of this article is that this is not a decorative contrast. It is the master distinction. Once a question is classified under the right logic, the answer follows. Misclassify it, and the statute reads like a pile of unrelated technicalities.

Start with the entitlement logic. Section 1811 of the Social Security Act, codified at 42 U.S.C. 1395c, establishes hospital insurance for the aged and disabled. Individuals who reach 65 with sufficient quarters of covered employment, and certain disabled individuals, are entitled to Part A without a premium. Financing arrives through the payroll tax, not through a voluntary purchase decision, which means the beneficiary never faces a price signal at the point of entry. Enrollment for most is automatic upon entitlement to Social Security or Railroad Retirement benefits. There is no initial enrollment period to miss, no general enrollment period to wait for, and no late enrollment penalty to fear, because there is nothing to be late for. The entitlement cannot be lost through nonpayment, because no payment is required. The government owes the coverage as a matter of statutory right, and the right attaches to status, not to a transaction.

Now the insurance logic. Section 1831, codified at 42 U.S.C. 1395j, establishes supplementary medical insurance as a voluntary program. Nobody is drafted into Part B. The statute creates an initial enrollment period, a general enrollment period for those who missed it, and special enrollment periods for people whose employer group coverage ends. Premiums are charged, and the statute increases the premium for each full year that enrollment was delayed without other creditable coverage. That penalty is the signature of insurance logic. A voluntary insurance pool faces adverse selection: if beneficiaries could wait until they were sick to enroll, the healthy would stay out, the pool would fill with the sick, and the financing would collapse. The late enrollment penalty is the statute's answer to that problem. It prices the option value of waiting. Entitlement logic needs no such device, because entitlement logic has no waiting problem to solve. Eligibility is automatic and financing is compulsory, so the statute does not need to bribe or threaten anyone into joining.

Consider how cleanly the eligibility questions sort themselves once the distinction is applied. Who gets Part A without a premium? Whoever has the work history the statute requires, which is an entitlement question with a yes or no answer. Who must buy Part A under the voluntary enrollment provisions? Whoever lacks that history and chooses to purchase coverage, which is an insurance question governed by premiums and enrollment periods. Who may decline Part B? Anyone, because Part B is a product and products can be refused. Who may decline premium-free Part A? In practical terms almost no one, because refusing an entitlement one has earned serves no purpose and the statute barely contemplates the gesture. The working-aged rules, which let people past 65 delay Part B while covered by employer group health plans and then enroll later through a special enrollment period, make sense only under insurance logic. They are the statute coordinating one insurance product with another, the way any insurer coordinates benefits.

The penalty questions sort themselves just as cleanly. There is no late enrollment penalty for premium-free Part A, and there cannot be one, because the concept of lateness has no purchase to attach to. The Part B late enrollment penalty exists precisely because Part B is a purchase. Premium Part A, the buy-in provision for those without sufficient work history, is the proof that the distinction is doing the work. When Congress grafted purchase logic onto Part A, it imported the insurance apparatus with it: premiums, enrollment periods, and penalties for late enrollment. The penalty followed the logic, not the letter of the part. Wherever the statute asks the beneficiary to buy, it penalizes waiting. Wherever it confers an entitlement, it does not.

The appeal questions follow the same pattern, though the mechanism is subtler. Section 1869 provides administrative and judicial review of Medicare determinations, and the right to appeal exists under both parts. But every appeal begins with a classification question: which part's logic governs this service, on this date, for this beneficiary? A hospital stay billed as an inpatient admission travels through the Part A review track, where contractors examine whether the admission met the inpatient criteria and whether the services were reasonable and necessary. The same days in the same bed, billed as outpatient observation, travel through the Part B track with different cost-sharing rules and different review standards. A skilled nursing facility claim fails or survives depending on whether the services were skilled, which is an entitlement-logic question about whether the statutory benefit was triggered, while a Part B physician claim fails or survives depending on medical necessity and coding, which is an insurance-logic question about what the purchased product covers. The appeal right is uniform. The thing being appealed is not, and the master distinction tells the advocate which set of rules to brief.

The objection writes itself. Parts C and D arrived after the original two-part design, and they appear to complicate the picture. They do not. Part C, the Medicare Advantage program, and Part D, the prescription drug benefit, are both voluntary, premium-charging, enrollment-period-driven programs, and the drug benefit carries its own late enrollment penalty. They are insurance logic all the way down. The master distinction survives every expansion Congress has enacted, because every expansion that asks the beneficiary to choose has imported the insurance apparatus, and every provision that confers coverage by status has retained the entitlement apparatus. The distinction is not a description of 1965. It is a description of how the statute thinks.

The termination rules confirm the pattern from the other side. Part B coverage can be terminated for nonpayment of premiums, because a product that is bought can be lost by not paying for it. Premium-free Part A cannot be terminated for nonpayment, because there is no payment to miss. Even the enrollment mechanics announce the distinction. Part B deems certain disabled beneficiaries enrolled and then gives them a chance to decline, which is insurance logic working in reverse: the statute assumes the purchase and permits refusal. Part A needs no deeming provision, because entitlement does not wait on a decision.

The practical stakes are large. Debates about means testing, about premium support, and about raising the eligibility age are usually argued as budget questions. Under the master distinction they are really arguments about which services should migrate from the entitlement column to the enrollment column, and about what happens to beneficiaries when they do. Moving a benefit from entitlement to enrollment does not merely change who pays. It changes the beneficiary's posture from rights-holder to customer, introduces adverse selection where none existed, and replaces automatic coverage with an enrollment decision that can be missed, delayed, and penalized. That is why the fiercest fights in Medicare policy are always, underneath, fights about the master distinction. The statute's two logics cannot be merged without changing what the program is.

## The Custodial Care Exclusion: The Gap That Built a Second System

Section 1862 of the Social Security Act, codified at 42 U.S.C. 1395y, lists the items and services for which no Medicare payment may be made. Paragraph (a)(9) of that section excludes expenses for custodial care, with a narrow carve-out for hospice. That single exclusion is the most consequential coverage gap in the program. Medicare is the health insurer of the American elderly, and the single largest health-related expense many elderly people face is long-term residence in a nursing home. The statute excludes it. Nearly everything about the financing of long-term care in the United States flows from that sentence.

Custodial care, as the statute and its implementing rules use the term, means assistance with the activities of daily living that does not require the skill of trained medical personnel. Bathing, dressing, eating, toileting, transferring from bed to chair, and supervision for safety all fall on the custodial side of the line. This is precisely the care that a frail person needs indefinitely once independent living becomes impossible. Medicare's skilled nursing facility benefit covers a different thing entirely: a limited, post-hospital benefit for skilled rehabilitation and nursing care, conditioned on a preceding qualifying inpatient hospital stay. It is episode-based by design. It was never a residence benefit, and the custodial exclusion is the statutory reason it never became one.

The widespread belief that Medicare covers nursing homes is therefore false at the level of the statute. The belief persists because the program's name is on the card in the resident's wallet, because hospitals discharge patients to nursing facilities, and because the skilled nursing benefit creates the impression of a broader commitment. But impression is not text. The text at 42 U.S.C. 1395y(a)(9) bars payment for custodial care, and no regulation can override a statutory exclusion. A beneficiary who needs a nursing home indefinitely, with no skilled need, has no Medicare benefit to claim. That is the starting point, and the statute has never moved it.

The exclusion is also the mechanical reason Medicaid became the country's long-term care payer. The mechanism is arithmetic, not ideology. Medicare excludes custodial care. Medicaid, the joint federal-state program for people with limited income and resources, covers nursing facility services as a core benefit of its state plans. A population that needs custodial care, and that Medicare will not pay for, has exactly one public payer available. Elderly people who enter nursing homes pay privately until their resources are exhausted, and at that point the means-tested program takes over. The spend-down pattern that dominates long-term care financing is not a policy anyone chose. It is the predictable result of two statutes placed side by side: one that excludes the service, one that covers it for the impoverished. The people flow toward the coverage that exists. The mechanics of how Medicaid's eligibility and state-plan provisions absorbed this population are explained in our [explanation of the Medicaid statute's structure](/2011/02/01/medicaid-statute-structure-explained/), which traces the provisions that turned a program designed for the poor into the payer of last resort for the middle class's nursing home bills.

Congress understood the gap from the beginning and repeatedly declined to close it. The 1965 enactment designed Medicare as insurance against the cost of acute episodes: hospital stays, physician services, short rehabilitative spells. Long-term custodial care was left out, partly from cost and partly from a judgment that it belonged to a different kind of program. The Medicare Catastrophic Coverage Act of 1988 expanded the skilled nursing benefit and added other protections, then was repealed in 1989 after a revolt by the very beneficiaries it was meant to help, which taught Congress that even popular expansions of Medicare carry political risk when financed through beneficiary premiums. The health reform legislation enacted in 2010 created a voluntary long-term care insurance program alongside Medicare rather than folding custodial care into the Medicare benefit, which confirmed the original judgment. Custodial care remains a separate insurance problem, addressed, if at all, outside the Medicare statute.

The master distinction illuminates why the gap is so durable. Long-term custodial care is nearly impossible to finance under insurance logic. The need is predictable rather than random, which defeats risk pooling. The healthy young will not voluntarily buy a product they may need in forty years, and the late enrollment penalty machinery that makes Part B work cannot be stretched across a lifetime of deferred need. Under entitlement logic, by contrast, the financing would require a large compulsory tax to cover a benefit whose costs grow with longevity. Neither logic offers a comfortable home for the benefit, so the exclusion survives. Medicaid fills the vacuum by default, through means testing, which is a third logic entirely: need-based public assistance. The American long-term care system is thus a three-logic patchwork, and the patchwork exists because 42 U.S.C. 1395y(a)(9) drew a line in 1965 that no Congress has been willing to erase.

## Skilled Versus Custodial: Where the Statute Draws the Line

The difference between skilled nursing care and custodial care is not a matter of clinical impression or of how sick the patient looks. It is a statutory classification with a precise basis, and the precision matters because the classification decides whether Medicare pays. The skilled nursing facility benefit rests on two interlocking provisions. Section 1819, codified at 42 U.S.C. 1395i-3, defines a skilled nursing facility as an institution primarily engaged in providing skilled nursing care and related services for residents who require medical or nursing care, or rehabilitation services for injured, disabled, or sick persons. Section 1862(a)(9), the exclusion already discussed, bars payment for custodial care. The benefit and the exclusion are mirror images. The benefit covers the skilled. The exclusion bars the custodial. The line between them is drawn by what the services require, not by where they are furnished.

What the statute requires for skilled care can be stated plainly. The services must require the skill of technical or professional personnel: registered nurses, licensed practical nurses working under registered nurse supervision, physical therapists, occupational therapists, speech-language pathologists, and similarly trained professionals. They must be ordered by a physician. They must be furnished for a condition that requires treatment, on a regular basis, as part of a plan of care. Intravenous medications, sterile dressing changes, physical therapy after a hip fracture, and skilled observation of a changing clinical condition all qualify, because none of them can be performed safely by an untrained person. Assistance with bathing, dressing, and eating does not qualify, even when the patient desperately needs it, because it does not require professional skill. The statute looks at the nature of the service, not the severity of the patient's need. A profoundly disabled patient receiving only custodial assistance is, for Medicare purposes, receiving excluded care. A recovering surgical patient receiving daily physical therapy is receiving covered care. Need alone never crosses the line. Skill does.

The line is drawn where it is for a structural reason. Medicare was built as insurance against acute episodes, and the skilled nursing benefit extends that design into the rehabilitation period that follows a hospital stay. The benefit exists so that a patient who no longer needs a hospital bed but still needs professional care does not have to choose between staying in the hospital and going without treatment. If the benefit covered custodial care, it would cease to be an episode benefit and would become a general nursing-home entitlement, which is exactly what the exclusion forbids. The line holds the program inside its original architecture. Every attempt to blur it, whether by providers billing custodial services as skilled or by beneficiaries arguing that their need is great enough to deserve coverage, runs into the same statutory wall. The question is never how much care the patient needs. It is what kind of skill the care requires.

In practice the classification is made through medical review. Contractors examine the medical record for physician orders, for documentation that skilled personnel furnished the services, and for evidence that the services were reasonable and necessary for the treatment of a condition. Documentation discipline therefore decides coverage as much as clinical reality does. A facility that furnishes genuinely skilled care but documents it poorly can lose payment on review, while the underlying clinical facts have not changed. Beneficiaries and families experience this as arbitrariness, and sometimes the process is uneven, but the mechanism is the statute working as written. The benefit pays for skilled services that are documented as such. It pays for nothing else.

### What separates skilled nursing care from custodial care in the statute?

The statute covers skilled nursing facility care when the services require the skill of licensed professionals, are physician-ordered, and are furnished for treatment. Custodial care, which the statute excludes at section 1862(a)(9), is assistance with daily living that does not require skilled personnel. The credentials of the provider and clinical necessity, not the setting, decide the classification.

## Observation Status: The Part A and Part B Boundary Made Visible

No dispute in Medicare illustrates the master distinction more vividly than the fight over observation status. A beneficiary arrives at the hospital, spends days in a hospital bed, receives hospital nursing care, eats hospital food, and is monitored by hospital staff. The question that decides her coverage is not what happened to her. It is how the hospital classified the stay. If the stay was billed as an inpatient admission, Part A entitlement pays for it. If the stay was billed as outpatient observation, Part B pays for the covered services as hospital outpatient services, with different cost-sharing and different rules. The same bed, the same nurses, the same days produce two different programs, two different bills, and two different downstream consequences. The Part A and Part B line is not an academic taxonomy. It is the difference between which logic governs the beneficiary's money and rights.

The mechanics begin with the admission decision. Under the statute and its implementing rules, inpatient status requires a physician order admitting the patient as an inpatient and a clinical judgment that inpatient care is reasonable and necessary. Observation is an outpatient status, ordered by a physician, for patients who need monitoring and assessment to determine whether admission is warranted or who need a defined period of outpatient treatment. Hospitals make the initial call through their physicians and utilization review committees, and the classification is recorded in the billing. Part A claims go to the contractor that processes institutional claims. Part B claims go to the contractor that processes outpatient and physician claims. From that fork, everything diverges: the deductible and coinsurance structures differ, the appeal tracks differ, and, most consequentially, the downstream benefits differ.

The downstream consequence that matters most is skilled nursing facility coverage. The statute conditions the SNF benefit on a preceding qualifying inpatient hospital stay. Days spent in the hospital under observation status do not satisfy that condition, because observation is outpatient care no matter how many nights the beneficiary slept in the hospital bed. A beneficiary who spends several days under observation and is then discharged to a nursing facility for rehabilitation can discover that Medicare will not pay for the nursing facility stay, not because the rehabilitation is not skilled, and not because the hospital stay did not happen, but because the qualifying inpatient stay never occurred in the eyes of the statute. The nursing home bill that follows is the observation-status problem in its most painful form. It is also a pure product of the master distinction. Part A entitlement logic triggers the SNF benefit only when Part A was engaged. Part B insurance logic, which governed the observation stay, triggers no such downstream benefit. The beneficiary's clinical experience was continuous. The statute's logic was not.

The dispute persists because the classification decision is genuinely difficult and genuinely consequential, which makes it contested from both directions. Hospitals face pressure to classify stays correctly, because inpatient admissions draw medical review and contractors can deny payment for admissions they deem not reasonable and necessary, while observation stays draw a different kind of scrutiny. Beneficiaries face the financial consequences of the classification without having participated in making it. The physician's order, the utilization review committee's determination, and the contractor's medical review can all point in different directions, and each has a plausible claim to authority. The statute gives the hospital the initial classification power, gives the contractors the review power, and gives the beneficiary the appeal right, and the three do not always agree.

### Why does observation status cost beneficiaries Part A coverage?

Observation status bills the hospital stay as outpatient care under Part B rather than as an inpatient admission under Part A. Because Part A entitlement attaches only to inpatient admissions, the stay generates no Part A claim, and the qualifying inpatient stay that the statute requires before skilled nursing facility coverage is never established, blocking that downstream benefit.

Coverage in practice is determined through a layered review process rather than by any single decisionmaker. The hospital's utilization review committee applies the admission criteria first. The Medicare contractors then review claims for medical necessity, applying national coverage determinations, local coverage determinations, and the manual guidance that interprets the statute's reasonable-and-necessary standard. How those interpretive rules are made, and how providers and beneficiaries can participate in their making, is the subject of our [guide to CMS Medicare rulemaking](/2011/02/15/cms-medicare-rulemaking-guide/), which explains the notice-and-comment machinery that turns statutory phrases into enforceable coverage criteria. When a claim is denied, the beneficiary may appeal through the administrative process the statute provides, and the appeal will revisit the same classification question: was this an inpatient admission that Part A covers, or an outpatient encounter that Part B covers? The layers do not always produce the same answer, which is why the observation-status problem generates a steady volume of appeals and a steady volume of beneficiary confusion.

The beneficiary's remedy runs through the same classification machinery. A denial rooted in the inpatient-versus-outpatient determination can be appealed administratively, and the appeal will re-examine the physician's order, the hospital's utilization review, and the medical record against the coverage criteria. Quality improvement organizations, the entities that review the medical necessity of hospital services, hear the early stages of these disputes, and unfavorable decisions can advance to administrative law judges and beyond. At every level the question is identical: which logic applied to these days in this bed. The forum changes. The question does not.

The master distinction explains the confusion and points to the only honest way to describe it. Beneficiaries experience Medicare as one program and expect its logic to be continuous across a single episode of illness. The statute is not continuous. It is two programs with two logics, and the boundary between them runs through the hospital itself, sometimes through a single hallway, separating the inpatient ward from the observation unit. A beneficiary cannot navigate that boundary by clinical intuition, because the boundary is not clinical. It is statutory. Understanding that the question is always which logic applies, entitlement or enrollment, Part A or Part B, is the beginning of every correct answer the program gives.


## The verdict: the text decides cases, the brochure decides nothing

Brochures tell a beneficiary what Medicare is supposed to feel like. The statute tells a court what Medicare is required to be. That difference is the whole point of this article, and it is the single habit a reader should carry away from the four-part structure. Whenever a Medicare question arises, the question is not what a summary says or what a plan advertisement promised. The question is what Title XVIII of the Social Security Act provides, where it provides it, and what conditions it attaches to the benefit. A reader who can answer those three questions can route nearly any dispute to the right provision before arguing about the facts at all.

Consider what the four parts make legible. A claim about a hospital stay that Medicare refused to pay belongs to Part A, and the controlling language is the definition of covered inpatient hospital services and the conditions on skilled nursing and home health that attach to them. A claim about a physician who charged more than Medicare approved belongs to Part B, and the controlling language is the assignment framework and the physician payment provisions. A dispute about a private plan that denied a service belongs to Part C, and the controlling language is the contract the plan signed with the Secretary and the requirement that the plan cover what Parts A and B cover. A dispute about a prescription a plan would not fill belongs to Part D, and the controlling language is the formulary and exceptions process the plan was required to establish under its contract. Each of these is a different section of the statute, with different conditions, different appeals language, and different places where a denial can be challenged. Routing the question correctly is most of the work, and it is the work a brochure never teaches.

The four-part structure also predicts where disputes will cluster. Disputes cluster at the boundaries the statute draws and at the conditions the statute attaches. The boundary between Part A skilled nursing coverage and noncovered custodial care produces litigation because the statute covers one and excludes the other, and the facts of a given stay will fall on one side or the other. The boundary between Part B physician services and excluded services produces disputes for the same reason. The conditions on Part C plan contracts produce disputes whenever a plan reads its obligations more narrowly than the statute reads them, because the contract incorporates the statutory baseline. The formulary rules of Part D produce disputes whenever a drug sits off formulary, because the statute created the formulary and also created the exceptions process that is supposed to catch the cases the formulary misses. Once a reader sees that coverage is a set of statutory definitions plus conditions, the dispute map becomes predictable. Find the definition, find the conditions, find the exceptions process, and the answer is usually there.

The parts also carry their own review machinery, and knowing the machinery is part of reading the statute. Determinations under Parts A and B move through contractor redetermination, reconsideration, administrative law judge hearings, the Medicare Appeals Council, and finally judicial review, with each stage governed by its own statutory and regulatory rules. Part C disputes begin inside the plan, through organization determinations and reconsiderations, before they leave the plan's hands. Part D disputes begin with coverage determinations and redeterminations under the plan contract. These paths look similar from a distance, but the statute assigns each one to its own part, and a misfiled appeal is a lost appeal. The reader who has routed the question to the right part also knows which door to knock on first, which is a practical payoff that no brochure explains because no brochure is organized by statutory section.

The financing structure deserves the same attention, because it explains why the parts behave differently. Part A draws on the Hospital Insurance trust fund, fed by payroll contributions, which is why entitlement under Part A is tied to insured work status and why enrollment is automatic for most people at age sixty-five. Part B draws on the Supplementary Medical Insurance trust fund, fed by enrollee premiums and general revenues, which is why enrollment is voluntary and why the statute is careful about enrollment periods and late penalties. Part C and Part D are financed through the same two trust funds by way of payments to private plans, which is why the plan contracts read the way they do: the government pays the plan to deliver the statutory benefit, and the plan's obligations are measured against the statute's baseline. A reader who sees the two trust funds behind the four parts understands that the enrollment differences, the premium differences, and the plan-contract structure are not accidents. They are the financing provisions working themselves out through the rest of the scheme.

None of this requires memorizing the statute. It requires respecting the hierarchy of authority that the statute sets up. Congress wrote the entitlement, the Secretary implements it through regulation, the plans contract under it, and the beneficiary experiences the result of all three. When those layers conflict, the higher one controls, and the higher one is the statutory text. That is why a reader who has absorbed the four-part structure reads differently from a reader who has only absorbed the brochure. The brochure reader asks what Medicare covers. The statute reader asks which part governs, what that part defines as covered, what conditions it imposes, and where a denial can be reviewed. The second question is harder. It is also the one that decides cases.

## What to carry forward

The habit transfers. Medicare is only one statute, and the method that unlocks it unlocks others. Read the definitions before the summaries. Separate the entitlement from the administration. Ask who is eligible, who pays, who decides, and where the decision can be challenged, because those four questions are what every benefits statute is built from, even when the statute uses different words for them. A reader who applies those questions to Title XVIII can apply them to any part of the Social Security Act, and a reader who can apply them to the Social Security Act can apply them to any federal benefits scheme. The specific provisions change. The architecture of questions does not.

Notice, too, how the four parts reward a particular kind of attention. The statute rewards the reader who slows down at the definitions section of each part, because the definitions control everything downstream. Part A defines inpatient hospital services, skilled nursing facility care, home health, and hospice with a precision that makes most coverage disputes resolvable on the face of the text. Part B defines medical and other health services as a broad category and then carves out exclusions one by one, which is why Part B disputes so often turn on whether a service falls inside an exclusion rather than inside a definition. Part C defines the plan's obligation by reference to Parts A and B, which is why the Part C reader must always hold the other two parts in mind. Part D defines covered drugs through the formulary mechanism, which is why the Part D reader must always check the plan's formulary and the exceptions process together. Different parts, different drafting techniques, same lesson: the answer is in the definitions, and the definitions repay close reading.

There is a further reason to keep the habit. Statutes are amended, regulations are rewritten, and plan contracts turn over every year, but the structure of the text remains the thing that survives the turnover. A brochure from any given enrollment season is obsolete the moment the season ends. The four parts of Title XVIII, with their distinct entitlements, enrollment mechanisms, financing streams, and coverage definitions, remain the framework inside which every later change has to fit. A reader who knows the framework can place any new development inside it on sight, because the development has to change a specific part, a specific definition, or a specific condition to change anything at all. That is the durable payoff of reading the statute instead of the summary.

Keep the table below close while reading. It compresses the four parts into the seven questions that matter for each: the statutory location, the source of entitlement, how enrollment works, where the money comes from, what is principally covered, and what is principally excluded. Every Medicare dispute can be placed somewhere in that table, and once placed, the dispute usually resolves into a question of statutory definition rather than a question of fairness. Fairness is for the legislature. Definition is for the reader, and the reader who holds the table holds the definitions.

Looking ahead, the method holds its value whatever comes next. New questions will arrive as amendments to specific sections, regulations interpreting specific sections, or plan contracts operating under specific sections, because that is the only way the system can change. The reader who holds the four-part map can place each development in the part it touches, check the definition it alters, and see whether the change reaches entitlement, enrollment, financing, or coverage. That is the stance this article has been building toward from the start. The statute is not a monument to be admired from a distance. It is a working document to be read closely, section by section, every time a question matters, and the reader who reads it that way will always have the advantage over the reader who read the brochure.

The advantage compounds. Each dispute routed to the right part, each definition read before the summary, each condition checked before the conclusion, makes the next reading faster and the next dispute easier to place. Over time the four parts stop feeling like four separate programs and start feeling like one scheme with four faces, which is what they are. The reader who reaches that point no longer needs to be told where a question belongs. The table says it, the definitions confirm it, and the text does the rest. That is the verdict, and it is also the method: trust the operative text, because it is the only version of Medicare that decides anything.

## Reading the section numbers

A reader new to statutory citation should know that the section numbers used throughout this article are the Social Security Act numbers, and they map to the United States Code in a predictable way. The Act numbering and the Code numbering run in parallel, with the Code number always traceable back to the Act section. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the difference between Medicare Part A and Part B?

Part A is Hospital Insurance. People who reach 65 with enough covered work history, along with certain disabled and chronically ill beneficiaries, are entitled to it automatically, and it pays for inpatient hospital care, skilled nursing facility stays, hospice, and some home health services. Part B is Supplementary Medical Insurance, a voluntary program that requires enrollment and a monthly premium; it pays for physician services, outpatient hospital care, diagnostic tests, durable medical equipment, and preventive services. The two parts draw on separate trust funds: Part A on the Hospital Insurance trust fund financed mainly by payroll taxes, and Part B on the Supplementary Medical Insurance trust fund financed by enrollee premiums plus general revenue. Cost-sharing also differs, with Part A built around hospital deductibles and coinsurance and Part B around an annual deductible and coinsurance.

### Q: What does Medicare Part A cover?

Part A pays for inpatient hospital services, including semiprivate room and board, nursing and related services, and drugs and supplies furnished during the stay. It covers skilled nursing facility care after a qualifying hospital stay, home health services in defined circumstances, and hospice care for terminally ill beneficiaries who elect it. Inpatient psychiatric hospital care is covered subject to a lifetime limit set by statute. Part A does not cover private-duty nursing, personal comfort items such as television or telephone, or a private room unless medically necessary. Coverage follows entitlement: beneficiaries generally qualify at 65 through covered employment, through disability, or through end-stage renal disease, and those without enough work history may buy into Part A by paying a premium.

### Q: What does Medicare Part B cover?

Part B pays for physician services in offices, clinics, and hospitals, including surgery, consultations, and diagnostic and therapeutic services. It covers outpatient hospital services, clinical laboratory tests, diagnostic X-rays, durable medical equipment such as wheelchairs and oxygen, ambulance transportation when medically required, and outpatient mental health services. Part B also pays for physical, occupational, and speech therapy, kidney dialysis, specified preventive screenings and vaccines, and a limited set of prescription drugs administered in clinical settings, such as infused and injectable medications. Home health services not tied to a prior hospital stay fall under Part B. Enrollees pay a monthly premium, an annual deductible, and coinsurance for most services under this part.

### Q: Who is eligible for Medicare?

Individuals 65 or older who are entitled to Social Security or Railroad Retirement benefits, or who have enough covered work history, receive premium-free Part A automatically. People under 65 qualify after receiving Social Security disability benefits for a statutory waiting period, and people of any age with end-stage renal disease or amyotrophic lateral sclerosis qualify under special rules. Those 65 or older without enough work history may enroll in premium Part A by paying a monthly premium. Part B is open to anyone 65 or older and to those entitled to Part A through disability or renal disease, but enrollment is voluntary and requires a premium. Citizens and lawfully present residents who satisfy the age, work, and disability rules complete the core eligibility picture.

### Q: Is Medicare Part B voluntary?

Yes. Unlike Part A, which attaches automatically for entitled beneficiaries, Part B requires an affirmative enrollment decision, and enrollees pay a monthly premium for it. Most people enroll during an initial enrollment period around their 65th birthday; those who delay without creditable employer coverage face a permanent late-enrollment penalty added to the premium. Special enrollment periods protect people who kept working past 65 with group health coverage. Beneficiaries may also disenroll from Part B. The voluntary design traces to 1965: Part B was the compromise layer, modeled on physician-sponsored insurance, added to the hospital insurance core so that doctors' services would be covered without making the entire program compulsory.

### Q: What does section 1801 of the Medicare statute say?

Section 1801 of the Social Security Act, codified at 42 U.S.C. 1395, states that nothing in the Medicare title shall be construed to authorize any federal officer or employee to exercise any supervision or control over the practice of medicine, the manner in which medical services are provided, or the operation of any institution, agency, or person providing health services. Congress added it in 1965 to reassure physicians and hospitals that Medicare would pay bills without dictating clinical decisions, answering American Medical Association fears of government-run medicine. Courts have read it as a limit on federal power rather than a grant of rights: it does not create an entitlement to any particular treatment or shield providers from valid payment rules.

### Q: How are Medicare Part A and Part B financed differently?

Part A is financed primarily through payroll taxes on earnings, collected under the Federal Insurance Contributions Act and credited to the Hospital Insurance trust fund, which pays Part A benefits and administration. Part B has no payroll tax; it draws on the Supplementary Medical Insurance trust fund, financed by monthly premiums paid by enrollees together with general revenue contributions from the federal budget that cover most of the program's cost. This split reflects the 1965 design: hospital insurance as an earned, work-based benefit and medical insurance as a subsidized voluntary supplement. Because the funding bases differ, the two trust funds face different long-range outlooks, with the Hospital Insurance fund dependent on wage growth and the SMI fund backed by general revenues.

### Q: What is Medicare Part C and where did it come from?

Part C is the private-plan alternative known as Medicare Advantage. Its roots lie in the 1970s and 1980s, when Medicare first contracted with health maintenance organizations on a cost or risk basis. The Balanced Budget Act of 1997 created the Medicare+Choice program, expanding the types of private plans that could contract with Medicare and revising the payment rules governing them. The Medicare Modernization Act of 2003 renamed it Medicare Advantage and reworked plan payments and bidding. Under Part C, a beneficiary enrolls in a private plan that receives a capitated payment from Medicare and must provide at least the benefits of Parts A and B, often adding extras such as dental or vision coverage.

### Q: What medical services does the Medicare statute expressly exclude from coverage?

Section 1862(a) lists the exclusions, and they apply across Parts A and B. Payment is barred for services that are not reasonable and necessary; routine physical checkups, except for screenings Congress later added; routine dental care; routine foot care; hearing aids and examinations to fit them; eyeglasses, except one pair after cataract surgery; cosmetic surgery, except to repair accidental injury or improve a malformed body member; custodial care; personal comfort items; most immunizations, except specified vaccines; experimental services; services furnished by immediate relatives; services payable under workers' compensation; services for which the beneficiary has no legal obligation to pay; and services furnished outside the United States, with narrow exceptions. These exclusions define the boundary of the program.

### Q: Does Medicare Part A or Part B cover outpatient prescription drugs?

Generally no. Parts A and B were built around hospital and physician services, and the statute excludes most drugs a beneficiary takes at home. Part A covers drugs furnished during a covered inpatient or skilled nursing stay as part of the facility payment. Part B covers a limited set of drugs administered incident to physician services: injectable and infused drugs given in a doctor's office, certain oral anti-cancer drugs, immunosuppressive drugs after a covered organ transplant, and drugs used with durable medical equipment such as infusion pumps. Broad outpatient prescription drug coverage arrived only with Part D, created by the 2003 Medicare Modernization Act, which operates as a separate voluntary benefit delivered through private plans.

### Q: What is the Medicare hospice benefit and which Part pays for it?

Hospice is paid under Part A. A beneficiary certified as terminally ill with a life expectancy of six months or less may elect hospice, waiving standard Medicare payment for curative treatment of the terminal condition in exchange for palliative care: nursing, physician services, drugs for symptom control, counseling, short-term inpatient and respite care, and home health aide services. The benefit runs in election periods, and the beneficiary may revoke the election at any time and return to standard coverage. Congress added hospice through the Tax Equity and Fiscal Responsibility Act of 1982 after demonstration projects showed the model served dying patients better than fragmented acute care. It remains one of the few benefits defined by the patient's goals rather than by a diagnosis.

### Q: Does Medicare pay for long-term custodial care in a nursing home?

No. Medicare pays for skilled nursing facility care only when it follows a qualifying hospital stay, requires daily skilled nursing or rehabilitation services, and is certified by a physician, and coverage is time-limited within each benefit period. Once care becomes custodial, meaning help with daily living activities without a skilled medical need, Medicare does not pay, even if the beneficiary remains in the facility. Long stays for chronic conditions, supervision for dementia, or assistance with bathing, dressing, and eating fall outside the statute. Medicaid, not Medicare, finances most long-term nursing home care for people who meet its financial eligibility rules, which is why families often confuse the two programs.

### Q: What is the home health services benefit under Medicare?

Home health covers part-time or intermittent skilled nursing care, physical therapy, speech-language pathology, occupational therapy, medical social services, and part-time home health aide services for a beneficiary who is homebound and under a physician-established plan of care. Part A pays when the beneficiary had a qualifying hospital or skilled nursing stay; otherwise Part B pays. The benefit grew rapidly in the 1980s and early 1990s, and the Balanced Budget Act of 1997 moved most home health spending under a prospective payment system and tightened eligibility rules. Coverage always requires a skilled need; purely custodial help, housekeeping, and meal preparation are excluded even when a homebound patient plainly needs them.

### Q: What preventive and screening services does Medicare Part B cover?

Part B pays for specified preventive services even though the statute generally excludes routine physicals. Covered services include mammography, Pap smears and pelvic examinations, prostate cancer screening, colorectal cancer screening, diabetes screening and self-management training, glaucoma screening for high-risk beneficiaries, cardiovascular screening blood tests, bone mass measurement, and an initial preventive physical examination for newly enrolled beneficiaries. Covered vaccines include influenza, pneumococcal, and hepatitis B for high-risk groups. Congress added these benefits one by one through later legislation, each carving an exception into the original exclusion of routine care, so the preventive list reflects decades of incremental lawmaking rather than a single design.

### Q: What does "reasonable and necessary" mean in Medicare coverage decisions?

Section 1862(a)(1)(A) bars payment for items and services that are not reasonable and necessary for the diagnosis or treatment of illness or injury, or to improve the functioning of a malformed body member. This is the central coverage gatekeeper across Parts A and B. CMS and its contractors apply it through national coverage determinations, local coverage determinations, and individual claim review, asking whether a service is safe, effective, medically appropriate, and furnished in the proper setting. The standard is why experimental treatments, cosmetic procedures, and services with insufficient evidence of benefit can be denied even when a physician ordered them, and it generates much of the litigation and rulemaking that surrounds Medicare coverage policy.

### Q: Does Medicare cover health care received outside the United States?

Only in three narrow statutory exceptions. Medicare pays for emergency inpatient hospital services in Canada or Mexico when the emergency arose near the border and the foreign hospital was closer than any adequate American one; for inpatient services in a foreign hospital closer to the beneficiary's American residence than the nearest adequate domestic hospital; and for care aboard a ship within American territorial waters or within six hours of an American port. Routine or planned care abroad, foreign physician services, and medical tourism are never covered. Beneficiaries who travel or live outside the country need other coverage, such as retiree health plans, travel insurance, or the health system of the country they are visiting.

### Q: What conditions must be met for Part A to pay for a skilled nursing facility stay?

The beneficiary must first have a qualifying inpatient hospital stay, then enter the skilled nursing facility within a defined window after discharge, and require daily skilled nursing or skilled rehabilitation services for a condition treated during that hospital stay. A physician must certify the need for skilled care and the plan of treatment. Coverage is limited to a set number of days per benefit period, with cost-sharing after an initial period funded by the Hospital Insurance trust fund. The statute requires a genuine inpatient admission; time billed as outpatient hospital services does not qualify. Once the need for skilled care ends, coverage ends with it, and any continued stay becomes non-covered custodial care.

### Q: What is the Medicare secondary payer rule?

The Medicare Secondary Payer provisions, added in the 1980s, make Medicare the secondary payer whenever another insurer bears primary responsibility: group health plans covering working beneficiaries aged 65 or older or disabled beneficiaries, automobile and liability insurance, no-fault insurance, and workers' compensation. In those situations the other payer must pay first, and Medicare pays only what remains within its allowed amount. The statute also gives the government a right of recovery, allowing it to recoup mistaken Medicare payments from the primary payer, the beneficiary, or another responsible party. These rules protect the trust funds by shifting costs to liable private and employer coverage before Medicare dollars are spent, and they create the coordination-of-benefits machinery behind millions of claims.

### Q: Does Medicare cover routine dental, vision, or hearing care?

Generally no. Section 1862(a)(12) excludes routine dental services, including cleanings, fillings, extractions, and dentures, covering dental work only when it is integral to a covered medical procedure such as jaw reconstruction after an accident. Routine eye examinations to prescribe glasses and eyeglasses themselves are excluded, with an exception for one pair after cataract surgery with an implanted intraocular lens. Hearing aids and the examinations to fit them are excluded outright. These exclusions date to the original 1965 statute and survived repeated repeal efforts through 2010. Beneficiaries typically pay out of pocket for these services or rely on Part C plans and supplemental coverage that choose to include them.

### Q: What is the "spell of illness" concept in Part A hospital coverage?

A "spell of illness," also called a benefit period, is the unit the statute uses to measure Part A inpatient coverage. It begins when a beneficiary is admitted to a hospital or skilled nursing facility and ends after the beneficiary has remained out of inpatient care for a continuous period defined by statute. Each new spell triggers a fresh inpatient deductible and a renewed set of covered hospital and skilled nursing days. The concept keeps a single prolonged episode of illness from drawing unlimited benefits while ensuring that genuinely separate episodes each receive full coverage. It underlies the Part A cost-sharing structure for hospital stays and post-hospital skilled nursing care and explains why readmissions after a long gap start the deductible anew.
