Citing the novel right in MLA: MLA style has specific works-cited and in-text forms for The Great Gatsby and its sources, so getting them exact matters, the correct edition, page references, and entry format being the difference between a credible apparatus and a flawed one. An essay can argue brilliantly about the green light, the ash valley, and the hollow hospitality of the Buchanan house, yet lose authority the moment its documentation slips, a misplaced period or a fabricated publisher making the reader doubt every page reference that follows. Documentation is the machinery under the argument, and MLA, now in its ninth edition, gives that machinery a fixed shape: one entry pattern for the novel, one pattern for a scholarly edition with an editor, one pattern for a journal article, one pattern for a book chapter, and one pattern for a digital text. Learn those five shapes and the whole essay holds together; guess at them and the apparatus creaks. The sections that follow build each shape from its first principles, so that by the end the writer can produce any of the five from memory and recognize any of them on sight.

The reason a dedicated guide matters is that The Great Gatsby is not cited the way a generic book is cited in every practical detail, even though it follows the generic book pattern at the structural level. The novel first appeared in 1925, and students meet it in many different physical forms: a paperback bought for class, a school edition with an introduction, a scholarly text with editorial notes, a library e-book, and a public-domain digital file. Each of those forms changes exactly one or two slots in the works-cited entry, and the temptation is to treat every copy as the same source. It is not the same source for documentation purposes, because the edition determines the publisher, the year, the editor when one exists, and the container when the text arrives through a database or e-book platform. The in-text citation, by contrast, barely changes at all: the author's name and a page number carry almost the entire load. That asymmetry, a stable in-text form paired with an edition-sensitive entry, is the single most useful thing to understand before touching the keyboard.

This guide works from the MLA Handbook, ninth edition, the edition published in 2021 that governs current MLA documentation. Everything here follows the container model that the eighth and ninth editions share: a citation names the author, the title of the source, and then the containers that deliver it, with other contributors, versions, publishers, dates, and locations filling slots in a fixed order. The novel's entry, repeated identically every time it appears in this guide, is the anchor:

Fitzgerald, F. Scott. *The Great Gatsby*. Scribner, 1925.

Memorize that line the way a musician memorizes a scale. The period after the author's name, the period after the italicized title, the comma after the publisher, and the final period are not decoration; they are the punctuation grammar of the system, and every other entry in this guide is a variation on this one. When the guide shows a journal article or a book chapter later, watch how the author's name keeps its inverted form, how the title of the smaller work takes quotation marks, how the title of the larger work takes italics, and how the same sequence of commas and periods carries through. The system rewards pattern recognition, and the pattern begins here.

Before the sections begin, a word on what this guide does not try to do. It does not choose your edition for you; the choice between a classroom paperback, an annotated scholarly text, and a digital file is a separate decision with its own logic, and the editions section below treats the documentation side of that choice at full length. It does not teach APA or Chicago forms either; a separate companion covers [APA citation forms](/2031/01/13/gatsby-cite-apa/) for writers whose course requires that style. What follows stays inside MLA territory and stays concrete: the exact entries, the exact in-text forms, worked examples for each source type, a walkthrough of a complete works-cited page, the errors that recur most often with before and after corrections, and practice tasks that turn the patterns into habit.

## Why the Novel Follows One Exact Pattern in MLA

The MLA works-cited entry for a book with one author is the simplest entry in the whole system, which is why getting it wrong is so conspicuous. The pattern is: author's name inverted, book title in italics, publisher, year. Four elements, three periods, one comma. There is no city of publication in the ninth edition, a detail that trips writers who learned an older style where the city appeared, and there is no access date for a print book. Applied to the novel, the entry reads:

Fitzgerald, F. Scott. *The Great Gatsby*. Scribner, 1925.

Consider why each element sits where it does. The inverted name, last name first, exists so the works-cited list can be alphabetized by surname; Fitzgerald files under F, not under the first name initial. The title in italics signals a self-contained work, the same treatment a film or a stand-alone book of poems receives, as opposed to the quotation marks that mark a smaller work inside a larger one. The publisher names the body that produced this particular text, and the year dates that production. For the first edition of the novel that publisher is Scribner and that year is 1925, the year Charles Scribner's Sons issued the novel. A reprint from another press changes those last two slots, which is why naming the actual copy in hand matters so much.

Students sometimes ask whether the entry should name the author's full first name or accept an initial, and the rule is simple: transcribe the name as it appears on the title page of the copy being cited. The novel's title page gives F. Scott Fitzgerald, and the entry preserves that form. The middle initial stays, the period after the initial stays, and no expansion to Francis is attempted. Transcription beats reconstruction everywhere in MLA; the entry describes the source as it exists, not as background knowledge says it should exist.

The hanging indent is the entry's visual signature and the detail most often skipped. In the final paper, the first line of the entry sits flush with the left margin and every following line indents one half inch. Most word processors apply this with a paragraph setting rather than with spaces or tabs, and the setting must be applied to the whole works-cited page rather than entry by entry. A page of flush-left entries looks unfinished to any instructor who grades documentation, because the indent is what makes each entry scannable as a unit. The sample works-cited page later in this guide shows the arrangement in full.

Alphabetization governs the order of entries, and the novel's entry participates in it like any other. Entries sort by the first word of the entry, which for the novel is Fitzgerald, ignoring the A, An, and The that would otherwise distort the order if an entry began with a title. When two works by Fitzgerald appear in one list, the author's name is written out for the first entry and replaced by three hyphens followed by a period for the second, with the entries sorted by title. A writer citing both the novel and a Fitzgerald short story would list the novel first if its title alphabetizes first, then the hyphen line for the story. The mechanics are fussy, but they are the same mechanics for every author, and mastering them once pays off across every course that uses MLA.

## A Sample Works-Cited Page, Annotated Line by Line

Abstract rules become concrete fastest through a full example, so this section builds a complete works-cited page for a hypothetical student essay and annotates every decision. The imagined essay argues that the novel's party scenes stage wealth as performance; it quotes the novel in a scholarly edition, draws on one journal article about symbolism, and uses one book chapter on Fitzgerald's narrative technique. Three sources, three entries, each demonstrating a different pattern from the artifact table.

The page opens with the centered heading Works Cited. Centered means centered, not left aligned and not decorated; the words carry no quotation marks, no italics, and no bold. The heading sits on its own line, and the first entry begins on the next line within the same double spacing that governs the whole paper. Everything on the page is double spaced, entries and heading alike, with no extra blank lines inserted between entries. The entries use the hanging indent throughout. These typographic facts sound trivial until a paper arrives with a left-aligned underlined heading and single-spaced entries, at which point the documentation looks like an afterthought regardless of the argument's quality.

The first entry, alphabetically, is the scholarly edition of the novel:

Fitzgerald, F. Scott. *The Great Gatsby: The Authorized Text*. Edited by Matthew J. Bruccoli, Scribner, 1991.

Walk the entry slot by slot. Fitzgerald, F. Scott. The surname leads because the list alphabetizes by surname; the comma marks the inversion; the period closes the author element. *The Great Gatsby: The Authorized Text*. The full title as the edition presents it, subtitle and all, in italics because the novel is a self-contained work; the period closes the title element. Edited by Matthew J. Bruccoli, The other-contributors slot: the editor's name in natural order, because this slot describes rather than alphabetizes, introduced by the label Edited by, and closed with a comma because more elements follow. Scribner, The publisher of this edition, followed by a comma. 1991. The year this edition was produced, closed with the final period. A student who actually read this edition cites its pages, and every parenthetical in the essay points to this entry through the surname Fitzgerald.

Notice what the entry does not contain. It does not contain the novel's original 1925 publication date, because the entry describes the 1991 edition, not the work's debut. It does not contain the city of publication, because the ninth edition dropped that element. It does not contain an access date, because a print book is stable. It does not contain the editor's name in inverted form, because only the lead author inverts. Each absence is as deliberate as each presence, and a verifier checking the entry against the artifact table can confirm every one.

The second entry is the journal article, shown with illustrative details:

Scholar, Jane. "Reading the Green Light: Symbol and Structure in *The Great Gatsby*." *Journal of Literary Studies*, vol. 12, no. 3, 2021, pp. 45-67.

The annotation follows the same method. Scholar, Jane. Inverted lead author for alphabetization; the entry files under S, after Fitzgerald's F. "Reading the Green Light: Symbol and Structure in *The Great Gatsby*." The article title in quotation marks, because an article is a smaller work inside a larger one; the novel's title nested inside keeps its italics, because a self-contained work does not lose its formatting inside a quotation. The period sits inside the closing quotation mark, closing the title element. *Journal of Literary Studies*, The container, italicized, followed by a comma. vol. 12, no. 3, The volume and number in MLA's abbreviated lowercase forms, each followed by a comma because the entry continues. 2021, The issue year, followed by a comma. pp. 45-67. The page range of the article within the issue, with the pp. abbreviation that belongs in the works-cited entry and never in a parenthetical, closed with the final period.

The third entry is the book chapter, also illustrative:

Writer, John. "Craft and Design in Fitzgerald's Novel." *Approaches to the American Novel*, edited by Ellen Editor, Academic Press, 2021, pp. 100-125.

Writer, John. Inverted, filing under W. "Craft and Design in Fitzgerald's Novel." Quotation marks for the chapter, period inside the closing mark. *Approaches to the American Novel*, The container in italics. edited by Ellen Editor, The contributors slot in natural order with its label, comma after because the entry continues. Academic Press, 2021, Publisher and year with the separating comma. pp. 100-125. The chapter's range within the book, final period.

Now read the three entries together as the finished page would show them, and check the alphabetization: Fitzgerald, Scholar, Writer. Correct. Check the hanging indents: each entry's first line flush left, wrapped lines indented. Check the double spacing: uniform. Check that every entry ends with a period. A final verification pass asks of each entry whether the in-text citations in the essay can find it: the novel's parentheticals use (Fitzgerald 47) and match the first entry's surname; the article's use (Scholar 49); the chapter's use (Writer 104). No parenthetical points to a missing entry, and no entry sits uncited. That two-way matching, every citation resolving and every entry used, is the functional definition of a correct works-cited page.

One more exercise sharpens the skill this walkthrough teaches. Take the same three entries and convert the scenario: the student instead read the novel as a plain-text file from a public archive. The first entry changes to the digital form, naming the archive as the container or publisher with the URL; the article and chapter entries stay as they are. The alphabetization does not change, because the lead author is still Fitzgerald. The in-text citations for the novel change only in their locators, chapters or sections replacing page numbers. Everything else on the page, the heading, the spacing, the indents, the punctuation grammar, remains identical. The container model absorbs the change without disturbing the system, which is exactly why the model replaced the old format-by-format memorization.

## Building the Works-Cited Entry Piece by Piece

The entry looks short, but each of its four slots carries decisions worth understanding, because the decisions recur in every other entry type. Start with the author slot. Fitzgerald, F. Scott. The inversion puts the surname first, the given name second, and a period closes the element. When an edition adds an editor, the editor's name enters a different slot, after the title, in natural order with the label editor or editors, as the edition section shows. The author slot never absorbs the editor, and the editor never replaces the author; the novel remains Fitzgerald's work even when a scholar prepared the text.

The title slot is next. *The Great Gatsby*. The italics mark a complete, independently published work. Capitalization follows title case for English titles in MLA: the first word, the last word, and all principal words capitalized, with articles, short prepositions, and coordinating conjunctions in lowercase unless they open or close the title. The novel's title is short enough that every word is principal, so The Great Gatsby appears exactly so. Subtitles would follow a colon in the same italics, but the novel has no subtitle, which keeps the slot clean.

The publisher slot names Scribner for the original publication, and here the ninth edition's abbreviation rules matter. University presses shorten University to U and Press to P, so University of Alabama Press becomes UP of Alabama, but a trade name like Scribner stands as it is. When the copy in hand is a reprint from another publisher, that publisher's name goes in the slot, because the entry describes the copy consulted. A student citing a recent Scribner paperback reprint would write Scribner followed by the reprint's year, not Scribner, 1925, since the entry dates the production of the copy, and the in-text page numbers must match that copy's pagination anyway.

The year slot closes the entry with a period. For the first edition the year is 1925. The temptation to write the year of first publication when citing a later reprint is one of the most common small errors in student papers, and it creates a quiet mismatch: the entry claims 1925 while every page reference comes from a recent reprint's pagination. Instructors who check documentation notice the mismatch because the reprint's introduction or copyright page betrays it. Date the copy, not the work's debut, and the entry stays honest.

A complete works-cited page walkthrough makes the abstract concrete. Imagine a short research essay that uses the novel itself, a journal article about the novel's symbolism, and a book chapter on Fitzgerald's craftsmanship. The works-cited page carries the centered heading Works Cited, the entries are double spaced throughout, and each entry uses the hanging indent. The novel's entry leads or follows according to alphabetization. A typical page would read, in order:

Fitzgerald, F. Scott. *The Great Gatsby*. Scribner, 1925.

followed by the article and chapter entries in their own correct forms, each a self-contained unit. The page contains no extra blank lines between entries beyond the double spacing, no bullets, no numbers, and no annotations; it is a list in the typographic sense but not in the bulleted sense, a distinction the ninth edition maintains carefully. The FAQ section at the end of this guide answers the fine points, from where the page sits in the paper to how the indent is formatted.

One more decision belongs in this section: what to do when the course or the library copy presents the novel inside a larger volume, such as a collected Fitzgerald or an anthology of American novels. Then the novel is no longer the outermost container; the collection is. The entry begins with Fitzgerald, gives the novel's title in italics, then names the collection in italics as the container, followed by the editor, publisher, year, and the page range of the novel within the volume. The pattern is the book-chapter pattern with the roles reversed in size, and the section on book chapters works through the mechanics. The key insight is that the entry always mirrors the physical or digital object consulted, and the object determines the containers.

## Signal Phrases and the Flow of Quotation

The signal phrase is the clause that introduces borrowed words, and it does more structural work than its modest size suggests. A signal phrase names the source, characterizes the borrowed material, and sets the grammatical terms on which the quotation enters the sentence. Because it names the author, it also triggers the shortened parenthetical, so mastering signal phrases is simultaneously mastering citation economy. Writers who treat the signal phrase as mere throat clearing, a mechanical "Fitzgerald says" before every quote, miss its capacity to carry interpretation into the quotation itself.

The verb in the signal phrase is the interpretation. "Fitzgerald writes" is neutral and always safe; "Fitzgerald insists," "the narrator concedes," "Daisy deflects" each smuggle a reading into the introduction. The verb should match what the quoted passage actually does, because a mismatched verb creates a small false promise: "Fitzgerald proves" before a passage that merely suggests something will irritate a careful reader. A useful discipline is to keep a mental roster of accurate verbs, observes, notes, describes, implies, stages, juxtaposes, and to choose the one the passage earns. The roster grows with practice, and the quotations start doing double duty, evidence and analysis arriving together.

Grammar governs the junction between the signal phrase and the quotation. When the signal phrase is a complete sentence, a colon introduces the quotation: Fitzgerald stages the novel's central irony in a single tableau: "the rich get richer while the poor watch from the valley of ashes" (47). When the signal phrase flows grammatically into the quotation, a comma or no punctuation serves: Fitzgerald describes the evening as "portentous" (47), where the quoted word completes the sentence's syntax. When the quotation is woven mid-sentence, the grammar must remain seamless, with the quoted words fitting the sentence as if the writer had written them, because a broken sentence around a quotation reads as carelessness even when the citation is perfect.

The page-only parenthetical after a signal phrase is the reward for this care. Once Fitzgerald's name stands in the sentence, (47) alone suffices, and a paragraph can move through several quotations with a lightness that the full (Fitzgerald 47) form repeated would destroy. The technique extends to paraphrase: "Fitzgerald presents the Buchanan house as gleaming before any guest arrives (47)" needs no surname in the parentheses because the sentence supplies it. The rule to internalize is that the author's name must appear once per citation unit, either in the prose or in the parentheses, never in both and never in neither.

A common failure mode is the dropped quotation, a quotation with no signal phrase at all, set into the paragraph like a stone into a wall. Dropped quotations force the reader to infer the connection between the borrowed words and the argument, and they waste the interpretive power of the verb. The fix is mechanical: no quotation enters the essay without an introduction that says who speaks and why these words matter here. The introduction can be as short as three words, "Fitzgerald's narrator notes," but it must exist. Instructors flag dropped quotations reliably, because they signal a writer who collected evidence without deciding what it proves.

Another failure mode is the overlong signal phrase that restates the quotation before it arrives, so the reader meets the idea twice and the quotation itself becomes redundant. "Fitzgerald shows that wealth is performative by showing the parties as performances of wealth: [quotation about parties]" says the same thing in the introduction and the evidence. The signal phrase should frame, not duplicate; it tells the reader what to watch for, and the quotation delivers it. A well-built signal phrase is a lens, not a summary.

The block quotation's signal phrase deserves its own note. Because the block is visually separated, the introduction usually ends with a colon after a complete sentence, and the colon acts as a drumroll the paragraph's rhythm can support. A fragmentary introduction before a block quotation feels unfinished, as if the writer trailed off; a complete sentence with a colon feels deliberate. After the block, the essay must resume with analysis, because a block quotation followed by a new topic strands the evidence. The paragraph that contains a block quotation should be the longest paragraph in its vicinity, with the analysis outweighing the borrowed text, or the essay starts to read as an anthology with commentary.

## In-Text Citation Mechanics for the Novel

The in-text citation for the novel is deliberately minimal: the author's surname and a page number in parentheses, with no comma between them and no abbreviation for page. The form is (Fitzgerald 47), where 47 stands for whatever page the quoted or paraphrased material occupies in the copy cited. When the author's name already appears in the sentence, the parenthetical shrinks to the page number alone, as in: Fitzgerald describes the Buchanan house as gleaming with reflected light (47). Both forms point the reader to the same works-cited entry, and the reader finds the entry by matching the surname.

The page number rule deserves emphasis because it is the most violated rule in the in-text system. MLA uses the page number bare, without p. or pp., in the parenthetical. The abbreviations p. and pp. survive in the works-cited entry for articles and chapters, where they mark page ranges of the source within its container, but they never appear in the parenthetical. Writing (Fitzgerald p. 47) is wrong in MLA, and it is wrong in a way that signals unfamiliarity with the style more loudly than almost any other small error. The bare number is the house style; learn it as a reflex.

Signal phrases, the clauses that introduce quotations, do real work in the in-text system beyond mere variety. A signal phrase like "Fitzgerald writes" or "the narrator observes" places the author's name in the sentence, which then allows the shorter parenthetical with the page number alone. This is not just elegance; it is the mechanism by which repeated citations stay readable. A paragraph that quotes three passages can name Fitzgerald once in a signal phrase and then carry three slim parentheticals with page numbers only, rather than repeating the surname three times. The guide returns to this technique under the question about clutter, because it is the single most useful paragraph-level skill in MLA prose.

Placement of the parenthetical follows the quotation's closing quotation mark but precedes the sentence's final punctuation, except in block quotations where the order reverses. So: Nick's narration calls the evening "a certain portentousness" that hangs over the party (47). The period lands after the parenthetical. In a block quotation, the period lands at the end of the quoted text and the parenthetical follows the period. The reversal looks odd until it is practiced, and then it becomes automatic. The block quotation rules themselves, including the four-line threshold and the half-inch indent, are worked through in the first question section below.

Paraphrase takes the same parenthetical as quotation. A writer who restates a passage in original words still owes the page reference, because the ideas come from the novel's specific location even when the wording does not. The parenthetical after a paraphrase usually sits at the end of the sentence containing the borrowed idea. Plagiarism checkers and instructors alike treat an unmarked paraphrase as the more serious offense, precisely because it is easier to pass off; the page number is what keeps a paraphrase honest.

### How do you quote a passage longer than four lines from the novel?

To quote a passage longer than four lines in an essay on The Great Gatsby, indent the whole quotation one half inch from the left margin, omit the quotation marks, keep the text double spaced, and place the parenthetical page reference after the closing punctuation.

The four-line threshold is measured in the essay's own lines, not the novel's, so a passage that runs long on the page of the paper triggers the block format even if it occupied fewer lines in the book. The threshold exists because a long quotation set inline would swamp the writer's own prose, and the block format gives the quoted text its own visual room. The introductory signal phrase typically ends with a colon when it forms a complete sentence, as in: The passage that closes the novel's first chapter of parties runs:

followed by the indented block. When the signal phrase is a fragment woven into the sentence, the colon is omitted and the grammar flows straight into the quotation. Either way, the block itself carries no quotation marks, because the indentation already marks the words as borrowed.

The parenthetical after a block quotation sits outside the final period, which is the one placement rule that reverses the normal order. In inline quotation the period follows the parenthetical; in block quotation the parenthetical follows the period. The logic is visual: the block's final period belongs to the quoted text's sentence, and the citation stands apart as the writer's own apparatus. A block quotation that ends with the novel's famous closing cadence would therefore show the period tight against the last quoted word, then a space, then (Fitzgerald 47) with no trailing period of its own beyond the one inside the parentheses. Writers who get this backwards usually do so because they apply the inline rule everywhere; the block rule is the exception worth memorizing separately.

Line breaks inside a quoted passage need care when the novel's prose runs in a rhythm the essay wants to preserve. For prose quotations, MLA does not require preserving the novel's line breaks; the text reflows as prose, and only a change of paragraph within the quotation is marked, with the first line of the new paragraph indented an extra quarter inch in the block. Verse would be a different matter, but the novel is prose, so the rule is simple: reflow, and mark paragraph breaks. This keeps long quotations readable without turning the essay into a facsimile of the book's typesetting.

### How do you cite dialogue between two characters in the novel?

To cite dialogue between two characters, reproduce each speaker's words in quotation marks within the essay's prose, keep the novel's speaker tags or identify the speakers clearly in the signal phrase, and close the quotation with one parenthetical page reference covering the whole exchange.

Dialogue in the novel arrives as quick exchanges, often with minimal speaker tags, and the essay writer's job is to keep the speakers clear without mangling the citation. The standard approach is to quote the exchange as a unit: "I hope she'll be a fool, that's the best thing a girl can be in this world, a beautiful little fool," Daisy says of her daughter (47). The page reference covers the whole exchange when the quoted lines share a page, and the signal phrase names the speaker so the reader is never guessing who talks. When the exchange spans two pages, the parenthetical gives the range, written without abbreviation as (47-48), and the works-cited entry stays unchanged.

A frequent temptation is to break a single exchange into several separately cited quotations, one parenthetical per line of dialogue. That approach clutters the paragraph and suggests each line was found independently, when in fact one continuous reading supplied them all. Quote the exchange as a unit and cite it once; the reader can find every line within the cited span. If the analysis then discusses each line separately in the following sentences, no new citation is needed for the discussion, because the original parenthetical already covers the passage under discussion. Only when the essay moves to a new passage does a new parenthetical become necessary.

Speaker tags inside the quotation deserve a decision before drafting. The novel's tags, "he said" and "she said" and their variants, are part of the quoted text and should be preserved when they carry meaning, as when a tag's verb choice reveals attitude. But a tag that merely identifies the speaker can be dropped in favor of the essay's own signal phrase, provided the omission is not hidden: the quotation must still read as a quotation, and any internal omission of words takes an ellipsis. The safest practice for beginners is to keep the tags as Fitzgerald wrote them and let the signal phrase do the identifying work outside the quotation marks. As control grows, selective trimming becomes safe.

### How do you keep repeated citations from cluttering a paragraph?

To keep repeated citations from cluttering a paragraph, name Fitzgerald in a signal phrase once, then use page-only parentheticals for each quotation or paraphrase that follows, since the surname already clearly established in the sentence points to the works-cited entry.

The clutter problem arises because beginners treat every quotation as an independent event requiring the full (Fitzgerald 47) form. A paragraph analyzing one scene might then show the surname four times in as many lines, which reads as stuttering. The remedy is the narrative citation: work the author's name into the sentence itself, and the parenthetical needs only the page number. Consider a paragraph that traces one image through three moments. The first sentence names Fitzgerald and carries (47); the next two sentences need only (52) and (58), because the author context persists across the paragraph. The reader's eye moves smoothly, and the documentation loses nothing.

The technique has a boundary worth respecting. The surname-in-the-sentence shortcut applies within the flow of a single discussion; when the essay moves to a new source, the new source's name must appear, and when it returns to Fitzgerald after a long stretch on another author, re-establishing the surname once is good practice. Within a paragraph devoted to the novel alone, though, the name appears once and the page numbers do the rest. This is also where the difference between the novel and the critical sources matters: the novel will be cited far more often than any secondary source, so the paragraph-level economy pays its largest dividends on the Fitzgerald citations.

A related economy concerns consecutive sentences that draw on the same page. MLA does not require a parenthetical on every sentence when one parenthetical clearly covers a run of sentences about the same passage. The writer states the scope, as in "The next three sentences paraphrase the passage on page 47," or more naturally lets the paragraph's logic make the coverage obvious, placing the parenthetical at the end of the run. Over-citing, a parenthetical on every sentence of a five-sentence paraphrase, reads as anxious; under-citing reads as careless. The middle path is one clear citation whose coverage the paragraph's structure makes evident.

### How do you write page ranges in MLA parenthetical citations?

To write a page range in an MLA parenthetical, give the first and last page numbers separated by a hyphen with no spaces and no p. or pp., as in (47-48), keeping the author's surname before the range when it has not appeared in the sentence.

The hyphen in a page range is the one place where a dash-like mark is required inside the citation, and it is a hyphen, not an en dash, which keeps the whole system consistent with the prohibition on longer dashes. For ranges within the same hundred, MLA permits shortening the second number, so pages 163 to 164 could appear as (163-64), though writing the full second number is never wrong and is clearer for beginners. The shortened form is a courtesy to the reader's eye, not a requirement, and consistency within the paper matters more than which form is chosen.

Page ranges appear most often when the quoted or paraphrased material crosses a page break in the cited copy. A passage that begins near the bottom of one page and finishes at the top of the next takes the range, and the reader can locate the material by checking both pages. When the material sits entirely on one page, the single number suffices, even if the sentence discussing it runs long. The range describes where the borrowed words are, not how much the writer has to say about them.

A special case arises with editions that number pages in roman numerals for front matter, such as an introduction by a scholar. If the essay quotes the introduction rather than the novel, the parenthetical uses the roman numerals as printed, as in (xii), matching the edition's own numbering. This is one more reason the edition must be identified correctly: the page references only work against the copy the entry describes. An essay that cites the novel's text uses arabic numerals from the novel's pagination; an essay that cites the introduction uses the front matter's numerals; mixing the two without noticing is a quiet way to send the reader to the wrong page.

### How do you cite an epigraph drawn from the novel?

To cite an epigraph drawn from the novel, place the quoted lines before the essay's first paragraph without quotation marks, add a separate attribution line naming the author below them, and give no parenthetical, since the works-cited entry already covers the source.

The epigraph is the one quotation in the paper that takes no parenthetical citation, because its attribution line does the identifying work. The format is spare: the quoted lines sit alone, often indented or set off, and below them appears the attribution, Fitzgerald's name and sometimes the novel's title. A reader who wants the source finds the novel in the works-cited list. The absence of a page number is conventional, not an oversight; epigraphs are decorative and orienting rather than evidentiary, so the apparatus stays light.

The discipline the epigraph teaches is restraint. Because it carries no page reference, it should be short, and because it stands outside the argument, it should earn its place by framing the essay's question rather than by showing off a favorite line. An epigraph that the essay never engages is ornament; an epigraph that the opening paragraph answers is architecture. Either way, the citation mechanics are the simplest in the paper, which makes the epigraph a good first exercise for a writer still nervous about the parenthetical system.

## Citing Editions: Scholarly, Reprint, and Annotated Texts

The edition question is where most Gatsby citations go wrong, not because the rules are hard but because the wrong copy is described. The works-cited entry must describe the copy actually consulted, and the copies in circulation differ in publisher, year, editor, and paratext. A classroom paperback, a scholarly edition with an editor's notes, and an annotated teaching text are three different sources for documentation purposes, even though the novel's words are largely the same. The entry changes to match the object, and the in-text citation follows along unchanged.

The scholarly edition with an editor is the most instructive case. Consider the authorized text edited by the Fitzgerald scholar Matthew J. Bruccoli, published by Scribner in 1991. The entry reads:

Fitzgerald, F. Scott. *The Great Gatsby: The Authorized Text*. Edited by Matthew J. Bruccoli, Scribner, 1991.

Every element of the base pattern survives, and one new element appears: the other-contributors slot after the title, holding the editor's name in natural order with the label Edited by. The editor's name is not inverted, because this slot is not alphabetized; it is a description, not an access point. The subtitle, The Authorized Text, is part of the title as the edition presents it, so it stays in the italics after a colon. The publisher and year describe this edition's production, not the novel's 1925 debut. The in-text citation remains (Fitzgerald 47), pointing to the page in this edition.

The reprint case is simpler but more often botched. A paperback reprint from a publisher other than Scribner takes that publisher's name and the reprint's year. The writer who read a recent Scribner paperback reprint writes Scribner followed by that reprint's year. The writer who read a mass-market edition from another press writes that press's name and year. The principle is transcription of the copy in hand, and the payoff is that the page numbers in the parentheticals match the pagination the entry implies. Nothing undermines a works-cited page faster than an entry that claims one edition while the page references betray another.

Annotated and classroom editions add layers without changing the skeleton. An edition with an introduction by a scholar takes the same editor slot when the scholar edited the volume, and the introduction itself can be cited separately as a work in a container if the essay quotes the introduction rather than the novel. That separate entry begins with the introduction author's name, gives the introduction's title in quotation marks or a description like Introduction, then names the novel in italics as the container. The mechanics mirror the book-chapter pattern, and the section on chapters works them through. The point to carry forward is that paratext has its own authorship; the introduction is the scholar's work, the novel is Fitzgerald's, and each gets its own entry when each is quoted.

The edition decision also interacts with the choice of which text to cite in the first place, a question this guide deliberately compresses. Selecting among a first-edition facsimile, a scholarly text, a classroom paperback, and a digital file involves textual, financial, and pedagogical considerations that belong to a different article, and the companion guide to [choosing the edition to cite for a Gatsby essay](/2030/12/30/gatsby-which-edition-to-cite/) develops them fully. For documentation purposes the rule is narrower and sufficient: describe the copy consulted, put the editor in the contributors slot when one exists, and date the production rather than the debut.

A final edition subtlety concerns multivolume or collected editions. When the novel appears inside a collected Fitzgerald, the collection becomes the container, and the entry grows a page range for the novel's location within the volume. The pattern is:

Fitzgerald, F. Scott. *The Great Gatsby*. *The Collected Novels of F. Scott Fitzgerald* (illustrative collection title), edited by [Editor Name], Publisher, Year, pp. 1-200.

The novel keeps its italics as the source, the collection takes italics as the container, and the page range closes the entry with pp. The in-text citation is still (Fitzgerald 47), because the parenthetical never names containers. The container model earns its keep here: the same four-slot base, stretched gracefully around a bigger object.

## Reprints, Book Clubs, and Facsimiles: Dating the Copy

The rule that the entry dates the copy rather than the work's debut sounds simple until the copies multiply, so this section works through the common cases a student actually encounters. The classroom paperback bought new is the baseline: its title page names a publisher and a year, often a recent one, and the entry transcribes both. A Scribner paperback reprint gives Scribner and the reprint's own year in those slots, transcribed from the title page. The writer who instead writes Scribner, 1925, perhaps from a feeling that the famous date belongs in the entry, creates the mismatch this guide has warned against: the entry claims the first edition while the page numbers come from the reprint. Instructors who teach the novel year after year know the reprints' paginations on sight, and the mismatch is visible to them the way a wrong note is audible to a musician.

The used bookstore copy adds a wrinkle, because the publication data on its title page may name a publisher the student has never heard of. Mass-market and book-club editions of the novel circulated widely, and their title pages carry their own publishers and years. The entry transcribes whatever the title page says, without embarrassment and without correction. There is no hierarchy of prestige in the works-cited list; a book-club edition cited correctly outranks a first edition cited incorrectly. The copyright page usually resolves any ambiguity, listing the print history in small type, and the title page's publisher name is the authority when the two seem to conflict.

The facsimile of the first edition is the interesting edge case. Some editions reproduce the 1925 text page for page, sometimes with an introduction explaining the reproduction. When the copy is a facsimile, the entry describes the facsimile edition, publisher and year of the facsimile, because that is the object consulted, and the introduction or afterword can be cited separately if quoted. The writer gains the 1925 pagination for the in-text citations, which is a genuine scholarly convenience, but the entry still dates the facsimile's production. Dating the copy is not about prestige; it is about retrievability, letting a reader find the exact object the page numbers describe.

School editions with introductions, notes, and discussion questions are the commonest case in secondary education, and they follow the scholarly-edition pattern with one simplification. When the edition names an editor, the editor enters the contributors slot as shown in the artifact table. When the edition instead names the writer of the introduction without calling anyone the editor, the introduction writer is not forced into the editor slot; the entry can simply carry the publisher and year, and the introduction, if quoted, gets its own entry as a work in a container. The principle remains transcription: describe the roles the title page actually assigns, and do not invent an editorship the book does not claim.

A final practical matter is the printing versus edition distinction. A book may go through many printings without becoming a new edition, and the entry does not record the printing; it records the edition's publisher and year. The number line on the copyright page, the descending row of digits that identifies the printing, is bibliographic trivia the works-cited entry ignores. What matters is which edition's text and pagination the writer used, and that is captured by publisher and year. When two copies from the same publisher and year differ in pagination, which happens with print-on-demand anomalies, the writer notes the discrepancy to the instructor rather than trying to encode it in the entry; the entry was not designed for that level of forensics, and honesty in prose covers what the form cannot.

## Citing Journal Articles and Book Chapters

The critical conversation about the novel lives in journal articles and book chapters, and citing them correctly is what separates a documented essay from a merely opinionated one. The journal article pattern adds containers to the base: the article is the source, the journal is the container, and a database can be a second container. The book chapter pattern is parallel: the chapter is the source, the book is the container, and the editor enters the contributors slot. Both patterns reward the same habit of mind as the novel's entry, which is to ask what object is being described and what delivers it.

A journal article entry, shown with illustrative placeholder details so the mechanics stay visible, looks like this:

Scholar, Jane. "Reading the Green Light: Symbol and Structure in *The Great Gatsby*." *Journal of Literary Studies*, vol. 12, no. 3, 2021, pp. 45-67.

Read the entry left to right as a series of answers. Who wrote it? Scholar, Jane, inverted for alphabetization. What is it? The article title in quotation marks, with the novel's title nested in italics inside the quotation marks, because a self-contained work keeps its italics even inside a smaller work's title. Where did it appear? The journal title in italics as the container. Which issue? Volume 12, number 3, abbreviated vol. and no. Which year? 2021. Where in the issue? Pages 45 to 67, marked with pp. because this is the works-cited entry, where the abbreviation belongs. The in-text citation is (Scholar 49), surname and page, no comma, no abbreviation.

When the article was read through a database, a second container follows the page range: the database name in italics and the DOI or stable URL. The entry would continue *, JSTOR*, doi:10.xxxx/xxxxx. or the stable link. The ninth edition prefers the DOI when one exists, because it persists when URLs rot. Many instructors now require the database container for articles found digitally, and including it is never wrong even when the print issue was also consulted, since the entry should describe the access path used.

The book chapter entry follows the same logic with the editor in the contributors slot:

Writer, John. "Craft and Design in Fitzgerald's Novel." *Approaches to the American Novel*, edited by Ellen Editor, Academic Press, 2021, pp. 100-125.

The chapter title takes quotation marks, the book title takes italics, the editor appears in natural order after the container title with the label edited by, and the page range closes the entry. The in-text citation is (Writer 104). Note that the editor's name is not inverted here; only the entry's lead author, the access point for alphabetization, takes the inverted form. A chapter with two authors follows the two-author rule from the book pattern: first author inverted, second in natural order, joined by and.

A review of the novel published in a journal is cited as a journal article with the review's own title, or with a descriptive label when the review is untitled. An untitled review takes the form: Review of *The Great Gatsby*, by F. Scott Fitzgerald. The descriptive label is not in quotation marks, and the rest of the entry follows the article pattern. Reviews are useful sources for reception history, and the untitled-review form is one of the most frequently misformatted entries, because writers try to invent a title where none exists. The label solves it honestly.

The volume and number abbreviations deserve a moment, because they are small and constantly wrong. MLA abbreviates volume to vol. and number to no., both lowercase, both followed by periods, and the words are omitted entirely when the journal uses only an issue number or only a season. A journal identified by season and year, common in literary quarterlies, appears as *Journal Name*, vol. 12, Fall 2021, with no number slot at all. The principle is transcription again: describe the issue as the journal numbers it, abbreviating only the two standard words.

## Citing Digital Texts and E-Books

Digital copies of the novel raise the one genuine complication in the in-text system, which is the absence of stable page numbers. A paperback has pages; a reflowable e-book has locations that shift with font size, and a web text may have no pagination at all. MLA's answer is pragmatic: cite what the text offers. When the digital edition numbers pages, use them exactly as the print form would. When it offers chapter or section numbers, cite those, as in (Fitzgerald ch. 3). When it offers paragraph numbers, those can serve. When it offers nothing, the parenthetical carries the author's name alone, and the signal phrase carries the descriptive burden, as in: In the third chapter Fitzgerald stages the first party at full volume (Fitzgerald). The works-cited entry then does the heavy lifting of identifying the exact digital object.

The works-cited entry for an e-book follows the book pattern with the delivery platform as a second container when the text was obtained through one. A Kindle edition obtained through a retail platform, shown with illustrative details, takes the form:

Fitzgerald, F. Scott. *The Great Gatsby*. Kindle ed., Scribner, [year of the digital file].

The version slot, Kindle ed., appears after the title, because the edition of the digital file is a version of the work. When the e-book comes through a library database, the database name forms the second container with the DOI or URL, mirroring the journal article's second container. The ninth edition treats the platform as a container precisely because different platforms can deliver different files, and the entry should let the reader retrace the writer's steps.

The public-domain web text is the commonest digital source for this novel, since the book entered the public domain in the United States in 2021 and became freely available in plain-text and e-book forms. An entry for such a text names the site or project as the publisher or container and gives the URL, with an access date added when the source is likely to change. The table's digital row shows the shape. The access date is optional in the ninth edition and is included when it helps the reader, which for a stable plain-text file it rarely does; for a page that is frequently revised, it earns its keep. The judgment call is the writer's, and either choice is defensible when made deliberately.

A persistent myth holds that digital sources need no page references at all and can be cited with the author's name alone in every case. The myth is half true: the name-alone parenthetical is correct when no locator exists, but when the digital text supplies chapters, sections, or paragraphs, those locators should be used, because they help the reader find the passage. The writer who cites (Fitzgerald ch. 3) is more helpful than the writer who cites (Fitzgerald) for the same passage, and helpfulness is the whole purpose of the apparatus. Cite the finest locator the text offers, and no finer.

Classroom platforms add one more wrinkle. When the novel is read inside a course pack or a learning management system, the entry should still aim at the most public version of the text available, naming the platform as the container when no better identification exists. An entry that names only the course site is honest but unhelpful to a reader outside the course; an entry that names the underlying edition or the public text plus the platform as container serves both honesty and retrievability. When in doubt, describe the object fully and let the containers do the work they were designed for.

## Access Dates, URLs, and the Retrievability Test

The ninth edition's treatment of web sources rests on a single question, which is whether the entry gives the reader a working path back to the source. The URL or DOI answers that question for most digital texts, and the access date answers it for the unstable ones. Understanding which element does what prevents both the clutter of unnecessary dates and the risk of a dead link with no record of when it worked.

The DOI is preferred whenever one exists, because it persists through site redesigns that break ordinary URLs. A journal article's entry therefore ends with the DOI when the database supplies one, and the URL appears only when no DOI is available. For the novel's public-domain web text, no DOI exists, so the entry gives the URL of the specific page or file consulted. The URL should be the shortest form that reaches the source, which usually means trimming tracking parameters and session identifiers that some sites append. A clean URL is both more stable and more readable, and the ninth edition explicitly permits shortening in this way.

The access date is optional and judgment-driven. MLA's guidance is to include it when the source is likely to change or when no publication date is available, and to omit it for stable, dated publications. A plain-text file of a public-domain novel on a well-established archive is the very model of a stable source; the file will not change, and the access date adds nothing. A web page that aggregates study notes, or a wiki-style resource that is edited continuously, is the opposite case, and the access date records when the writer saw the version cited. The student who includes access dates on everything is not wrong in a punishable sense, but the clutter signals uncertainty about the rule; the student who includes them selectively shows judgment.

PDF scans of the novel occupy a middle ground worth understanding. A scanned page image of a print edition carries that edition's pagination, which means the in-text citations can use ordinary page numbers, and the works-cited entry describes the print edition with the scan's location as a second container. The entry thus combines the print pattern with the digital locator: Fitzgerald, F. Scott. *The Great Gatsby*. Scribner, 1925. *Archive Name* (illustrative), URL. The parentheticals behave exactly like print citations, because the pages are the print edition's pages. This is the best of both worlds for a writer who needs stable pagination without owning the book, and it is the form to use whenever the scan identifies its source edition.

The retrievability test is the final check on any digital entry. Read the entry as a stranger would and ask whether it leads to the source: does it name the site or project, does the URL resolve to the text rather than a search page, does the version or container information distinguish this file from the many other copies online? An entry that fails the test usually fails for vagueness, naming "the internet" as the source or giving a homepage URL instead of the text's address. Precision here is a courtesy with a practical payoff, because instructors do check sources, and a working path earns the benefit of the doubt that a dead end does not.

One caution closes the section. The ease of copying a URL tempts writers to cite the search result rather than the source, pasting the address of a results page or a preview snippet. The entry must describe the source itself, the text read, not the path taken to find it. A search engine is a finding aid, not a container, and it never appears in the entry. The container is the site, archive, or platform that hosts the text, named in italics, with the URL pointing at the text's own page. Keep the finding and the citing separate, and the digital entries stay clean.

## Guessing the Format and Mixing Styles: What Goes Wrong

The complication this guide promised to address honestly is the habit of guessing the format or mixing styles, and it deserves a direct treatment because it is the most common way a good essay's documentation fails. Guessing means assembling an entry from memory of a different style, from a high-school handout for an older MLA edition, or from the auto-generated output of a citation tool that was fed bad metadata. Mixing means starting in MLA and drifting into APA habits mid-paper, parentheticals with years and commas appearing beside MLA page-only forms, or a reference list titled References instead of Works Cited. Both habits share a root cause, which is treating documentation as decoration rather than as a system with internal logic.

The guessed entry usually fails in predictable places. The author's name appears in natural order, Fitzgerald, F. Scott becoming F. Scott Fitzgerald, which breaks alphabetization. The title takes quotation marks instead of italics, demoting the novel to the status of an article. The publisher slot fills with the city of publication, a ghost from older editions. The year migrates into the parenthetical, producing (Fitzgerald, 1925), which is APA's logic wearing MLA's clothes. Each error is small; together they announce that the writer never learned the system, and an instructor reading the first page of the works-cited list forms a judgment before reaching the argument.

Before and after pairs make the corrections tangible. Before: F. Scott Fitzgerald, "The Great Gatsby", New York: Scribner, 1925. After: Fitzgerald, F. Scott. *The Great Gatsby*. Scribner, 1925. Before: (Fitzgerald, p. 47). After: (Fitzgerald 47). Before: a works-cited page titled References with entries in APA order. After: the centered heading Works Cited with hanging indents and MLA punctuation. The after versions are not just more correct; they are calmer, each element in its slot, each period doing its job. The visual difference between a guessed page and a systematic one is visible at a glance, which is why instructors trust the systematic page faster.

Citation generators deserve a candid assessment. The tools are fast and often right, and refusing them outright is snobbery; but they are only as good as their metadata, and their metadata for reprints, edited editions, and database articles is frequently wrong. The generator that lists the wrong publisher, drops the editor, or invents an access date produces an entry that looks authoritative and is quietly false. The defense is verification against the patterns in this guide: run the generator, then check each slot against the table, confirming the author inversion, the title italics, the contributors slot, the publisher, and the year against the copy in hand. Five checks, thirty seconds, and the tool becomes an assistant instead of a hazard.

Mixing styles mid-paper usually starts with the parenthetical. A writer who learned APA in a psychology course writes (Fitzgerald, 1925) from muscle memory, then corrects to (Fitzgerald 47) after noticing, leaving both forms in the draft. The fix is a dedicated proofreading pass for documentation alone, reading the paper backwards from the works-cited page through each parenthetical, checking that every in-text citation has its surname-first form and every entry has its MLA punctuation. Documentation proofreading is a separate pass from argument proofreading because the eye hunts different prey; the argument pass reads for sense, the documentation pass reads for slots and punctuation.

A subtler guessing failure deserves its own mention, because it passes every punctuation check while still being wrong: the mismatched pagination. The writer cites the novel from a classroom paperback but copies the works-cited entry from a friend's scholarly edition, or from a generator fed the wrong ISBN, so the entry is flawless and the page numbers point to a different book. Every parenthetical then sends the reader to the wrong page, and the error is invisible to any check that reads the entry alone. The defense is the copy check from the drafting-day list: build the entry from the object in hand, and confirm that a sample of page references actually lands where the entry says they should. An entry and its pagination are a matched pair, and verifying them together is the one check that catches what punctuation checks miss.

The deeper cost of guessing and mixing is not the lost points, though points are lost. It is the erosion of the essay's credibility at the exact moment the writer needs it most. A works-cited page is a promise that the writer handled sources carefully, and a reader who spots a broken promise in the apparatus wonders what else was handled carelessly. The novel's own theme of performed credibility, the parties staged to impress, the persona assembled for effect, makes the point with uncomfortable neatness: an apparatus that only looks documented is the scholarly equivalent of a facade. Build the real thing, slot by slot, and the argument stands on it.

## Quoting Criticism: Weaving Secondary Sources into the Argument

Citing the novel correctly is only half the documentation task; the other half is handling the critics, scholars, and reviewers whose work the essay engages. Secondary sources enter the essay for three reasons: to borrow a reading worth extending, to oppose a reading worth refuting, or to establish a fact about the text's history. Each use takes the same citation mechanics, but the prose around the quotation changes, because the writer's relationship to a living or recent scholar differs from the relationship to Fitzgerald.

The signal phrase for a scholar names the person and, on first mention, gives enough identification for the reader to place them: "the Fitzgerald scholar Matthew J. Bruccoli observes" or "as the critic Jane Scholar argues." Subsequent mentions use the surname alone. The identification matters because the works-cited entry's lead author must match the name the prose uses; a signal phrase that calls someone "one critic" while the parenthetical says (Scholar 49) forces the reader to do the matching work the writer should have done. Name the scholar in the prose, and the parenthetical needs only the page.

Quoting criticism well means quoting the claim, not the decoration. A journal article's most quotable sentence is usually its thesis sentence or the sentence where the evidence turns into an argument; the surrounding literature review, however elegantly written, rarely earns quotation. When the borrowed claim is short, it goes inline with quotation marks: Scholar contends that the green light functions less as a symbol than as a "structuring absence" in the novel's imagery (49). When the borrowed passage runs long, the block format applies exactly as it does for the novel, with the same four-line threshold and the same reversed parenthetical placement. The mechanics do not change between primary and secondary sources; only the entry's containers change.

Paraphrasing criticism is more common than quoting it, and it carries a special obligation. A paraphrase of a scholar's argument must be faithful enough that the scholar would recognize it, and the parenthetical must make clear where the scholar's idea ends and the writer's begins. The cleanest technique is the bookend: open with the attribution, "Scholar reads the parties as staged performances of wealth (49)," develop the idea in the writer's own extension, and close the paragraph's borrowing with a final parenthetical if the discussion ran long. What the writer must never do is paraphrase a scholar's distinctive reading without attribution and then present the extension as if the whole chain were original; the reader who knows the scholarship will spot the borrowing, and the reader who does not is being misled.

Disagreement with a source takes the same apparatus as agreement, with the signal phrase carrying the stance. "Scholar argues X, but the novel's second chapter suggests Y (49)" puts the citation where the borrowed claim sits and the writer's counter where it belongs, outside the quotation's authority. The parenthetical does not endorse the claim; it locates it. Writers sometimes fear that citing a source they dispute weakens their essay, when the opposite is true: engaging a real counterargument with proper documentation is the strongest move in academic prose, and the citation is what proves the counterargument was engaged fairly rather than straw-manned.

The works-cited entries for secondary sources must be complete even when the essay uses them briefly. A single borrowed sentence still requires the full article or chapter entry, because the reader's right to verify does not scale with the length of the borrowing. This is where the sample works-cited page walkthrough pays off: the article and chapter entries are built the same way whether the essay leans on them heavily or touches them once. Completeness is not proportional; it is binary, and the binary choice is always to include the full entry.

## The Drafting-Day Citation Checklist

A checklist used during drafting catches the errors that proofreading later misses, because the decisions are still warm and the copy is still in hand. Run through these checks when the draft is complete but before the works-cited page is finalized, with the book or file open beside the keyboard.

First, confirm the copy. Pick up the actual book or open the actual file and read the title page: author as printed, title as printed, publisher, year. Transcribe those four facts onto a scratch sheet and build the entry from the sheet, not from memory. If the copy is a reprint, the year on the sheet is the reprint's year. If an editor is named, the name goes on the sheet in the contributors slot. This single habit eliminates the largest class of errors, the described-a-different-copy mistake, because the entry is built from observation rather than recollection.

Second, check the inversion and the italics. The author's surname leads, the given name follows, the novel's title is italicized, and the article and chapter titles in the list are in quotation marks with their containers italicized. Read down the left margin of the works-cited page: a column of surnames, each followed by a comma, is the visual signature of a correct list. Any entry that begins with a first name or a title where a surname should be stands out immediately in that column, which is why the margin check is faster than reading each entry in full.

Third, verify every parenthetical against its entry. For each (Fitzgerald 47) in the body, confirm that the works-cited page holds a Fitzgerald entry and that the page number falls within the cited copy's pagination. For each page-only parenthetical, confirm that the sentence or the paragraph's flow names Fitzgerald, so the shortened form is legitimate. For each block quotation, confirm the reversed placement, citation after the final period. This is the documentation proofreading pass, done separately from the argument pass, reading for slots and punctuation rather than sense.

Fourth, test the two-way match. Every parenthetical surname must resolve to an entry, and every entry should be cited at least once in the body; an uncited entry raises the question of why it is listed. Skim the body for names that appear only in the works-cited page and for parentheticals whose surnames appear nowhere in the list. The match is the functional core of the system, and a paper that passes it has documentation that works even if a comma is misplaced somewhere.

Fifth, run the generator audit if a citation tool was used. For each generated entry, check the author inversion, the title formatting, the contributors slot, the publisher, and the year against the scratch sheet from the first check. Generators most often fail on reprint years, dropped editors, and invented access dates; those are the three slots to scrutinize. Thirty seconds per entry is enough, and the habit turns the tool from a hazard into an assistant.

Sixth, read the page as a stranger. Look at the finished works-cited page and ask whether it could be mistaken for another style: an APA reader should not find years in the parentheticals, a Chicago reader should not find footnotes, and no entry should carry a city of publication. Then check the banned details of this guide's own house: no guessed editions, no invented article titles, no placeholder page numbers presented as real. The page should look calm, each element in its slot, each period doing its job. When it does, the apparatus disappears into the background, which is exactly where it belongs.

## Citing the Novel Across Different Essay Types

The citation patterns stay constant, but their distribution changes with the essay's kind, and anticipating the distribution helps the writer plan the works-cited page before drafting begins. A close-reading essay that treats a single passage uses the novel almost exclusively: dozens of parentheticals, nearly all page-only after the first signal phrase, and a works-cited page with a single entry. The documentation challenge here is paragraph-level economy, the clutter problem solved by narrative citation, because the same surname would otherwise appear forty times. The works-cited page is trivial; the in-text flow is everything.

A comparative essay that sets the novel beside another work doubles the apparatus without doubling its difficulty. Two stand-alone book entries, alphabetized by surname, each with its own parentheticals, and the shortened-title rule only if one author contributed both works. The new decision is comparative rather than documentary: when a paragraph discusses both novels, the parentheticals must make clear which page belongs to which book, and the signal phrases must name the author each time the discussion switches sources. A paragraph that quotes Fitzgerald and then, two sentences later, quotes the other author without re-establishing the name leaves the reader guessing which parenthetical belongs where. Name the author at every switch, and the two apparatuses stay untangled.

A research essay that engages criticism carries the fullest apparatus: the novel, several articles, perhaps a chapter, each with its own entry and its own parenthetical stream. Here the planning matters most, because the works-cited page must be built alongside the drafting rather than after it. The practical method is to create the entry the first time a source is quoted, pasting it into a running works-cited draft, so no source is ever cited without its entry existing. Writers who postpone the page to the end inevitably discover a parenthetical whose source they can no longer identify, a surname without an entry, and the reconstruction under deadline is where guessing begins. Build the page as you go, and the two-way match takes care of itself.

A personal or reflective essay that uses the novel as a touchstone rather than an object of analysis still cites it. The common student error is to assume that an essay about one's own reading experience needs no documentation, as if the personal frame exempted the borrowed words. It does not: any quotation from the novel takes its parenthetical, and the works-cited entry appears, because the reader still needs to locate the passage. The apparatus may be light, a single entry and a handful of citations, but lightness is not absence. Instructors who assign reflective essays check documentation precisely because students assume it is optional.

An exam essay written under time pressure is the special case where the apparatus compresses. Most instructors do not require a works-cited page for in-class essays, but they do expect parenthetical page references, and the novel's edition is usually the course text everyone shares. The writer's job is therefore the in-text form alone: surname and page, or page alone after a signal phrase, placed correctly relative to quotation marks and final punctuation. Practicing the parenthetical until it is automatic pays off here, because the exam writer has no table to consult and no time to deliberate. The patterns learned for the research paper become reflexes in the exam room, which is the deeper reason to learn them as patterns rather than as entries to look up.

## What Instructors Actually Check

It helps to know what happens on the other side of the desk, because the checks an instructor runs are narrower and more mechanical than students fear, and knowing them turns documentation from anxiety into routine. The first check is the skim: the instructor flips to the works-cited page and reads down the left margin, looking for the column of inverted surnames, the hanging indents, and the centered heading. This takes seconds and sorts papers into two piles, the documented and the guessed, before a single entry is read closely. A page that passes the skim earns the benefit of the doubt; a page that fails it invites scrutiny of everything.

The second check is the spot verification. The instructor picks one or two parentheticals from the body, often from the opening pages, and confirms that the surname resolves to an entry and that the page number is plausible for the cited edition. This is why the edition must match the copy: an instructor who taught the novel from the authorized text knows its pagination, and a parenthetical pointing to a page that does not exist in that edition is caught instantly. The check is not adversarial; it is the instructor confirming that the apparatus works as advertised. Papers whose citations resolve cleanly are rarely examined further.

The third check is the style consistency scan. The instructor looks for the tells of mixed styles: years in parentheticals, a References heading, p. abbreviations in citations, natural-order author names. Each tell suggests the writer assembled the documentation from incompatible sources, and a cluster of tells suggests the writer never learned any single system. This check is where citation generators do their quiet damage, because a generated entry with the wrong year format looks authoritative to the student and wrong to the instructor in the same glance. The generator audit from the checklist exists precisely to catch these before submission.

The fourth check, applied to stronger papers, is the source quality glance. The instructor reads the works-cited entries' containers and notes what kind of conversation the essay joined: scholarly editions and journal articles signal research; a bare novel plus web summaries signals less. This is not a citation-form check but a research check, and it affects the essay's ethos. An essay that cites the novel in a scholarly edition, one peer-reviewed article, and one book chapter has joined the critical conversation at its proper level, and the entries' correctness is what makes that participation legible.

None of these checks requires the instructor to verify every comma, which is reassuring: the system is designed so that correct patterns are visible at a glance and errors announce themselves the same way. The writer's job is therefore not perfection under a microscope but consistency under a skim, and consistency is achieved by the habits this guide teaches, one entry pattern, one in-text form, the copy in hand, and a final verification pass. The instructors are not hunting; they are confirming, and a paper that gives them clean confirmations moves quickly to what matters, the argument.

## Verdict

The position this guide takes is unqualified: learn the five patterns cold, describe the copy in hand, and never guess. The works-cited entry for the novel is four elements long and takes thirty seconds to write correctly once the pattern is internalized; the in-text citation is a surname and a page number; the edition, article, chapter, and digital variations are the same pattern stretched around different containers. There is no part of this system that rewards improvisation and no penalty for the deliberate writer beyond the initial learning time. The writers who lose marks on documentation are not the writers who find MLA hard; they are the writers who never decided it mattered. Decide that it matters, keep the artifact table within reach while drafting, verify every generated entry slot by slot, and the apparatus will do what an apparatus should do, which is disappear into the background and let the argument be judged on its merits.

There is a final reason to take the position this strongly, and it concerns the writer's future rather than any single grade. Every discipline the student will meet has its own documentation dialect, and MLA is the most forgiving place to learn the underlying discipline: describe the source exactly, point to it consistently, and let the reader verify. The habits transfer. A writer who can build a clean MLA works-cited page can learn Chicago notes or APA references in an afternoon, because the mental model, source plus container plus locator, is the same everywhere. The writer who never learned any system starts over with each new course. Learn this one properly, and every later system is a variation rather than a new subject, which repays the initial effort many times over across a college career.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you cite The Great Gatsby in MLA?

Cite the novel with a works-cited entry that names Fitzgerald, gives the italicized title, and closes with the publisher and year, paired with in-text citations that give the surname and page number. The entry reads: Fitzgerald, F. Scott. *The Great Gatsby*. Scribner, 1925. In the essay body, a quotation or paraphrase takes a parenthetical like (Fitzgerald 47), or the page number alone when Fitzgerald's name already appears in the sentence. Longer quotations of more than four lines become block quotations with the citation after the final period. The edition cited must match the copy consulted, so a reprint or scholarly edition changes the publisher, year, or editor slots while the in-text form stays the same.

### Q: What is the MLA works-cited entry for the novel?

The entry is: Fitzgerald, F. Scott. *The Great Gatsby*. Scribner, 1925. The author's name is inverted for alphabetization, the novel's title appears in italics as a self-contained work, the publisher names the body that produced the copy cited, and the year dates that production. Three periods and one comma separate the four elements, and the entry uses a hanging indent with the first line flush left. When the copy in hand is a reprint, the publisher and year change to match it; when a scholarly edition has an editor, the editor's name follows the title with the label Edited by. Repeat this exact line identically every time the novel's entry appears in notes or drafts so the documentation stays internally consistent.

### Q: How does the MLA in-text citation work?

The in-text citation points the reader from the essay body to the works-cited entry using the author's surname and a page number, written as (Fitzgerald 47) with no comma and no page abbreviation. When the sentence already names Fitzgerald in a signal phrase, the parenthetical shrinks to the page number alone, as in (47). The parenthetical follows the closing quotation mark and precedes the sentence's final period, except in block quotations where it follows the final period. Paraphrases take the same form as quotations, because the page number locates the borrowed idea. Every parenthetical must match an entry on the works-cited page, and every entry should be cited at least once in the body.

### Q: How do you cite editions and digital sources in MLA?

Cite the edition actually consulted by adjusting the publisher, year, and contributor slots while keeping the author and title fixed. A scholarly edition adds the editor after the title: Fitzgerald, F. Scott. *The Great Gatsby: The Authorized Text*. Edited by Matthew J. Bruccoli, Scribner, 1991. A reprint changes the publisher and year to the reprint's own. A digital text adds the delivery platform as a container or version, as in a Kindle edition entry, and the in-text citation uses the finest locator the text offers, such as a chapter number in (Fitzgerald ch. 3), when no stable page numbers exist. Never date a reprint with the novel's original 1925 publication year while citing the reprint's pagination.

### Q: Why not guess or mix citation styles?

Guessing produces entries that look documented but fail slot by slot, with natural-order names, quoted titles, cities of publication, and APA-style parentheticals with years, each error quietly telling the reader the system was never learned. Mixing styles mid-paper, such as MLA page-only parentheticals beside APA author-year forms or a list titled References, breaks the internal consistency the whole apparatus depends on. Readers and instructors judge the argument's care by the documentation's care, so a guessed or mixed apparatus erodes credibility before the thesis is even evaluated. Learning the five patterns in this guide takes less time than repairing a guessed bibliography under deadline, and verification against the artifact table catches the errors generators introduce.

### Q: Why does correct MLA matter for credibility?

Correct MLA matters because documentation is the visible proof that a writer handled sources carefully, and readers extend the judgment they form about the apparatus to the argument itself. A works-cited page with exact entries and consistent parentheticals tells the instructor that quotations were checked, editions were identified, and page references can be verified, which makes the essay's claims easier to trust. Errors in the apparatus raise the opposite inference, suggesting hurry or indifference that may extend to the reading of the novel. In research writing the citation system is also a shared language; using it fluently signals membership in the academic conversation, while fumbling it marks the writer as an outsider regardless of how perceptive the analysis is.

### Q: How do you format the hanging indent in MLA?

The hanging indent leaves the first line of each works-cited entry flush with the left margin and indents every following line one half inch. Apply it with the paragraph formatting setting in the word processor rather than with spaces or tabs, selecting the whole works-cited page so every entry receives the same treatment. The setting is usually found under paragraph indentation options labeled hanging or special indent. A page of flush-left entries looks unfinished because the indent is what makes each entry readable as a unit, letting the eye jump from one surname to the next down the left margin. Check the indent in print preview, since on-screen wrapping can disguise a missing setting.

### Q: Where does the works-cited page go in an MLA paper?

The works-cited page is the last page of the paper, following the body and any endnotes, and it continues the paper's pagination rather than restarting numbering. It carries the centered heading Works Cited, with no quotation marks, no italics, and no bold, and the entries are double spaced throughout with the hanging indent applied. No extra blank lines separate the entries beyond the double spacing, and the page contains no annotations or commentary. When the paper includes an appendix, the appendix follows the works-cited page in most instructor preferences, though course guidelines take precedence. The page exists so every parenthetical in the body has a matching full entry.

### Q: Do you italicize or quote The Great Gatsby in your essay title?

Italicize the novel's title in your essay title, because MLA treats independently published works, including novels, with italics rather than quotation marks. An essay title like *The Great Gatsby* and the Performance of Wealth would italicize the novel's title within the larger title, keeping the rest of the title in standard title case. When the essay title appears on the paper's first page, the formatting follows the same rule as in the body. Quotation marks are reserved for smaller works such as articles, chapters, and short stories. Getting this right in the title matters because it is the first documentation decision the reader sees.

### Q: How do you cite a foreword or introduction by another author?

Cite a foreword or introduction as a separate work when the essay quotes it, because the paratext has its own author distinct from Fitzgerald. The entry begins with the introduction author's inverted name, gives the introduction's title in quotation marks or the descriptive label Introduction, then names the novel in italics as the container, followed by the edition's editor when one exists, the publisher, the year, and the page range of the introduction. The in-text citation uses the introduction author's surname and page, not Fitzgerald's. Quote the novel's text and the introduction in the same paper, and each gets its own entry with its own parentheticals.

### Q: How do you cite a book chapter about The Great Gatsby?

Give the chapter author's inverted name, the chapter title in quotation marks, the book title in italics as the container, the editor in natural order with the label edited by, the publisher, the year, and the chapter's page range with pp. An illustrative entry reads: Writer, John. "Craft and Design in Fitzgerald's Novel." *Approaches to the American Novel*, edited by Ellen Editor, Academic Press, 2021, pp. 100-125. The in-text citation is the chapter author's surname and page, as in (Writer 104). When the chapter has two authors, the first is inverted and the second appears in natural order joined by and. The editor never replaces the chapter author in the lead position.

### Q: What punctuation separates the elements of a works-cited entry?

Periods close the author element and the title element, commas separate the publisher from the year and the elements inside containers, and a final period closes the entry. The pattern for the novel shows it exactly: Fitzgerald, F. Scott. *The Great Gatsby*. Scribner, 1925. The comma after the author's first name belongs to the inversion, the period after the name closes the author element, the period after the italicized title closes the title element, the comma after the publisher separates it from the year, and the final period closes the entry. Inside containers, commas separate the container title from the editor, the publisher, the year, and the page range. Consistent punctuation is what makes entries scannable.

### Q: How do you alphabetize the works-cited list when authors share a surname?

Alphabetize by the author's surname first, then by first name when surnames match, so two different authors named Scholar sort by their given names. When the same author has two entries, write the name out fully for the first entry and replace it with three hyphens followed by a period for the second, sorting the two by title. Entries that begin with a title rather than a name alphabetize by the first significant word, ignoring A, An, and The. Numbers and symbols sort before letters in most implementations. The goal is a list the reader can scan down the left margin without hesitation, which is why the hanging indent and the inversion work together.

### Q: How do you cite two editions of Gatsby in the same paper?

Give each edition its own works-cited entry, since the entries describe different objects with different publishers, years, or editors. To keep the in-text citations distinct, add a shortened title to the parenthetical, as in (Fitzgerald, *Gatsby: Authorized Text* 52) for the scholarly edition versus (Fitzgerald, *Gatsby* 47) for the classroom paperback, following MLA's rule for distinguishing works by the same author. Introduce the distinction in the prose on first use so the reader understands which parenthetical points to which entry. Papers that compare editions genuinely need both entries; papers that merely consulted two copies should pick the one actually quoted and cite it alone.

### Q: How do you cite a paraphrase of a Gatsby passage in MLA?

Cite a paraphrase with the same parenthetical a quotation would take, the author's surname and page number, because the page locates the borrowed idea even when the wording is original. Place the parenthetical at the end of the sentence or sentences containing the paraphrased material, as in: The Buchanan house is described as gleaming and portentous before any guest arrives (Fitzgerald 47). When Fitzgerald's name appears in the sentence, the parenthetical shrinks to the page number alone. An unmarked paraphrase is a more serious documentation failure than an unmarked quotation, precisely because it is easier to pass off, so the page number is what keeps restated ideas honest.

### Q: What is a container in MLA citation?

A container is the larger work that holds the source being cited, such as the journal that holds an article, the book that holds a chapter, or the database that delivers an e-book. MLA builds entries from the source outward: author and title of the source first, then the container's title in italics, then the container's other contributors, publisher, date, and location. A journal article can have two containers, the journal and the database, each with its own publisher and location slots. The novel cited as a stand-alone book has no container, which is why its entry is the shortest in the system. Thinking in containers turns every unfamiliar source into a familiar pattern.

### Q: How do you cite a review of the novel published in a journal?

Cite a review as a journal article, giving the reviewer's inverted name, the review's title in quotation marks when it has one, and the journal title in italics as the container with volume, number, year, and page range. When the review is untitled, use a descriptive label instead of inventing a title: Review of *The Great Gatsby*, by F. Scott Fitzgerald. The label is not placed in quotation marks, and the rest of the entry follows the article pattern. Reviews are valuable sources for reception history, showing how the novel was read in its own time, and the untitled-review form is worth memorizing because inventing a title where none exists is one of the most common small fabrications in student bibliographies.

### Q: How do you cite a Kindle edition of Gatsby in MLA?

Name Fitzgerald and the italicized title, then add the version in the version slot, followed by the publisher and the digital file's year: Fitzgerald, F. Scott. *The Great Gatsby*. Kindle ed., Scribner, [year of the digital file]. The version slot records the file's edition, and the publisher and year describe the digital production rather than the novel's 1925 debut. When the e-book was obtained through a library platform, add the platform as a second container with the DOI or stable URL. The in-text citation uses the finest locator the file offers, such as a chapter number in (Fitzgerald ch. 3), or the author's name alone when the file supplies no locator at all.

### Q: How do you abbreviate publisher names in MLA works-cited entries?

Shorten University to U and Press to P in academic press names, so University of Alabama Press becomes UP of Alabama and Oxford University Press becomes Oxford UP. Omit business words like Company, Incorporated, and Limited, but keep a trade name's distinctive form, so Scribner stays Scribner. When a publisher's name includes the name of a person, keep it, and when two publishers are listed, name both. The abbreviations exist to keep entries compact without losing identifiability, and they apply only to the publisher slot. A publisher name transcribed exactly as the title page gives it, minus the standard abbreviations, is always the safe choice.

### Q: How do you cite an anthology that includes Fitzgerald's short stories?

Cite the story as the source and the anthology as the container: give the story author's inverted name, the story title in quotation marks, the anthology title in italics, the editor in natural order with the label edited by, the publisher, the year, and the story's page range. An illustrative entry reads: Fitzgerald, F. Scott. "The Diamond as Big as the Ritz." *Anthology of American Short Fiction* (illustrative), edited by Ellen Editor, Academic Press, 2021, pp. 200-240. The in-text citation uses the story author's surname and page. When the paper also cites the novel, the novel keeps its own stand-alone entry, and the two Fitzgerald entries sort by title with the three-hyphen rule.
