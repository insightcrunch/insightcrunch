Read the first description of Tom Buchanan again and notice what Fitzgerald refuses to tell you. You learn the shape of his mouth, the set of his eyes, the pack of muscle moving under his coat, the way his riding boots strain at the lacing. You do not learn what he thinks, wants, or fears. Masculinity studies and Great Gatsby criticism intersect exactly at that refusal, because the lens starts from the premise that manhood in this novel is something bodies and gestures and purchases assert rather than something men simply possess. Tom is introduced as a physique because his physique is his argument, and once you see that, you cannot unsee how hard every other man in the book is working to make an argument of his own.

That is the payoff of this particular lens, and it is worth stating before the theory, because the theory can sound abstract until you watch it do work on a page. A plain reading of Fitzgerald's novel gives you a love story wrecked by class and carelessness. A masculinity reading gives you something less comfortable: a group of men measuring themselves against a standard none of them invented, and a novel that quietly tracks what the measuring costs. Tom brutalizes because brutality is the only currency he trusts. Gatsby builds an entire biography because the standard would otherwise disqualify him. George Wilson has neither the body nor the money, and the novel disposes of him accordingly. Manhood here is not a trait. It is a contest, and the contest has a scoreboard.

## What Masculinity Studies Is, in Plain Terms

Masculinity studies emerged out of feminist theory in the 1980s as scholars turned the analytical tools of gender criticism toward men. The founding insight is simple and easily misunderstood: if femininity is socially constructed, then so is masculinity, and the second construction is harder to see precisely because it presents itself as the neutral default. Women in most literature are marked as gendered; men are simply people. Masculinity studies removes that exemption and asks what work a text does to produce, police, and reward a particular version of manhood.

The field's most durable contribution is the idea that masculinity is plural and hierarchical. There is never one masculinity in a given society but a set of them arranged in a rank order, with one version holding cultural authority and the others positioned as subordinate, complicit, or marginalized. The authoritative version does not have to be the most common one. It has to be the one against which the others are measured and found wanting. Men who cannot embody it still tend to endorse it, because endorsing it is how they claim partial credit.

A second contribution matters for reading Fitzgerald: masculinity is relational. It is defined against femininity, against other masculinities, and against the constant threat of being read as insufficiently manly. This makes it inherently unstable and therefore performative. A man who possessed manhood securely would not need to demonstrate it. The demonstration is the evidence of the insecurity, which is why the loudest performances tend to belong to the most threatened positions.

The third contribution is the concept of cost. Masculinity studies is not an exercise in sympathy for powerful men, and it does not soften the harm those men do. It insists instead that a gender order which rewards dominance, emotional suppression, and the accumulation of status extracts a price from everyone inside it, including its apparent winners, and that a novel which shows the price is doing analytical work rather than merely reporting behavior.

### What is a masculinity-studies reading of The Great Gatsby?

A masculinity-studies reading treats manhood in the novel as a constructed and ranked social position rather than a natural trait. It asks how Tom, Gatsby, Wilson, and Nick each perform masculinity, which version holds authority, what each man risks by falling short, and what the competition costs the men and the women around them.

## The Questions the Lens Asks of a Text

Applying the lens is a matter of asking a stable set of questions and letting the passages answer. The first question is descriptive: what versions of manhood does this text put on the page, and how does it introduce each one? Fitzgerald is unusually generous here, because he gives every significant male character a physical introduction that doubles as a gender argument.

The second question concerns hierarchy. Which version of manhood does the world of the text treat as authoritative, and how do you know? The test is not who is admired but who sets the terms. A man holds the authoritative position when other men must define themselves in relation to him even in his absence.

The third question is about maintenance. What labor keeps a masculine position intact, and what happens when the labor fails? Masculinity is never finished, so the lens looks for the repeated acts by which a man renews his claim: the display, the purchase, the insult, the physical assertion, the story told about the past.

The fourth question concerns policing. How does the text show men correcting other men, or correcting themselves, when the performance slips? Policing is where the construction becomes visible, because nobody needs to enforce a natural fact.

The fifth question is the one students most often skip, and it is where the strongest essays live. What does the arrangement cost, and who pays? The costs are rarely distributed evenly. In Fitzgerald's novel the men compete, and the bill arrives at addresses in the valley of ashes.

## Masculinity Studies and The Great Gatsby: Tom Buchanan's Body as Argument

Nick's first look at Tom is a masculinity reading waiting to be written. Tom stands on the porch in riding clothes with his legs apart, and Nick reports that he was "a sturdy straw-haired man of thirty, with a rather hard mouth and a supercilious manner". The description then moves to the eyes: "Two shining arrogant eyes had established dominance over his face and gave him the appearance of always leaning aggressively forward." Dominance is the operative noun, and Fitzgerald applies it to the relation between Tom's eyes and his own face before applying it to Tom's relation with anyone else. The man dominates himself first. His features are already in a hierarchy.

Then comes the body proper. Nick notes that the riding clothes cannot hide "the enormous power of that body", that Tom seems to fill his boots until the top lacing strains, and that "you could see a great pack of muscle shifting when his shoulder moved under his thin coat". The sentence that follows is the one to build an essay on: "It was a body capable of enormous leverage", and Fitzgerald then names it a cruel body. Leverage is a mechanical term. It describes a capacity to move other things, and it converts Tom's physique into an instrument of force rather than an object of admiration.

What makes this a masculinity reading rather than a character sketch is the next observation. Nick records that Tom's voice adds "to the impression of fractiousness he conveyed", and that Tom seemed to say, without saying it, "just because I'm stronger and more of a man than you are". Fitzgerald hands the reader the comparative structure explicitly. Tom is not simply a man. He is more of a man than the man beside him, and the surplus is the point. Masculinity here is a relative quantity, measured against a rival, and Tom's whole bearing is a running claim to the larger share.

The claim needs maintenance, and the novel shows the maintenance. Tom's hospitality is a sequence of assertions. He turns Nick around by the arm. He announces his acquisition of a string of polo ponies. He boasts, at his own party in chapter seven, about the architectural joke of his stable and garage. He reads a race-panic book and repeats its thesis at dinner, insisting that "Civilization's going to pieces" and that the dominant race must watch out or lose control. The book gives him a vocabulary for a status anxiety he cannot otherwise name. The claim to racial superiority is doing the same work as the claim to physical superiority, and Nick sees it: there is something pathetic in his concentration, because the assertion is compensatory rather than confident.

### Why does Fitzgerald introduce Tom through his body before his mind?

Because Tom's body is his argument. Fitzgerald front-loads muscle, boots, hard mouth, and arrogant eyes so the reader meets a claim to dominance before meeting a person. The order of the description tells you that Tom's standing rests on physical assertion, and that the assertion must be constantly renewed to hold.

The violence follows from the logic rather than interrupting it. Tom's hand on Daisy's finger leaves a bruise, and Daisy names the cause with a word Tom cannot tolerate. She calls the injury the price of marrying "a brute of a man, a great, big, hulking physical specimen of a", and the sentence breaks off because Tom interrupts. His objection is precise. He hates the word hulking. He does not deny the brutality; he denies the noun that makes his body sound like an unlovely object rather than an authority. Daisy repeats it anyway. In two lines of dialogue the novel stages the entire dynamic: the body asserts, the wife names the assertion, and the man polices the language because the language exposes the performance.

The apartment scene in chapter two takes this from bruise to fracture. When Myrtle chants Daisy's name in defiance of Tom's prohibition, Fitzgerald reports the correction in a sentence stripped of adverbs: "Making a short deft movement, Tom Buchanan broke her nose with his open hand." Deft is the word that should stop a reader. It implies practice. The masculinity Tom performs is not rage overflowing but a technique maintained, and the novel's flat syntax refuses to dignify it with drama. A masculinity reading takes that sentence as the clearest statement in the book about what the authoritative position is licensed to do.

## Gatsby's Manufactured Manhood

If Tom inherits a masculine position and defends it with force, Jay Gatsby manufactures one and defends it with narrative. The lens reads him not as a romantic exception to the novel's gender order but as its most industrious participant, a man who understood the specification and built to it.

The specification has three components in the world of the novel: money, a body that carries authority, and a past that entitles you. Gatsby solves the first with bootlegging and bonds. He solves the second partially, through a bearing Nick describes with unusual care, an elaborate formality of speech and the famous smile that concentrates on you with an irresistible prejudice in your favor. He solves the third by fabrication, and the fabrication is where the performance shows its seams.

Gatsby's account of himself to Nick in chapter four is a masculine resume delivered under pressure. He was educated at Oxford because all his ancestors were. He collected jewels in the capitals of Europe. He hunted big game. Then, he says, came the war: "It was a great relief, and I tried very hard to die, but I seemed to bear an enchanted life." He was promoted, and he reports that "I was promoted to be a major, and every Allied government gave me a decoration", listing the honors until the list becomes its own kind of confession. Nick's reaction is diagnostic. He nearly laughs, because the phrases are so worn that they evoke a character leaking sawdust at every pore. The performance is legible as performance, and Gatsby has to produce the Montenegrin medal and the Oxford photograph to steady it.

The photograph and the medal matter more than students usually notice. Gatsby carries physical proof of manhood in his pocket. No man secure in his position needs documentary evidence, and no man with Tom's advantages would think to assemble it. The props are the tell.

### Is Gatsby's manhood a performance rather than a possession?

Yes, and the novel says so through evidence. Gatsby carries a medal and a photograph to authenticate his past, rehearses formal speech, and rebuilds an entire identity from James Gatz upward. A man who held the masculine position by inheritance would not need documents. The proofs demonstrate that the manhood is constructed and requires maintenance.

The origin story in chapter six confirms it. James Gatz becomes Jay Gatsby at seventeen, on the shore of Lake Superior, in the act of rowing out to warn Dan Cody's yacht. The name change is the founding act of a self-authored masculinity, and Fitzgerald frames it as a conception rather than a decision. Cody then supplies what a father would have supplied in a different life: money, exposure to the manners of wealth, and a model of the self-made man in his final, dissipated form. Gatsby's manhood is apprenticed rather than inherited, and the apprenticeship leaves marks. He does not drink, because he watched what drink did to Cody and to Ella Kaye's arrangement with him.

Then Louisville, where the gender order becomes explicit in the plot. Gatsby is a young officer with no money and no family standing behind him, and Nick reports that he was "liable at the whim of an impersonal government to be blown anywhere about the world". The uniform provides temporary cover. Fitzgerald states the mechanism plainly: the uniform hid the fact that Gatsby had no comfortable family behind him, so he took what he could. The novel is describing a masculine position borrowed from an institution rather than owned, and the borrowing expires when the war does.

That is why the Daisy plot is a masculinity plot as much as a love plot. Winning Daisy back is not only romantic restitution. It is the final credential in a masculine resume, the one purchase that would convert new money into standing. Gatsby's demand that Daisy say she never loved Tom is not a lover's insecurity alone; it is a competitor's demand that the scoreboard be corrected retroactively.

## Wilson and the Emasculated Position

George Wilson occupies the bottom of the novel's masculine hierarchy, and Fitzgerald builds the position out of physical detail before the plot ever asks anything of him. Nick's first sight of him is of a man wiping his hands on a piece of waste, and the description is unsparing: he was "a blond, spiritless man, anaemic, and faintly handsome". Every term subtracts. Spiritless removes vitality, "anaemic" removes blood, and faintly handsome grants an attractiveness immediately qualified into insignificance. Tom got muscle and leverage. Wilson gets a deficit.

The garage completes the portrait. The interior is unprosperous and bare, the only vehicle a dust-covered wreck crouching in a corner. A man's workplace in this novel is a statement of his position, and Wilson's states failure. When Tom arrives, the interaction is a demonstration of rank: Tom slaps him on the shoulder, dangles the sale of a car he has no intention of hurrying, and treats the promise as a leash. Wilson's business depends on the goodwill of the man sleeping with his wife, which is as concentrated an image of subordinated masculinity as American fiction offers.

Myrtle's entrance seals it. She walks through her husband as if he were a ghost and shakes hands with Tom, looking him flush in the eye. Fitzgerald gives her an immediately perceptible vitality, and the contrast is structural rather than incidental. The wife has the vitality the husband lacks, and she transfers it to the man who has more of it still. Later, at the apartment, Myrtle explains her marriage in terms that translate class contempt into gender contempt: she thought Wilson knew something about breeding, and she concludes that "He wasn't fit to lick my shoe". The insult is about status, and in this novel status and manhood are the same measurement taken twice.

### How is George Wilson emasculated in the novel?

Fitzgerald strips him systematically. Wilson is introduced as "spiritless" and "anaemic," works a failing garage, depends financially on his wife's lover, and is described by his neighbor Michaelis as one of the worn-out men who laughs in an agreeable, "colourless" way. Michaelis then names the position exactly: he was his wife's man and not his own.

Michaelis supplies the summary judgment, and it is the single most useful sentence in the novel for this lens. Wilson, he observes, was one of these worn-out men who sat in the doorway watching the traffic, and "When anyone spoke to him he invariably laughed in an agreeable, colourless way", and then the verdict: "He was his wife's man and not his own." Ownership is the metaphor, and it is the same metaphor the whole gender order runs on. To be a man in this world is to possess yourself and, by extension, a woman. Wilson possesses neither.

What Wilson does next is what the lens predicts. Denied every legitimate route to masculine standing, he reaches for the one route the hierarchy leaves open to men at the bottom: he locks his wife in a room and he takes up a gun. He tells Michaelis, calmly, "I've got my wife locked in up there", and Michaelis is astonished, because Wilson had never seemed capable of such a statement. The astonishment is the point. Assertion is out of character for Wilson precisely because assertion is the currency he has never held, and when he finally spends it the effect is catastrophic. He also reaches for divine sanction, telling Michaelis "I told her she might fool me but she couldn't fool God", importing an authority from above because he has none of his own.

Tom's role in the ending completes the diagram. Tom sends Wilson toward Gatsby's house with a piece of information, and the man at the top of the hierarchy uses the man at the bottom as an instrument, exactly as Tom's body was introduced as an instrument of leverage. The valley of ashes does not merely receive the ash of the rich. It receives their violence, laundered through a broken man's hand.

## The Masculinity Contest: A Findable Artifact

The clearest way to hold the reading in mind is to lay the men side by side and name what each performs, what secures the performance, and what it costs. The table below is the masculinity contest scorecard, and it is the artifact this article contributes to the wider body of Great Gatsby criticism.

| Man | Masculinity performed | What secures it | Where it is threatened | The cost, and who pays |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tom Buchanan | Inherited physical dominance, expressed as force and entitlement | Old money, a body of enormous leverage, social rank, race panic as a backup argument | Daisy's word hulking, Gatsby's money, his own suspicion that civilization is slipping | Myrtle's broken nose and death, Wilson's ruin, Daisy's shrinking; Tom pays nothing |
| Jay Gatsby | Self-authored manhood, expressed as accumulation and display | New money, a fabricated past, medals and photographs, the mansion and the parties | Tom's investigation, the phrase Mr. Nobody from Nowhere, Daisy's refusal to erase her history | His own life, and the dignity of a funeral no one attends |
| George Wilson | Almost none; a residual claim to owning a wife and a business | Nothing durable; his standing depends on Tom's goodwill | Every scene he appears in | Myrtle's death, Gatsby's death, his own suicide |
| Nick Carraway | Detached observer masculinity, expressed as judgment withheld | Yale, family standing, the bond business, the pose of tolerance | His attraction to Gatsby's grandeur and his passivity in every crisis | Complicity; he watches the machinery run and files a report |
| Meyer Wolfshiem | Criminal competence, expressed as connection and nerve | Reputation, a network, the memory of the fixed World's Series | Respectability, which he neither has nor seeks | Exclusion; he stays away from the funeral to protect the position |

Two features of the table deserve comment. First, the column that records what secures a performance is the column that records money. Every masculine position in the novel is underwritten financially, which is why a masculinity reading of Fitzgerald converges quickly with a class reading without collapsing into it. Second, the cost column is the one that does not balance. The men at the top of the hierarchy pay least, and the costs of their performances are borne by a woman with a broken nose, a woman under the wheels of a car, and a man in a garage.

## The Plaza Suite as the Contest Made Explicit

Chapter seven is where the novel stops implying the competition and stages it. The setting is a rented suite at the Plaza, the weather is unbearable, and the pretext for the confrontation is trivial. Fitzgerald arranges the scene so that two men fight over a woman who is present and who is, for most of the scene, spoken about rather than to.

Tom opens with a question phrased to establish jurisdiction: "What kind of a row are you trying to cause in my house anyhow?" The possessive is the argument. Daisy tries to reframe the dispute as a failure of self-control, and Tom's incredulous repetition of the word shows how he hears it: an accusation that he is not master of himself, which in this order is the same as saying he is not a man. His counterattack is a status insult. He says the latest fashion is to sit back and let "Mr. Nobody from Nowhere" make love to your wife, and the phrase is precisely calibrated. It does not accuse Gatsby of being a criminal. It accuses him of having no origin, and origin is the credential Gatsby cannot forge.

Tom then escalates to the two charges that finish the contest. He calls Gatsby "a common swindler who'd have to steal the ring he put on her finger", converting Gatsby's money from a masculine asset into a mark of illegitimacy. Then he produces the investigation: "I found out what your 'drugstores' were." The drugstore revelation matters less as a legal fact than as a demonstration of capacity. Tom can afford to have a man looked into. Gatsby cannot afford to be looked into. That asymmetry, not the bootlegging, is what wins the room.

Gatsby's counterpunch is the demand that Daisy renounce her past with Tom entirely. It fails, and the failure is instructive. Daisy will not say she never loved Tom, and the moment she declines, Gatsby's claim collapses, because his entire masculine case rested on the retroactive correction of the record. Tom senses the collapse and performs magnanimity, sending Daisy home in Gatsby's car. The gesture is the most contemptuous act in the novel. It says that the rival is now so thoroughly beaten that he can be trusted with the prize.

### How does the Plaza scene stage a contest between men?

Tom and Gatsby argue over jurisdiction, origin, and money while Daisy sits between them as the trophy and the judge. Tom deploys the phrase Mr. Nobody from Nowhere and the drugstore investigation to disqualify Gatsby's manhood on grounds of birth and legitimacy, and Gatsby's demand that Daisy erase her past collapses under her refusal.

Read this way, the scene answers a question students often raise about motive. Why does Tom fight so hard for a wife he neglects and betrays? Because the wife is not the object at stake. Standing is the object, and Daisy's departure would be a public ruling that a self-made man from nowhere outranked him. Tom's frantic energy in the suite is the energy of a man defending a title, and Fitzgerald makes the point almost comic by having Tom fetch a bottle of whisky, act as host, and call his rival old sport in mockery.

## Nick Carraway and the Masculinity of the Observer

A masculinity reading that stops at the three obvious contenders misses the narrator, who has a position in the hierarchy and a stake in describing it. Nick opens the novel with a claim about temperament, reporting his father's advice and the resulting habit: "I'm inclined to reserve all judgements". He then judges everyone. The gap between the stated disposition and the practice is usually treated as a question of reliability, and it is, but it is also a question of gender performance. Withholding judgment is a posture of superiority available to a man who has nothing to prove because his credentials are already filed. Nick has New Haven, three generations of Middle Western prominence, and a family clan with a founding myth about the Dukes of Buccleuch.

His masculinity therefore does not require display, and he mistakes the absence of display for the absence of participation. He accompanies Tom to the apartment and stays. He arranges the reunion Gatsby wants. He is present when Tom breaks Myrtle's nose and reports it without intervening. He handles the funeral because no one else will. Nick's mode is service to other men's projects combined with commentary on them, and the lens names this a complicit position: a man who does not enforce the hierarchy but benefits from it and keeps its records.

The chapter two sequence in Mr. McKee's apartment is where the novel's gender arrangements go briefly strange, and any honest masculinity reading should say so rather than either overclaiming or ignoring it. The narration fractures into ellipses, Nick finds himself standing beside McKee's bed while McKee sits in his underwear with a portfolio, and the chapter ends with Nick half asleep at Pennsylvania Station. The passage has generated a substantial line of queer-theoretical interpretation, and it is legitimate to note that the novel's most conventionally masculine chapter, the one containing the broken nose, is also the one where the narration loses its footing. A masculinity reading does not need to settle what happened to observe that the text's control of its own telling falters at exactly the point where its gender arrangements are most violently on display.

## The Men on the Margins

Two further figures sharpen the hierarchy. Meyer Wolfshiem holds a masculine position built entirely on competence and nerve, and Fitzgerald marks it with the cuff buttons Wolfshiem identifies as the finest specimens of human molars and with the claim that he fixed the World's Series in 1919. Nick's response is telling: the idea staggers him, not because of the crime but because he had thought of the fixed series as a thing that merely happened rather than something one man could do to the faith of fifty million people. Wolfshiem's manhood is the manhood of causal power, and it operates outside the legitimacy the other men are competing for. It is also, in Fitzgerald's handling, rendered through antisemitic caricature, and a responsible reading names that rather than absorbing it.

Dan Cody supplies the historical layer. He made a fortune in metals and arrived on a yacht as the residue of an older frontier masculinity, the self-made pioneer at the point where the pioneering has curdled into drink and dependency. Cody is what Gatsby's model looks like at the end of its life, and Gatsby's refusal to drink is the apprentice's correction of the master's error. The novel places the two together to suggest that the self-made man is not a stable identity but a phase, and that the phase after it is not dignified.

Henry Gatz arrives at the end with a photograph of the house and a copy of Hopalong Cassidy containing his son's boyhood schedule of self-improvement. The schedule is the most poignant document in the novel for this lens: rising at six, dumbbell exercise and wall-scaling, studying electricity, practicing elocution and poise, reading one improving book or magazine a week. It is a program for the manufacture of a man, written by a boy who had read the instructions the culture published and believed them. The father's pride in the schedule and in the photograph of the mansion tells you the belief was inherited before it was acted on.

## The Historical Manhood the Novel Is Arguing With

A lens works better when it knows what the characters were measuring themselves against, and the standard in the American 1920s was itself under strain. Nineteenth-century American manhood had been organized around production: a man's standing rested on land, a trade, a farm, a shop, or a business he could point to. By the time Fitzgerald's characters reach Long Island in 1922, that arrangement has been eroded by industrial consolidation and by the growth of salaried, office-bound work. Nick's bond business is the emblem. He goes East to learn a trade that produces nothing, sells nothing tangible, and can be described only as being in the bond business, which everybody he knew was in, so he supposed it could support one more.

The culture had already registered the strain and had produced compensations. Theodore Roosevelt's rhetoric of the strenuous life in 1899 urged physical vigor and struggle as the antidote to a manhood grown soft on comfort. Frederick Jackson Turner had announced in 1893 that the frontier was closed, removing the geography in which self-making had traditionally been performed. Organized sport, physical culture, and the boys' adventure book expanded into the space that work had vacated. Fitzgerald puts every one of these in the novel and lets them curdle. Tom's national reputation is athletic rather than professional, an acute limited excellence reached at twenty-one, after which everything savors of anticlimax. The book on the flyleaf of which young James Gatz wrote his program of self-improvement is Hopalong Cassidy, a Western, and the program he wrote inside it includes wall-scaling, an activity with no application whatever in the life of a Minnesota farm boy except as a rehearsal for a frontier that no longer existed.

Dan Cody carries the same history in his person. He made his money in Nevada silver and Montana copper, veteran of every rush for metal since seventy-five, and Nick names him the pioneer debauchee who brought back to the Eastern seaboard the savage violence of the frontier brothel and saloon. The self-made man of the previous century arrives on Long Island as a wealthy alcoholic being managed by a woman who will take his fortune. Gatsby's education in manhood begins with a live demonstration that the model has no third act.

Then came the war, and the war did two contradictory things to American ideas of manhood at once. It offered the largest available proof of masculine worth, a uniform and a rank and the possibility of the kind of decoration Gatsby lists to Nick. It also removed masculine agency completely, since a soldier is, in the novel's own phrase for Gatsby's position, liable at the whim of an impersonal government to be blown anywhere about the world. Both effects are visible in the text. Gatsby's proudest credential and his most humiliating vulnerability come from the same source, and the uniform that gave him access to Daisy's house is described as an invisible cloak that might slip from his shoulders at any moment.

Set this history beside the four men and the hierarchy stops looking arbitrary. Tom holds a nineteenth-century masculine authority, physical and proprietary, without doing any of the work that once justified it. Gatsby performs a twentieth-century masculine authority, financial and self-authored, and is refused recognition on the grounds of origin. Wilson attempts the older producer model, a garage and a wife and a plan to move West, and the economy has left no room for it. Nick occupies the salaried, observational position that the century is actually producing, and he has no idea what to do with it except write about the other three.

### What historical crisis of manhood is the novel responding to?

American manhood built on production, land, and the frontier had been undermined by salaried office work and by the closing of the West, and the war supplied both a credential and a demonstration of powerlessness. Fitzgerald's men are competing for a status whose original economic foundation has already gone, which is why every claim requires such elaborate maintenance.

## Clothes, Bodies, and the Economy of Display

Fitzgerald is a novelist of surfaces in the exact sense that a masculinity reading requires: he understands that a man's clothes are an argument about his body and that a man's body is an argument about his standing. The novel therefore conducts much of its gender business through fabric.

Tom is introduced in riding clothes on his own porch in the middle of the afternoon, and Nick notes that not even the effeminate swank of the outfit could hide the enormous power of that body. The sentence is doing two jobs. It concedes that the costume is decorative, verging on the feminine, and it insists that the body underneath overrules the costume. That is the security of the inherited position: Tom can wear anything, because the body underwrites the claim. Nobody else in the novel has that latitude.

Gatsby has the opposite relationship with clothes, and the shirt scene in chapter five is the most misread passage in the novel for exactly this reason. He keeps a man in England who sends a selection at the beginning of each season, spring and fall, and he throws the shirts one by one onto the table, sheer linen and thick silk and fine flannel, striped and scrolled and plaid, coral and apple-green and lavender and faint orange, monogrammed in indian blue. Students routinely read Daisy's collapse into the pile as materialism, and it is, but it is also a masculinity scene. Gatsby is not showing Daisy his wealth. He is showing her the accumulated evidence that he has become the kind of man whose shirts arrive from England, and he is showing it because the evidence is all he has to offer in place of the standing he was born without. The shirts are the same argument as the medal and the photograph, made in silk.

His pink suit follows the same logic and is used against him. Tom sneers that an Oxford man would not wear a pink suit, and the sneer works because it identifies the outfit as a claim rather than a preference. Nick, notably, defends the suit at the end, calling it a gorgeous pink rag that made a bright spot of color against the white steps, and the reversal is Nick's one act of loyalty in the entire book. Wilson, at the bottom, has no clothes worth describing. He appears wiping his hands on a piece of waste, and the piece of waste is his costume.

Even the parties belong to this economy. Gatsby throws them and does not attend them in any ordinary sense, standing apart on the marble steps, not drinking, watching. He is not enjoying his masculinity; he is exhibiting it, and the exhibition has an audience of one who does not come. Nick, dressed up in white flannels at the first party he attends, is trying on a costume for a world he has not earned either, and his discomfort among the swirls and eddies of people he did not know is the discomfort of a man who suspects his own display.

## What the Women See

A masculinity reading is incomplete if it takes the men's self-assessments at face value, and Fitzgerald built in three women who see the performances from outside and evaluate them accurately.

Daisy names Tom's body as a hulking physical specimen and is corrected. She also delivers the sharpest one-line assessment of Tom's intellectual pretensions in the novel, whispering that they have got to beat them down while winking ferociously toward the sun as Tom expounds his race theory. Her irony is not admired within the novel and is barely noticed by the men, which is precisely the position the gender order assigns her: she can see the machinery and cannot touch it. Her verdict on the best a girl can be in this world, uttered about her daughter, is that she hopes she will be a beautiful little fool, and the wish is a rational calculation about a system in which seeing clearly buys a woman nothing.

Myrtle evaluates her husband in the vocabulary of class and finds him unfit to lick her shoe, then transfers her allegiance to the man with more leverage. She has read the hierarchy correctly and tries to move up it by attachment rather than by achievement, which is the only route available. The novel then punishes her for the attempt with a violence that is both literal and structural: Tom breaks her nose for saying Daisy's name, and she dies running toward a car she believes is his.

Jordan is the most interesting case, because she performs a masculinity of her own. Nick describes her as a slender, small-breasted girl with an erect carriage that she accentuated by throwing her body backward at the shoulders like a young cadet. She is a professional athlete, she is dishonest in the way the novel elsewhere codes as masculine self-interest, and she is described as being absolutely in training while declining a cocktail. Fitzgerald grants her the independence the men are fighting for and then has Nick discard her in a short conversation, uncomfortable with her hardness in a way he never is with Tom's. The lens reads Nick's discomfort as a policing action: a woman who takes up a masculine position gets more scrutiny from the narrator than a man who breaks a woman's nose.

### Do the women in the novel see through the masculine performances?

They do, and they gain nothing from seeing. Daisy names Tom's body accurately and is corrected on vocabulary, then wishes her daughter into beautiful foolishness as a survival strategy. Myrtle reads the hierarchy correctly and tries to climb it by attachment. Jordan adopts a masculine independence and is quietly discarded by the narrator.

## One Scene, Read Line by Line: The Garage in Chapter Two

Lenses prove themselves at the level of the sentence, so it is worth taking a single short scene and running the method through it without skipping. The garage visit in chapter two lasts about two pages and contains the entire hierarchy.

Nick and Tom arrive because Tom has decided, on a whim, that Nick will meet his girl. The decision is announced rather than proposed. Tom takes hold of Nick's elbow and forces him from the car, and Nick records that Tom's determination to have his company bordered on violence, then names the assumption behind it as supercilious. Before a word is exchanged with Wilson, the scene has established that Tom moves other men's bodies and that Nick permits it.

The building is described next, and the description is a status report. Two of the three shops are dead, one for rent and one an all-night restaurant reached by a trail of ashes. Wilson's garage advertises repairs and cars bought and sold, and the interior is unprosperous and bare, its only vehicle a dust-covered wreck of a Ford crouching in a dim corner. Fitzgerald gives the wreck a verb of animal fear. The place where Wilson performs his manhood contains one broken object doing an impression of a living thing.

Wilson then appears, wiping his hands on a piece of waste, and receives his introduction as "a blond, spiritless man, anaemic, and faintly handsome". Nick adds that a damp gleam of hope sprang into his light blue eyes when he saw them. Hope is the correct word and it is devastating, because what Wilson hopes for is a used car from the man who is sleeping with his wife. Tom's greeting confirms the arrangement. He slaps Wilson jovially on the shoulder, the same gesture of casual physical management he applied to Nick, and asks how business is. Wilson's reply that he cannot complain is the answer of a man who has understood that complaint is not available to him.

The negotiation that follows is not about a car. Wilson asks when Tom will sell him the vehicle he has been promised. Tom says next week and mentions that his man is working on it. Wilson makes the mildest possible complaint, wondering whether the man works slowly, and Tom answers coldly that he does not and threatens to sell the car elsewhere after all. Wilson's retraction is immediate and slightly panicked, his voice fading off mid-sentence. What is being transacted is not a car but the confirmation of rank. Tom does not need the money and does not want the sale hurried; he wants the demonstration that a man's livelihood can be suspended in the air by another man's mood. Later, on the road, he supplies the summary himself, telling Nick that Wilson is so dumb he does not know he is alive.

Myrtle's entrance is stage-managed by Fitzgerald to complete the diagram. Nick hears footsteps on the stairs, and the thickish figure of a woman blocks out the light from the office door. She is in the middle thirties and faintly stout, and Nick notes that she carried her flesh sensuously as some women can, that her face contained no facet or gleam of beauty, and that there was an immediately perceptible vitality about her as if "the nerves of her body were continually smouldering". Vitality is the exact quality Wilson has been denied by his own introduction. The husband is spiritless; the wife smolders. She then walks through her husband as if he were a ghost and shakes hands with Tom, looking him flush in the eye.

Three details in that sentence carry the whole reading. She walks through him, which is the language of insubstantiality rather than of avoidance. He is a ghost, which is the state of a man who has lost his claim to occupy space. And she meets Tom's eye directly, an act of equality she does not extend to her husband. Then, without turning around, she speaks to Wilson in a soft, coarse voice, telling him to get some chairs so somebody can sit down, and Wilson hurries toward the little office and mingles immediately with the cement color of the walls. Fitzgerald dissolves him into the architecture in a single clause.

Nothing in this scene is about masculinity in any explicit way, and everything in it is organized by the hierarchy. Two pages, one whim, one slap on the shoulder, one deferred car, one woman walking through her husband, one man the color of his own wall. A student who can read those two pages this closely does not need a theory to be convincing; the theory has already done its work by telling the student what to look at.

## The Reading This Produces: Manhood as a Contest No One Wins Cleanly

Put the pieces together and a single claim emerges, and it is worth naming because a named claim is what a reader can carry into an essay. Call it the rigged contest reading. The men of Fitzgerald's novel compete for a masculine status defined by dominance and underwritten by wealth. The contest is rigged, because the definition was set by the men who already hold inherited money and physical authority, and it is rigged in a second sense, because even the men who satisfy the definition are shown to gain nothing they value from satisfying it. Tom's brutality, Gatsby's performance, and Wilson's emasculation are not three unrelated character traits. They are three positions in one structure, and the structure destroys most of the men who enter it while leaving the man at the top intact but diminished.

The evidence for the rigging is distributed across the novel. Consider what each man wants and what the contest gives him. Wilson wants to leave the valley with his wife and start again in the West. He gets his wife's corpse on a work table and a bullet. Gatsby wants Daisy and the annulment of the five years since Louisville. He gets a swimming pool and a funeral attended by his father, Nick, some servants, and the man with owl-eyed glasses. Tom wants to remain what he has always been, and he does, but Fitzgerald has already told us at the start that Tom peaked at twenty-one and that everything afterward has the flavor of anticlimax. Nick reports that Tom drifts, seeking a little wistfully for the dramatic turbulence of some irrecoverable football game. The winner is a man in permanent aftermath.

Notice too what the contest does to the men's relationships with each other. There is no friendship between men in this novel that is not transactional. Gatsby and Nick come closest, and Nick's warmth arrives fully only after Gatsby is dead and unable to make claims on it. Wolfshiem's declared sentiment for Gatsby evaporates at the funeral, and he explains himself in a letter about not being able to get mixed up in it. Tom's associations are memberships rather than attachments. A gender order that arranges men in a rank order also forbids them the solidarity that might make the rank order visible, which is why the competition can run unexamined for the length of a novel while everyone in it feels vaguely that something is wrong.

Notice, finally, where the costs land. Myrtle is struck by a car driven away from the confrontation between two men. Daisy retreats into carelessness and lets Gatsby absorb the consequence. Jordan, whose masculinity-adjacent independence the novel treats with a mixture of admiration and suspicion, is discarded in a conversation of four sentences. The women in this book are not incidental casualties of a masculine competition. They are the medium the competition is conducted in.

### Does the novel endorse or criticize the manhood it depicts?

The novel criticizes it, but by structure rather than by statement. Fitzgerald never editorializes about masculinity. He arranges outcomes: the man of pure force ends unpunished and hollow, the man of performance ends dead, the man with no standing ends a killer and a suicide, and the observer goes home. Design carries the judgment.

## The Counter-Reading: Isn't Masculinity Simply a Given?

The strongest objection to everything above is also the most common student instinct, and it deserves a fair statement before it is answered. The objection runs like this. Tom is a bully, Gatsby is a romantic, and Wilson is a weak man; calling these positions in a constructed gender hierarchy is a fashionable overlay on ordinary characterization. Men in 1922 behaved as men behaved. Fitzgerald was writing individuals, not a gender system, and reading him otherwise imports a vocabulary he never had into a book that does not need it.

The objection has real force, and any reading that cannot meet it is not worth defending. Three lines of evidence meet it.

The first is the novel's own comparative grammar. Fitzgerald does not describe Tom as strong. He describes Tom as conveying the sense that he is stronger and more of a man than the man he is talking to. The comparison is in the text, not in the theory. A novelist writing pure individual characterization has no reason to phrase dominance as a relative measurement against a rival.

The second is the policing. Tom's objection to the word hulking is the behavior of a man defending a category, not a man defending himself. He does not say the injury was an accident, which would defend him personally. He says he hates that word, which defends the account of what kind of body he has. Categories only need policing when they are constructed, and Fitzgerald wrote the policing into a domestic scene where nothing else is at stake.

The third is the schedule on the flyleaf of Hopalong Cassidy. A boy in 1906 wrote down a program for becoming a man: exercise and wall-scaling, the study of electricity and of needed inventions, the practice of elocution and poise, a weekly improving book, a savings target, and a resolution to be better to his parents. That document is a construction manual. It exists in the novel because Fitzgerald wanted the reader to see that manhood in this culture came with published instructions, and that a boy could read them, follow them, and still be called Mr. Nobody from Nowhere at the age of thirty-two by a man who never had to follow anything.

The counter-reading survives in a weakened form worth keeping. Fitzgerald had no theoretical vocabulary for gender construction, and a reading that implies he did is anachronistic. What he had was an observer's eye and a novelist's structural intelligence, and the lens does not require his agreement. It requires only that the patterns be in the text, and they are.

## The Limits of the Lens

An honest application of any critical method names what it cannot do, and a masculinity reading of this novel has four real limits.

It can flatten Gatsby. The lens is very good at showing that his manhood is manufactured and very poor at accounting for the quality Nick calls an extraordinary gift for hope. Read only through gender, Gatsby becomes a striver executing a status program, and the novel's insistence on something incommensurable in him gets lost. The best essays hold both, treating the gender analysis as an account of the means and reserving the question of the ends for other tools.

It can slide into apology. Naming the costs of masculinity for men is analytically necessary and morally risky, because the paragraph after the analysis is where a careless writer suggests that Tom is also a victim. He is not. He breaks a woman's nose, sends a grieving man to kill, and retreats into money. A masculinity reading explains the logic of his behavior; it does not distribute the suffering evenly.

It can crowd out class. Every masculine position in this book is financed, and at some point the analysis of manhood becomes an analysis of capital wearing a gendered costume. Rather than treating this as contamination, strong essays name the overlap and specify which factor is doing the explanatory work in a given scene. The Plaza confrontation is settled by Tom's capacity to fund an investigation, which is a class fact expressed in a gender contest.

It can under-read the women. Focusing on the men risks reproducing the very centering the field was invented to disrupt. Daisy, Myrtle, and Jordan are not simply the terrain on which men compete, and a reading that treats them only as stakes has adopted Tom's perspective while claiming to analyze it. This is the point at which a masculinity essay should link outward rather than pretend to cover everything.

## How to Build an Essay Around This Lens

The method transfers to any assessment, and the sequence below is the one that produces defensible paragraphs rather than theory-flavored summary.

Begin with a claim about structure rather than about a character. A thesis that says Tom is toxically masculine is a description; a thesis that says the novel arranges its men in a hierarchy whose terms are set by inherited wealth and enforced by physical assertion is an argument, because it can be tested against passages and could be wrong.

Choose three passages that sit at different levels of the hierarchy, not three passages about the same man. The introduction of Tom, the account of Gatsby's uniform as an invisible cloak, and Michaelis's verdict that Wilson was his wife's man and not his own will carry an entire essay, because together they show the same structure operating at the top, the middle, and the bottom.

Quote small and analyze the grammar. Leverage, dominance, deft, spiritless, and Fitzgerald's "colourless": these are single words doing structural work, and an examiner rewards a reader who can show that Fitzgerald's diction encodes the hierarchy. Long block quotations invite paraphrase; a five-word quotation invites analysis.

Build in the counter-reading before the conclusion rather than after it. Concede that Fitzgerald had no theory of gender construction, then show that the comparative phrasing, the policing of the word hulking, and the schedule on the flyleaf put the construction in the text regardless.

Name the cost and name who pays. The strongest final movement in a masculinity essay on this novel is the observation that the competition is conducted between men and settled on the bodies of women and poor men. That is a claim with evidence behind it, and it converts a lens exercise into a reading.

## Closing Verdict

The masculinity lens earns its place in Great Gatsby criticism because it explains something the standard readings leave unexplained: why the men in this novel behave with such urgency about matters that bring them so little. Tom has everything the culture says a man should want and spends the book defending it against a threat that barely exists. Gatsby has manufactured everything the culture says a man should have and cannot convert it into the one recognition he wants. Wilson has nothing and reaches for the only assertion available to him, which is destruction. These are not three temperaments. They are three answers to the same question, and the question was set by a gender order that promised standing in exchange for dominance and delivered dominance without standing.

The verdict this article defends is that Fitzgerald wrote a book about a contest of manhood that no participant wins cleanly, and that he registered the rigging without having a name for it. The evidence is in the comparative phrasing of Tom's introduction, in the documentary props Gatsby carries, in the systematic subtraction that constitutes Wilson, and in a boy's handwritten schedule for turning himself into a man. Read the novel with that structure in view and the ending stops looking like bad luck. A woman is killed on a road between two men's houses, a man is shot in a pool by another man carrying a third man's information, and the man at the top shakes hands with the narrator on Fifth Avenue and walks into a jewelry store. That is not a tragedy of romance. It is the settlement of a contest, and the settlement is exactly what the rules always predicted.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is a masculinity-studies reading of The Great Gatsby?

A masculinity-studies reading treats manhood in the novel as a constructed and hierarchical social position rather than a natural fact about men. It asks which version of masculinity holds authority in the world of the book, how each male character performs and maintains his claim to it, what happens when a performance fails, and what the arrangement costs. Applied to Fitzgerald, the lens produces a reading in which Tom Buchanan's physical dominance sets the standard, Gatsby manufactures a rival claim out of money and invented biography, George Wilson occupies the emasculated position at the bottom, and Nick Carraway files a report from a complicit distance. The method grew out of feminist theory in the 1980s and shares its core premise: gender is something a culture produces and enforces, not something bodies simply have.

### Q: How does Tom Buchanan perform aggressive physical masculinity?

Fitzgerald introduces Tom as a body before he introduces him as a person. Nick records a sturdy straw-haired man with a hard mouth and a supercilious manner, then two arrogant eyes that have established dominance over his own face, then the pack of muscle shifting under his coat and a body capable of enormous leverage. Tom's assertions follow the same logic across the book. He turns people physically, boasts about his polo ponies and his stable, imports a race-panic book to give his status anxiety a vocabulary, bruises Daisy's finger, and breaks Myrtle's nose with a movement Fitzgerald calls deft. The word deft implies practice, which is the giveaway: Tom's masculinity is a maintained technique rather than a temper.

### Q: How does Jay Gatsby perform a self-made manhood?

Gatsby builds his manhood from published specifications and defends it with narrative rather than force. He renames himself at seventeen, apprentices himself to Dan Cody, accumulates money through bootlegging and bonds, and constructs a biography featuring Oxford, jewel-collecting in European capitals, big-game hunting, and a major's commission with decorations from every Allied government. When Nick nearly laughs at the recital, Gatsby produces a Montenegrin medal and an Oxford photograph, and the documents are the tell. A man who held masculine standing by inheritance would never think to carry proof. The boyhood schedule his father brings to the funeral confirms the pattern: dumbbell exercise, the study of electricity and needed inventions, the practice of elocution and poise, all written down as a construction manual.

### Q: How is George Wilson emasculated in the novel?

Fitzgerald subtracts rather than describes. Wilson first appears wiping his hands on a piece of waste and is called "a blond, spiritless man, anaemic, and faintly handsome," each term withdrawing something the novel has already granted Tom. His garage is unprosperous and bare, his only vehicle a dust-covered wreck, and his livelihood depends on the goodwill of the man sleeping with his wife. Myrtle walks through him as if he were a ghost. His neighbor Michaelis supplies the verdict, noting that Wilson "laughed in an agreeable, colourless way" and that "He was his wife's man and not his own". Ownership is the governing metaphor of the novel's gender order, and Wilson owns nothing, including himself.

### Q: How do the men of the novel compete over masculine status?

They compete over three currencies that the book treats as interchangeable: money, physical authority, and a past that entitles you. Tom holds all three by inheritance and never has to make an explicit case. Gatsby acquires money and builds a past, which is why Tom's decisive attack at the Plaza is on origin rather than wealth, delivered in the phrase Mr. Nobody from Nowhere. Wilson holds none of them and can only assert a residual claim to owning his wife. The competition is conducted through display, insult, purchase, investigation, and finally violence, and it is settled not by the better man but by the man who can afford to have his rival looked into.

### Q: Is masculinity a natural given or a costly construct in the book?

The novel's own details make the construction visible. Fitzgerald phrases Tom's dominance comparatively, as the sense that he is stronger and more of a man than the person opposite, which is a relative measurement rather than a property. Tom polices the word hulking rather than the accusation of brutality, and categories only require policing when they are made. Gatsby carries documents to authenticate his manhood. A seventeen-year-old boy writes a schedule for manufacturing himself into a man. Each of these is evidence that the standard is external and must be met rather than internal and simply possessed, and the costs appear in the outcomes: a dead man in a pool, a dead man in the grass, and a dead woman on a road.

### Q: What does masculinity studies add that a plain reading misses?

A plain reading treats Tom as a bully, Gatsby as a romantic, and Wilson as a weak man, which leaves three unrelated character sketches and a plot driven by coincidence. The lens converts them into three positions in one structure and makes the ending legible as a consequence rather than an accident. It also explains the novel's oddest emphases: why the first description of Tom is anatomical, why Fitzgerald bothers to include a boyhood schedule, why the Plaza argument turns on birth rather than adultery, and why Michaelis is given a summary judgment about ownership that has nothing to do with the inquest he is testifying at.

### Q: Which passages should I use for a masculinity essay on Gatsby?

Choose passages from different levels of the hierarchy rather than three about one man. Tom's introduction in chapter one supplies dominance, leverage, and the unspoken claim to being more of a man. The account of Gatsby in Louisville in chapter eight supplies the invisible cloak of his uniform and the admission that he had no comfortable family standing behind him. Michaelis's assessment in chapter eight supplies the verdict that Wilson was his wife's man and not his own. Add the hulking exchange from chapter one for the policing, the Plaza confrontation from chapter seven for the contest made explicit, and the flyleaf schedule from chapter nine for the construction manual. Six short quotations will carry a long essay.

### Q: How does masculinity studies differ from gender theory as a lens?

Gender theory is the wider approach and covers the whole system, including femininity, the relations between the two, and the way the novel positions Daisy, Myrtle, and Jordan. Masculinity studies is the facet that turns those tools toward men, on the principle that the unmarked category needs examining precisely because it presents itself as neutral. In practice the facet asks narrower questions: which manhood holds authority, what maintains it, how is it policed, what does it cost. An essay that tries to do both usually does neither well, so it is worth deciding early whether the argument is about the novel's gender system or specifically about the contest among its men.

### Q: What does Daisy's hulking jibe reveal about Tom's manhood?

It reveals that Tom's authority is a category he defends rather than a strength he possesses. Daisy shows a bruised knuckle, names Tom as the cause, and calls the marriage the price of taking on a great, big, hulking physical specimen. Tom objects crossly to the word hulking, even in kidding, and Daisy repeats it. What he does not do is deny hurting her. The correction is aimed at the vocabulary rather than at the act, because hulking recasts his body as an ungainly object instead of an instrument of legitimate authority. In a single domestic exchange Fitzgerald shows the performance, the naming of the performance, and the enforcement that follows.

### Q: Does the novel endorse or criticize the manhood it depicts?

It criticizes it structurally rather than editorially. Fitzgerald never comments on masculinity as such, so the judgment has to be read out of the arrangement of outcomes. The man of pure inherited force survives, unpunished and hollowed, drifting after an irrecoverable football game. The man who built a manhood from instructions dies in his own pool. The man with no standing kills and then kills himself. The women who serve as the medium of the competition are struck, discarded, or retreated into carelessness. A novel that arranges results this consistently is making an argument, and the argument is that the contest damages nearly everyone who enters it and rewards only the man who never had to compete.

### Q: How does Meyer Wolfshiem fit a masculinity reading?

Wolfshiem occupies a masculine position built on causal power rather than legitimacy. His authority is demonstrated by the claim that he fixed the World's Series in 1919, which staggers Nick less as a crime than as evidence that one man could act on the faith of fifty million people. He wears cuff buttons he identifies as the finest specimens of human molars, a display of nerve rather than taste. He does not compete for respectability and therefore cannot lose it, which is why he survives while Gatsby does not, and why he declines to attend the funeral. Fitzgerald renders him through antisemitic caricature, and a responsible reading names that rather than passing over it.

### Q: What are the limits of a masculinity-studies approach to Gatsby?

Four limits matter. The lens flattens Gatsby, explaining his means beautifully and his extraordinary gift for hope not at all. It risks sliding into apology, since naming the costs of masculinity for men sits one careless sentence away from treating Tom as a victim, which the text does not support. It competes with class analysis, because every masculine position in the novel is financed and the decisive move at the Plaza is a purchase rather than a display. And it can reproduce the centering it was designed to disrupt, treating Daisy, Myrtle, and Jordan as terrain rather than as characters. Strong essays name these limits rather than writing around them.

### Q: How does the Plaza scene stage a contest between men?

The chapter seven confrontation puts two men in a rented suite arguing about jurisdiction while the woman they claim to be arguing over sits between them. Tom opens by asserting ownership of the house, hears Daisy's appeal to self-control as an accusation that he cannot govern himself, and attacks Gatsby's origin rather than his conduct with the phrase Mr. Nobody from Nowhere. He escalates to calling Gatsby a common swindler and then produces the results of an investigation into the drugstores. The decisive asymmetry is capacity: Tom can afford to have a rival examined and cannot himself be examined. Gatsby's demand that Daisy erase her marriage collapses because his case depended on rewriting the record.

### Q: Can Nick Carraway be read through masculinity studies?

Yes, and the reading sharpens the reliability question. Nick opens by claiming he is inclined to reserve all "judgements" and then judges everyone, and the posture of withheld judgment is available to him because his credentials are already filed: New Haven, three generations of Middle Western prominence, a family clan with a founding myth. He does not perform manhood because he does not have to, and he mistakes not performing for not participating. He accompanies Tom to the apartment, arranges the reunion, watches a woman's nose broken without moving, and buries the man the contest killed. The lens calls this a complicit position, benefiting from the hierarchy while keeping its records.

### Q: How do I build a thesis using a masculinity lens on the novel?

Argue about structure, not temperament. A thesis stating that Tom is toxically masculine only describes; a thesis stating that Fitzgerald arranges his male characters in a hierarchy whose terms are set by inherited wealth and enforced by physical assertion can be tested and could be wrong, which is what makes it an argument. Support it with passages from three levels of the hierarchy, quote in fragments of five or six words so the analysis targets diction, concede that Fitzgerald had no theoretical vocabulary for gender construction, and then show that the comparative phrasing, the policing of a single word, and a boyhood schedule put the construction in the text anyway. Finish by naming who pays.
