Restoring Shakespeare's text to the stage was not an event but a transfer of authority, and Romeo and Juliet is the play that proves it. For roughly a century after 1748, the tragedy that English-speaking audiences applauded was a licensed adaptation: David Garrick's version, with Rosaline erased, the bawdy laundered, a funeral procession interpolated between the acts, and a tomb scene rebuilt so that the lovers could wake, recognise each other, and die in conversation. The restoration that displaced that version is usually told as a simple homecoming, the true text returning from exile in 1845 and 1847. The documentary record supports a slower and more instructive story. Garrick's additions fell in stages, at different speeds in America and Britain, on the stage and in print, while the habits of cutting that adaptation had normalised survived inside productions that advertised themselves as faithful. What changed first was not the wording of the play but the answer to a prior question: whose word governed the stage, the author's page or the manager's taste. The verdict argued here is that the restoration of the text was the consequence of that transfer, not its cause, and that every station on the road back, from Charlotte Cushman's Romeo in 1845 to the scholarly promptbooks of the twentieth century, is best understood as a negotiation over authority conducted in the vocabulary of fidelity.

The distinction matters because the word restoration conceals two different achievements. A repertory can be restored without a text being restored: Garrick himself restored the play to the centre of the English repertory in 1748, rescuing it from Thomas Otway's Roman rewrite, and he did so by adapting it. A text, by contrast, is restored only when the words performed are the words the early editions transmit, including the words a given age finds indecorous, inconvenient, or theatrically expensive. Between those two achievements lies the whole history traced in this article. The stations are well documented. Otway's Caius Marius held the stage from 1680. Theophilus Cibber's scaffold of 1744 prepared the hybrid Garrick inherited. Garrick's version, examined change by change in the account of [David Garrick's Adaptation of 1748](/2027/08/27/david-garrick-adaptation-1748/), then held the British stage for ninety-seven years, a span closed on the stage by Cushman's restoration of Shakespeare's conception of the ending in 1845 and by Samuel Phelps at Sadler's Wells in 1847, and closed in print only in 1882, when an edition restored Rosaline and let Romeo die at once after the poison. Around and between those brackets stand the half-restorations that the clean story omits: actor-managers who dropped the tomb duet and kept the cuts, antiquarian productions that buried a shortened text under archaeological scenery, scholarly editions that restored the reading text a generation before the acting text followed. The InsightCrunch restoration timeline, set out below, gathers the stations into a single reference, and the sections that follow test each station against the question the timeline raises: what, exactly, did each restorer restore?

The table's last column is the argument in miniature. At nearly every station, something persisted: a cut, a habit, a claim of fidelity stretched over a shortened evening. The sections below take the stations in groups, asking at each what the restorers said they were doing, what the surviving evidence shows them doing, and where the difference between the two exposes the real engine of the change, which was a migration of trust from the performer who improved the text to the document that corrected the performer.

## What the Stage Had to Unlearn: The Adapted Inheritance

No restoration can be measured until the thing restored from is described without caricature. The adapted Romeo and Juliet of the long eighteenth century was not a vandalised ruin; it was a coherent theatrical settlement, defended by intelligent men on grounds their audiences ratified nightly. Francis Gentleman, writing in The Dramatic Censor in 1770, defended the interpolated funeral procession with a candour that still startles: nothing, he argued, could be better devised to render the play popular, since three-fourths of every audience are more capable of enjoying sound and show than solid sense and poetical imagination. The defence is worth taking seriously because it states the settlement's terms. The manager's first duty ran to the house, not to the archive; the text was material for an evening, and an evening that emptied the benches served neither the author nor the art. Within those terms, Garrick's alterations were not corruptions but craftsmanship. Rosaline's erasure concentrated the hero's heart. The laundered bawdy fitted the play to a mixed audience. The procession gave the fourth act a spectacle equal to the fifth act's grief. The tomb conversation repaired what the age felt as a defect in the original disposition, the lovers dying unrecognised, the irony of the close legible to readers but, on the settlement's reasoning, wasted on spectators who had paid to feel.

The settlement also had a textual logic that its critics later found convenient to forget. Garrick's printed version announced its own mixed parentage on its title page and in its advertisement, where the manager acknowledged building on Cibber and borrowing the waking circumstance from Otway, who had taken it from the Italian novella tradition. The adaptation, that is, presented itself as a restoration of the story against the play, an appeal from Shakespeare's disposition of the tomb to the sources behind Shakespeare, where the lovers do meet awake. An age that regarded the author as a great but rough genius, writing for a barbarous stage and standing in need of a polite century's finish, could receive that appeal as scholarship. The point is not that the reasoning was sound; the sections below argue it was not. The point is that restoration, when it came, had to defeat an argument, not merely a habit, and arguments are defeated by other arguments. The nineteenth century's restorers therefore appear in the record as polemicists as much as practitioners, armed with reviews, prefaces, and editions, and the tomb scene became their chosen battlefield precisely because it was the ground where the adaptation's argument was strongest and most exposed.

Against that background, the shape of the original close can be stated plainly, since the restoration's object was a specific disposition of knowledge and time. In the text the early editions transmit, Romeo drinks the poison in the vault and dies beside Juliet's body, speaking his farewell to a woman he believes dead: "Thus with a kiss I die" (5.3). Juliet wakes after his death is complete, finds the cup in his hand, reads it as evidence, hears the watch approach, and takes the dagger with a speed that gives the scene its terrible economy: "O happy dagger! This is thy sheath; there rust, and let me die" (5.3). The Friar arrives behind the event he designed. The audience alone has possessed the whole truth throughout, and the scene's power is the power of that solitary possession, irony sustained to the final line. Garrick's disposition inverts every term: the poison slowed, the waking advanced, knowledge shared, farewell exchanged, death duetted. The vault's wider stage life, from procession to modern minimalism, is traced in [Staging the Tomb: The Final Scene on Stage](/2027/12/17/staging-the-tomb-final-scene/). To restore the text was therefore not to recover a few dropped lines but to restore an entire theory of how a tragedy should end, from mutual recognition to dramatic irony, and to ask audiences to surrender the most affecting ten minutes in the repertory in exchange for a colder and greater design. That the exchange was eventually made, and made by performers rather than professors, is the fact the movement has to explain.

### What exactly fell when Garrick's tomb interpolation fell?

The interpolation that fell was the lovers' added conversation in the vault, some sixty-seven lines in the count the series record uses, in which Juliet wakes while Romeo lives and the two exchange recognition and farewell before dying. Its fall restored the poison's speed and returned the close to irony.

The fall also removed the duet itself and left the audience alone with a truth the characters never share: a death completed in ignorance, a waking addressed to a corpse, the Friar arriving behind the event he designed.

The answer's brevity conceals the size of the surrender, and the surrender explains the timetable. Managers did not cling to the conversation out of ignorance of the original; the original stood in every scholarly edition on their shelves. They clung to it because it worked, because the duet manufactured an effect that the ironic close deliberately refuses, and because a profession judged by receipts does not voluntarily retire its most reliable effect. The conversation's survival in print after its stage fall, traced below, is the cleanest evidence that the obstacle to restoration was never knowledge. It was incentive. The restoration begins, on this reading, at the moment a performer's reputation could be built by refusing the effect rather than by delivering it, and that moment has a name and a date: an American tragedian, playing the lover herself, in 1845.

## Half-Restorations: The Nineteenth Century Before Cushman

The decades before 1845 are where the clean story does its worst damage, because they contain the evidence that restoration was a gradient, not a gate. The acting texts of the early nineteenth century, the Kemble editions and their provincial descendants, still carried Garrick's disposition as the play's standard form, yet the culture around those texts was changing its mind in print. Editors of the reading text, from the variorum tradition onward, printed Shakespeare's ending as the ending and Garrick's as an appendix, so that by the 1820s a reader and a playgoer inhabited different tragedies: the study possessed the ironic close, the theatre the duet. That divergence is itself a station in the movement, because it created the restoration's precondition, a public that could be told it was being given back something it already half owned. The scholarly edition did the slow work; the stage restorers harvested it.

William Charles Macready embodies the gradient in a single career. His managements at Covent Garden and Drury Lane in the late 1830s and 1840s raised the scholarly temperature of London Shakespeare production: rehearsals lengthened, antiquarian care increased, and in the tragedy of the Scottish play's great rival, King Lear, he restored the Fool and the tragic ending that Nahum Tate's happy version had displaced for a century and a half. Macready's Lear of 1838 is the era's emblem of stage restoration, and its example hangs over the Romeo and Juliet record precisely because Macready's own practice with the text remained a manager's practice, cutting for length and effect inside a frame of professed respect. The lesson the career teaches is that restoration in this period named an attitude before it named a text: a posture of deference toward the author, compatible with several hundred lines of cuts, and distinguishable from the old licence chiefly by the direction of its apologies. The adapter of 1748 altered and boasted; the manager of 1838 altered and regretted. Regret, institutionalised, became the thin end of the wedge, because a theatre that apologises for its cuts has already conceded the principle by which the cuts will eventually be refused.

Scenic development pushed the gradient the other way. The same decades that apologised for cutting invested heavily in spectacle, and spectacle consumes the one resource a full text needs, which is time. A production that pauses for a procession through built Verona streets, for moonlight effects and crowd scenes choreographed like opera, must find its minutes somewhere, and it finds them in the verse. The pattern recurs through the century and reaches its paradox under Charles Kean at the Princess's Theatre in the 1850s, where productions advertised as archaeologically faithful, correct in costume and architecture to a degree no earlier stage had attempted, delivered a text shortened to make room for the correctness. Kean's Shakespeare was a museum that talked, and the talking was abridged. Fidelity had migrated from the words to the things, and the migration was not hypocrisy: it expressed a genuine Victorian conviction that the past could be honoured by reconstructing its surfaces. The restoration of the text had to wait for a counter-conviction, that the past of a play lives in its language, and the counter-conviction arrived, characteristically, from the margins of the profession, from a small Islington theatre with cheap seats and a manager who printed his intentions.

## The Kemble Inheritance: The Acting Text at the Century's Turn

The generation that inherited Garrick's settlement administered it through a document of its own, the acting edition associated with John Philip Kemble, and that document's character explains why the early nineteenth century changed so little while believing it changed so much. Kemble, tragedian and manager, scholar in his tastes and antiquarian in his instincts, issued the play in the form his theatres played it, and his prestige certified the form as the tragedy's responsible text: Garrick's disposition, Garrick's tomb, the whole settlement tidied by a more fastidious hand. The fastidiousness is the point. Kemble's era experienced itself as a refinement of the eighteenth century's coarser licence, a raising of production standards, pronunciation, and pictorial taste, and in every department except the document it was one. The text itself, the words spoken and their arrangement at the close, passed from Garrick's hands to the new century's almost unexamined, protected by the very prestige of the man who transmitted it. Restoration had to wait, in part, for Kemble's kind of authority to exhaust its credit, since an acting text warranted by the age's most respected tragedian was harder to indict than an actor-manager's vanity project of 1748.

The provinces magnified the inheritance. A London acting edition became, by post and by touring, the working text of companies who never saw the capital's stages, and its cuts acquired the force of tradition in places where tradition was the only archive. Historians of the movement who read only London evidence therefore underestimate the settlement's mass: the adapted play was not one production's habit but a distributed system, reprinted, stocked, and memorised across two continents, and its displacement required not a decision but a generation of contrary decisions in hundreds of playhouses. When the contrary decisions came, they came unevenly for exactly this reason, and the timeline's serene rows conceal a churn of local adoptions and relapses that the promptbook record, where it survives, barely begins to map. The restoration, measured at the level of the system rather than the premiere, was the turnover of a national stock of promptbooks, and stocks turn over at the speed of wear, bankruptcy, and death as much as at the speed of ideas.

## Cushman 1845: Restoration as a Performer's Manifesto

Charlotte Cushman's Romeo is the hinge of the movement, and the series record fixes its terms: in the United States in 1845 she restored Shakespeare's conception of the ending, playing Romeo opposite her sister Susan's Juliet, ninety-seven years after Garrick's version was first performed. What she restored, at the level of the tomb, was the disposition described above: the death completed before the waking, the waking addressed to a corpse and to the Friar, the irony returned to the audience's keeping. The ownership fence of this article requires the station to be kept at passage length as biography, since a sibling article owns Cushman the performer; what belongs here is the station's function inside the movement, and its function was to relocate the source of authority. The restoration did not descend from a university or rise from a readership. It was enacted by the leading tragedian of the American stage, in the part of the lover, as a professional wager that fidelity itself could be the attraction. The wager's terms reversed the settlement of 1770. Gentleman had defended the procession on the ground that most of an audience prefers show to sense; Cushman's production bet that a reputation, and a house, could be built on sense, on the advertised recovery of what the author wrote, and the bet succeeded.

The wager also clarifies what restoration meant in 1845, because Cushman's acting text was nobody's idea of a complete Shakespeare. Nineteenth-century evenings were shortened as a matter of course, hers included, and the claim her production made was narrower and shrewder than completeness: that at the point where the adaptation had most famously improved the author, the author's disposition would be played. Restoration, at its first station, was thus selective and symbolic, a recovered ending standing as earnest for a recovered allegiance. That selectivity is not a stain on the achievement; it is the mechanism by which the movement advanced. Each restorer recovered the passage whose recovery the moment could applaud, and left the surrounding cuts for a later conscience. Rosaline, whose restoration in print waited until 1882, could not be recovered by a stage that still needed the hero's heart undivided for its pathos; the procession, a piece of pure show, survived in various hands long after the conversation fell. The movement's history is the history of which passage became recoverable when, and the tomb came first because the tomb was where the adaptation's argument, and the original's counter-argument, stood in the plainest opposition.

George Fletcher's thirty-eight pages in the Westminster Review of September 1845 belong to the same station, and their date is not a coincidence to be noted but a mechanism to be understood. Fletcher demolished Garrick's alterations wholesale as the product of "the overweening vanity and meretricious taste of a mere actor, though a great one", and dismissed the dirge of the procession as paltry and sentimental. Print polemic and stage practice, the review and the Romeo, arrived together because they were two expressions of one cultural fact: the licence to improve the author had been withdrawn by the audience that mattered, the reading and reviewing class whose judgement managers could no longer afford to flout. Fletcher's rhetoric overshoots, as demolition rhetoric does; Garrick's taste was neither meretricious nor mere, and the article on his adaptation in this series renders the fairer verdict that he restored a repertory by the only means his stage understood. But the overshoot is the evidence. A critic in 1770 defending the procession could assume the house agreed with him; a critic in 1845 attacking the whole Garrick settlement could assume the same. Between the assumptions lies the transfer of authority this article is tracing, and Fletcher is its loudest single document.

## Phelps at Sadler's Wells: Restoration as a Programme

If Cushman made restoration an event, Samuel Phelps made it a policy. His management of Sadler's Wells from 1844, in the Islington house he ran with Mary Amelia Warner and then alone, mounted the greater part of the Shakespeare canon across eighteen seasons, and did so on a stated principle that the plays would be given with their poetry substantially intact, their texts treated as the production's first document rather than its raw material. The restoration of Shakespeare's ending to the London stage in 1847 belongs inside that programme, and its place inside a programme is what distinguishes the station from Cushman's. The American restoration was a tragedian's wager on a single play's close; the Islington restoration was a manager's standing order applied across a repertory, Romeo and Juliet taking its turn in a sequence that returned play after play to something nearer the transmitted text. Where the wager proved an audience would come, the programme proved a theatre could be run that way, season after season, at popular prices, without the apology of spectacle doing the selling.

### Did Phelps play the whole play?

No production of the period played the whole play, and Phelps's did not either; a working repertory evening required shortening, and his promptbooks belong to a theatre, not a library. What distinguishes his shortening is its direction of loyalty: the cuts served the transmitted text's shape rather than an adapter's redesign.

The ending stood as Shakespeare disposed it, the poetry was the production's advertised substance, and restoration at Sadler's Wells meant the text governed and the manager trimmed, where for a century the manager had governed and the text supplied material.

The distinction in that answer is the distinction the whole movement turns on, and it deserves a paragraph of its own because posterity has repeatedly mistaken Phelps for a completist and then convicted him of hypocrisy from his own promptbooks. The promptbooks show cuts; the posters show claims; the naive reading calls the gap a fraud. The better reading, argued here, is that Phelps represents the moment the hierarchy inverted while the arithmetic lagged. Garrick's theatre cut from a position of ownership and printed the cuts as improvements; Phelps's theatre cut from a position of stewardship and experienced the cuts as concessions. A steward's concessions shrink under pressure from the document he stewards, and the subsequent history is exactly that shrinkage: each generation of self-described restorers inherited both the posture and a slightly smaller residue of cuts, until the residue itself became the scandal that William Poel and his successors organised against. To ask whether Phelps was faithful is to ask the wrong question of 1847. The right question is what his theatre believed it was answerable to, and the answer, the printed text of the author, is the answer the eighteenth century would not have given.

Sadler's Wells also matters for its address. Restoration did not begin at the patent theatres with their fashionable houses and their investment in the old settlement; it began in Islington, before an audience the West End regarded as suburban, under a manager who had been a provincial actor and a member of Macready's company, formed in the very managements whose respectful cutting he now outbid. The geography repeats a pattern the movement shows again with Poel's marginal venues and the early Granville-Barker seasons: reform enters from the profession's edge, where the sunk costs of the settlement are smallest and a distinctive fidelity can be a house style rather than an apology. The patent stages, committed to spectacle as their competitive weapon, received restoration last and, in the Lyceum's case, translated it into spectacle's own language, a translation examined below.

## The Slow Clock of Print: Editions, Acting Texts, and the 1882 Turn

Print tells the movement's truth about itself because print has no receipts to protect on a given night and therefore reveals preference rather than necessity. The Library of Congress survey of the altered printings, used throughout this series, records editions of 1794, 1814, 1819, and 1874 adopting Garrick's alterations, the added conversation among them: a sequence that spans the adaptation's stage reign and extends nearly three decades beyond Phelps. Each of those editions is an act of re-choice. An editor in 1819, with the scholarly text open beside him, prefaced the waking scene and defended it; an editor in 1874, a generation after the stage restoration, still found the conversation the ending to print. The sequence disposes of the comforting theory that the adaptation survived by inertia. It survived by repeated preference, in the medium where preference is most deliberate, and it fell there only when the editorial mainstream changed its criterion from effect to evidence.

The 1882 edition is therefore the print movement's Cushman moment, and its contents repay attention: it restored Rosaline to the text and let Romeo die at once after taking the poison, without the deathbed conversation. The pairing is elegant and diagnostic. Rosaline belongs to the play's first movement, the tomb conversation to its last; the edition that surrendered both ends of the Garrick settlement at once was announcing that the criterion had changed for the whole text, not merely for its most famous crux. Print, having been the adaptation's last stronghold, became the restoration's registrar, and the acting text, that hybrid instrument compiled for a production and sold in the lobby, began its long convergence toward the scholarly text. The convergence was slow because the acting edition answered to a different master: it printed what a given company played, cuts and all, and visiting the promptbook record of the later nineteenth century means watching the registrar and the lobby copy disagree for decades, the edition whole, the performance shortened, each calling itself Shakespeare.

Textual scholarship's role in this convergence is easy to overstate and wrong to omit. The nineteenth century's great editorial labours, the Cambridge edition of 1863 to 1866 under Clark and Wright and the New Variorum under Horace Howard Furness from 1871, did not descend into the playhouse with instructions; no manager cut or restored a line because a collation told him to. Their work changed the climate in which such decisions were defended. A variorum page, with its column of early readings and its sediment of commentary, makes the transmitted text visible as an object with a history, and makes an adapter's silent substitution visible as exactly that. Once the play existed for the reading public as a documented artefact, the stage's freehold in it became harder to assert in print controversy, and controversy was the restorers' native element. The scholar's contribution to the stage restoration was thus jurisdictional: scholarship established that the text had custodians other than the profession that performed it, and that those custodians could audit the profession's work. Jill Levenson's Oxford edition, two centuries on, would build its promptbook archive on precisely that jurisdiction, the edition as the theatre's auditor and memory.

## Spectacle's Counter-Claim: Kean, Irving, and the Fidelity of Things

The strongest counter-argument the restoration faced was not Garrick's ghost but the Victorian stage's own magnificence, and it must be given its full force before the verdict can be earned. Charles Kean's Princess's Theatre productions of the 1850s mounted Shakespeare with an antiquarian conscience new to the English stage: costumes researched, architecture reconstructed, Verona built rather than indicated. The claim implicit in such production was that fidelity is a property of the whole evening, and that a stage picture true to the Italian past honoured the play's world more substantially than an uncut text recited against flats. Henry Irving's Lyceum Romeo and Juliet of 1882, the production examined in [Henry Irving and Ellen Terry (1882)](/2027/10/01/henry-irving-ellen-terry-1882/), carried the claim to its summit in the very year print surrendered Rosaline: a production of celebrated beauty, pictorial, musical, atmospheric, and cut with a freedom the eighteenth century would have recognised, including, the Yale survey notes, the curtailing of the action after Juliet's death that eighteenth-century precedent had normalised. The sibling article on that production owns its record; here the Lyceum stands as the counter-claim's masterpiece, proof that in 1882 a London audience could be offered fidelity and spectacle in one evening and receive, in the text, a shortened play.

### Was Irving's Lyceum production a restoration?

In scenery, costume, and professed reverence it presented itself as one, and the presentation was sincere within its own definition of the word. In text it was not: the acting version cut freely, shaped the evening toward its stars, and curtailed the close in the adaptation's inherited manner.

The production is therefore the movement's clearest specimen of fidelity divided against itself, the fidelity of things at its historical maximum in the same season that the fidelity of words achieved its print landmark across the river of the book trade.

The adjudication this article owes the counter-claim is not a sneer at scenery. Spectacle kept the play before large publics through decades when a bare-stage fidelity would have played to the converted; Irving's Verona formed the imaginations of spectators who then read the whole text in the editions the same culture sold them, and the pictorial stage and the whole text advanced together as properties of the play's prestige, however sharply their custodians quarrelled. The quarrel itself, though, was real, and it was about time. Every minute of built Verona was a minute taken from spoken Verona, and the promptbooks arbitrate the dispute without sentiment: where the scenery grew, the text shrank. The restoration of the words therefore required not merely a change of textual conscience but a change of scenic economy, a willingness to let language carry what lumber had carried. That willingness is the contribution of the movement's next stations, and it arrived, once again, from the margin.

## Poel and the Platform: Restoration Changes Its Meaning

William Poel, founder of the Elizabethan Stage Society in 1894, redirected the movement by redefining its object. The actor-managers had restored, or failed to restore, a text; Poel proposed to restore the conditions under which the text was written to be heard: a platform stage without pictorial scenery, continuous action without act intervals for scene-shifting, costume of a generalised Elizabethan cast, and speech delivered at a speed the pictorial stage had forgotten. His Romeo and Juliet, like his other revivals, was a scholar's production in the hands of a practitioner who had measured the Fortune's dimensions and read the quartos as performance documents. The textual consequence followed from the scenic premise with logical neatness: a stage that spends nothing on lumber can afford to spend everything on lines, and Poel's performances gave substantially more of the transmitted text than the great houses gave, at a pace that made the gift practicable. Restoration, under Poel, quietly changed its meaning from the recovery of passages to the recovery of a medium.

The change of meaning carried a cost the record should register. Poel's reform played to small audiences in halls and society venues; the commercial stage could admire it as archaeology and ignore it as competition, much as it had once absorbed fidelity as a poster claim while cutting in the promptbook. The reform's commercial impossibility is the standing rebuke to any simple narrative in which truth defeats habit by being true. What carried Poel's premises into the mainstream was translation, the process by which a margin's practice becomes a centre's style once the economics can be made to work, and the translator was Harley Granville-Barker, whose Savoy productions of 1912 applied simplified staging, speed, and textual respect inside a fashionable London house. Granville-Barker's later Prefaces argued the doctrine in prose of unusual penetration: that the play's construction is its meaning, that cutting for effect destroys the proportions the author built, and that the actor's business is to serve a design larger than the evening's applause. With the Prefaces, the restoration acquired its theory, a century after it acquired its first stations; the movement that began as a tragedian's wager ends its formative period as a director's philosophy.

## The Twentieth Century: Fidelity as Prestige

By the time John Gielgud staged the play at the New Theatre in 1935, in the celebrated alternation recorded as [Gielgud and Olivier Alternate Romeo (1935)](/2027/10/15/gielgud-olivier-alternate-romeo-1935/), the values the restorers had fought for had become the values a prestige production advertised. The sibling article on the alternation owns the production's record; its station here is the movement's terminus, the point at which a substantially respected text, verse spoken as the production's chief claim on attention, and a director's shaping intelligence openly acknowledged as such, could be assumed as the normal furniture of a serious Romeo and Juliet. The distance travelled is best measured against 1748. Garrick's audience expected the manager to improve the author and applauded the improvements as the manager's genius; the 1935 audience expected the company to serve the author and applauded the service as the company's distinction, reserving its sense of individual genius for the speaking of the verse and the intelligence of the conception. The alternation itself, two leading actors exchanging the lover and the wit, was a star device of a kind the old settlement would have relished, and its presence inside a textually respectable production is a useful final complication: the restoration never abolished the star system or the theatrical appetite for display. It subordinated them, on the best nights, to a document.

### When did print and stage finally agree on the ending?

Print surrendered Garrick's conversation in the edition of 1882 that also restored Rosaline, and the leading stages had surrendered it a generation earlier, in America in 1845 and in London in 1847. Agreement between the performed and printed endings therefore dates from the later nineteenth century.

The agreement remained untidy at the edges, since American stages played the added conversation as late as 1899 and popular memory kept the waking longer than either medium.

That lag, between the metropolitan stage, the printed edition, the provincial and American stage, and the audience's stored expectation, is the movement's final lesson in method. Restoration is not one variable but a bundle, and its historians go wrong when they date the bundle by any single strand. The tomb conversation had at least four lives, in the London repertory, in the American repertory, in the reading edition, and in the opera house, where Gounod's Roméo et Juliette of 1867 built its close on Garrick's disposition and carried the waking to audiences who never entered a spoken theatre. A movement that ends at four different times in four different media is not badly dated; it is correctly described only by the bundle, and the InsightCrunch restoration timeline's columns, which separate what changed from what persisted at every station, exist to enforce that discipline.

## Why the Tomb Had to Fall First

The order of the restoration, tomb first and first act last, was not an accident of chronology but a consequence of legibility. A manifesto requires a gesture an audience can read in a single evening, and only the tomb offered one: the difference between the duet and the swift deaths is perceptible to a spectator who knows nothing of quartos, because it is a difference in what happens, in who speaks, in how long grief is allowed to sing. Restoring Rosaline, by contrast, changes an emphasis; restoring the bawdy changes a register; restoring the public scenes changes a frame. Each reform is real, and none can headline a campaign. Cushman's choice of the ending as her declaration therefore reveals a strategist's understanding of her medium: the theatre persuades by events, and the restoration needed an event before it could have a programme. Phelps supplied the programme in 1847, but the programme's credibility rested on the event of 1845, on a tragedian having already staked a reputation on the proposition that Shakespeare's disposition, played straight, could hold a house.

The tomb's priority also had a polemical logic. The conversation was the adaptation's most defensible component, the one its champions cited when the whole settlement was indicted, since its defence could appeal past Shakespeare to the Italian sources where the lovers meet awake. To attack the settlement at its strongest point, as Fletcher did in print and the restorers did in practice, was to deny the defence its refuge: if the waking fell, nothing else in the adapted text could claim a higher warrant than habit. The strategy carried a risk the record should acknowledge, because it made the movement's early reputation hostage to a single scene's reception, and a colder house in 1845 might have set the documentary cause back a generation. That the risk was taken, by a performer with everything to lose, is why 1845 rather than any quieter date opens the movement's heroic period; and that the risk succeeded is why the tomb remains, in every later account, the restoration's emblem, the scene in which the audience can be shown, rather than told, what changed when the document took custody of the stage.

## The Restoration's Witnesses: Memoir, Review, and Receipt

The movement's evidence survives in three kinds of witness, and their disagreements are as instructive as their agreements. The review speaks for the moment's articulate opinion, Fletcher demolishing in 1845 what Gentleman had defended in 1770, and its testimony is biased toward controversy, since a review exists to judge and judges most memorably at extremes. The memoir speaks for the profession's self-image, managers and tragedians recounting their fidelity in retirement, and its testimony is biased toward intention, crediting the rememberer with the purest motives the record allows. The receipt, the house's nightly arithmetic preserved in account books and benefit records, speaks for the audience's behaviour rather than its opinions, and its testimony is biased toward the popular, registering what filled seats whether or not the filling was approved. A history built on reviews alone will overstate the restoration's suddenness, since reviewing turned in a season; a history built on memoirs will overstate its purity; a history built on receipts will understate its meaning, since houses filled for spectacles the movement opposed and for restorations it blessed with the same indifference. The sound method, practised in this article's sources wherever they permit, is triangulation: the claim of a manifesto checked against the promptbook, the promptbook against the receipts, the receipts against the review that reported the night a failure or a triumph.

Triangulation also explains the movement's most persistent source problem, the gap between announced and performed texts that runs from Kemble's editions to Irving's programmes. Announcements belong to the memoir class of evidence, statements of self-image made in advance; promptbooks belong to the record of behaviour. Where the two diverge, the historian's duty is not to cry hypocrisy but to locate the pressure that bent the intention, an interval, a star's indisposition, a scene that would not shift in time, and the location of that pressure is usually the movement's real history at that station. The restoration advanced exactly where intention, economics, and opinion could be made to point the same way, and its historians serve it best by reporting all three witnesses even when, especially when, their stories refuse to merge into the clean homecoming that commemoration prefers.

## The Critics' Quarrel, Adjudicated: Effect Against Evidence

Three named witnesses frame the disagreement every account of the restoration must adjudicate, and a fourth supplies its theory. Francis Gentleman, the theatre critic of 1770, defended the adapted settlement from inside its own logic: the procession and the pathos served the audience that existed, and a manager who scorned that service would play Shakespeare to empty benches and serve the author worse. George Fletcher, the performance critic of 1845, prosecuted the same settlement from the new jurisdiction: the alterations were a mere actor's vanity, and the text stood in judgement over the profession that had improved it. Edward Dowden, the textual scholar whose Arden introduction printed Garrick's added dialogue entire, held both verdicts at once in the act of scholarship itself, condemning the conversation as an interpolation while preserving it as a document, and observing that its motive had been idealised in Gounod's opera. Harley Granville-Barker, practitioner and critic in one, converted the prosecution into a doctrine of production: fidelity is not antiquarian obedience but the practical discovery that the play's effects are built into its proportions, so that cutting for effect defeats effect itself.

The adjudication argued here accepts Fletcher's jurisdiction and rejects his rhetoric. The jurisdiction, that the transmitted text audits the stage, is the restoration's founding principle, and the stations from Cushman onward are unintelligible without it. The rhetoric, that the adaptation was meretricious vanity, fails against the record Gentleman represents: the settlement was intelligent, popular, and for its century arguably necessary, and its masterpiece, the tomb conversation, manufactured an experience audiences went on wanting after the jurisdiction changed, as its operatic afterlife proves. Gentleman, conversely, is accepted on the facts of his century and rejected on the principle he builds from them. That most of an audience prefers show to sense was a fair report of 1770; that the preference settles the text's claims is the proposition the nineteenth century withdrew, and its withdrawal is the restoration. Dowden's double posture, condemn and preserve, is adjudicated the scholar's correct settlement of the whole quarrel: the conversation has no claim on the stage and every claim on the archive, and confusing those claims, in either direction, is the error the quarrel's extremists share. Granville-Barker then supplies the synthesis the movement needed, because his doctrine returns effect to the fidelity side of the argument. A production need not choose between the author's design and the audience's experience once it understands that the design is the experience's engineering; the ironic close, played at the speed and in the proportions the text prescribes, out-affects the duet it replaced, and the restoration's final argument is not that fidelity is dutiful but that fidelity works.

### Was the restoration ever complete?

Completeness is a standard no century has met, including the centuries that coined the demand. Every production shortens the play somewhere, by lines, by tempo, or by the silent arithmetic of casting and interval, and the promptbooks of the most professed restorers preserve their concessions.

The movement's achievement is therefore not a finished text on stage but a reversed presumption: alteration must now argue its case before the document, where for a hundred years the document had to argue its case before the alteration.

The verdict on how and why the text was restored follows from the stations and the quarrel together. The text was restored because the culture relocated authority over the play from the profession that performed it to the document that transmitted it, and the relocation happened in print before it happened in the theatre, in reviewing and editing before in management. Its agents were hybrid figures who stood in both jurisdictions at once: a tragedian who wagered a reputation on an ending, a manager who ran a suburban theatre on a textual policy, a scholar who printed the interpolation he condemned, a director who theorised the proportions he staged. Its pace was set by incentives, not by knowledge, since the knowledge, the printed original, sat on every manager's shelf throughout. Its incompleteness is structural rather than shameful: a living stage will always negotiate with a fixed text, and the restoration's legacy is the terms of the negotiation, which require the stage to keep its side of the bargain in the open, argued from the document, where Garrick's century struck its bargains in the confidence that the document had no standing to object. The cliche that the stage always played Shakespeare is thus doubly wrong. The stage played Garrick, Cibber, and Otway in Shakespeare's name for the better part of two centuries, and the recovery of the name's true text was the work of generations who treated the recovery as the profession's honour rather than its humiliation. That is the history the timeline records, and it is a better story than the homecoming myth it replaces, because it credits the restorers with the difficult thing they actually did: not finding a lost play, but changing their minds, in public, at professional cost, about whose play it was.

## What "Restored" Meant Inside the Promptbooks

The word restored, applied to a nineteenth-century production, names a claim before it names a fact, and the promptbooks are where the claim goes to be audited. A promptbook, the marked copy from which a production was run, preserves the performed text in the only form that cannot flatter it: lines struck through, passages transposed, entrances reassigned, effects cued against speeches. Read across the century, the promptbook record tells a more granular story than the parade of famous restorations, because it shows restoration proceeding passage by passage and motive by motive, with different parts of the play recovering at different rates for different reasons. The tomb recovered first because its recovery was a manifesto. The first act recovered last because its recovery was an inconvenience. Between those poles, every scene had its own timetable, set by what the scene cost to restore and what its restoration bought.

The economics of the individual passage explain the sequence better than any history of taste. Restoring the tomb conversation's original disposition cost a production its most reliable effect and bought it a reputation for seriousness; once Cushman had shown the reputation could fill a house, the purchase became rational for ambitious managements. Restoring Rosaline cost the first act its clean sentimental line and bought nothing an audience could applaud on the night, since the gain, a hero whose heart has a history, registers across an evening rather than in a moment. Restoring the bawdy cost a production its claim on family audiences, the broadest and most dependable market the Victorian theatre possessed, and bought a scholarly credit that school parties could not be sold. Restoring the public scenes, the Prince's judgements, the citizens, the full machinery of the feud, cost playing time and crowd wages and bought a political frame that star-centred marketing had no use for. Each passage therefore waited for the constituency that valued it: the ending for the tragedians, the text's architecture for the scholar-managers, the language entire for the reformers of the platform stage. The movement, seen from the promptbooks, is a queue of passages awaiting their purchasers.

This granular view also corrects the moralised version of the story in which enlightened restorers defeat benighted adapters. The promptbooks show the same managements restoring and cutting in the same production, often in the same act, and the mixture was not hypocrisy but triage. A manager with three hours, a star's contract, a scene dock full of built Verona, and a public educated by reviews to demand Shakespeare, allocated fidelity where it showed and economy where it hid. The allocation is the honest object of study, because it reveals what each generation actually prized when prizing had a price. Judged by allocation, the nineteenth century prized the ending's disposition first, the poetry's bulk second, and the text's completeness a distant third; the twentieth century, schooled by Poel's arithmetic and Granville-Barker's doctrine, reordered the list until completeness could be defended as the condition of the other two. The restoration timeline's columns, what changed and what persisted, are simply this allocation written as a chronology.

A further correction follows for the idea of the acting text itself. Posterity, possessing the scholarly edition as its default play, tends to imagine the acting text as a fallen version of it, the whole minus whatever a manager removed. The promptbooks suggest the opposite genealogy: for most of the period, the acting text was the play's primary public form and the scholarly edition its commentary, a document of the study whose authority over the stage had to be established rather than assumed. The restoration, in this light, is the process by which the commentary annexed the performance, the study's text becoming the stage's master copy, with the promptbook demoted from constitution to annotation. Editors such as Levenson, whose Oxford edition built its performance record from a collected archive of promptbooks, complete that annexation in scholarship: the promptbook survives, honoured and minutely read, as evidence about a text it no longer governs. What the restorers restored, at the deepest level, was a hierarchy of documents.

One further exhibit belongs in this section because it condenses the hierarchy into a single stage direction. The transmitted text ends the tomb sequence with the Prince's summation and the fathers' reconciliation over the bodies, a public close in which the city, not the lovers, speaks last. The adapted tradition, impatient with public speech after private catastrophe, curtailed that close, in some hands to a gesture, keeping the evening's focus on the bier. The restoration of the full close, the watch, the letters, the Prince's allocation of pardon and punishment, "Some shall be pardon'd, and some punished" (5.3), therefore carried a meaning beyond its lines: it restored the play's frame, the feud's civic cost counted by the community that paid it, against the settlement's concentration on the pair. A production's handling of those final hundred lines is still the quickest diagnostic of its custody, since the frame is what a star-shaped evening most readily sacrifices and what a document-shaped evening most stubbornly keeps, and the promptbooks across two centuries can be sorted, with rough accuracy, by nothing more than the fate of the Prince's last speech.

## Rosaline and the Bawdy: The Passages That Came Back Last

Rosaline's long exile is the restoration's most instructive subplot because her absence was the adaptation's least visible cut. A missing procession can be noticed; a missing first love cannot be missed by an audience that has never been shown her, and the heroine-shaped hole she left was filled by a simpler story, in which Romeo's heart begins with Juliet and the tragedy becomes a fable of love at first sight opposed by a feuding world. The transmitted text tells a harder story. Its Romeo enters sighing for Rosaline, is teased by Benvolio for the doting, not loving, eye, and goes to the Capulet feast to compare his swan with crows: "I'll go along, no such sight to be shown, / But to rejoice in splendour of mine own" (1.2). The shared sonnet at the feast, "If I profane with my unworthiest hand" (1.5), lands differently when the hand belongs to a practised Petrarchan rather than an innocent: the play's argument about love's language, its suspicion that fine phrases are a transferable currency, depends on the hero having spent the currency before. Garrick's erasure of Rosaline therefore altered the play's thesis while appearing to alter only its cast list, and the criticism of the adapted century, reading the lovers as pure origin, inherited the alteration as an interpretation.

That inheritance explains why Rosaline's return waited for print and for 1882. A stage restoration of Rosaline required re-educating an audience's first act in public, against the grain of the fable the culture cherished, and it offered no single scene in which the restoration could be applauded as Cushman's tomb had been. Print could proceed quietly: an editor restoring the early scenes faced no receipts, only the document, and the edition of 1882 that restored Rosaline alongside the swift tomb death was applying one criterion at both ends of the play. The pairing deserves its fame within the movement, because it demonstrates that the restoration's completion, such as it was, arrived when the criterion changed from the scene's effect to the text's integrity. A criterion of effect restores famous passages; a criterion of integrity restores forgotten ones, and Rosaline, precisely because nobody's favourite scene depended on her, is the integrity criterion's purest trophy.

The bawdy's return followed a slower curve still, governed by the sociology of the audience rather than by editorial conscience. The adapted text's laundered language had fitted the play to the mixed and family audiences on which the nineteenth-century theatre's economics rested, and Mercutio's full flight, the Queen Mab speech's earthier registers, the Nurse's whole idiom, carried a price in respectability that managers calculated exactly. Scholarly editions restored the language to readers generations before the stage dared it entire, and the stage's gradual readmission of the play's earth tracked the audience's own changing composition, the slow conversion of Shakespeare from a family possession into an adult classic. Here the restoration's agent was neither a tragedian nor an editor but the audience itself, ageing into a public that no longer required the national poet to be a governess. The sequence of returns, tomb, architecture, Rosaline, bawdy, is therefore a map of the obstacles in ascending order of social difficulty: a theatrical effect, a scholarly principle, an interpretive inconvenience, and a moral settlement. The movement conquered them in that order because that was the order in which their defenders could be outargued.

## The Procession's Afterlife and the Waking in the Opera House

Garrick's funeral procession, the dirge-borne bearing of Juliet to the Capulet monument between the fourth and fifth acts, was the adaptation's most spectacular interpolation and, in its way, its most honest: pure show, defended as show by Gentleman in 1770, and beloved accordingly. Its stage life inside the spoken tragedy faded as the century's taste turned, yet the procession's patterns proved migratory. The Hodgson toy-theatre sheets of 1823, itemising the Covent Garden staging in twelve groups of figures, preserved the spectacle in miniature for children, fixing the procession as the tragedy's visual signature for a generation that met the play first in paper. When Charles Kemble's company carried the play to Paris in 1827 and Harriet Smithson's Juliet conquered the city, Hector Berlioz built his dramatic symphony Roméo et Juliette of 1839 around the memory, and its funeral convoy, with its choral dirge for the dead virgin, transposed the Drury Lane procession into the concert hall. The procession thus survived its own play's restoration by emigrating into media where spectacle is the substance rather than the competitor: the toy theatre, the symphony, and, in the waking's case, the opera.

Gounod's Roméo et Juliette of 1867 is the emigration's capital. Opera's native currency is the duet; a heroine who wakes to a corpse can sing an aria, but an opera built on the lovers requires their voices together, and Gounod's close follows Garrick's disposition, Juliet reviving while Romeo lives, the farewell sustained in music before the dagger ends it. Dowden, printing Garrick's dialogue in condemnation, noted that its motive had been idealised and exalted in the opera, and the observation completes the adjudication this article has been building. The waking was not bad art; it was art answering to a different medium's necessities and a different century's theory of feeling. The spoken stage's restoration withdrew it because the spoken stage had changed its theory, relocating the tragedy's power from shared recognition to solitary irony. The opera house, whose theory never changed, kept it without embarrassment, and kept it before publics across Europe and America whose stored image of the ending was therefore Garrick's long after the playhouses had surrendered it. One consequence persists in classrooms: readers meeting the printed close for the first time sometimes receive the swift, unshared deaths as an anticlimax or even a mistake, because the culture's operatic and pictorial memory has already told them how the scene goes. The restoration won the stage and the edition; the adaptation, in exile, kept the imagination's default settings, and teachers of the play still spend their first hour on the tomb evicting a tenant who left the theatre in 1847.

## Provinces, Tours, and the Geography of Delay

Restoration had a geography, and its map explains the movement's staggered dates better than any single theatre's history. The metropolitan stage, where reviews were written and reputations audited, moved first and announced loudest; the provincial and touring circuits, where the play lived for most of its audience, moved at the pace of stock scenery, inherited promptbooks, and local expectation. A touring company carried its Romeo and Juliet as a capital asset, its cuts built into its properties and its business arrangements, and revised that asset only when revision paid. The altered ending's American survival to 1899, recorded in the Yale survey, belongs to this geography: not a failure of intelligence on a distant stage, but the normal conservatism of a repertory system in which a proven effect is a property and properties are not discarded on the word of a London review. The movement's historians, working from metropolitan archives, habitually date the restoration by its announcements; the geography suggests dating it by its arrivals, town by town, in the promptbooks of companies whose names no history keeps.

The geography also ran in the other direction, and the reverse current is the more interesting. The first stage restoration happened in America, before the London restoration, at the hands of a performer whose authority the metropolis had not granted and would spend decades digesting. Reform's address at Sadler's Wells, a suburban house outside the patent theatres' fashionable orbit, repeats the pattern inside a single city. Again and again, the initiative sits at the margin, where the settlement's sunk costs are smallest and a distinctive fidelity can function as a young management's or a touring star's competitive signature. The centre's role is ratification: the Lyceum's prestige, the fashionable success of Granville-Barker's Savoy seasons, the New Theatre's alternation in 1935, each certifying for the mainstream what the margin had proved could draw. A history of the restoration written as a history of its ratifications will always lag the true history, which is a history of margins, wagers, and arrivals. The timeline in this article therefore keeps the unfashionable stations in the same type as the famous ones, on the argument that the movement happened where the table's rows are densest, not where the applause was loudest.

Colonial and continental circuits extend the map beyond this article's scope, and the boundary should be marked honestly: the play's German, French, and Russian stage lives, treated in sibling articles of this series, ran on their own clocks of translation and adaptation, often preserving in other languages dispositions the English stage had retired, so that the waking and the procession enjoyed foreign afterlives independent of their English obituaries. The English restoration was an event in one theatrical culture, exported unevenly, and its claim to be the play's homecoming holds only within the jurisdiction of the English document. The qualification strengthens rather than weakens the article's thesis. Authority over a classic is always local before it is general, vested in a specific profession's customs and a specific print trade's criteria, and the restoration's staggered, geographic, unfinished character is what a transfer of such authority actually looks like when measured rather than mythologised.

## The Acting Edition and the Lobby Copy

Between the scholarly edition and the promptbook stood a third document, the acting edition, printed for sale in the theatre lobby and the bookseller's shop, and its history is the restoration's history in paperback. The acting edition claimed to print the play as performed, and for the adapted century it printed Garrick's disposition as the tragedy's public text, carrying the conversation and the cuts into the hands of readers who might never see a scholarly edition. Its commercial logic was circular and powerful: the lobby copy recorded the performance, the next provincial production played from the lobby copy, and the record became the prescription. Breaking that circle required the scholarly edition to acquire a public authority the lobby copy could not match, and the nineteenth century's editorial monuments, Cambridge and Variorum, supplied exactly that, mass and prestige in the study's favour. By the century's end the acting edition had begun its long retreat toward the scholarly text, printing more of the play than any company played and labelling the surplus for the reader, an inversion of its founding purpose that marks the annexation's completion in the marketplace.

The lobby copy also exposes a quiet truth about the restorers' audiences. The spectator who bought the acting edition at Sadler's Wells or the Lyceum bought a claim as much as a text: the claim that the evening just witnessed stood in a certified relation to the author's words. Under the settlement, that certification was the manager's name; Garrick's name on a title page warranted the version as Garrick warranted it. Under the restoration, certification migrated to the document itself, to title pages announcing the text as Shakespeare's, restored, complete, or as performed at a named scholarly standard, and the migration tracks the movement's thesis in miniature. Authority that had been personal, the tragedian's and manager's charisma underwriting a version, became documentary, the edition's collation underwriting a production. Modern marketing of the classic play, with its boasts of uncut texts and original practices, is the lobby copy's descendant, still selling certification; the difference is that the certificate now cites the document rather than the man, which is the restoration's whole achievement stated as a publishing practice.

## Dowden's Appendix: Scholarship Preserves What It Condemns

Edward Dowden's Arden edition of 1900, the scholarly monument of the restorations' first century, performed an act whose ambiguity this article has already used as evidence and which deserves a fuller reading. Dowden condemned Garrick's added dialogue and printed it entire in his introduction, preserving for every future reader the interpolation his edition existed to displace. The gesture is usually read as scholarly completeness, and it is; but it is also the restoration's characteristic settlement with its enemy, preservation without performance, the archive opened as the stage closed. In that settlement the whole quarrel finds its equitable end. Garrick's conversation loses its claim to be played and gains an impregnable claim to be read, cited, and taught, and its author's genius is honoured in the only court whose verdicts are permanent. The adaptation's partisans, from Gentleman onward, had asked that the scene be judged by the pleasure it gave; the archive grants the pleasure a perpetual record while withdrawing the stage on which it was given, a judgement more devastating than any review because it converts a living rival into a specimen.

The specimen's afterlife in scholarship completes the pattern. The added dialogue became, across the twentieth century, one of the most studied passages in the play's whole transmission, edited, counted, sourced to Otway and the novella, and weighed by historians of taste, while whole scenes of the transmitted text went comparatively unexamined. Restoration thus produced its own irony: the document's victory made the defeated interpolation famous, a celebrity of the footnote, and the fame is a backhanded confirmation of the scene's original power. Scenes that merely tidy an author are forgotten, as Garrick's quieter surgeries were; the tomb conversation is remembered because it supplied an experience its century could not surrender without a fight and later centuries could not study without respect. The verdict of this article has held both truths together throughout, Fletcher's jurisdiction and Gentleman's facts, and Dowden's appendix is their material form: the prosecution's brief and the defence's masterpiece, bound in one volume, filed under the author's name.

## The Interval, the Curtain, and Other Silent Editors

The restoration's enemies included several that never wrote a line. The act interval, lengthening as scenery grew complex, edited the play by fatigue, pressing managers to shorten the text the intervals framed. The curtain call, the star's contract, the benefit night's custom of a favourite scene, the censor's eye on the language, the price of candles and then of gaslight labour, the railway timetable that governed a touring company's last act: each exerted a silent editorial pressure, and most of the pressures pointed the same way, toward the shorter, softer, more spectacular play. A history of the restoration that confines itself to stated intentions will miss how much of the adapted text was written by logistics. Conversely, the document's victory was assisted by logistics of its own: cheap scholarly editions that put the whole text in every schoolroom, railways that let a London promptbook standardise a circuit, and the long run, which amortised a full text's rehearsal across a season rather than a week. Phelps's repertory economics, many plays kept in rotation before a loyal local house, suited textual seriousness as the patent houses' spectacular novelties never could, and the fit between an economic model and a textual conscience is not a coincidence to apologise for but a mechanism to record. Ideas about the author's rights travelled on the vehicles that could carry them, and the restoration's stations are, among other things, a list of vehicles: the star's wager, the suburban repertory, the scholarly tome, the platform society, the director's season.

Remembering the silent editors also disciplines the movement's praise. The restorers were brave at the points where bravery was needed, the tomb above all; but they were also carried by changes in print prices, theatre economics, and audience composition that no manifesto caused, and the fullest account credits the carriers as well as the champions. That distribution of credit is the mature form of the story the homecoming myth tells in miniature, and it is the form in which the timeline's rows should be read: each station a meeting of an intention with a vehicle, a conscience with a circumstance, the document's long patience rewarded when the century's machinery finally ran in its direction.

## From Manager to Director: The New Custodian

The restoration's last transformation concerns the office that holds the play in the theatre. The eighteenth century's custodian was the actor-manager, whose authority was proprietary and personal, and whose alterations carried his signature as their warrant. The nineteenth century's restorers were transitional figures in that office: Cushman a star who spent charisma on fidelity, Phelps a manager who subordinated charisma to policy, Irving a star-manager who spent the office's old freedoms inside the new rhetoric, so that his promptbooks and his posters tell the movement's two stories at once. The twentieth century completed the transition by inventing a new custodian, the director, whose authority is interpretive rather than proprietary and whose signature is a conception rather than a text. Granville-Barker, practitioner, theorist, and the Prefaces' author, is the hinge figure, since his productions claimed authority precisely by serving the document, and his prose explained why service was the higher form of mastery.

The director's arrival solved one problem and created the standing problem of the modern stage. It solved the custody question in the document's favour: a director who cuts must now defend the cut as interpretation, in the open, against a text whose claims reviewers and editions articulate. It created the problem that interpretation, thus licensed, can reshape a production as thoroughly as any adapter while wearing fidelity's colours, through emphasis, casting, tempo, and the scenography that replaced lumber with light. The restoration's heirs therefore police a subtler border than Cushman's generation faced, between the document's government and the conception's, and the policing is done in the same arena the nineteenth century built: print controversy, armed with promptbooks. None of this diminishes the restoration's verdict. The movement set the terms on which the modern stage argues with the play, document first, and every subsequent quarrel, however heated, has been conducted inside those terms. A transfer of authority is not measured by the perfection of what follows it, but by the impossibility of returning to what preceded it, and no management since Phelps has been able to advertise an improvement of Shakespeare with Garrick's serene confidence that the advertisement would be believed.

## The Scholar in the Theatre: How Editions Entered the Rehearsal Room

The restoration's least visible mechanism was the physical migration of the book. For the adapted century, the edition and the playhouse occupied separate buildings in the culture's geography: the study, where the text was whole, and the theatre, where the text was whatever the promptbook said. The migration that joined them can be traced in the changing furniture of the rehearsal room. An eighteenth-century company rehearsed from parts, each actor holding a scroll of one role's lines and cues, with the whole play existing only in the prompter's copy; a performer's relation to the text was therefore to a fraction of it, and loyalty to the fraction was the professional virtue. The nineteenth century's lengthening rehearsals, its reading of the play entire at the table, its managers who arrived with editions annotated against other editions, altered the performer's relation to the document before they altered a single line of it. Macready's diaries record a manager tormented by his texts; Phelps's seasons presume a company that studies; the scholar-managers of the later century quoted collations in controversy as naturally as they quoted receipts. The book entered the rehearsal room first as a manager's private conscience and ended as the company's common property, and that social fact, more than any manifesto, is the restoration's mechanism at the level of daily work.

The migration explains a feature of the record that puzzles readers of the movement's polemics: the restorers' obsessive concern with small readings, a word in the Mab speech, the assignment of a speech, the punctuation of the tomb. Under the settlement, such minutiae belonged to the study and bored the theatre; under the restoration they became professional currency, because a company that rehearses from the document inherits the document's problems. Every crux the editors debated was, potentially, a decision a stage manager had to make by Friday, and the editions' apparatus, variants marshalled and preferences argued, entered the theatre as a decision-making instrument. The theatrical consequence ran deeper than accuracy. A text with a documented history invites a different posture from its performers than a text received as a script: the posture of custodianship, in which the actor holds something older and larger than the production, and in which fidelity can be felt as a craft satisfaction rather than resented as a constraint. The twentieth century's prestige of verse speaking, the sense that the words themselves are the event, presumes that posture in every performer, and the posture is the restoration's most durable bequest to the profession's inner life. Garrick's actors served a version; Phelps's served a text; the modern actor serves a transmission, three centuries of documents and decisions carried in a single rehearsal copy, and the service begins, as the restorers taught, with the book open on the table.

## Speed, Verse, and the Sound of a Restored Text

A restored text sounds different, and the movement's acoustic history deserves a section of its own because sound was the ground on which the old settlement made its last stand. The adapted evening had been built for declamation: statuesque delivery, pointed lines, applause harvested at the exits of great speeches, the verse treated as a series of arias for the tragedian's instrument. Garrick's cuts and interpolations served that sound; the tomb conversation is written to be pointed, each speech landing its sentiment before the next begins. The transmitted text resists the method. Its scenes interlock at speed, its shared lines and broken exchanges require an ensemble pace that declamation breaks, and its close, twenty lines of life for the waking Juliet, works only if played faster than grief's dignity prefers. Restoring the text therefore implied restoring a tempo, and the implication frightened a profession whose stars had built their instruments for the other music. Critics of the early restorations complained, in effect, that the play had been hurried; the complaint records the moment the design's engineering began to outvote the soloist's display.

Poel's platform made the acoustic argument physical. Without scene changes to pause for, with the verse thrown across a bare stage at conversational speed, the play's proportions, which the Prefaces would soon theorise, became audible as structure: the feast's glitter against the street's violence, the garden's expansiveness against the cell's haste, the tomb's terrible compression after the long lyrical dilation of the fourth act's lamentations. Granville-Barker's Savoy audiences heard those proportions in a fashionable house, and the hearing converted the restoration from a textual position into an aesthetic experience that could be preferred on the night, without scholarship, by ears alone. That conversion is the movement's completion in the theatre, the point at which fidelity stopped needing to argue from duty because it could argue from pleasure. The modern commonplace that Shakespeare plays better whole is a memory of that conversion, generalised; it was not a commonplace in 1845, when the whole text's champions had to promise an audience that deprivation, the duet surrendered, the procession gone, would feel like gain. The restorers' rhetoric strains under that promise in their prefaces; their successors inherited a world in which the promise had become a platitude, which is what victory looks like in the history of taste.

## What the Audience Had to Relearn

Every transfer of authority over a classic is also a re-education of its audience, and the restoration's pedagogy proceeded scene by scene. The audience of the settlement had been trained to expect certain payments at certain moments: the procession's splendour at the fourth act's close, the duet's release in the tomb, the hero insulated from any love before the heroine, the language kept within the parlour's bounds. The restorers withdrew the payments one by one and asked the house to accept substitutes of a different currency: irony for release, pattern for splendour, a complicated hero for an insulated one, the play's full earth for its laundered sentiment. Re-education on that scale is slow because the payments were not arbitrary; they were the forms in which generations had learned to feel the tragedy, and an audience asked to surrender them was being asked to surrender its own memories of weeping in that theatre, perhaps at a mother's side. The resistance the restorations met, politely in the reviews and stubbornly in the provinces, was therefore not philistinism but loyalty, the house defending its stored experience against the document's correction, and the movement's long timetable is in part the time required for one audience's memories to be replaced by another's.

The pedagogy succeeded by recruiting the audience's pride. The restorers' masterstroke, repeated from Cushman's advertisements to the Lyceum's programmes to the modern uncut marathon, was to make fidelity a distinction the audience could share: to sit for the whole text, to prefer the ironic close, to know why Rosaline matters, became marks of the serious playgoer, and seriousness, flattered, filled seats. A scruple of the study became a fashion of the stalls, and fashion accomplished what argument alone had not, carrying the document's government into the commercial centre the reformers could not reach by conscience. Posterity should record the mechanism without condescension. The history of taste advances by such recruitments, and the restoration's genius, across its long century, was to make the audience a party to the document's victory rather than its victim, so that the house that had once applauded Garrick's improvements learned to applaud their removal as an improvement of its own.

## Measuring the Movement: A Method for Its Historians

The restoration repays the historian who measures it and punishes the one who merely dates it, and a closing word on method is owed before the verdict's consequences are drawn for readers of the play. Four habits of measurement run through this article and constitute its claim on the subject. First, the bundle habit: never date the movement by one strand, since the London stage, the American stage, the printed edition, and the operatic afterlife each run on separate clocks, and a date true of one is false of the others. Second, the promptbook habit: prefer the marked copy to the manifesto, the allocation of fidelity under pressure to the profession of fidelity at leisure. Third, the passage habit: track recoveries scene by scene and motive by motive, since the tomb, the first act, and the bawdy returned for different reasons at different speeds, and the average of their stories is no story at all. Fourth, the authority habit: at every station ask whose word governed, the performer's charisma, the manager's policy, or the document's evidence, because the movement's plot is the migration among those three answers, and its text-level events are symptoms of the migration.

Applied together, the four habits dissolve the homecoming myth and replace it with a more demanding and more credible narrative: a century-long negotiation, conducted in reviews, editions, promptbooks, and wagers, over the custody of the English stage's central possession. The negotiation's outcome was never total and never final, since custody of a living classic is renegotiated by every generation that stages it, and the contemporary quarrels over cuts, concepts, and original practices are the restoration's continuation by other means. What the nineteenth century settled, and what no later quarrel has unsettled, is the venue and the evidence: the argument now happens in the open, before the document, with the promptbook as witness. Garrick's century argued in the confidence that the document had no counsel; every generation since has argued in its presence. To call that settlement the restoration of Shakespeare's text to the stage is accurate enough, provided the phrase is heard as the name of a process and not of a party, a process the timeline in this article sets out station by station, with its persistences left standing in the final column where they belong.

## The Classroom: The Restoration's Second Front

The stage and the edition had a silent partner throughout the movement, the schoolroom, and its influence ran in both directions. Cheap texts put the whole play into pupils' hands at prices the adapted century never offered, and a generation that had read the swift tomb death at a desk arrived at the theatre already partisan for the document, an audience pre-restored by its education. Teachers, in turn, inherited the quarrel as a pedagogical convenience: the adapted ending, with its duet and its arraigned fathers, is easier to feel with than the ironic close, and generations of introductions had to argue classes out of a preference the opera house and the picture book had planted in them before the first lesson. The classroom thus became the restoration's second front, the place where its victory had to be re-won annually against the culture's stored version, and the effort left a deposit in the play's modern reputation. The tomb scene is now the most glossed passage in school editions precisely because it carries a double history, the text's disposition and the adaptation's ghost, and the glosses teach both at once: what Shakespeare wrote, and what the stage for a century preferred instead.

That double teaching is, in the end, the restoration's most valuable legacy to the play's readers. A classic whose stage life was unbroken and unaltered would have no history to learn from itself; this play's broken custody, its century in another man's shape, turns its performance record into an instrument for reading the text. Knowing that Rosaline could be erased for ninety-seven years sharpens the eye for her function in the first act. Knowing that the tomb could sing a duet for a century sharpens the ear for the silence Shakespeare arranged instead. Knowing that fidelity had to be invented, marketed, and fought for sharpens the judgement brought to every modern production that claims the word. The restoration's historians did not merely recover a text; they recovered the questions by which the text is read, and the classroom, where those questions are now routine, is the proof that the recovery outlasted the controversies that achieved it. A pupil who learns that the duet reigned for ninety-seven years, that Rosaline waited until 1882 for her restoration, and that the swift close had to be wagered for by a tragedian and programmed by a suburban manager, has learned something no plot summary can teach: that the play in the book is a survivor, and that survival, for a classic, is a profession's repeated decision rather than a fact of nature.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch restoration timeline, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch restoration timeline is the table in this article listing the stations of the text's return, from Otway's displacement of the play in 1680 through Garrick's adaptation, the printings that adopted his altered ending, the stage restorations of 1845 and 1847, the print restoration of 1882, and the reforms of Poel, Granville-Barker, and the 1935 generation. The pattern it exposes is separation: at almost every station, what changed and what persisted stand side by side, additions falling while habits of cutting survived, so that restoration appears as a gradient measured in promptbooks rather than a single homecoming with a date.

### Q: Why did cuts persist in productions that advertised fidelity to the text?

Cuts persisted because a nineteenth-century evening had fixed limits and growing appetites. Scenery, processions, crowd scenes, and musical intervals consumed time that only the verse could supply, and a manager's reputation depended on the visible splendour of the whole at least as much as on the completeness of the spoken text. Fidelity, in the period's working definition, described a posture toward the author rather than a word count: deference in the preface, reverence in the programme, and shortening in the promptbook. The cuts were therefore not felt as betrayals by the men who made them. They were the ordinary arithmetic of a theatrical economy in which language and lumber competed for the same minutes, and lumber was winning.

### Q: How did Samuel Phelps's management at Sadler's Wells differ from the patent theatres' Shakespeare?

Phelps ran Sadler's Wells in Islington from 1844 on a policy the great houses did not attempt: the greater part of the canon across eighteen seasons, at popular prices, with the poetry treated as the production's substance rather than its material. Where the patent theatres competed through spectacle and star engagements, Phelps competed through repertory and textual seriousness, restoring Shakespeare's ending to the London stage in 1847 as one item in a standing programme rather than as a novelty. His theatre still shortened its texts, as every working theatre did, but the shortening served the transmitted play's shape. The difference was jurisdictional: at Sadler's Wells the document governed and the manager trimmed, while at the patent houses the manager governed and the document supplied.

### Q: What did George Fletcher's Westminster Review essay of 1845 accomplish?

Fletcher's thirty-eight page demolition of Garrick's alterations in the Westminster Review of September 1845 withdrew, in the period's most respectable reviewing venue, the licence under which the adaptation had operated for a century. By naming the changes the product of a mere actor's vanity and meretricious taste, the essay relocated judgement over the stage text from the profession to the reading public, and it did so in the same season that Cushman's American restoration enacted the same relocation in the theatre. Print polemic and stage practice were not cause and effect in a simple line; they were twin expressions of a cultural decision that the author's page outranked the manager's tradition. The essay's rhetoric overshot its target, but its timing made it the restoration's loudest single document.

### Q: Why did the added tomb conversation survive in print after the stage dropped it?

Print obeys incentives different from the repertory's, and the conversation survived there because readers, like audiences before them, valued the experience it supplied. The Library of Congress survey records editions of 1794, 1814, 1819, and 1874 adopting Garrick's alterations, each a fresh editorial choice made with Shakespeare's text available for comparison, and prefaces defended the retention on grounds of taste and even of fidelity to the Italian sources. An editor risks nothing on a given night by printing the scene readers expect, and the stored public version of the ending had been formed by a century of performance. The conversation's print life therefore measures the depth of the expectation the stage restorers were fighting: the waking was not a stage habit merely, but the culture's memory of how the tragedy ended.

### Q: What makes the edition of 1882 a turning point if the stage had already restored the ending?

The 1882 edition matters because it closed the restoration in the medium that had held out longest, and because of what it restored together. In a single editorial act it returned Rosaline to the play's first movement and let Romeo die at once after the poison, without the deathbed conversation, at the play's last. The pairing shows the criterion changing for the whole text rather than at one famous crux: effect yielded to evidence as the editor's standard. The stage restorations of 1845 and 1847 had recovered an ending under the pressure of performance; the 1882 edition registered the recovery as the reading public's settled text, and from that point the acting edition and the scholarly edition could begin their long convergence.

### Q: How did scenic spectacle work against the restoration of the words?

Spectacle worked against the words through the arithmetic of the evening. Built scenery required intervals for shifting, processions and crowd scenes consumed playing time, and lighting and musical effects lengthened every act, so the minutes for a fuller text had to be found by cutting that text. Charles Kean's Princess's Theatre productions of the 1850s state the paradox in its purest form: archaeologically researched costume and architecture, offered as historical fidelity, mounted over a shortened acting text. The fidelity was genuine but it attached to things rather than language, and the promptbooks arbitrate the result without sentiment. Where the scenery grew, the verse shrank, and the restoration of the words had to wait for a scenic economy willing to let language carry the evening.

### Q: What did William Poel restore that the actor-managers had not attempted?

Poel restored conditions rather than passages. Through the Elizabethan Stage Society from 1894, he staged the plays on a platform without pictorial scenery, in continuous action, at a speed the pictorial stage had abandoned, arguing from the evidence of the early theatres that the text had been written for exactly such delivery. The textual dividend followed from the scenic premise: a production spending nothing on lumber could afford to spend its evening on lines, and Poel's performances carried substantially more of the transmitted text than the great houses offered. His reform played to small audiences and changed no receipts directly. Its importance is definitional, since it shifted restoration's meaning from recovering dropped speeches to recovering the medium the speeches were built for.

### Q: How did Harley Granville-Barker turn restoration into a theory of production?

Granville-Barker's Savoy productions of 1912 applied the reformers' premises, simplified staging, speed, and respect for the text, inside a fashionable London house, and his Prefaces then argued the doctrine behind the practice. The argument's core is that the play's construction is its meaning: proportions, parallels, and the pacing of knowledge are engineered into the text, so that cutting for local effect destroys the larger effects the design exists to produce. Fidelity, on this account, is not antiquarian obedience but practical intelligence, the discovery that the author's engineering outperforms the manager's improvisation. The Prefaces gave the restoration its theory a century after its first stations, and the theory's elegance is that it returns theatrical effect, the adaptation's home ground, to the fidelity side of the quarrel.

### Q: What is the difference between restoring a repertory and restoring a text?

A repertory is restored when a play returns to the stage's active stock and holds its place there; a text is restored when the words performed are the words the early editions transmit. Garrick achieved the first in 1748 and obstructed the second for ninety-seven years, since the version through which his century knew the play erased Rosaline, laundered the bawdy, interpolated a procession, and rebuilt the tomb. The distinction disciplines the whole subject, because many celebrated restorations restored one and not the other: a manager could rescue a neglected play by adapting it, and a scholarly edition could restore a text no stage would yet perform. The movement traced in this article is the long pursuit of the second achievement by a profession that had congratulated itself on the first.

### Q: Why did American stages keep the added tomb conversation after British stages dropped it?

The conversation survived in America because the American repertory had been formed inside Garrick's settlement from its beginnings, the tragedy having crossed the Atlantic in the adapted version with the first English companies of the 1750s, and provincial and touring conditions conserved successful effects long after metropolitan taste revised them. The Yale editors' survey records the deathbed conversation played in an American production as late as 1899, half a century after Cushman's American restoration of the original ending. The lag is not evidence of American backwardness but of how repertories actually change: a standard effect, embedded in stock productions and audience expectation across a continent of theatres, retires town by town rather than by proclamation, and the last performances of the old ending belong to the geography of touring.

### Q: How did the great scholarly editions change what happened on stage?

The Cambridge edition of 1863 to 1866 and Furness's New Variorum from 1871 changed the stage indirectly, by changing the jurisdiction under which stage decisions were defended. A variorum page displays the transmitted text as a documented artefact, its early readings collated and its commentary sedimented, and against that display an adapter's silent substitution becomes visible as a substitution rather than as the play. No manager restored a line because a collation ordered it; managers worked in a climate where print controversy could audit their promptbooks, and the restorers were skilled controversialists. Scholarship's gift to the stage was therefore custodial rather than technical: it established that the text had guardians outside the profession, and the profession's freedom narrowed accordingly.

### Q: Why did fidelity become a selling point rather than a scholarly scruple?

Fidelity became saleable when a performer's or a manager's distinction could be built on it, and Cushman's 1845 Romeo is the founding case: the restoration of Shakespeare's ending was advertised as the attraction, and the house ratified the advertisement. Phelps converted the same claim into a house style at Sadler's Wells, and the prestige productions of the twentieth century inherited the conversion, marketing textual respect and verse speaking as marks of seriousness. The mechanism is the market's usual one. A scruple becomes a selling point when a public exists that can recognise it and prefers it, and the nineteenth century's editions and reviews had educated exactly that public. The restorers did not escape theatrical economics; they found the niche in which economics rewarded the document.

### Q: How do surviving promptbooks correct the claims made on posters and in prefaces?

Posters and prefaces record intentions and advertisements; promptbooks record the evening. A promptbook's markings show where lines were cut, transposed, or reassigned, where effects were timed, and how the performed text differed from the text the management praised in print, and the comparison repeatedly qualifies the restoration's public story. Professed restorers preserved cuts their prefaces apologised for, and Irving's Lyceum promptbook tradition preserves a shortened evening beneath a rhetoric of reverence. The promptbook is therefore the movement's honest archive, and the modern scholarly edition, Levenson's Oxford above all, treats it as such, building performance history from the marked copies rather than from the memoirs. Reading the movement through its promptbooks converts its history from a parade of claims into a measurable gradient.

### Q: What remained of Garrick inside productions that considered themselves post-Garrick?

Habits outlived the text that taught them. The Yale survey notes Henry Irving following eighteenth-century precedent in cutting most or all of the action after Juliet's death, keeping the sentimental shape of the evening inside a professedly Shakespearean frame; star-centred reshaping, the privileging of the lovers' pathos over the play's public frame, and the expectation that a manager's taste would silently adjust the document all persisted likewise. Garrick's deepest legacy was not any single alteration but a theory of the manager's rights, and theories retire more slowly than scenes. Measuring the restoration by the fall of the famous additions alone therefore overstates it; the movement's truer measure is the slow withdrawal of the licence those habits embodied, a withdrawal completed only when alteration had to argue its case in public.

### Q: Why was the tomb scene the test case for the whole restoration?

The tomb concentrated every term of the quarrel in one scene. It was the adaptation's most beloved invention and its most exposed, a sixty-seven line conversation built on a slowed poison and an advanced waking, defended as feeling and attacked as interpolation; it was also the place where the original's contrary design, irony sustained by an audience's solitary knowledge, stood in the plainest opposition. A management's tomb therefore declared its jurisdiction more legibly than any other passage: play the duet and the manager governed, play the swift deaths and the document governed. Cushman and Phelps chose the tomb for their manifestos because the choice was unmistakable, and the print editions' long defence of the conversation shows the same legibility working for the other side.

### Q: What did the restorers surrender when they gave up the tomb conversation?

They surrendered the most reliable effect in the repertory: a mutual farewell, recognition exchanged before death, the fathers arraigned by the dying, and ten minutes in which an audience's feeling was manufactured by the most direct means the theatre possessed. The surrender was real, and accounts that treat it as costless misunderstand why the conversation survived a century. What the restorers received in exchange was a different order of power, the ironic close in which the audience alone holds the truth, Juliet's executive swiftness with the dagger, and the Friar's lateness as the design's bleak signature. The exchange defines the movement's wager: that the author's engineering, trusted whole, would out-affect the adapter's addition, a wager Granville-Barker's doctrine later stated as theory and the twentieth-century stage ratified in practice.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch restoration timeline to argue that authority changed before the text did?

An essay should read the timeline's two right-hand columns against each other, station by station. At 1845 the authority changed while most of the text still stood cut; at Sadler's Wells the document governed while the manager still trimmed; in print the criterion changed in 1882 for passages the stage had settled decades before, and at the Lyceum in the same year professed authority and performed text pointed in opposite directions. Sorting the stations by which column moved first yields the thesis the table supports: the restoration was a jurisdictional event before it was a textual one, the transfer of the play's custody from the profession's taste to the document's evidence, with the wording following the custody at different speeds in different media.

### Q: Did the restoration change how audiences understood the lovers themselves?

It changed the lovers by changing the design that frames them. Garrick's settlement concentrated the tragedy on pure and persecuted love: Rosaline erased, the bawdy laundered, the tomb rebuilt for mutual farewell, the parents arraigned by the dying pair. The restored text returns the complications the settlement removed: a hero who loves twice in a week, a heroine whose swiftness is executive rather than swooning, deaths unshared and unreconciled, and a public frame in which the feud's cost is counted by survivors rather than absolved by a duet. Criticism written inside the adapted century inherited the adaptation's lovers, and much nineteenth-century sentiment about the play is unintelligible until that inheritance is recognised. Restoring the text therefore restored a sterner pair of protagonists, and the modern reading of the tragedy begins from their recovery.

### Q: What should a first-time reader take from the restoration's history before seeing the play performed?

The history teaches a reader to ask, of any performance, whose text is being played and on what authority the departures rest. A production is an argument about custody: the promptbook's cuts, the ending's disposition, the presence or absence of Rosaline and the procession's descendants all declare whether the document or the manager governs, and the declaration is legible once the stations are known. The reader who knows that the waking duet reigned for ninety-seven years, that print surrendered it only in 1882, and that the ironic close had to be fought for by performers, watches the tomb with the movement's question in mind. That question, what the stage owes the page, is the restoration's permanent bequest to its audiences.
