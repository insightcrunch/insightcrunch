Verona was already a literary place, dense with three centuries of accumulated meaning, long before the lovers' names were attached to it, and that single fact overturns the most common assumption readers bring to the tragedy. The familiar account credits Shakespeare with a stroke of scenic genius: an exotic Italian city chosen for its heat, its balconies, its Catholic colouring, its distance from London. The documentary record runs the other way. By the time the story reached a London playhouse, Verona had been built by Dante as the refuge of the exiled poet and the court of the della Scala, furnished by chroniclers and humanists as a seat of Roman antiquity and signorial splendour, and fixed as the address of the two feuding houses by Luigi da Porto in the early sixteenth century. Shakespeare never saw the city, never needed to see it, and never chose it. He received it, the way he received the plot, the potion, the friar and the tomb, down a chain of Italian, French and English retellings in which the place-name was one of the most stable elements transmitted. The verdict of the source record is plain: the setting is an inheritance, not an invention, and the play's power over place comes from what Shakespeare did inside walls that other writers had already raised.

That verdict matters because it changes what a reader should praise and what a reader should question. If Verona were a free choice, its fitness for the tragedy would testify to the playwright's eye. Since the city arrived with the story, the critical questions become different and more interesting ones. What did the name Verona already say to the readers and hearers who met the tale at each stage of its journey? What had Dante, the chronicle tradition and the novella writers deposited in the name before Da Porto pinned the lovers to it? Why did every reteller in the chain, working in three languages across seventy years, keep the city fixed while changing almost everything else, the heroine's age, the length of the action, the manner of the deaths, the moral of the ending? And what did an English audience of the 1590s hear in the name, when Italy itself carried a charged double reputation in English print? Those are the questions this study answers, staying deliberately on the near side of the play: the endpoint is the city Shakespeare inherited, the place as the sources and the wider literature built it, held apart throughout from both the staged city that later articles examine and the real streets on the Adige.

## Why Is Verona the Setting of Romeo and Juliet?

The short answer is that no single author decided the matter in the way the question implies. Masuccio Salernitano, whose tale of Mariotto and Giannozza in Il Novellino (1476) supplied the plot skeleton of secret marriage, exile, sleeping draught and tomb, set his story in Siena, not Verona. The tale entered Verona only when Luigi da Porto, a soldier and man of letters from Vicenza, rewrote it in the years before his death in 1529, naming the lovers Romeo and Giulietta, christening their houses Montecchi and Cappelletti, and placing the whole action in the city on the Adige under the signoria of Bartolomeo della Scala. From that moment the city never moved. Matteo Bandello kept it in his Novelle (1554), Pierre Boaistuau kept it in his Histoires tragiques (1559), Arthur Brooke kept it in The Tragicall Historye of Romeus and Juliet (1562), William Painter kept it in The Palace of Pleasure (1566), and the London stage received it with the name already fused to the story. A full account of [where and when the play itself is set in Verona](/2022/03/04/romeo-and-juliet-setting-verona-explained/) belongs to the play's own geography; the concern here is the prior question of how the name earned its place in the tale at all.

### Did Shakespeare choose Verona for Romeo and Juliet?

No. The city reached him as part of the story's fixed furniture, transmitted unchanged through every version he could have read, and no document of the period shows him weighing Verona against any alternative place. His immediate source, Brooke's poem, opens its action in the city, and the play's Chorus simply repeats the delivery in its first breath:

"Two households, both alike in dignity, / In fair Verona, where we lay our scene" (Prologue, lines 1 to 2). The phrase "where we lay our scene" is a stage direction wearing verse dress. It announces receipt, not selection, and the whole tradition behind the line treats the place as given, the way the names of the houses are given, a parcel handed down intact from Da Porto's novella through Bandello, Boaistuau and Brooke to the playhouse.

### Why did every reteller keep Verona and change everything else?

Because the city did work that no other element could do. A named place with a known ruling house, a datable signoria and a reputation for faction gave the tale the weight of record rather than fable, and each reteller inherited that credential along with the plot.

Bandello could enlarge the Nurse and thicken the tomb scene, Boaistuau could darken the moral weather, Brooke could stretch the action across months and scold the lovers in his preface, and none of them needed to re-verify the address, because the address carried its own authority. Places in a transmitted story behave like the names of persons: they are the load-bearing facts a retelling cannot alter without breaking the claim that this is the same story. Verona survived every translation for the same reason Romeo did. It identified the tale.

## The Real Verona the Name Carried: River, Arena and Signoria

Before Verona was a literary place it was a real one, and the force of the literary construction depends on keeping the two distinct while tracing what the real city contributed to its own legend. The Roman city stood at a bend of the Adige where the river loops around the old centre, on the road system that tied Italy to the Brenner pass and the north. Its amphitheatre, the Arena, raised in the first century of the Roman Empire, remained the largest single fact of the streetscape for every later century, a stone argument that the place had been considerable since antiquity. Medieval Verona grew inside and around that Roman skeleton, governed through the thirteenth century's turbulence first by the regime of Ezzelino III da Romano, whose hold on the city from 1236 to 1259 became a byword for tyranny, and then by the della Scala, the Scaligeri, whose signoria ran from Mastino I's rise in 1262 to the fall of Antonio della Scala in 1387. For a hundred and twenty-five years the city was a court: fortified, expansionist, ceremonially splendid, and famous across Italy as a refuge and employer of exiles, soldiers, poets and scholars pushed out of other cities by faction.

That courtly fact is the ground note of everything later writers built. The Scala lords made Verona a place where displaced men of talent collected, and the city's literary identity begins there, in patronage rather than in romance. Dante Alighieri, exiled from Florence in 1302, found his first shelter at the Scala court; Petrarch passed through and worked in its cathedral library; chroniclers recorded the dynasty's wars and pageantry as events of Italian significance. When Da Porto, two centuries later, needed a city in which two great houses could plausibly defy their ruler and fill the streets with armed retainers, he did not have to invent one. The Scala past gave him a datable frame in which a prince ruled from above the factions, exactly the political geometry the story requires, and the name Verona brought the whole apparatus with it in a single word. The real city's contribution to the legend was not a pair of lovers, for whom no document exists, but a structure: a signore above, great households below, and a court that had once gathered the most famous exile in Italian literature.

### What did the della Scala make of Verona before the novella writers used it?

They made it a capital with a reputation that outlasted their rule. Under Cangrande I, who governed from 1311 to 1329 and extended the city's power across the Veneto, Verona stood as one of the great courts of Italy, praised for open-handed hospitality to the displaced and feared for the reach of its armies.

Dante's exile gave the court its enduring literary advertisement, but the dynasty also left walls, tombs and churches that kept its name visible in the streetscape long after 1387. A reader of 1530 meeting a tale set "in Verona in the time of Bartolomeo della Scala" met a place with a known character: brilliant, faction-ridden, ruled by a lord whose authority both households had reason to test. The novella writers inherited that character ready-made.

## Dante's Verona: The Scala Court as the Exile's Refuge

The first great literary construction of Verona is Dante's, and it is a construction of the city as shelter. Driven from Florence, Dante spent his first years of exile as a guest of Bartolomeo della Scala in 1303 and 1304, and returned for a longer residence under Cangrande I in the years before his final move to Ravenna. In January 1320 he stood in the church of Sant'Elena in Verona and delivered a Latin disputation, the Quaestio de aqua et terra, before the city's clergy, a public performance by Europe's most famous exile in the city that had taken him in. The Commedia repays the debt openly. In Paradiso 17, Cacciaguida prophesies the exile and names its consolation: "Lo primo tuo refugio e 'l primo ostello / sarà la cortesia del gran Lombardo / che 'n su la scala porta il santo uccello" (Paradiso 17, lines 70 to 72), in the standard rendering, the first refuge and inn of the exile would be the courtesy of the great Lombard who bears the holy bird upon the ladder, the ladder being the Scala arms and the Lombard Bartolomeo himself. A few lines later the canto turns to Bartolomeo's young kinsman Cangrande, in whom Dante lodges hopes for Italy's renovation that no living ruler fulfilled. Verona enters the greatest poem of the age as the place where a wronged man is received with courtesy by a great lord: the city of the refuge, defined by the generosity of its signore.

The same poem gives Verona a second, darker register. In Purgatorio 18 Dante meets the abbot of San Zeno, the city's great abbey, who speaks of Verona under the empire of the good Barbarossa and complains of the present lord's line, a small scene that assumes the reader knows the abbey, the city and its ruling house as landmarks of Italian public life. And in Purgatorio 6, the canto of Italy's shame, Dante cries out the names that would matter most to the later story: "Vieni a veder Montecchi e Cappelletti, / Monaldi e Filippeschi, uom sanza cura: / color già tristi, e questi con sospetti" (Purgatorio 6, lines 106 to 108). The Montecchi were a Veronese house, rooted at the castle of Montecchio; the Cappelletti were known to Dante's first commentators as a house of Cremona. In the poem the names are not lovers' families and not a Veronese feud. They are exhibits in an indictment of an Italy where great houses destroy themselves while the emperor neglects his garden, two names yoked by alliteration and ruin rather than by any quarrel between them. How Dante's naming of the two houses reached the tale, and what the earliest commentators made of it, is treated in full in the study of [Dante's Montecchi and Cappelletti](/2025/11/28/dante-montecchi-cappelletti-feud/); the present point is narrower. For two centuries before Da Porto, the most widely read poem in Italy had taught its audience that Verona was the Scala court where exiles found refuge, and had filed the names Montecchi and Cappelletti in the memory of every literate Italian as emblems of houses ruined by faction. Da Porto's materials were already arranged on the shelf. He had only to reach for them.

The Dantean deposit explains a feature of the tale that no other source can. Da Porto dated his story to the signoria of Bartolomeo della Scala, the very lord whose courtesy Paradiso 17 had made proverbial, and set his feuding houses beneath the rule of the one Veronese prince whom Italian literature had already consecrated. The choice was not antiquarian decoration. It plugged the new tale into the oldest and strongest current in the city's literary identity: Verona as the place where a great signore holds the ring over warring households, the political picture Dante had painted in canto after canto. A story of two houses destroying themselves under a prince too weak to stop them reads, against the Dantean background, as the Scala idyll inverted. The refuge city of the Commedia becomes, in the novella, the city where the lord's ring-holding fails and the houses consume the young. Whether Da Porto intended that inversion or simply reached for the most resonant frame available, the frame was Dante's gift, and it reached Shakespeare, diluted but intact, through the chain.

## Chronicles, Courts and Libraries: Verona in the Record Before the Novelle

Between Dante's poem and Da Porto's novella, Verona accumulated two further layers of literary identity, one from the chroniclers who wrote the city into Italian history and one from the humanists who wrote it into the map of classical learning. Giovanni Villani's Nuova Cronica, the great Florentine chronicle continued into the fourteenth century, recorded the Scala ascendancy, Cangrande's wars and the dynasty's fall as matters of peninsular consequence, so that any Italian reader of history met Verona as a power whose doings belonged in the common record. The Paduan humanist Albertino Mussato had earlier given the region its classical tragedy, the Ecerinis, on Ezzelino's tyranny over Verona and its neighbours, written in Senecan Latin verse and read across learned Italy: Verona as the stage of a tyrant's rise and fall, in the idiom of Roman drama, two centuries before an English stage took up the city. Local historiography added civic memory of its own, and by the fifteenth century Flavio Biondo's Italia illustrata, the foundational humanist geography of Italy composed in the middle of that century, enrolled Verona among the peninsula's notable cities with its Roman remains set out for readers who would never travel. The city was, in the strict sense, illustrated: described, measured against antiquity, and filed in the reference works of educated Europe.

Petrarch's Verona is the humanist layer at its most concrete. In 1345, working in the cathedral chapter library, Petrarch found and copied the manuscript of Cicero's letters to Atticus, a recovery that helped found the modern study of Cicero's private voice and, with it, much of humanist letter-writing itself. The episode fixed Verona in the mental geography of learning as a place where antiquity survived in cupboards, waiting for the right reader. A city that had sheltered Dante's exile and preserved Cicero's letters carried a double credential no inventor of tales could buy: it was famous as a refuge of letters in both the vernacular and the classical sense. When the novella tradition turned toward Verona, it turned toward a city whose name already signified, to Italian readers, courts, factions, antiquity and books. The lovers did not make the name interesting. The name was interesting for two hundred years before the lovers were attached to it, and the attachment succeeded because of that prior interest.

Boccaccio completes the prehistory by showing the Scala court already functioning as a novella setting a century and a half before Da Porto. In the Decameron's seventh tale of the first day, the professional storyteller Bergamino, slighted by Cangrande at Verona, shames his host back into generosity with an improvised tale, and the episode's whole point depends on the audience knowing whose court this is: a byword for magnificence and for the rewards of wit. The Decameron assumes Verona the way Da Porto's novella would later assume it, as a known quantity, a court where a great lord's favour is the prize and his temper the weather. Between Boccaccio's frame and Da Porto's, the city's novella identity was stable: the Scala court as the natural stage for stories about fortune, favour and the sudden turns of household politics. What no writer before Da Porto had done was hang a love tragedy on that stage. The stage itself, however, was built, lit and known to every reader in Italy, and that is the sense in which the setting of the world's most famous love story was ready before the story existed.

## Luigi Da Porto Sets the Tale in Verona

Everything in the city's long literary preparation converges on one act of placement. Luigi da Porto was a Vicentine gentleman-soldier, wounded in the Friuli campaigns of the Italian Wars, writing in forced retirement at his villa at Montorso. His Giulietta e Romeo, composed in the years around 1524 and printed at Venice after his death, most likely in 1530 or 1531, retells the Sienese plot that Masuccio had published in 1476 and performs three decisive operations on it at once. It moves the action from Siena to Verona. It renames the lovers and, crucially, gives their families the names Montecchi and Cappelletti, the Dantean pair. And it frames the whole as received fact: in the novella's own framing, Da Porto says he heard the story from Peregrino, a Veronese archer in his company, who told it as a thing that had happened in his city in the days of Bartolomeo della Scala. The dedication, to his kinswoman Lucina Savorgnan, presents the tale as a soldier's offering drawn from the talk of soldiers. Every element of the frame is an argument for the setting. The teller is a Veronese. The date is the Scala signoria. The place is named not as scenery but as testimony.

Why Verona, for a writer who was not Veronese and whose plot came from Siena? The source scholarship gives converging answers, and together they amount to a verdict on the mechanics of place in the novella tradition. Verona was near, known and visitable from Da Porto's Friuli world, a city he could claim the way a veteran claims the geography of his campaigns. Its Scala past supplied the political architecture: a signore above two armed households, with Mantua close by as the natural place of exile, the geography of banishment the plot requires. Its Dantean associations supplied the family names and the aura of factional ruin. And its fame as a court of refuge made the tale's catastrophes read as the dark side of a documented brilliance. Da Porto's own life added a private pressure that critics have long noted: a soldier invalided out of the wars, writing of young men destroyed by a feud and a young woman destroyed by an enforced marriage, in a city whose great age of household violence lay safely in the past. The setting is the one element of the tale that is entirely his contribution in its final form, and it proved the most durable thing he made. ### What did Da Porto actually add to the story when he moved it?

The move was the making of the tale. Siena gave the plot: secret marriage, exiled husband, sleeping draught, tomb, double death. Verona gave the plot its names, its prince, its politics and its claim on belief, and those four gifts proved more transmissible than any incident.

A feud needs a city that can hold it: streets where retainers brawl, a ruler whose edict both houses fear, a friar whose cell sits inside the walls and a vault where the great family buries its dead. Da Porto found every one of those fittings in the Verona that Dante, the chroniclers and the Scala legend had already built, and he fixed them to the story so firmly that no later reteller, in any language, thought to move the action again.

The pattern the lineage exposes is the asymmetry of transmission. Incidents mutate at almost every stage: the heroine's age drops, the timescale contracts from months to days, the manner of Giulietta's death changes, a parasite swordsman grows into Mercutio. The place never mutates at all. From Da Porto's page onward, Verona is the one constant, copied with the fidelity copyists reserve for names of persons, because in the economy of a transmitted story the city functions as a name: it identifies the tale the way a title does. That is why the question "why Verona" has no answer at the level of Shakespeare's intentions and a complete answer at the level of the record. Verona is the setting because a Vicentine soldier, reaching for the strongest available frame for a borrowed Sienese plot, set it in the city Dante had made synonymous with faction under a great lord, and because every hand after his found the fusion too authoritative to undo. The lineage also disciplines the opposite error. Verona was not an arbitrary label pasted on by Da Porto in a vacuum. He chose it because it was already a literary place, built by two centuries of poetry, chronicle and humanist geography, and the proof of its prior construction is that his choice stuck instantly and permanently. Invented settings in the novella tradition drift between tellings. This one never drifted, because it was never merely invented.

## The Chain Holds the City: Bandello, Boaistuau, Brooke, Painter

The transmitters after Da Porto each rewrote the tale in their own image, and the record of what they changed throws the fixity of the place into relief. Bandello, the Dominican turned courtier whose Novelle appeared at Lucca in 1554, took Da Porto's plot nearly entire and expanded it with a dramatist's instincts: the Nurse grows, the apothecary scene in Mantua gains its haggling, the tomb sequence lengthens. His frame insists on Verona with a chronicler's emphasis, presenting the story as an event of the city's Scala past that had come to him through named telling, and the insistence is itself a literary act. Bandello understood that the tale's credit rose with the specificity of its address, and he spent that credit freely while adding nothing to the geography. Boaistuau, carrying the tale into French in the Histoires tragiques of 1559, moralised it for a northern audience, hardening the providential frame and sharpening the deaths into warnings, yet the city crosses the Alps without losing a syllable of its identity. A French reader who had never heard the Adige's name learned it as the place where this happened, which is how place-names travel: not by description but by attachment to a story that demands retelling.

The English stage of the journey completes the naturalisation. Arthur Brooke's Romeus and Juliet of 1562, a long poem in poulter's measure worked from Boaistuau's French, opens its argument by planting the action in Verona and spends three thousand lines inside the city's walls, its friar, its feast, its tomb. Brooke's preface scolds the lovers as disobedient and the friar as a superstitious meddler, a thoroughly Protestant English frame, yet the Catholic Italian city beneath the scolding is reproduced without embarrassment, down to the Franciscan machinery on which the plot depends. William Painter's prose version in the second volume of The Palace of Pleasure (1566) did the same work for readers of story collections. By the time a London company took up the tale in the mid 1590s, English audiences had been reading Verona for thirty years. The name needed no footnote, no map, no apology. It arrived as what a generation of print had made it: the Italian city where the two houses fought. A diagram of [the sources of the story set out as a family tree](/2025/09/12/sources-of-romeo-and-juliet-family-tree/) shows the same chain from the plot's side; from the place's side, the chain is even simpler, because the place enters once, at Da Porto's desk, and is never renegotiated.

One consequence of that fixity deserves emphasis, because it governs how the play should be credited. Shakespeare's Verona is thin in local fact and thick in received function, and the thinness is the tell. The play names streets and places sparingly, leans on generic stage-Italian furniture, and makes its single boldest geographical stroke, the exile to Mantua, directly from the sources, where Mantua had served as the place of banishment since Da Porto. A playwright inventing a city researches or fabricates its texture; a playwright inheriting one uses it as a given and spends his invention elsewhere, on tempo, on Mercutio, on the compression of months into days. The play's Verona is exactly the second kind of city. Even its most famous line of place-praise is formulaic in the precise sense of being a received formula: "fair Verona" is the epithet of a city already famous, the adjective a well-known place wears when a Chorus introduces it to hearers who are expected to nod.

## The England That Watched: Italy as Passion, Feud and Ritual

What did the name Verona mean in the London of the 1590s, to hearers who would never see Italy? English print had spent half a century constructing an Italy for them, and the construction was double: a land of high civility and high corruption, of the best manners and the worst morals, of courts that taught Europe courtesy and cities that taught it poisoning. William Thomas, clerk of the council to Edward VI, published The History of Italy in 1549, the first substantial English account of the peninsula's states, and gave English readers the Italian city as a political form: proud, factional, governed by signori and republics whose intrigues filled his pages. Roger Ascham's Scholemaster of 1570 fixed the moral pole of the image, warning that the travelled Englishman came home Italianate, and quoting the proverb that an Englishman Italianated is a devil incarnate. John Florio's language manuals, the Firste Fruites of 1578 and the Second Frutes of 1591, worked the other pole, drilling merchants and gentlemen in the Italian of courts and crediting Italy with the refinements of speech, dress and fencing that young London coveted. Thomas Nashe's Unfortunate Traveller of 1594 sent its hero through an Italy of brilliant, lethal courts. The playhouse synthesised what the books polarised. On stage, Italy was where household honour was armed, where friars heard confessions and mixed potions, where a prince's peace could be broken by a thumb bitten in a public place.

Verona fitted that composite exactly, and the fit explains why the inherited setting needed no adjustment for the English market. The tale required a Catholic city, because its plot runs on confession, a secret church marriage, a friar's pharmacology and a consecrated tomb, machinery that post-Reformation London could watch only at an Italian distance. It required a city of armed households under a single prince, because its public scenes run on the threat that private quarrel will become civic war, Escalus warning in the play's first scene of "three civil brawls, bred of an airy word" (1.1) disturbing the quiet of the streets. And it required a climate of the imagination in which passion moved at Italian speed, the heat that English writers from Ascham onward blamed and envied in the same sentence. Shakespeare did not have to build any of that. The books had built Italy, Da Porto had built Verona inside Italy, and the audience brought the weather with it. The setting's contribution to the tragedy is therefore best understood as pre-suasion: before a word of the feud is spoken, the place-name has already told the audience that households here are states, that love here is urgent, and that the Church here is near enough to marry, absolve and bury.

### Why could the plot only work in a Catholic Italian city?

Because every mechanism of the action is Italian and Catholic in the sources before it is anything in the play. A friar who can marry a couple in his cell, hear a daughter's confession, compound a sleeping draught from his garden and lay her in a consecrated family vault is not portable to Protestant London without breaking the plot's joints.

The novella world takes him for granted because its Verona takes the old religion for granted. The play inherits the machinery with the city and never has to explain it, which is the quiet proof that the setting is load-bearing. Move the tale to a city English audiences imagined as Protestant, reformed or merely English, and half the instruments on Friar Laurence's table vanish.

## Literary Verona and the Real City: The Gap the Tale Inherited

The brief for this study demands the complication be faced squarely: the Verona of the tale is not the Verona of record, and the distance between them is part of what Shakespeare inherited. No document places a Romeo Montecchi or a Giulietta Cappelletti in the city, no chronicle records their deaths, and no evidence shows the two houses ever fought each other anywhere, the Montecchi being Veronese and the Cappelletti, in Dante's early commentators, Cremonese. The feud as a Veronese event is a literary product that hardened into civic folklore. The hardening began early and happened, strikingly, inside Verona's own literature. Leandro Alberti's Descrittione di tutta Italia of 1550, a work of geography rather than fiction, retails the lovers as part of the city's past within twenty years of the novella's printing. Girolamo dalla Corte's Istoria di Verona of 1592 went further, writing the pair into the history of the city as persons of its Scala age, a few years before the play reached the London stage. A Veronese historian and an Italian geographer, neither of them romancers, had already converted Da Porto's placement into record before Shakespeare wrote a line. The playwright thus inherited not merely a setting but a setting with a counterfeit pedigree, a fiction that Italian print was already certifying as fact.

Keeping the two Veronas distinct clarifies rather than diminishes the tale's use of place. The real city gave the story a structure: the signore, the households, the Adige geography that puts Mantua a day's ride away as the natural address of exile, the antiquity that let the tale pose as old. The literary city gave the story its persons and its passion. What no honest account may do is spend the real city's credit on the literary city's inhabitants. The Scala were real, and their court sheltered Dante; the Arena is real, and stood through every century of the tale; the feud and the lovers belong to the page. Scholarship that blurred that line fed the later industry of balconies and tombs that the play's own setting studies must then unbuild. The distinction matters to this study's verdict as well. Shakespeare inherited Verona twice over: as a place constructed by literature out of a real city's materials, and as a place already misremembered, by the time it reached him, as the documented home of a documented pair. Both inheritances are visible in the play's confidence. The Chorus does not argue for Verona. It cites Verona, the way a chronicle cites a place where things are known to have happened, and the citation works because a century of print, Italian, French and English, had taught audiences to accept the citation without checking it.

## The Prince Above the Houses: From Bartolomeo della Scala to Escalus

The most consequential piece of the inherited setting is not a street or a balcony but a political figure: the ruler who stands above both houses and whose failure to govern them is the tragedy's civic plot. Da Porto's frame installs that figure when it dates the tale to the signoria of Bartolomeo della Scala, and the choice deserves to be read as precisely as any line of the play. Bartolomeo ruled Verona from 1301 to 1304, the first years of Dante's exile and the opening of the city's great century. In the literary memory Da Porto inherited, Bartolomeo was not a tyrant of the Ezzelino stamp. He was the gran Lombardo of Paradiso 17, the lord whose courtesy to a wronged exile Dante had made the measure of signorial virtue. To set a feud under Bartolomeo was therefore to set it under the best ruler the literary tradition could supply, and to let the story's disorder measure itself against a remembered standard of order. The novella does not labour the point; it does not need to. A reader formed by the Commedia brought the standard along with the name, and the tale's catastrophe gained its civic meaning from the gap between the lord Verona remembered and the peace the lord in the tale cannot keep.

Every transmitter preserved the figure while anglicising or gallicising his dress, until in the play he stands as Escalus, Prince of Verona, entering the first scene over drawn swords with the edict that defines the city's constitution: the peace is the prince's, the brawls are breaches of it, and the penalty has escalated to death because lesser penalties have failed. That constitution is the Scala signoria translated into stage terms. A city of households requires an arbiter who is not a householder; a prince who is himself kin to the fallen, as Escalus is to Mercutio and Paris, discovers at the end that the arbiter's neutrality was a fiction, and his closing audit, "Where be these enemies? Capulet, Montague, / See what a scourge is laid upon your hate" (5.3), is delivered by a ruler counting himself among the punished. The line of descent from Bartolomeo's literary reputation to Escalus's literary function is the clearest case in the whole study of an inherited place doing inherited work. The prince is a feature of the setting before he is a character, the signore-shaped hole in the city's government that Da Porto found ready-cut in Verona's past. Shakespeare's contribution was to make the hole speak, not to cut it.

### Where does the ruling prince of the tale come from?

He comes from the signoria that made Verona famous. The historical della Scala ruled the city as lords above its factions for a hundred and twenty-five years, and the literary tradition, Dante at its head, had already converted that rule into an emblem:

the great Lombard whose courtesy sheltered the exile, the ladder borne on the arms, the court where order flowed from one man's household. Da Porto reached for that emblem when he dated his tale to Bartolomeo's years, and each retelling kept the dating, so the play's prince descends from a remembered Veronese lord rather than from any observation of Italian government made in London.

## The City in Its Fittings: Walls, Cell, Vault and Road

A transmitted setting carries furniture as well as a ruler, and the furniture of the lovers' city was likewise fixed before the play. The sources supply a city of walls and gates through which the exiled husband must pass and repass at mortal risk; a friar's cell at the city's religious heart, where marriages can be made in secret because the Church's geography sits inside the civic one; a great family's vault where the dead of one house lie together and a living girl can be laid among them; and a road to a second city, Mantua, near enough for a servant to ride with news and far enough for a letter to miscarry. Each fitting answers to the real Verona's plan as the tradition understood it: the walled circuit on the Adige, the Franciscan and Dominican houses within the walls, the monumental tombs the Scala age raised for its great families, and the Mantuan road that made the Gonzaga city Verona's natural counterpart and refuge. Da Porto did not survey these fittings. He received them as the common property of the Scala legend and the novella's own needs, and his successors copied them with the plot.

The distinction between fittings received and fittings observed runs through the play's texture and settles arguments that local-colour criticism sometimes misfiles. When Romeo cries that "there is no world without Verona walls, / But purgatory, torture, hell itself" (3.3), the walls he means are the inherited ones, the circuit that defines banishment as a death of the social self in every version since Da Porto sent his Romeo to Mantua. The line's power is real, and it is the power of a convention perfectly inhabited, not of a place inspected. Likewise the vault: the Capelletti vault of the novella, in Da Porto placed at the church of San Francesco, becomes the Capulet monument of the play without a change of function, a piece of civic-religious architecture whose horror depends on the audience accepting, as the sources taught them to accept, that a great Veronese house buries its line in one consecrated room. To read these fittings as Shakespeare's Verona is to credit the last tenant with the building. The building is older, and its age is its authority: three generations of readers had walked its rooms before the playhouse audience entered them, which is why the playhouse audience needed no tour.

## Critics on the Setting: A Disagreement Adjudicated

Source scholarship, setting criticism and modern editing agree on the chain; they disagree, genuinely and instructively, on what kind of act Da Porto's placement was, and the disagreement is worth adjudicating because the verdict reached here depends on it. Geoffrey Bullough, whose Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare (1957) remains the standard documentary map of the play's descent, treated the Verona setting as part of the tale's transmitted substance: Da Porto took his story's frame from the Sienese plot, its family names from the world Dante had made famous, and its city as the natural home of those names, after which the setting passed through Bandello and the French and English versions without alteration. On Bullough's showing, the place needs no special explanation beyond the chain itself, because in a chain the stable elements explain themselves by the copying that preserves them. The editor's tradition concurs. Jill L. Levenson's Oxford edition of the play (2000) presents a dramatist working at speed from Brooke's poem, receiving its Verona as he received its plot, and Levenson's introduction directs readers who want the setting explained to the sources rather than to any Italian journey or scenic research by the playwright. For the documentary line, inheritance is not a thesis. It is the default the evidence leaves standing.

Olin H. Moore, in The Legend of Romeo and Juliet (1950), pressed harder on the prior question and reached a different emphasis. Moore examined the claims that the tale preserved a genuine Veronese tradition, a real feud and real lovers behind the novella, and dismantled them: no chronicle, no record, no pre-Da Porto telling joins the lovers to the city, and the historicity asserted by later Veronese writers rests on the novella itself read backward as evidence. For Moore, Da Porto's placement was therefore an act of construction, the decisive creative stroke of the whole legend, in which a Vicentine author assembled a Sienese plot, Dantean names and a Scala frame into a composite that only looks like recovered history. Against both, Louise George Clubb's work on the Italian materials behind the English stage, above all Italian Drama in Shakespeare's Time (1989), relocates the question. Clubb argues that the Italy of the London playhouses was a theatrical system, a stock of conventions, places and personae, theatregrams in her coinage, through which English dramatists thought; on that account the Verona an English audience received was never a documentary place at all, but a module in a stage language, and its meanings were the meanings the module carried in performance: foreign licence, Catholic machinery, household statecraft.

The disagreement, stated cleanly, is over where the setting's authority comes from: from the documents that transmitted it (Bullough), from the invention that fabricated it (Moore), or from the stage conventions that consumed it (Clubb). Adjudicated on the record assembled in this study, the verdict is that each account captures one stratum of a three-layer fact, and that the strata are ordered in time. Moore is right about the origin: the fusion was made, not found, and the real-lovers tradition is a back-formation with no documentary root, as the absence of any pre-1530 attestation and the Cremonese Cappelletti jointly prove. Bullough is right about the mechanism: once made, the fusion was transmitted with name-like fidelity, which is why no alternative setting ever competed with it in any language. Clubb is right about the reception: what London consumed in the 1590s was the module, the Italy of the stage, and the documentary chain mattered to that consumption only as the guarantee baked into the module's confidence. Where the accounts conflict, the documentary test governs. Against any residual claim that Da Porto recorded rather than composed his Verona, the record is silent where it should speak, and silence in chronicle-rich Verona is evidence. Against any claim that the setting was Shakespeare's free scenic choice, the chain is unanimous where it should vary. The adjudicated verdict stands with this study's thesis: Verona is an invented fusion that behaved, from the moment of its invention, exactly like a fact, and the play received it in the second of those characters while owing its existence to the first.

### What does the lineage pattern prove about how stories travel?

The question belongs properly to the map set out in full above, where the artifact is tabled stage by stage, but its one-line answer can stand here: the lineage is the record of Verona passing from Dante's poem through chronicle, humanist geography and novella into Da Porto's fusion and down the French and English chain to the play.

The pattern it exposes is that place in a transmitted story behaves like a name, copied with a fidelity that incidents never enjoy, so that the setting of the tragedy was the most conservative element in its whole transmission.

## What Verona Contributes: The Verdict on an Inherited Place

What, then, does Verona contribute to Romeo and Juliet, if the playwright neither chose it nor saw it? The verdict of this study is that the inherited city contributes the tragedy's entire civic physics, the system of forces inside which the private passion becomes a public catastrophe, and that it contributes that system precisely because it was inherited complete. Consider what the setting supplies before any character speaks. It supplies scale: households great enough to fill streets with retainers and to make their quarrel a matter of state, a scale the Scala legend had advertised for two centuries. It supplies law: a prince above the houses whose peace defines crime and whose kinship to the victims corrupts his arbitration, a constitution received from the signorial frame Da Porto dated to Bartolomeo. It supplies machinery: a Catholic city in which a friar can marry, counsel, drug and bury, the plot's every instrument racked in one man's cell. It supplies tempo: an Italy of the English imagination where blood ran hot and honour was a hair-trigger, so that three days of action read as climate rather than contrivance. And it supplies the epitaph's authority: a place already certified by print as the scene of these events, so that the Chorus can open with a citation instead of an argument and Escalus can close the audit of the dead in a city the audience believes it knows.

Mercutio's dying cry is the inheritance speaking at the play's centre. "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1) curses not two families in the abstract but the Veronese institution the sources built, the household as armed state within the state, and the curse lands because the audience has been taught by the setting itself to understand houses as Verona understands them. Strip the inherited city away and the cry loses its grammar; set the tale in a place without the Scala past, the Dantean names and the signorial frame, and households shrink into families, a prince into a magistrate, a feud into a quarrel. Shakespeare's genius in the matter of place was recognitional rather than inventive. He saw what the received city could be made to carry, compressed its timescale until its pressures fused, and voiced its constitution in a prince and its pathology in a plague-cry. The credit for Verona belongs to the line of makers who built and transmitted it: Dante, who consecrated its court and filed its fatal names; the chroniclers and humanists, who documented its power and its antiquity; Da Porto, who performed the fusion; and the chain that refused, for seventy years and three languages, to let the fusion lapse. The cliche thinks Shakespeare chose Verona. The record shows Verona, long prepared, choosing its most famous story as much as the story chose it, and the play inheriting a city whose meanings were older, richer and more durable than any single playwright's invention could have made them in a season. That is the contribution of a received place: it arrives with its authority already earned, and it lets the tragedy spend every line on the lovers instead of on the ground beneath them.

## The Verona Da Porto Knew: A Venetian Fortress on the Adige

One more layer separates the literary city from the map, and it is the layer of Da Porto's own lifetime. When he wrote, the Scala had been gone for nearly a hundred and forty years. Verona had given itself to Venice in 1405 and stood, in the early sixteenth century, as the republic's great mainland fortress, a walled garrison city on the road to the Brenner, valued for its walls and its river crossing rather than for any court. The Italian Wars made the point brutally: after the League of Cambrai's victory at Agnadello in 1509, Verona passed under Imperial occupation and was recovered by Venice only in the settlements of 1516 and 1517, battered in the process. Da Porto fought in that world, in the Friuli campaigns of the same wars, and was carried home wounded from it. The living city of his adulthood was thus a frontier stronghold of a maritime republic, its signorial age a memory curated by tombs, churches and chronicles. His novella's Verona, by contrast, is set two centuries back, under Bartolomeo della Scala, in the city's age of independent lordship. The placement was an act of deliberate archaism. Da Porto reached past the Venice-ruled fortress of his own day to the signoria of the literary tradition, because only that older Verona supplied the constitution his plot required: a native lord above native houses, in a city that was itself a state.

The archaism matters for the play because it explains why the setting stands so oddly outside its own century on stage. Shakespeare's Verona has no Venetian overlord, no Spanish or Imperial garrison, no sixteenth-century politics at all; its prince answers to no one, and its great houses move in a civic world that had ceased to exist in Italy long before Da Porto wrote, let alone before the play. That purity is inherited twice over. Da Porto stripped the contemporary city away to recover the Dantean one, and the chain transmitted the recovered city as though it were the place itself. Readers who fault the play for an Italy that never existed are therefore aiming at the wrong defendant and the wrong century. The unreality is a source feature, designed in the 1520s by a soldier who preferred the Verona of the Scala legend to the Verona of the Venetian garrison, and preserved thereafter because a legendary city serves a legendary plot better than a garrison town can. Even the geographers conspired in the preference. Sebastian Münster's Cosmographia of 1544, the century's most widely read description of the world, presented Verona to northern readers with its Roman antiquities to the fore, and the city views of Braun and Hogenberg's Civitates orbis terrarum, appearing from 1572, showed the Adige loop and the Arena as the city's portrait, a place visualised across Europe as classical and medieval at once, with its present tense politely out of frame. By the time the name reached a London playhouse, Verona had been archaicised in print for decades. The play's floating, undated Italy is not carelessness. It is the accurate reproduction of the only Verona print had ever offered its audience.

### Why does Da Porto set the tale two centuries before his own time?

Because the plot needs a constitution that his own Verona no longer had. In the 1520s the city was a Venetian fortress, ruled from another city by a republic that permitted no native prince and no armed household politics of the old kind.

Under Bartolomeo della Scala, by contrast, the literary record supplied exactly the required machine: one lord above great houses, in a city-state whose peace was the lord's personal charge. The backward step in time is the enabling condition of the feud, the exile and the prince's edict alike, and every later version keeps the archaism because the machine stops working without it.

## Siena's Loss, Verona's Gain: The Plot Before the Place

The cleanest proof that Verona was a choice made once, by one author, with the whole literary tradition at his elbow, is the tale's prior address. Masuccio Salernitano, the pen name of Tommaso Guardati, published Il Novellino at Naples in 1476, and its thirty-third tale tells of Mariotto Mignanelli and Giannozza Saraceni of Siena: a secret marriage before a friar, a killing, exile to Alexandria, a feigned death by draught, a tomb, a return, a second death, and the wife's retreat to a convent rather than Giulietta's death beside the body. The skeleton is unmistakably the lovers' plot, and its city is Siena, a place with its own factional fame, its own great houses, its own civic pride. Nothing in the Sienese version failed. The plot functioned perfectly well on the Arbia. What the Sienese version lacked was transmissibility of the kind the Veronese version achieved, and the difference between the two is the whole argument of this study in miniature.

Siena gave the tale a plot and a pair of names nobody remembered. Verona gave it a pair of names Dante had filed in the memory of Italy, a ruling house whose courtesy to the national poet was proverbial, and a frame of received fact, the archer's testimony, the signore's dates, the air of chronicle. Retelling follows credit, and the Veronese version carried more credit at every point where a copyist or translator had to decide what the story was. Bandello did not consider re-Sienising the tale; Boaistuau and Brooke did not weigh the claims of the Arbia against the Adige. The contest was over before it began, because Da Porto had attached the plot to the strongest literary place available to it, a city whose name already performed, for any Italian reader, the work of a paragraph of exposition. Siena's loss also exposes the contingency inside the inheritance. Had Da Porto never written, the plot might have travelled as a Sienese curiosity or not travelled at all, and no English audience would have heard of Mariotto. The setting that feels inevitable, so fused with the lovers that the names and the city now gloss each other in every language, rests on one writer's placement decision in the 1520s. Inheritance is not the opposite of invention. It is invention that succeeded so completely that later ages mistook it for topography.

## The Name at Work Elsewhere: Verona in the Period's Wider Literature

A place-name's literary capital can be measured by how writers spend it when the lovers are not involved, and by that measure Verona's capital was high and rising through the sixteenth century. The Dante commentaries, printed from the 1480s onward as the Commedia became a fixture of the presses, glossed the Montecchi and Cappelletti of Purgatorio 6 for every new generation of readers, explaining the houses' ruin and fixing the couplet in the common stock of Italian literary knowledge through which Da Porto's borrowing would later be recognised. Humanist geography kept the city in the reference shelf beside the poem: Biondo's Italia illustrata in the fifteenth century, Alberti's Descrittione in 1550, Münster's Cosmographia in German and Latin editions across the century, and the engraved city books that followed, all presented Verona as a standard entry in the catalogue of notable places, the kind of city a serious book about Italy was expected to include. The name also did quieter work in the literature of courts and conduct, where the Scala survived as an exemplum of magnificence and of fortune's reversals, the dynasty's rise and extinction offering moralists a complete arc within a single family's memory. None of this writing concerns the lovers. All of it maintained the asset the lovers' tale would draw on: a city that educated readers across Europe already knew how to feel about.

England furnishes the neatest control experiment, because English writers had no stake in Veronese patriotism and used the name purely as received stock. When the young Shakespeare of The Two Gentlemen of Verona, an early comedy, sent his hero Valentine out of Verona toward Milan, he reached for the same name with the same confidence, and committed the same kind of geographical blitheness the tragedy shows, giving the inland city a tide and a ship's passage, because the name signified Italian youth, service and love-intrigue to his audience whether or not the map agreed. The comedy's Verona is not the tragedy's, and neither is observed; both are drawn from the common English stock of Italy that the books of Thomas, Ascham and Florio had built, in which Verona ranked as a known city for known purposes. The control experiment isolates the variable this study has tracked throughout. Given the name without the tale, the playwright still treats it as pre-interpreted ground, famous before he touches it, requiring no establishment. The tale's Verona and the comedy's Verona are two withdrawals from one account, and the account was opened by Dante, the chroniclers and the geographers long before any Englishman drew on it.

### Did any other English writer treat Verona as a ready-made setting?

The clearest case is the same playwright in a different genre. The Two Gentlemen of Verona builds its opening movement on the city as the natural home of a young gentleman setting out for a greater court, and it assumes the audience will accept Verona's Italian colouring.

Its dukes and its service culture are assumed with it, without a line of scene-setting beyond the name itself. Other English Italianate plays of the period distribute their action among Venice, Florence, Milan and Naples on the same principle, choosing from a short list of pre-interpreted cities. Verona's presence on that list, for comedy as for tragedy, is the footprint of its print fame rather than of any English traveller's report.

## Reading Backward: What the Inheritance Permits and Forbids

The verdict that the setting was inherited is not an antiquarian trophy; it changes the rules of interpretation for every reader who follows it back into the play. It forbids, first, the biographical romance in which the playwright walks the Adige in imagination so intense it counts as knowledge: no evidence supports an Italian journey, the sources explain every local feature the play contains, and Occam's razor, applied by Levenson's edition and Bullough's collection alike, leaves the journey with nothing to explain. It forbids, second, the crediting of the tragedy's civic design to a single act of world-building. The households-as-states, the prince above them, the friar inside them and the vault beneath them form a system because Da Porto's frame assembled a system, out of Dante's names, the Scala constitution and the novella's needs. What interpretation may credit to Shakespeare is the system's activation: the compression that makes the constitution fail in days, the voices that make the inherited prince audit himself and the inherited houses hear their pathology named by a dying wit. Inheritance explains the machine. It does not touch the hand that runs the machine at full speed.

The inheritance permits, correspondingly, a richer account of the play's effects than the invention story allows. A setting received as fact lets the Chorus speak with a chronicler's authority in the play's first fourteen lines, staking the whole action on a place the audience has been trained by thirty years of English print to accept as the feud's documented home. It lets the dramatist spend his exposition budget on character rather than geography, because the geography arrived pre-exposited. And it lets the ending land with the weight of record: Escalus's audit and the fathers' golden statues close a story that has posed, since Da Porto, as something that happened in a real and famous city, under a real and famous lordship, and the pose survives in the play as the feeling readers name when they say the tragedy seems true. The seeming was engineered in the 1520s, maintained by the chain, and perfected, not created, in the 1590s. To restore the sources is not to diminish the play. It is to see, at last, the exact joint where the tradition's work ends and the playwright's begins, and that joint runs through the name Verona itself.

## The Geography the Sources Drew: River, Road and Second City

The inherited Verona came with an inherited map, and the map's few firm lines did heavy work in every version. The Adige is the first of them. The river that loops around the old city made Verona a crossing and a tollgate between Italy and the north, the reason for the Roman foundation, the wealth of the medieval commune and the strategic value that kept armies interested in the walls. In the literary construction the river works more quietly, as the boundary of the known: inside the loop, the households, the prince and the friar; beyond it, the road and the world's indifference. The second line is the Mantuan road. Mantua, the Gonzaga city in its lakes some forty kilometres south, enters the tale at Da Porto's desk as the appointed place of exile, close enough for a servant's ride and a friar's letter to matter, far enough for both to fail. The pairing is geographical good sense dressed as fate. A banished Veronese gentleman of the Scala age would naturally be received, watched and supplied with news in Mantua, and the plot's whole machinery of messages, miscarriages and a poison bought in exile depends on that believable distance. Shakespeare's Balthasar rides a real road. The sources had surveyed it for him.

The third line of the inherited map is negative, and the more telling for it: the sources draw almost nothing else. No source lingers on Veronese topography, names its quarters, or walks its streets, and the play inherits the reticence exactly. What passed down the chain was not a cityscape but a civic diagram, walls, prince, houses, cell, vault, road, complete in its relations and empty of ornament. That emptiness is the strongest internal evidence for the inheritance thesis, stronger even than the documentary chain, because it is the fingerprint of copying. Writers who invent places invent detail; writers who transmit places transmit structure. The Verona of the novella, of Bandello, of Brooke and of the play is one diagram at four removes, and the diagram's endurance explains a paradox of performance, that the tragedy can be staged in any city-shaped space at all, since what the audience must recognise is not Verona's look but Verona's constitution. The real city's geographers, Alberti and Münster and the makers of the city books, were meanwhile filling in the look for readers of another kind, stone by stone and woodcut by woodcut. The two Veronas, the diagram and the portrait, circulated side by side through the same century in the same printshops, and the play descends from the diagram. A diagram that spare could survive three translations intact precisely because there was so little ornament to lose, and its survival is what let a London audience recognise the city at a glance in the Chorus's second line.

## Why the Fusion Held: Print, Authority and the Weight of a Name

It remains to explain the fusion's permanence in terms a sceptic can test, because "the chain preserved it" names the fact without giving its cause. Three causes operated together. The first is the authority gradient of the tale's new materials. Da Porto had bolted a minor Sienese novella to the two strongest fixtures in the Italian literary system, Dante's poem and the Scala legend, and no later reteller had an incentive to unbolt them, since each transfer of the tale borrowed credit from the fixtures. A translator choosing between Masuccio's Siena and Da Porto's Verona was choosing between an unknown frame and a famous one, and famous frames win such choices without being argued. The second cause is print itself. Da Porto's novella was reprinted; Bandello's Novelle became a European bestseller in the age of the story collection; Boaistuau and Brooke carried the tale into the two vernaculars with the largest new reading publics; and every edition re-impressed the compound of names, Romeo, Giulietta, Montecchi, Cappelletti, Verona, as a single memorised unit. Print fixes what manuscript culture lets drift, and the compound had been in print for sixty-five years before the play. The third cause is the shape of the name itself. Verona is short, sonorous in Italian, French and English alike, metrically handy, as the Chorus's line shows, and semantically empty to foreign ears in a way that lets it fill with the tale's meanings rather than competing with them. Cities with louder prior meanings in English ears, Venice with its merchants and Moors to come, Florence with its Medici, would have taxed the story with their own associations. Verona arrived famous in Italy and conveniently blank in England, a combination no other Italian city offered, and the tale filled the blank so completely that the city and the lovers now define each other for the whole world.

Those three causes also mark the limits of the verdict, and honesty requires them stated. The inheritance thesis does not claim that Verona's prior literary life dictated the play's meanings, only that it supplied the play's ground and much of its grammar. It does not claim Da Porto foresaw the fusion's career; he made a local, artful decision whose consequences outran any intention. And it does not demote Shakespeare to a copyist of settings, since the same chain that fixed his city left him the timescale, the cast and the language to reinvent, which he did. What the thesis claims is narrower and stronger: that the most famous stage direction in English drama, "In fair Verona, where we lay our scene", is a receipt for goods delivered by other hands, and that criticism which begins from the receipt, rather than from an imagined act of scenic invention, reads everything downstream of it more accurately.

## Houses as States: The Veronese Meaning of Household

The Chorus's first phrase, "two households, both alike in dignity", is usually read as a synonym for families, and the inherited setting corrects the reading. In the Italian city of the Scala age, a great house was not a family in the domestic sense but a consorteria, a sworn kindred of blood relations, in-laws, clients, retainers and armed servants, housed in a fortified quarter, often with its own tower, its own church patronage, its own burial place and its own foreign policy. Such houses made war and peace, contracted marriages as treaties and kept feuds as assets across generations. Verona's literature knew the type intimately, because the Scala themselves had risen as the greatest of the city's houses before becoming its lords, and the chronicles record a civic life organised as a balance among armed kindreds under a signore strong enough to tax, judge and occasionally hang them. When Dante filed Montecchi and Cappelletti among the ruined houses of Italy, his first readers pictured not surnames but establishments: halls, towers, clients and tombs. Da Porto, naming his lovers' houses from Dante's couplet, imported that whole establishment into the tale in two words, and the play inherits the import intact. Capulet entertains a hall full of guests while a masked enemy walks among them; Montague keeps a household whose servants open the play in arms; the Prince treats the heads of houses as powers to be summoned, fined and threatened like minor princes. None of that scale is explained, because the setting explains it. A London audience, whose great households were retinues in decay under a centralising crown, watched an Italian civic form its own history had outgrown, and the exoticism of the form is part of the tragedy's charge: a world where kinship is still a constitution.

This is the deepest stratum of the inheritance, and the one that most rewards the source-first reading this study has argued. The feud of the play is unintelligible as a private grudge; its brawls are foreign policy conducted in streets, its marriages are alliances, its tomb is a state vault, and its prince's failure is a constitutional collapse, all of which the Veronese setting supplies as background law. Critics who treat the feud as a given of the plot rather than a product of the place miss the mechanism the sources built. Masuccio's Siena could have supplied households too, and did, but it was Verona's name, Dante-stamped and Scala-ruled, that certified the scale to three generations of readers and made the certification heritable in three languages. The lovers die, in the end, of a constitution: of the fact that in the city the sources built, there was no institution between the house and the prince, no court of appeal from kinship except a friar's cell, and no self a young person could be outside a household's dignity. Verona contributes that constitution. It is the tragedy's true first cause, and it was complete, on the record, before Shakespeare was born.

## Stone Memory: Tombs, Walls and the Architecture the Legend Cited

The literary construction had a quarry, and part of its persuasiveness came from stone that any traveller could be shown. The della Scala raised over their dead the Arche Scaligere, the Gothic tomb monuments beside Santa Maria Antica in the heart of the city, where Cangrande's effigy rides above his sarcophagus and the dynasty advertises itself in marble to every passer-by. A city whose signori monumentalised their household in the public street taught its storytellers, by example, that a great house is a thing built to be read. The tale's family vault, the consecrated room where one house gathers its line, is the domestic scale of the same civic habit, and the tradition's confidence in pointing to tombs as evidence, a habit Alberti's geography and Dalla Corte's history both display when they retail the lovers, grows from a streetscape in which tombs genuinely were public documents. The walls worked the same way. Verona's circuits, Roman in origin, Scala and Venetian in their rebuildings, were the most legible sentence in the city's text: inside, jurisdiction, sanctuary and the prince's peace; outside, the road, Mantua and exile. When the inherited fiction made banishment its central legal terror, it cited an architecture every Italian reader could picture. Place in this tale is never decoration, because the place had spent two centuries building the very objects, tombs and walls and a lord's monuments, that the fiction needed as its instruments. The direction of debt at the endpoint can be stated in one line and left there: the city that visitors now seek for the lovers' sake was itself created, as an object of literary pilgrimage, by the same centuries of writing that created the setting, and the pilgrimage descends from the construction rather than from any event the construction commemorates. The geographers' Verona and the novella's Verona thus corroborate each other in print while describing different cities, one of stone and one of story, and the confusion of the two is the founding error the source record exists to correct.

The religious geography deserves the same precision, because the tale's most famous room stands in it. Da Porto placed the lovers' tomb at the church of San Francesco in Verona, the Franciscan house whose precinct held the vault his fiction required, and the empty sarcophagus long shown in that quarter became the physical peg on which the legend's claim to fact was hung for later centuries of visitors and historians alike. The choice of a Franciscan address was not local colour. The friar who drives the plot is a Franciscan in every version, and his order's urban geography, a great preaching church inside the walls, cells for counsel, a garden for simples, a churchyard for burial, is the operating system of the story's secret life. Verona's real ecclesiastical map, San Zeno's abbey across the river, the cathedral where Petrarch found his Cicero, the Franciscan and Dominican houses within the circuit, gave the tradition a complete set of consecrated interiors in which marriages, confessions and burials could plausibly hide from the prince's peace. The play inherits the map as the Capulets' monument and the Friar's cell, unnamed churches doing named work. Once again the pattern holds: the fittings are civic and documentary in origin, assembled by the sources from a real city's sacred plan, and transmitted as structure while every word spoken inside them was reinvented.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Verona lineage, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch Verona lineage is the map, tabled in this study, of how the city passed from Dante's Commedia through Boccaccio, Petrarch, the chroniclers and the humanist geographers into Da Porto's novella, and then through Bandello, Boaistuau, Brooke and Painter to the play. Each stage of the table records what the name Verona already meant at that stage and what the stage handed on. The pattern it exposes is asymmetry: incidents mutate at nearly every retelling, the heroine's age, the timescale, the manner of her death, while the place never mutates at all. From Da Porto onward the city is copied with the fidelity copyists reserve for the names of persons, because in a transmitted story a place functions as an identifier, the fact that certifies the tale as the same tale. The lineage therefore reverses the usual account of the setting. Verona was not chosen to fit the story at the end of the chain. It was built for two centuries before the story joined it, fused to the story by one writer in the 1520s, and preserved thereafter by the ordinary mechanics of transmission, which protect names and sacrifice everything else first.

### Q: Which city did Masuccio set his earlier version of the tale in, and what changed when the story moved?

Masuccio Salernitano set the tale in Siena. His story of Mariotto and Giannozza, the thirty-third tale of Il Novellino, printed at Naples in 1476, already contains the secret marriage before a friar, the killing, the exile, the sleeping draught, the tomb and the disastrous return, but its households carry no famous names and its city is the Sienese republic on the Arbia. When Luigi da Porto moved the action to Verona in the 1520s, he changed the plot's furniture less than its credit. Verona supplied what Siena could not: the Dantean family names Montecchi and Cappelletti, a ruling house whose courtesy Dante had made proverbial, a datable signoria under Bartolomeo della Scala, and a frame of received testimony, the Veronese archer who told the tale as his city's history. The move converted a competent novella into a story with a pedigree, and the pedigree is what travelled. Bandello, Boaistuau, Brooke and Painter all inherited the Veronese version and none looked back to Siena, because a reteller keeps the frame that carries the most authority, and after Da Porto the authority was entirely on the Adige side of the comparison.

### Q: What did the name Verona suggest to an Italian reader before any lover was attached to it?

It suggested a great court, a famous exile and a classical past. Above all it suggested the della Scala: the signoria that ruled the city from 1262 to 1387, made it one of the powers of Italy under Cangrande I, and gathered displaced men of talent at a court praised across the peninsula for its hospitality. It suggested Dante, who had sheltered there in his first exile and repaid the shelter in Paradiso 17, where the Scala courtesy is the prophecy's bright exception to Italy's ruin. It suggested antiquity within reach: a Roman amphitheatre standing in the streets, a chapter library where Petrarch had recovered Cicero's letters in 1345, inscriptions and remains that humanist geographers catalogued for Europe. And it suggested faction, the armed households whose wars the chroniclers recorded and whose ruined names Dante had filed in Purgatorio 6 as exhibits of Italy's disorder. Passion, lovers and balconies belong to the name's later life. In its first literary centuries Verona signified lordship, refuge, antiquity and faction, which is exactly the set of meanings a tale of feuding houses under a prince needed its setting to carry before the first line was read.

### Q: How does the dedicatory letter to Lucina Savorgnan position the Verona tale?

It positions the tale as a soldier's received testimony rather than a poet's invention. Da Porto dedicated Giulietta e Romeo to his kinswoman Lucina Savorgnan and framed the story as something that had come to him in the talk of the wars, told by Peregrino, a Veronese archer in his company, who presented it as an event of his own city in the days of Bartolomeo della Scala. The dedication does several kinds of work at once. It excuses the tale's roughness by assigning it to campfire narration. It borrows the authority of an eyewitness chain, soldier to soldier, for events two centuries old. And it anchors the fiction in Verona with the strongest fastening available to a novella: not the author's assertion, which a reader may discount, but a named native informant and a named ruling lord, the two fixtures by which Italian readers tested a tale's claim to be history. The play inherits that positioning at several removes, in the Chorus's chronicler tone, which likewise offers the action as a matter of record rather than of invention.

### Q: What does Dante praise about the Scala court in Paradiso 17?

He praises its courtesy to the exile, embodied in Bartolomeo della Scala. In the canto where Cacciaguida foretells Dante's banishment from Florence, the prophecy pauses on its one consolation: "Lo primo tuo refugio e 'l primo ostello / sarà la cortesia del gran Lombardo / che 'n su la scala porta il santo uccello" (Paradiso 17, lines 70 to 72), the first refuge and inn of the exile will be the courtesy of the great Lombard who bears the holy bird upon the ladder, the ladder being the Scala arms. The praise is precise in its terms. Dante does not celebrate Verona's wealth, walls or armies, though all were considerable. He celebrates the moral quality of a lord who receives a wronged stranger, the signorial virtue of hospitality raised to a principle of government. The canto then turns to Bartolomeo's young kinsman Cangrande, on whom Dante rests hopes for Italy's renewal, so that the Scala court carries, in the poem's economy, the counter-image to Florence's ingratitude: the city that casts out against the court that takes in. Every later writer who used Verona, Da Porto at their head, drew on that deposited credit, whether or not the allusion was conscious.

### Q: What happened in Verona in 1345 that made the city matter to humanists across Europe?

Petrarch recovered Cicero's private letters there. Working in the cathedral chapter library in 1345, Petrarch found and copied the manuscript of Cicero's Epistolae ad Atticum, the correspondence that revealed the Roman statesman thinking aloud to his friend, and the discovery became one of the foundation episodes of humanism. The letters changed what scholars thought a classical author could be: not a monument of set orations but a voice in daily negotiation, doubt and affection, and they gave the new learning a model for its own letter-writing, the familiar epistle as an instrument of thought. For Verona the episode's significance is its effect on the city's reputation. A place whose cupboards preserved what Rome had lost, and whose library admitted the right reader at the right moment, entered the mental geography of every scholar in Europe as a storehouse of antiquity, a city where the classical past was not a ruin to be viewed but a text to be found. That reputation, two centuries old when Da Porto wrote, is one of the strata beneath the tale's setting: Verona signified learned survival, the past kept safe inside walls, which is the exact atmosphere a novella posing as recovered history required.

### Q: How does Boccaccio present Cangrande's Verona in the Decameron?

He presents it as the byword court, the place where magnificence and wit are tested against each other. In the seventh tale of the first day, the storyteller Bergamino, neglected by Cangrande after arriving at Verona in hope of the lord's largesse, corrects his host with an improvised tale about Primasso and the Abbot of Cluny, whose generosity shames the Scala lord back into open-handedness. The episode's machinery depends entirely on the audience's prior knowledge. Cangrande's court must already signify the summit of Italian hospitality for the slight to sting and the recovery to delight, and Boccaccio spends no line establishing the reputation he exploits. That economy is the evidence this study keeps citing: by the middle of the fourteenth century Verona under its signore was already a stock setting in the novella tradition, a place a tale could be sent to the way a letter is sent to a known address. The lovers' tragedy would later move into a house the Decameron had helped build, where household politics is the natural element of story.

### Q: What did Giovanni Villani's chronicle preserve about Verona's signori that later writers inherited?

It preserved the Scala as documented Italian history. Giovanni Villani's Nuova Cronica, the great Florentine chronicle of the age, recorded the rise of the della Scala, Cangrande's campaigns and conquests, the dynasty's dealings with emperors, popes and communes, and its place in the peninsula's wars, treating Verona's doings as matters of common record rather than local boast. The importance of that treatment for the later story is easy to miss because chronicles feel like background, but background is precisely the service performed. A novelist of the 1520s who dated his fiction to Bartolomeo's signoria was dating it inside a documented frame that any educated reader could verify from the standard histories, and the verification reflex, the sense that the tale's scaffolding checks out, extended credit to the tale's persons by association. Villani and his successors also preserved the texture of the Scala world, its embassies, sieges and sudden deaths, from which the tradition of Verona as a city of armed houses drew its confidence. The play stands at the end of that documentary line as well as the novella line. Its Verona feels historical because, at the level of the frame, history is exactly what it is built from.

### Q: How does Flavio Biondo's Italia illustrata construct Verona for readers who would never see it?

It constructs the city as a classical site with a documented pedigree. Flavio Biondo's Italia illustrata, composed in the middle of the fifteenth century, was the founding work of humanist geography, a region-by-region account of Italy that set each city's Roman origins, surviving antiquities and notable history before the educated reader. Verona takes its place in that catalogue as a city whose amphitheatre, gates and inscriptions certify an ancient importance continuing into the present. The construction matters to this study because of its audience and its method. Biondo wrote for the republic of letters, for readers in Rome, Florence, and eventually in print across Europe, most of whom would never stand in the Piazza Bra. For them Verona existed as Biondo's entry: ordered, ancient, significant, a name with its credentials attached. That is the form in which the city circulated for the century before Da Porto, a reference-work fact rather than a traveller's impression, and the form explains the tale's later portability. A setting already standardised by the geographers could cross languages without losing identity, because every new reader, Italian, French or English, could meet the same certified Verona in the same kind of book.

### Q: Why did Verona under the della Scala attract exiles and poets in Dante's lifetime?

Because the court made hospitality a policy and the policy a reputation. The Scala signoria, rising in a peninsula of warring communes where every factional turn expelled a fresh company of the defeated, understood that displaced talent was cheap at the price of lodging. Dante, exiled from Florence in 1302, found his first refuge with Bartolomeo della Scala in 1303 and 1304 and a longer residence under Cangrande I in later years, and he was the most famous of a numerous class of soldiers, scholars, poets and secretaries washed up by other cities' revolutions. The attraction was mutual and rational. The exile gained protection and a stipend; the lord gained counsellors, propagandists and the lustre of a court that the age's greatest poet praised in the age's greatest poem. Verona's geography helped, a rich city on the Adige athwart the roads between Lombardy, the Veneto and the Empire, where a displaced man could wait within reach of home. The literary consequence is the one this study traces: a city whose name meant refuge to the generation that formed Italian literature, long before it meant a tomb to the readers of the lovers' tale.

### Q: What does Leandro Alberti's description of Italy add to the Verona story's claim to be history?

It adds the authority of a work that is not fiction. Leandro Alberti's Descrittione di tutta Italia, printed at Bologna in 1550, is a geography in Biondo's line, a sober catalogue of the peninsula's regions, cities and antiquities. In its treatment of Verona the book retails the lovers' story as part of the city's own past, within roughly twenty years of the novella's first printing. The significance of the retailing lies in its genre. A novella claims a reader's belief lightly and loses nothing when the claim is doubted; a geography files its contents as knowledge, and a tale filed in a geography acquires the documentary status of the shelf it sits on. Alberti's page is therefore one of the earliest exhibits in the story's long confusion of categories, the moment when Da Porto's placement begins its conversion into record. Later Veronese historiography completed the conversion, but the geographer began it, and began it outside Verona, which shows the conversion was not merely local patriotism. Print itself was doing the work: a story attached to a famous city, reprinted in a famous description of that city, hardened into the kind of fact that subsequent writers cite rather than test.

### Q: How does Girolamo dalla Corte's history of Verona handle the lovers?

It handles them as persons of the city's past. Girolamo dalla Corte's Istoria di Verona, printed in 1592, only a few years before the play reached the London stage, writes Romeo and Juliet into the history of Verona under the Scala, treating the pair as figures of record, not inventions of a novella. The handling is the endpoint of the process this study has traced from Alberti onward: a fiction of the 1520s, absorbed by a geography in 1550, is certified by a civic history in 1592, each genre lending the tale a heavier documentary frame than the last. Dalla Corte wrote as a Veronese for Veronese, and his certification shows the story become civic memory, its truth resting on nothing firmer than its own circulation. When Shakespeare's Chorus introduced "fair Verona" to a London audience, the city of the tale had already been furnished, by its own historian, with the lovers as documented inhabitants. The playwright inherited not merely a setting but a setting whose fiction Italian print had already naturalised as fact, which is one reason the play's own chronicler tone sounds so little like a boast.

### Q: What work does the Adige do in the early accounts of Verona's identity?

The river explains the city, and the early accounts treat it as the first fact from which the rest follow. Verona stands where the Adige, descending from the Alpine passes, bends in a great loop before entering the plain, and that loop dictated the Roman foundation, the line of the walls and the city's enduring role as the tollgate between Italy and the north. Chroniclers and geographers open their Veronas with the river because the river is the cause of everything they go on to list: the fertility of the surrounding plain, the wealth of the market, the strategic value that brought emperors, signori and armies to the walls. In the literary construction the Adige does quieter work. It draws the boundary of the tale's world, the circuit within which the prince's peace, the friar's cell and the houses' quarrel operate, and beyond which lies the road to Mantua and the indifference of exile. A reader of the sources absorbs that geography as part of the name's stored meaning. The play inherits the stored meaning intact, needing the river only as the audience's prior knowledge that this city is a crossing, a stronghold and a world complete in itself.

### Q: How did the Arena function in the city's image before the play?

It functioned as the standing proof of antiquity. The Roman amphitheatre at Verona, raised in the first century of the Empire and known simply as the Arena, survived the Middle Ages as the largest single object in the city's streetscape, reused and never effaced. For the humanist geographers it was the headline exhibit in Verona's entry, the stone certificate that the place had been considerable since the age of the Caesars and that its modern fame rested on ancient foundations. For the wider literary construction its function was atmospheric: it set the air. A city that conducted its modern life in the shadow of an imperial monument presented itself to every writer as a place where the past was not past, where a story set two centuries back stood among buildings that made two centuries look brief. The tale that Da Porto planted in Verona drew silent credit from that depth of field. Its events pose as old, and the Arena made oldness visible. No version of the story names the amphitheatre, and none needed to. The monument belonged to the name's stored meaning, part of what a reader pictured, unbidden, at the word Verona.

### Q: What did William Thomas's History of Italy offer English readers about cities like Verona decades before the play?

It offered the first systematic English account of Italy as a system of states. William Thomas, a clerk of Edward VI's council, published The History of Italy in 1549, surveying the peninsula's republics, signorie and kingdoms for an English readership that had almost no other native source. The book's Italy is a chessboard of proud, faction-ridden cities, governed by princes and councils whose intrigues Thomas narrates with a clerk's fascination, and its cumulative effect on English readers was to make the Italian city-state legible as a political form wholly unlike their own centralised monarchy: smaller, richer, more violent in its household politics and more sophisticated in its statecraft. For the later history of the play, Thomas matters as the head of the line of print that built the audience's Italy. A Londoner of the 1590s who had never opened Thomas still inhabited the mental world he had opened, through Ascham, Florio and the stage's own conventions. When such a hearer met Verona in the Chorus's second line, the name arrived with forty years of English print behind it, pre-interpreted as the kind of place where great houses and a prince contend, and where a feud is a form of politics.

### Q: Why did English writers warn against Italian travel while English stages filled with Italian cities?

Because the warning and the fascination were the same response to the same Italy. Roger Ascham's Scholemaster of 1570, the period's loudest English warning, condemned the travelled Englishman who came home Italianate in religion, morals and manners, and quoted the proverb that an Englishman Italianated is a devil incarnate. Yet Ascham's Italy is condemned precisely for its power to attract: its learning, courtesy, music and fencing were the refinements English gentlemen crossed the Alps to acquire, and the fear was that corruption came attached. The stage exploited both poles of the charge. Italy at a distance offered English audiences the spectacle of everything home forbade, armed households, confessional intrigue, potions, duels, sudden passion, framed by the reassurance that these were foreign customs, delightful to watch and safe to disown. Verona served that double market perfectly. It was distant enough to license the feud, the friar and the tomb, and famous enough, through the print tradition this study traces, to need no introduction. The warnings kept Italy dangerous; the danger kept Italy saleable; and the inherited setting of the tragedy sits exactly at the intersection, a place an audience had been taught to desire and distrust in the same breath.

### Q: How much of Shakespeare's Verona can be traced to Brooke's poem?

Nearly all of it, because Brooke's poem is the channel through which the inherited city reached the playwright in English dress. Arthur Brooke's Tragicall Historye of Romeus and Juliet of 1562, worked from Boaistuau's French, plants its action in Verona from its opening argument and keeps the lovers inside the city's walls, its feast, its friar's cell and its vault for the poem's three thousand lines. The play's Verona reproduces the poem's civic diagram feature for feature: the prince above the houses, the brawling retainers in public places, the Franciscan machinery of marriage and potion, the exile's road to Mantua, the family monument where the action ends. What the play adds is tempo, voice and Mercutio, not topography. Even the Chorus's presentation of the city as a matter of record follows the poem's own posture, for Brooke likewise retails the tale as a known history rather than a fresh invention. In the matter of place, the inventory of debts is the compliment the evidence pays the tradition. Shakespeare's Verona is Brooke's Verona, and Brooke's is Da Porto's, and the line of custody is unbroken, documented and, on the point of the setting, undisputed by Bullough's collection or Levenson's edition.

### Q: Why does the Chorus call the city "fair Verona" before any household is named?

Because the city is the play's first given, and the epithet announces its status. The Prologue opens, "Two households, both alike in dignity, / In fair Verona, where we lay our scene" (Prologue, lines 1 to 2), and the order of information is the order of the inheritance: before the audience hears of houses, feud or lovers, it is handed the place, complete with the adjective a famous place wears when it is introduced to hearers expected to recognise it. "Fair" is not description but courtesy title, and its use in the play's second line performs the whole argument of this study in miniature. Shakespeare does not build Verona, praise it into existence or argue for its fitness. He cites it, the way the sources had cited it for seventy years, as the known scene of a known story, and the audience's readiness to accept the citation had been manufactured by the chain of print behind the line, Italian, French and English. The households are then named inside the city's frame, as the sources always name them, tenants of a dignity the place possessed before they were attached to it and will outlast their story in the world's memory.

### Q: How should an essay argue that Verona was inherited rather than invented?

An essay should argue from the chain, the silence and the diagram, in that order. The chain comes first: exhibit the transmission, Masuccio's Sienese tale of 1476, Da Porto's Veronese fusion of the 1520s, Bandello in 1554, Boaistuau in 1559, Brooke in 1562, Painter in 1566, the play in the mid 1590s, and show that the city enters once, at Da Porto's desk, and is never altered afterward. The silence comes second: no document before Da Porto joins the lovers, the houses or the feud to Verona, the Cappelletti belong in the early commentators to Cremona, and a chronicle-rich city that records nothing of the tale's alleged events is itself evidence that the events are literary. The diagram comes third: the play's Verona reproduces the sources' civic structure, prince, houses, friar, vault, Mantuan exile, while lacking the local detail an inventor or observer supplies, the fingerprint of copying rather than creation. Against the essay, expect the claim that a great dramatist must have chosen his setting; the record shows he chose everything except the setting. Credit him with the activation of an inherited city, and let the inheritance stand as the tradition's achievement.

### Q: Which features of the play's Verona were already fixed in the sources before Shakespeare wrote?

The whole civic diagram was fixed: the two great households as armed establishments under a single prince; the prince's peace as the law the brawls offend; the public street as the feud's theatre; the friar's cell as the city's secret interior, where marriages are made and potions compounded; the heroine's enforced match with a count as the household's treaty; the husband's exile to Mantua and the road between the cities as the plot's lifeline; the family vault as the ending's place; and the reconciling audit over the bodies. Da Porto assembled most of the diagram, Bandello thickened its scenes, Boaistuau darkened its moral weather and Brooke Englished it entire, so that the playwright received a city as completely furnished as a stage set from a previous production. His alterations are famous and real, the compression of months into days and the deepening of the Nurse and Mercutio, but they are alterations of occupancy, not of architecture. The walls within which Romeo finds no world outside them were standing, on the documentary record, before Shakespeare was born, and criticism honours both the builders and the tenant when it keeps the two contributions distinct.
