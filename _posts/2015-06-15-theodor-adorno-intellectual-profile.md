Theodor Adorno is usually introduced as a philosopher who disliked popular music, and the introduction is not false so much as badly out of proportion. He was a trained composer who studied in Vienna with Alban Berg, a pianist of near professional standard, a music critic who published hundreds of reviews before he published a book of philosophy, and a thinker whose central procedures were learned at the piano and the score desk rather than in the seminar room. To read him without the music is to read a score as if it were a legal contract: the words are all present, and the thing they were arranged to do escapes. This profile therefore reverses the usual order. It begins with the formation, because in Adorno's case the formation is the method, and it treats the famous doctrines, negative dialectics, identity thinking, the critique of standardised culture, as consequences of a way of listening that he acquired before he was twenty-five and never abandoned.

![Theodor Adorno, an intellectual profile, negative dialectics and the musical method - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-35.webp)

Three facts about him are persistently garbled in short accounts, and a profile has a duty to set them straight before anything else. The first is that the most quoted sentence he ever wrote, the remark that writing poetry after Auschwitz is barbaric, was substantially qualified by its author in print seventeen years later, in a passage almost nobody quotes alongside it. The second is that his notorious writings on jazz are not a defensible position misread by hostile critics: they are his weakest work, written largely about commercial European dance-band music rather than about the tradition later generations mean by the word, and dismissive in ways that the excuse of his sources does not cover. The third is that the man who died in 1969, weeks after student activists disrupted his lectures and occupied his institute, was not a conservative defending order against the young. His positions were consistently on the left, and the confrontation that closed his life was a quarrel inside the left, which is precisely why it wounded both sides as it did. A profile that defends everything about its subject is not a profile but an advertisement, and Adorno, who spent a career arguing that thought begins where affirmation stops, deserves better than an advertisement.

## A Frankfurt childhood organised around music

He was born on 11 September 1903 in Frankfurt am Main, and christened Theodor Ludwig Wiesengrund. The double inheritance packed into that name governed everything that followed. His father, Oscar Alexander Wiesengrund, was a prosperous wine merchant whose family was of Jewish origin and who had converted to Protestantism. His mother, Maria Calvelli-Adorno della Piana, was a Catholic of Corsican and Genoese descent who had sung professionally, appearing at court and municipal theatres before her marriage. Her maiden name, Adorno, was the name the son would take for his public life, first hyphenated as Wiesengrund-Adorno and then, after emigration, standing alone with only the initial W. preserved between his first name and his fame. That migration of names is sometimes treated as a curiosity and sometimes as concealment. It was neither. It was the ordinary sign of a divided household, German and Mediterranean, Jewish and Catholic, commercial and artistic, in which the boy grew up bilingual in the only sense that mattered to him: the languages of business and of art were both spoken at home, and neither was allowed to reduce the other to a decoration.

The household in the Sachsenhausen district of Frankfurt was, by the standards of the German bourgeoisie, unusually saturated with performance. His mother sang. Her sister Agathe, who lived with the family, was a singer and a pianist of real accomplishment, and the child had two musicians for daily company. He learned the piano early and seriously, played four-handed arrangements of the classical repertoire with his aunt, and absorbed the habit, decisive for everything later, of knowing works from the inside, at the keyboard, where their construction is under the fingers, rather than from the audience's distance. Biographers return repeatedly to the summers the family spent at Amorbach in the Odenwald, a small town he remembered all his life with a tenderness that appears, transfigured, in the most austere of his books. The point of these details is not charm. A child formed this way does not encounter music as an ornament applied to life from outside. He encounters it as a discipline with standards, a craft in which a wrong note is not an opinion, and in which the difference between a passage that merely continues and a passage that discovers something is audible to anyone trained to attend. That ear, trained before the philosophy began, is the instrument his whole career would use.

The house stood in the Seeheimer Strasse in Sachsenhausen, the solid district south of the Main where Frankfurt's prosperous families kept their villas and their pianos, and the firm's cellars, the Wiesengrund wine business, lay within the geography of the child's walks. The detail is worth its sentence because the later work is unintelligible as the product of an outsider: he was raised at the centre of the world he would indict, fed at its table, formed by its highest culture in its most accomplished domestic form, and the indictment therefore carries the weight of evidence rather than the energy of resentment. When the mature writer describes the bourgeois interior, its furniture of feeling, its tact and its evasions, he is describing the rooms he grew up in, with an accuracy his hosts' descendants recognised, sometimes with discomfort, as portraiture from life.

Schooling at the Kaiser Wilhelm Gymnasium in Frankfurt put the prodigy on public display. He skipped a class, finished at the top of his cohort, and graduated in 1921, at seventeen, already publishing music criticism in local papers. The precocity was not the brittle kind. On Saturday afternoons during the war years, the teenage Adorno had read Kant's first Critique aloud, page by page, with Siegfried Kracauer, a family friend fourteen years his senior and already an established writer and architect turned sociologist. Kracauer remembered the boy arguing with Kant rather than admiring him, treating the text as a score to be realised rather than a monument to be saluted. That habit, the refusal to let a canonical text sit still under reverence, stayed. When readers later complained that Adorno's philosophy would not sit still either, that it kept undoing its own formulations at the moment they hardened, they were meeting the Saturday afternoons again, extended across fifty years.

The childhood had its shadow, and the shadow belongs to the formation too. He was six when the First World War began and eleven when it ended in Germany's collapse; the wine firm that sustained the household's ease was battered by the postwar inflation that dissolved the German middle class's savings and certainties in the single year of 1923, when he was twenty and the world of secure bourgeois cultivation in which he had been raised demonstrated, in his own family's ledger, its fragility. Biographers have connected the lifelong preoccupation with damage, with the false whole and the sheltered interior that cannot hold, to this early instruction in how quickly a civilisation's furniture can be carried out of the house. The connection should be filed as plausible rather than proven, with the caution he himself taught about biographical explanation. What is documented is the precocity of the response: the boy who had been formed by the most protected interior in Frankfurt spent his career arguing that interiors, concepts, artworks and lives are all to be judged by how honestly they register the storm outside their walls, and the argument began, in the essays of the late nineteen twenties, almost as soon as the storm had passed over his own street.

He entered the university at Frankfurt in 1921 and took his doctorate with startling speed, completing it in 1924 with a dissertation on Edmund Husserl's phenomenology, directed by Hans Cornelius, a neo-Kantian of the older school. The dissertation is a youthful work and reads like one, but its subject already matters: phenomenology was the reigning attempt to describe experience before concepts digest it, and the question of what objects are before thought sorts them into classes would become the lifelong question of his philosophy. At Frankfurt he also formed the relationships that organised his intellectual life. He met Max Horkheimer, nine years older, in the early nineteen twenties, and through Kracauer he met Walter Benjamin in 1923, beginning a friendship and an argument that lasted until Benjamin's death in exile. And he read Marx, as nearly everyone of his generation and milieu did, in the aftershock of war, revolution and failed revolution, when the question of why the predicted transformation of society had not arrived was not an academic puzzle but the central unsolved problem of European politics. What distinguished Adorno's path through that shared inheritance was that he approached it carrying a training nobody else in his circle possessed. Before he was a Marxist, before he was a philosopher in any professional sense, he was a composer in formation, and the next stage of the formation took him to Vienna.

## Vienna, Berg, and the discipline of the score

In 1925 Adorno went to Vienna to study composition with Alban Berg. The decision deserves to be weighed rather than noted, because Vienna in those years was the capital of the most demanding musical modernism in Europe, and Berg was its most lyrical master. Berg had been Arnold Schoenberg's pupil, a member of what is called the Second Viennese School, and his own music, the operas Wozzeck and the unfinished Lulu, the Lyric Suite, the Violin Concerto written near the end of his life, joined the rigour of twelve-tone construction to an expressive warmth that the method was not supposed to permit. To study with Berg was to submit to a regime. Adorno worked on counterpoint, on strict forms, on the anatomy of transitions, and he played piano with Eduard Steuermann, the pianist Schoenberg's circle trusted with its most difficult scores. He composed steadily: string quartet pieces that were performed and published, songs to texts by Stefan George and Georg Trakl, orchestral pieces, works in which a young composer of genuine gift tried to speak the hardest musical language of his time as a native. He also wrote criticism at speed for the journal Musikblätter des Anbruch, the house magazine of advanced music, reviewing performances with a severity that made enemies and a precision that made his reputation among musicians. He was twenty-two, twenty-three, twenty-four. Philosophy, in these years, was not abandoned, but it was not the centre. The centre was the desk where a phrase had to justify itself against the whole of a movement or be struck out.

What Vienna gave him can be stated as three disciplines. The first was the discipline of immanent listening: the conviction that a work's meaning is not carried by its themes as a cart carries goods, but emerges from how every element mediates every other, so that understanding means following relations rather than collecting statements. The second was the discipline of the fragment that resists: in a Berg score the detail that does not fit, the appoggiatura that arrives a fraction too late, the colour that the construction did not predict, is not a blemish but the location of the truth, because it registers what the system could not absorb. The third was the discipline of refusal: modern composition as Berg and Schoenberg practised it treated the easy resolution, the cadence that arrives because convention expects it, as a lie told to the ear, and trained the composer to prefer an honest dissonance to a purchased consonance. Any reader who knows Adorno's philosophy will recognise these three disciplines immediately under their philosophical names. Immanent listening becomes the immanent criticism that judges a concept by its own claims. The resisting fragment becomes the non-identical, the remainder every concept leaves behind. The refusal of the easy cadence becomes negative dialectics itself, the scored refusal of premature resolution in thought. The claim of this profile, its namable claim, can be put in one sentence: his philosophy is musical analysis transposed, and its obscurities are the obscurities of a method that hears arguments the way a musician hears voice leading.

### What did Adorno learn in Vienna?

Composition with Alban Berg, counterpoint and strict form, piano under Eduard Steuermann, and criticism written to deadline for a journal of advanced music: a craft learned before doctrine. The lessons were relational. A phrase means what its neighbours make it mean, a detail resisting the construction carries the truth, and easy resolution is a lie told to the ear.

Those years also fixed his hierarchy of musical value in ways that would later cause trouble, and honesty requires the trouble to be recorded here rather than postponed. The Vienna circle defined progress in music as the progressive mastery of material: Schoenberg's emancipation of dissonance and twelve-tone method stood at the summit, and everything that declined the summit, neoclassicism, Gebrauchsmusik, jazz influenced dance music, popular song, was ranked as regression, whatever its pleasures. Adorno absorbed this hierarchy at the age when hierarchies settle, and he defended it for forty years with formidable intelligence. Parts of the defence remain unsurpassed as analysis. Parts of it, above all where it touches popular music, harden into something the evidence cannot carry, and the jazz sections of this profile will say so without varnish. But the hierarchy was never, even in Vienna, simple snobbery about audiences. It was a claim about material: that musical material has a history, that the stock of available gestures wears out as conventions exhaust it, and that a composer who reaches for the exhausted gesture is not innocent but is quoting a dead language and calling the quotation freshness. One can dispute the claim, and later sections will dispute its applications. One cannot understand anything he wrote without knowing that for him it was not a preference but a finding, as impersonal as an acoustical fact.

Vienna ended, as it had to, in the recognition that the double life could not continue at full stretch on both sides. Adorno's compositions were respected; they were not, he came to judge, the work of a major composer, and the judgement, which biographers report him making on himself with characteristic unsentimentality, freed the energy that philosophy was claiming. He returned to Frankfurt at the end of the nineteen twenties, took up the academic career in earnest, and began to write the essays in which the musician and the Marxist first confront each other on the page. But the return should not be mistaken for a conversion. He continued to compose, continued to write about music more than about any other single subject across the whole of his life, and continued to insist, in print and in argument, that the philosophy was answerable to the music and not the reverse. The young man who had chosen Berg had chosen the teacher of his thought, and the choice held for forty-four years.

## The musical method: how Adorno thinks

Every difficult thinker poses the same practical question: by what procedure does the mind on the page actually move? With Adorno the question has an unusually concrete answer, and giving it is the work of this section. His procedure, here called the musical method, can be broken into habits that any careful reader can verify in the texts. The first habit is to begin from the object rather than from the definition. Where a conventional treatise fixes its terms and then applies them, Adorno descends into a particular, a chord, a slogan, a sentence from a novel, a protocol sentence from a logical positivist, and lets the category emerge, if it emerges at all, from the pressure the particular exerts. The second habit is the constellation. Instead of arranging concepts in a hierarchy under a master term, he sets them around the object like instruments around a theme, each lighting the object from an angle the others cannot reach, none promoted to the solo that silences the rest. The third habit is parataxis, side by side placement without subordination, learned from the aphorism and from musical texture alike: elements are juxtaposed so that their relations sound, rather than being marched in single file under a commanding thesis. The fourth habit is the micrological gaze, the conviction, announced openly in the dedication of his most famous book, that the smallest detail, a greeting, a gift, a hotel corridor, is a cell in which the whole of a damaged society can be read, because the whole is nowhere else available except as it sediments in particulars. The fifth habit is the refusal of the final chord. A piece of Adorno's prose does not conclude by resolving its tensions into a position the reader can carry away like a souvenir. It ends when the constellation has been sounded long enough for the reader to hear what no single concept could say, and the apparent inconclusiveness is the method's honesty, not its failure.

### What is a constellation in this method?

A constellation is his alternative to the system: a set of concepts arranged around an object so that each illuminates what the others miss, none subordinated to a master term. Borrowed from musical texture and from Benjamin, it lets thought approach what the object is by circling it, where a definition would only record what the concept has already decided.

The musical method explains both his power and his price, and a profile should quote the price in full. The power shows wherever the object under his gaze is one that conventional concepts genuinely falsify: the standardised song whose sameness its admirers experience as variety, the philosophical system whose completeness is achieved by amputating the experiences that do not fit, the administered routine whose reasonableness requires not noticing what it costs. In such cases the descent into the particular, the constellation, the sounded chord of mutually correcting concepts, produces knowledge that a propositional summary cannot reproduce, because the knowledge is in the relations. The price is paid by the reader. Arguments conducted this way cannot be excerpted, cannot be bullet pointed, cannot be skimmed by a busy person and reconstructed later. They demand the sustained attention that a difficult score demands, replaying included. Adorno knew the price and defended it on principle: a thought that can be detached from its movement and still be the same thought, he held, was probably never a thought but a classification wearing thought's clothes. Whether that defence fully justifies the difficulty is a fair question, and the objection section of this profile will press it. What cannot fairly be said is that the difficulty is accidental, a failure of clarity in a writer who could not do better. The man wrote radio talks, newspaper pieces and lecture courses of exemplary lucidity when the genre required lucidity. The difficulty of the major prose is a compositional decision, the decision that certain truths about a false whole can only be stated by a form that refuses to pretend the whole is true.

It also explains his strange position inside Marxism, the position this series exists to map. Adorno took from Marx the critique of political economy, the analysis of the commodity form, the conviction that consciousness is socially mediated down to its finest fibres. He declined, increasingly, the philosophy of history that in orthodox hands accompanied those tools: the guaranteed direction of events, the assigned historical role of a single class, the confidence that the concept of history and history itself coincide. A musician knows that a score is not the performance and that the map of a work's structure is not the work. Transposed, that knowledge becomes a standing suspicion of any theory that presents its schema as the reality it schematises. His Marxism is therefore real and heterodox at once, a Marxism of the object against the system, and the comparison with the classical doctrine is treated in its own study in this series, [critical theory vs classical marxism](/2015/09/01/critical-theory-vs-classical-marxism/). What belongs here is the temperament the method produced: sceptical of every identity between concept and thing, loyal to the things themselves with a fidelity that his critics mistook, from opposite directions, for either pessimism or elitism. It was neither. It was a musician's loyalty to what is actually sounding.

Immanent criticism, the method's critical form, deserves its own statement here, because it is the procedure a reader meets on every page of the mature work and the one most often mistaken for mere paradox. To criticise immanently is to refuse the external tribunal: the critic does not measure the object, a philosophy, a constitution, a symphony, against a standard imported from elsewhere, because the imported standard is exactly what the object will rightly reject as foreign. Instead the critic takes the object's own claim with full seriousness, more seriously than the object takes it, and follows the claim into the object's details until the details convict or vindicate it. A society that professes freedom is judged by the unfreedom its own professions disclose when pressed. A philosophy that promises reconciliation is judged by the remainders its own system cannot file. A chorus that promises triumph is judged by the voice leading that does not triumph. The procedure explains his prose's characteristic figure, the argument that grants everything to the opposing case and then shows the case collapsing from the weight of its own concessions, and it explains his impatience with criticism as denunciation, the external kind, which he regarded as the laziest form of the identity thinking he opposed: the object filed, once again, this time under the file marked guilty.

One more consequence of the method needs stating early, because it organises the biography that follows. Adorno's writings fall into two great registers. There is the music writing proper, reviews, essays on composers, the sociology of music, in which the method operates on its home ground and is at its most concrete and verifiable. And there is the philosophical writing, in which the method is transposed to objects, society, metaphysics, morality, that resist it more strongly, with results that range from the revelatory to the exasperating. Readers who start with the philosophy often conclude that the man is a fog. Readers who start with the music writing usually conclude the opposite: that here is one of the most exact intelligences of the century, whose fog in the philosophical register is the shadow cast by an exactness trying to operate where exactness has no native instruments. His own estimate, to judge by the distribution of his labour across five decades, placed music first. The profile follows his estimate.


His own compositions from those years repay a paragraph, because their fate instructed him. The two pieces for string quartet, the songs on poems by George and Trakl, the orchestral fragments, were performed, published and respectfully reviewed; they were also, by the pitiless standard of the circle that had trained him, the work of a gifted epigone of Berg rather than the discovery of a new voice. Adorno heard the verdict before anyone pronounced it, and the hearing may be the most consequential act of listening in his life. A lesser intelligence protects itself at that moment, blames the audience, the age, the conspiracy of the mediocre. His instead converted the finding into a vocation: if the music in him was destined to be commentary rather than creation, the commentary would be raised to a level at which the distinction lost its sting. The theorist of the non-identical begins here, in a young composer's exact knowledge of the gap between his concept of a work and the work he could actually write. The most charming evidence of the ambition he renounced is the unfinished Singspiel he drafted in the early nineteen thirties from Mark Twain, The Treasure of Indian Joe, a Tom Sawyer opera whose surviving numbers show a composer of wit and genuine melodic gift attempting the impossible reconciliation his whole generation dreamed of, the new music wedded to a popular subject without condescension in either direction. He put the fragment aside as the German catastrophe gathered, and the musicologists who recovered it after his death heard in it the road not taken, the light theatre composer he might have become in a century that had left the road open. He never mentioned the fragment in print. Its existence, among the papers of the philosopher of damaged life, stands as the biography's smallest and most eloquent exhibit: the gaiety was real too, and the renunciation of the gaiety, for the sake of the work the century actually required, was a choice and not a temperament.

## The young critic: editorship, the Schubert essay, the actuality of philosophy

Between the return from Vienna and the catastrophe of 1933, Adorno lived the most various years of his life, and their variety explains the range of the mature work. He edited, in all but title, the journal Der Anbruch from 1928 to 1931, steering the house magazine of advanced music through the factional wars of musical modernism, commissioning, translating, attacking and defending with a polemical energy that made the journal's office a second education. He wrote the essay on Schubert of 1928, a piece his admirers rank with the finest musical prose of the century, in which the lyric landscape of Schubert's music, its wandering themes and unresolving distances, is read as the physiognomy of a subjectivity that history had already stranded. He wrote on Berg's Wozzeck, on the radio, on the gramophone, on jazz's European cousins, on the sociology that music was acquiring as a discipline, and he began to formulate, in the essay on the social situation of music published in the Institute's journal in 1932, the first systematic draft of the position he would hold for life: that music is not a decoration hung on society but a register in which society's contradictions become audible before they become speakable, and that the critic's task is to read the register without either worshipping or despising the needle.

The academic counterpart of these years is the inaugural lecture of 1931, The Actuality of Philosophy, delivered when he took up his teaching post at Frankfurt. It is a young man's manifesto and reads, astonishingly, like the programme of the next forty years already complete. Philosophy, he argued, has lost its old confidence that thought and reality coincide in a total system; the systems are ruins, and the honest philosopher works among the ruins, arranging the fragments of the sciences and of experience into constellations that light up what no discipline owns. The word constellation is present, the micrological loyalty is present, the refusal of the first principle is present. What the lecture lacks, and what the life was about to supply, is the political weight: the knowledge of what a society does to the minds it damages, which no Frankfurt seminar of 1931 could have taught with the authority that exile, Los Angeles and the return would teach it. Read against the later books, the lecture is a score in short notation, everything indicated, nothing yet orchestrated. The orchestration is the biography.

## Kierkegaard, the first book, and the closing of Germany

The academic career he resumed in Frankfurt ran on a track that German universities laid down with bureaucratic serenity: the doctorate, then the Habilitation, the second thesis that licensed a scholar to teach. Adorno's Habilitation, completed in 1931 under the theologian and philosopher Paul Tillich, examined the Danish writer Søren Kierkegaard, and the book drawn from it, Kierkegaard: Construction of the Aesthetic, appeared in 1933, in the very weeks when the republic whose universities had trained him was being dismantled. The coincidence has often been noted because it is emblematic beyond mere irony. The first book of the Frankfurt School's most demanding philosopher was a study of the nineteenth century's great anatomist of inwardness, published as inwardness itself was about to be made a crime of evasion by one side and a luxury of despair by the other.

The book matters for the profile because it is the first full performance of the musical method on a philosophical object. Kierkegaard's authorship is famously indirect: pseudonyms, sermons, diaries, aesthetic novellas nested inside ethical treatises, a body of work that refuses to be summarised because its meaning is distributed across voices that contradict one another by design. Adorno read it as a score is read. He treated the pseudonyms as registers, the categories of the aesthetic, the ethical and the religious as movements, and he asked what the whole construction does rather than what its author professes. His finding was double edged and it set the pattern of his mature criticism. Kierkegaard's protest against the system, against Hegel's total mediation, was honoured as genuine; the flight into pure inwardness that the protest performed was judged a failure, because inwardness sealed against objects becomes a furnished room in which the self admires its own depth while the world that produced the despair proceeds untouched outside the door. The aesthetic, in the young Adorno's construction, is the sphere where this failure becomes visible as form. Readers of the later work will recognise the gesture instantly: take the protest seriously, take it more seriously than its author did, and show where its own logic delivers it into the hands of what it opposed. That is the whole of his critical procedure in embryo, and it was complete, recognisably, by his thirtieth year.

Two friendships of the Frankfurt years belong to the formation as surely as the teachers do, because they supplied the young scholar with his two great external measures. The first is Georg Lukács, whose History and Class Consciousness of 1923 Adorno read as a student with the shock of recognition that a generation shared: here was Marxism recovered as philosophy, the commodity form traced into the structure of consciousness itself, reification named as the universal fate. Adorno's mature work can be read, without much distortion, as a lifelong negotiation with that book, accepting the diagnosis of reification while refusing the remedy Lukács proposed, the identical subject object of history embodied in a class and its party. The refusal hardened into polemic late, in the essay of 1958 that arraigned the older man's enforced reconciliation with the socialist realism of the East, but the essay's severity is the severity of a debtor, and Adorno said as much in its pages: the categories he was turning against their author had been learned from the author. The second friendship is Ernst Bloch, the philosopher of hope, whose exuberant utopianism stood at the opposite pole of Adorno's temperament and whose company he sought for fifty years, in Frankfurt, in exile, in the recorded conversations of their old age in which the two veterans argue, with undiminished appetite, about whether the world as it is can be thought from the standpoint of what it is not. The dialogue between the melancholic and the prophet is one of the century's great intellectual double acts, and it corrects the portrait of the solitary mandarin as effectively as any document can: the thought that refuses consolation was formed in weekly argument with the thought that refused despair, and neither man would have been complete without the other's refusal.

### How did Kierkegaard shape his first book?

Kierkegaard gave the young scholar an authorship built from masks, pseudonyms and nested voices, a whole that could not be summarised without being destroyed. Reading it taught the method: treat the voices as registers in a score, honour the protest against Hegel's system, and show where the retreat into inwardness betrays the protest it carries.

Meanwhile the institutional ground was being prepared and then removed with a speed that the book's serenity makes almost indecent. In 1930 Horkheimer became director of the Institute for Social Research in Frankfurt, the privately endowed research institute whose programme of interdisciplinary materialism became the organisational form of what the world now calls the Frankfurt School, whose collective history is charted in the series study of [the Frankfurt School complete guide](/2015/06/01/frankfurt-school-complete-guide/). Adorno, though not yet formally of the Institute, moved in its orbit, published in its journal, the Zeitschrift für Sozialforschung, and found in Horkheimer a collaborator of a kind rarer than friendship: a man whose administrative genius and philosophical ambition were both placed, for decades, at the service of a shared project. When the National Socialists came to power in 1933, the Institute, endowed by a Jewish family and staffed largely by scholars of Jewish descent or Marxist conviction or both, emigrated as a body, first to Geneva and then to New York. Adorno's own position was more exposed and more ambiguous. His teaching licence was revoked under the regime's racial legislation, since his father was of Jewish birth, yet he was not in the first wave of emigration, and he attempted for a time to sustain a musical career inside Germany under worsening conditions, an episode his biographers treat, correctly, as a miscalculation born of the universal failure to imagine how far the regime would go. In 1934 he left for Oxford, registering at Merton College as an advanced student, and began the long exile that would take him, by way of England, to the United States and to the strangest and most productive collision of his life: the collision between critical theory and American empirical research.

## Exile in England, and the approach to America

The Oxford years, from 1934 to 1937, were fallow in appearance and fertile in fact. He worked on a manuscript against Husserl that would not be published for two decades, the studies toward a metacritique of epistemology, in which the phenomenology of his dissertation was turned against its own claim to found knowledge on pure givenness. He visited Frankfurt under the lengthening shadow, watched the space for any life resembling his old one contract to nothing, and in 1937, in London, he married Gretel Karplus, a chemist from Berlin who had taken her doctorate and built a career in a glove factory's laboratory and management, and who became, for the remaining thirty-two years of his life, the still centre of it: first reader, manager of the practical world, and eventually, with Rolf Tiedemann, the editor of his unfinished last book. The marriage is a fact of the biography rather than a theme of the work, but its stability underwrites the work's existence, through exile, penury that was never quite penury, fame that was never quite fame, and the return to a country that had murdered the world they were born into.

The Oxford position was, formally, a curious one: a scholar of thirty one with a German doctorate and a Habilitation, enrolled as an advanced student because the English university had no shelf for his qualifications, working in the Merton library on the Husserl manuscript by day and writing for the emigré and Viennese musical press by night. He crossed the Atlantic in 1937 to inspect the Institute's American situation at first hand, spent weeks in New York in Horkheimer's circle, and returned to England persuaded, before the appointment existed, that his future lay across the ocean rather than in the Oxford fellowship that never materialised. The marriage to Gretel Karplus in London that September settled the personal side of the equation; the invitation to the Princeton project, arriving within months, settled the professional side, and the couple sailed for New York in February 1938. The English sojourn thus stands in the biography as an antechamber, three years of suspended status in which the former Privatdozent learned the exile's essential curriculum: that credentials are local, that a reputation does not cross a border unaccompanied, and that the work must be capable of beginning again from nothing. The lesson is inscribed in the prose's later suspicion of every inherited authority, including, in the end, his own.

He also used the English years to keep his musical journalism alive and to think, harder than before, about what the new media were doing to listening. Radio was no longer a novelty; it was becoming the acoustic environment of whole nations, and the young critic who had written about the gramophone record as early as 1927, about its needle curves and the shrinking of musical time, understood earlier than most that the question of music's future would be decided not in the concert hall but in the technology of distribution. That understanding is the bridge to America. When Horkheimer, established in New York with the Institute now housed at Columbia University, invited him in 1938 to direct the music study of a large radio research project at Princeton, funded by the Rockefeller Foundation and directed by the Viennese émigré sociologist Paul Lazarsfeld, Adorno accepted a post that placed his method inside an institution organised by the opposite method. The encounter is usually told as a comedy of mutual incomprehension, the mandarin theorist against the cheerful quantifiers. It was that. It was also one of the most consequential methodological collisions in twentieth-century social science, and its consequences run through everything he wrote afterward about the culture industry, standardisation and the regression of listening.

## The radio project: critical theory meets administrative research

The Princeton Radio Research Project was a landmark of the new American social science: foundation money, a programme of studies on radio's effects, and a director, Lazarsfeld, who was building the discipline of empirical communication research with extraordinary entrepreneurial energy. Lazarsfeld's own later account of their collaboration is generous and bemused in equal measure; he had hired Adorno, he wrote, for the music study, and discovered a collaborator who regarded the project's basic instruments, the questionnaire, the listener panel, the programme analyser that recorded likes and dislikes second by second, as symptoms of the very condition he had come to study. Adorno's position was not obscurantism. It was a principled claim about method, stated in his memoranda with increasing clarity: that to ask listeners what they like, and to aggregate the answers, presupposes that liking is a fact about persons rather than a product of the apparatus that delivers the music, trains the taste and then consults it. The administered questionnaire, in his view, could register the effects of standardisation but could not perceive standardisation itself, because the categories of the instrument were drawn from the world as the industry had already arranged it.

Out of that friction came work of permanent value, most of it unpublished in his lifetime. He drafted a large theoretical manuscript on radio music, analysing the radio voice, the symphony broadcast's compression of dynamic range, the plugging of songs, the way repetition under commercial conditions converts familiarity into a substitute for quality. He wrote the essay on the fetish character in music and the regression of listening, published in the Institute's journal in 1938, which argued that listening itself was being retrained by the market: from structural hearing, in which a listener follows a work's internal logic, toward a distracted, atomised consumption of quotable bits, star performances and trademark sounds. And with the young researcher George Simpson he produced the study of popular music published in 1941, in which the paired concepts of standardisation and pseudo-individualisation received their classic statement: the products are built to a schema so rigid that the details can be interchanged, and then dressed with cosmetic differences, a vocal mannerism, a novel orchestration, whose function is to let the consumer experience the identical as a choice. The full theory of the culture industry that these studies fed belongs to its own article in this series, [the culture industry explained](/2015/07/15/culture-industry-explained/), and is deliberately not reproduced here. What belongs to the profile is the biographical yield. America taught Adorno what his enemy looked like at its most advanced. Germany had shown him fascism; America showed him the market's softer administration of consciousness, and his mature work is unintelligible unless both lessons are kept in view at once.

### Why did the Princeton radio project frustrate him?

The project measured radio's effects by asking listeners what they liked, aggregating the answers into data. He held that the liking itself was manufactured by the apparatus being studied, so the instruments could register everything except the one thing that mattered: the standardisation that produced the preferences the questionnaires so politely recorded and returned as findings.

The collaboration ended in 1941 with the music study defunded and its director unrepentant. Lazarsfeld, to his credit, never treated the episode as a simple failure, and the two men's later published exchange, in which Lazarsfeld coined the distinction between administrative and critical research to describe their difference, remains the most civilised account ever given of a methodological divorce. Adorno, for his part, carried the experience into his methodological conscience. He never again dismissed empirical research as such; the postwar Frankfurt Institute under his direction ran empirical studies of groups, of political attitudes, of the authoritarian character's German afterlife, and he defended the survey and the interview as instruments. What he refused, permanently, was the innocence of instruments: the assumption that a method is a neutral window rather than a second object, itself shaped by the society under study, requiring the same critical scrutiny as any text. American colleagues who met the refusal as arrogance missed its origin. It was not the arrogance of a European mandarin toward a young science. It was the report of a man who had watched a science of effects, funded and framed by the broadcasting industry, study broadcasting for three years and produce, as he saw it, an elaborate documentation of everything except the industry.


The memoranda he left behind in the project's files, gathered after his death into the manuscript he called Current of Music, show how much of the mature theory was drafted at Princeton in the guise of research reports. There is the analysis of the radio symphony, in which the broadcast's compression of the dynamic range and the domestic scale of reception shrink the Beethovenian whole into a sequence of quotable moments, the listener's attention retrained from structure to highlight before the culture industry chapter had a name for the retraining. There is the study of the plugging system, the economy of repetition by which a song is made familiar until familiarity passes for merit, which remains the founding document of a sociology of the hit. And there is the unfinished theory of radio itself, the phantom of the broadcast voice, intimate and authoritative at once, addressing millions as a bedside companion, which anticipates with uncanny precision the media theory of the following century. That all of this was written as internal memoranda for a project whose director could not use it, and filed for thirty years, is the kind of irony his own theory savoured: the critique of the administered world, produced inside the administration, shelved by it, and surviving as its most durable product. Lazarsfeld, who kept the files, deserves the credit the record gives him, the administrator as unwitting archivist of the critique of administration.

## America judged: the exile's verdict on his refuge

The American decade invites a summary judgement of its own, because his published verdict on the country that saved him is so often quoted in its harsh half only. The harsh half is real. He found in the United States the culture industry in its pure state, unresisted by the residues of an older high culture that in Europe still obstructed the circuit; he found the anti-intellectualism of a democracy of consumers, the conformism of the adjusted personality, the transformation of every spontaneity into a marketed preference, and he recorded the findings with an acidity that American readers have never quite forgiven, the more so since the refugee's ingratitude was added to the indictment. But the memoir of his American years that he composed in 1968, the scientific experiences of a European scholar in America, tells the second half with equal explicitness, and it surprises readers who know only the slogans. He defends the American university against the European mandarin's contempt, praises the absence of feudal deference in intellectual life, the readiness of American colleagues to test an idea by its yield rather than by its pedigree, and records his gratitude for the empirical disciplines that had humiliated his first memoranda and improved his thought. The exotic, he concluded, was not America but Europe's image of America, and the exile who had arrived with the image left with the experience.

The double verdict is, once more, the method keeping faith with the object against the concept, including the concept of his own theory. A cruder dialectician would have made America the simple confirmation of the culture industry thesis. He made it the thesis's hardest case and its partial correction, a society whose administered culture coexisted with a democratic informality and an institutional generosity to refugee scholars that the old world, for all its cathedrals of high culture, had withdrawn at the decisive moment. The exile's private letters complete the picture with its comedy: the Frankfurt dialectician reporting home on Hollywood premieres and desert drives, on the citrus trees and the impossibility of walking anywhere, homesick and fascinated in the same sentence. Neither the acidity nor the gratitude should be excerpted without the other. Together they describe an intelligence doing, in public, the thing it demanded of everyone else: refusing to let a whole be summed by its worst province or its best.

## The jazz essays: the honest verdict

It is time to keep the promise made at the outset. Adorno's writings on jazz, principally the essay published in the Institute's journal in 1936 under the pseudonym Hektor Rottweiler, and the jazz pages of the fetishism essay and later occasional pieces, are the weakest sustained work he produced, and the criticism they have attracted over the decades is, in its main lines, justified. A profile organised around admiration for his method has more reason than most, not less, to state this plainly, because the failure is instructive: it is the failure of his own method when the method is not practised, when the constellation is replaced by a verdict brought ready made to an object the thinker did not stay with long enough to hear.

Consider first what he was actually listening to. The jazz available to a German critic in the nineteen thirties, on records, on radio, and in the dance halls and revue theatres of Berlin and Frankfurt, was overwhelmingly the commercial dance-band music of the period: arranged, sweetened, rhythmically regularised for social dancing, performed largely by white European and American orchestras whose product was designed for the entertainment market he was analysing. The great Black American tradition, the New Orleans and Chicago masters, the Kansas City bands, the music that later listeners mean, without qualification, when they say jazz, reached Germany thinly, late and in fragments, through a distribution system his own theory would have predicted to filter exactly that way. He heard some of it; the record shows references to Armstrong and to Ellington, the latter granted a kind of condescending exception. But the centre of the tradition, its improvisatory discipline, its blues grammar, its communal virtuosity, was not the object in front of him when he wrote. The object in front of him was the Schlager's syncopated cousin, and about that object some of his observations were acute.

They were acute, and they do not save the essays. Three charges stand. The first is that he generalised from the commodity to the form: because the commercial product was standardised, he concluded that jazz as such was standardisation, its syncopation a mechanical ornament laid over an unaltered beat, its improvisation a stock of interchangeable formulae, its hot passages pseudo-individual decoration on an invariant chassis. Applied to the dance bands, the description often fits. Applied to a Parker solo, to take the case that history supplied within a few years of his American arrival, it is not a description at all but a refusal to listen, since the solo's whole being consists in the non-interchangeable invention the theory declared impossible. The second charge is that he did not do the work his own method required. The musical method, as this profile has described it, demands immanent attention: descend into the particular, follow the relations, let the object correct the category. The jazz essays proceed in the opposite direction, from category to object, and the tell-tale sign is the prose, which lacks the loving concreteness, the bar by bar patience, that distinguishes his writing on Berg or on Beethoven. He analysed jazz the way his worst critics would later analyse him: from outside, by schema, with the conclusion in the luggage. The third charge concerns tone. The essays are not merely wrong about much of their object; they are contemptuous, and the contempt is aimed downward, at the pleasures of dancers and radio listeners, in a register he would have recognised as ideology in anyone else's prose. The pseudonym under which the main essay appeared has sometimes been read as his own half acknowledgment that the piece stood apart from his standards.

### What was Adorno actually hearing when he wrote about jazz?

Mostly the commercial dance-band music of nineteen thirties Europe: arranged, sweetened, rhythmically regular for social dancing, filtered through records and radio. The central tradition, New Orleans, Chicago, Kansas City, the improvising masters, reached Germany thinly and late. His sharpest observations fit that commodity. His generalisations about the form exceeded everything his evidence could carry.

What can be said in partial mitigation should be said once, and then left. The analytical core of the jazz essays, the claim that the entertainment industry standardises its product and decorates the standard with pseudo-individual touches, was developed simultaneously in the popular music study, where its object really was the industrial product, and there it remains a permanent contribution, the ancestor of every serious critique of formulaic production since. His error was jurisdictional: he extended a valid theory of an industry into a judgement on a musical tradition the industry was simultaneously exploiting and excluding, and he lacked, or declined, the curiosity that would have taken him to the clubs and the race records where the tradition actually lived. There is also a darker contextual note that biographers record and that explains without excusing: in the Germany he had fled, jazz was being denounced by the regime as degenerate and racially alien, and some of his polemic reads as an attempt, perverse in outcome, to refuse the völkisch terms by agreeing that the commodity was debased while denying the regime's racial explanation of the debasement. The attempt failed. A Marxist who finds himself, on any question of taste, sounding like the philistines has a duty his own dialectic imposed: to suspect his premises. On jazz, uniquely, he did not suspect them enough.

Nor did he revisit the verdict with fresh ears when the evidence changed around him. The essay of 1953 on the perennial fashion of jazz, published in Prisms, returned to the subject after a decade and a half in America, after bebop had remade the music's harmony and rhythm in clubs a taxi ride from his Los Angeles desk, and substantially repeated the judgements of 1936, now dressed as a theory of fashion: jazz as the eternal return of the same under the mask of the new. The essay is sometimes cited by his defenders as proof that the position was considered rather than ignorant. It is better cited as the record of a refusal to reconsider, the one place in the corpus where the thinker who taught that the object must correct the concept declined to let an object, which had in the interval produced Charlie Parker, correct his verdict. In the typology of listening published nine years later, the jazz expert appears as a type, granted a connoisseur's knowledge and denied structural hearing in the same breath, a classification that tells the reader more about the classifier's formation than about the classified music. The failure propagates in one direction only, fortunately. It damages the music sociology's lower storeys and the man's reputation for fairness. It does not touch the analyses in which the method was genuinely practised, and the honest profile quarantines it there, named and bounded, where it belongs.

The mature music writing tells, by contrast, what the method could do when it stayed with its object. The Philosophy of New Music, written in exile and published in 1949, reads Schoenberg and Stravinsky as the two poles of modern composition, progress and restoration, with a technical intimacy no philosopher of the century matched. The book on Wagner, drafted in the late nineteen thirties and published in 1952, anatomised the leitmotiv as an early form of the advertising jingle, an identification tag for listeners who must be spared the effort of following development, and thereby founded a way of reading the nineteenth century's greatest opera composer as a prophet of the culture industry he never saw. The essays on Beethoven, on Mahler, on Berg himself, collected in the volumes of musical writings that grew all his life, remain, for working musicians, the striking fact about him: that a philosopher wrote about their art as one of them, with the score open. None of that redeems the jazz essays. It does establish that the failure was local, a district where formation, hierarchy and wounded pride were allowed to overrule method, and the locality of the failure is itself a finding about the man: his intelligence was not a uniform substance but a discipline, and where the discipline was not applied, the intelligence performed no better than anyone else's.

## Standardisation, not popularity

The jazz verdict licenses a correction of the largest popular misreading of Adorno, the belief that his objection to mass culture was an objection to its popularity, to the fact that ordinary people enjoyed it. The objection, stated carefully, is almost the reverse. His target was never the audience and never the mere fact of wide appeal. His target was a mode of production: the industrial manufacture of cultural goods to a schema, in which the whole is fixed in advance and the parts are permitted only the appearance of life, so that the consumer's pleasure is administered back to him as the feeling of choice among equivalents. Popularity enters the analysis only as the industry's alibi, the claim that because millions buy the product, the product must be what millions want, a claim his radio years had taught him to hear as the apparatus congratulating itself on the preferences it had installed. A folk song, a music hall turn, a dance tune made by its performers for its occasion, could be as popular as anything and fall outside the critique entirely, because nobody had standardised it in a factory of culture. Conversely a string quartet manufactured by formula for a prestige market falls inside the critique, whatever its ticket price. The distinction is structural, not evaluative, and half the polemics against him dissolve when it is observed; the other half, which concern the tone and the jazz and the narrowness of the canon he defended, remain standing, and this profile leaves them standing.

### What separates standardisation from popularity?

Standardisation names a way of making: the whole fixed in advance by schema, the parts interchangeable, the differences cosmetic, so that choice among products conceals identity of product. Popularity names only a fact of reception: many people take pleasure in a thing. His critique addresses the factory, not the crowd that the factory supplies and quotes as its excuse.

There is a corollary that his defenders underuse and his critics rarely grant. If the objection is to standardisation, then the defence of difficult art in his work is not a defence of difficulty as a social marker, a shibboleth of the educated, though it often functioned socially in ways he was too honest not to half know. It is a defence of the artworks in which, as he judged, the parts had not been pre-digested by the whole: the late Beethoven quartets, the Berg operas, the Beckett plays and the Kafka novels he championed, works whose every detail must be earned by attention because their construction refuses to do the listener's work in advance. Such works, on his account, are the storage form of an experience the administered world is organised to prevent: the experience of something that is not already known, classified and priced. One may reject the canon, or the metaphysics of experience behind it, or both. One should at least reject them as what they are, a theory of production and attention, and not as what they are not, a distaste for the crowd. The crowd, in his bleakest formulations, is not the villain of the analysis but its principal victim, trained out of the very capacities whose exercise the difficult work preserves. Whether that is a democratic sentiment in aristocratic dress or an aristocratic sentiment with a democratic alibi is the genuine question his work leaves open, and it returns, at full strength, in the objection section below.


A late clarification belongs to this section, because it removes the excuse most often offered for misunderstanding him. In a radio lecture of 1963, published as the résumé on the culture industry, he explained the terminology itself: the draft of the wartime book had spoken of mass culture, and the authors replaced the phrase with culture industry precisely to exclude, from the start, the interpretation favourable to the industry's defenders, the interpretation that the culture in question arises spontaneously from the masses themselves. The substitute phrase was chosen to say that the consumers are not the subjects of the process but its objects, worked on by a machinery in which production, distribution and the engineering of demand form a single circuit. Critics who accuse the theory of despising the audience therefore have the theory's own philology against them, and the accusation has to be relocated to its proper address, which is not the doctrine but the tone, where this profile has already lodged it. The same lecture concedes, with a dryness that approaches wit, that the theory's totalising manner invites misreading as a counsel of despair, and declines to soften the manner on that account, on the ground that the partial truth, prettified, is the whole untruth's favourite disguise. It is as close as he came to writing his own user's manual, and readers daunted by the wartime book are directed to it as the author's own authorised summary, a genre he otherwise refused on principle.

## Philosophy of New Music: the dialectic in the material

The jazz section ended by pointing to the book in which the method, applied to music he revered, achieved its most concentrated result, and the book deserves its own account. Philosophy of New Music was drafted in the early nineteen forties, in the Los Angeles exile, and published in 1949; it consists of two large essays, on Schoenberg and on Stravinsky, held in deliberate, unresolved tension. The Schoenberg essay argues that the emancipation of the dissonance and the twelve-tone method represent the truth of the musical material at its historical stage: the inherited stock of tonal gestures had exhausted itself, its cadences had become lies the ear could no longer believe, and Schoenberg's severity was the honesty of a composer who accepted the material's own verdict. But the essay is dialectical where a manifesto would be simple, because it follows the method into its own danger: twelve-tone technique, born as liberation of every note from hierarchy, hardens into a new domination of the whole over the parts, a row administered like a plan, freedom converting itself into its opposite at the moment of its triumph. Readers of the Dialectic of Enlightenment will recognise the figure instantly. It is the same figure, the reversal of emancipation into administration, traced in the inner history of musical technique rather than in the history of reason, and it confirms what this profile has claimed from the start: the philosophy and the music analysis are one thought in two registers, each supplying the other's evidence.

The Stravinsky essay is the polemic, and it has worn less well in its verdicts than in its perceptions. Stravinsky stands for restoration: the great composer as archaist, borrowing the masks of dead styles, sacrificing the subject's expression to a collective objectivity that Adorno, writing in the shadow of the European catastrophes, heard as the aesthetic rehearsal of authoritarian submission, the individual dissolved in the ritual. The reading of the scores is, again, formidably exact; nobody has described the rhythmic mechanism of The Rite of Spring, its blows and its petrifications, with more penetration. The judgement built on the reading is another matter. It underrates the irony that saturates the borrowed styles, the intelligence of the mask as mask, and it enforces the Vienna hierarchy at exactly the point where the hierarchy becomes a Procrustean bed, measuring a major composer by his refusal of a path he never sought. Later musicology has largely reversed the pairing's sympathies, and Adorno himself, in the nineteen sixties, conceded ground in an essay on Stravinsky that stands as a partial palinode. A profile records both facts without embarrassment. The book remains the century's most consequential philosophy of music precisely because its errors are the errors of a method that commits itself, in public, to the object, and can therefore be corrected by the object, as its author, late and partially, corrected this one.

## Beethoven, Mahler, and the recovery of the nineteenth century

The musical method's most beautiful results came not with the moderns he championed but with the classics he inherited, and two figures dominate that recovery. Beethoven occupied him for thirty years in fragments, sketches and essays toward a book on Beethoven that he never assembled; the fragments, published after his death, show a reading of the middle and late works as the bourgeois era's self knowledge in sound, the sonata's drama of theme and development heard as the individual subject's career, its triumphs audited in the late quartets, where the conventions of the form survive as quotations of themselves and the music's truth migrates into the cracks of a disintegrating whole. The concept of late style that has since become a commonplace of criticism, applied far beyond music, descends from these pages: the idea that a master's final works do not reconcile but fracture, leaving the conventions bare and the subject withdrawn, a catastrophe inscribed in form. Mahler, the second figure, received the finished monograph, published in 1960, that Beethoven never did. Mahler's symphonies, built from the worn material of marches, folk tunes, café music and funeral processions, the very stock of the banal, are read as the novel of music: a world assembled from quotations that the composition both honours and breaks, in which the low material is neither ironised from above nor celebrated from below but allowed, in the breaking, to tell the truth about the world that discarded it. The Mahler book is the gentlest Adorno wrote, and the most useful corrective to the caricature of the mandarin, since its whole labour is to demonstrate that profundity and the commonplace are not enemies, that a theme the street whistles can carry, under the pressure of form, the weight that a manufactured sublimity cannot. The critic of the culture industry and the lover of Mahler's barrel organ quotations are the same man, and the sameness is the method's credential.

## Darmstadt, and the aging of the new music

The postwar decades gave the musician a second institutional life that profiles of the philosopher routinely omit. At the Darmstadt summer courses, the annual gathering where the European musical avant garde rebuilt itself after the war, Adorno lectured from the early nineteen fifties onward to the generation that defined postwar composition: Boulez, Stockhausen, Nono, Ligeti in its orbit, the young men for whom the Philosophy of New Music was a charter. His seminars on Beethoven, on the sociology of music, on Vers une musique informelle, the plea of 1961 for a music that would find its form in the act of composition rather than receive it from a system, formed part of the courses' conscience, the reminder inside the avant garde's own citadel that technique is not truth. The reminder became a polemic in the essay of 1955 on the aging of the new music, which committed the scandal of applying his dialectic to his own camp: total serialism, the organisation of every parameter by precompositional systems, was arraigned as the administered world rebuilt inside the stronghold of its opposite, the row become a bureaucracy, the composer a functionary of his own material. The Darmstadt establishment received the essay as treason from the house philosopher, which in a sense it was, and in the sense that mattered to him it was loyalty, the refusal, practised on his friends since the Benjamin letters, to exempt the objects he loved from the criticism he owed them. That the essay's warning reads now as the period's most prescient document on the avant garde's institutionalisation is the usual epilogue of his polemics, and it should be entered in the record beside the jazz essays as their antitype: here the method was practised, on the home ground, against the home team, and the verdict has worn accordingly.

## Schoenberg: the master he never studied with

A precision is owed here, because the loose phrase that Adorno was Schoenberg's pupil has attached itself to the biographies and it blurs the most interesting feature of the relationship. He never studied with Schoenberg. His teachers were Berg, Schoenberg's pupil, and Steuermann, Schoenberg's pianist, and the chain of transmission matters because Adorno's position in the school's civil wars was that of the second generation's theorist rather than the founder's disciple: close enough to the source to claim its authority, free enough of the personal bond to reinterpret the inheritance against the founder's own intentions. Schoenberg, for his part, received the brilliant young advocate with a suspicion that ripened into dislike. He distrusted interpreters on principle, suspected the Philosophy of New Music of annexing his life's work to a philosophy he had not commissioned, and observed acidly that he had never learned anything from the book's authorial apparatus. The famous chill between the two men, conducted across the Los Angeles exile in which both were stranded, is usually told at Adorno's expense, and the telling is half just: the younger man's deference was real and was never rewarded.

Yet the advocacy stands as one of the century's consequential acts of criticism. At the moment when Schoenberg's music was dismissed, in America nearly as completely as in Germany, as cerebral construction without expressive life, Adorno's reading insisted on the opposite: that the twelve-tone works were the most expressive music of the age precisely because their construction refused to counterfeit an expressiveness the age had forfeited, that the method was not a system imposed on feeling but feeling's last honest form. The reading armed two generations of performers and composers, Boulez and the Darmstadt school among them, even where they cursed the armorer, and it fixed the terms in which the Second Viennese School is still discussed. Adorno's late essay on the difficult Schoenberg, written after the founder's death, performs the final turn of the dialectic he had practised on every revered object since Kierkegaard: the master honoured, the master's self understanding corrected, the work defended against the intentions of its maker by the pupil he never accepted. It is the musical method in its purest biographical instance, loyalty and insubordination in a single gesture, and no account of the formation is complete without it.

The devotion to Berg outlasted the apprenticeship by four decades and produced, at the very end, the most personal of his books: the monograph on Berg published in 1968, subtitled the master of the smallest link, in which the old pupil anatomised the teacher's art of transition, the art of the smallest connective passage raised to the dignity of the whole, with a tenderness and a technical command that no other writer on Berg has approached. The book is the autobiography the author never wrote, displaced into the analysis of the man who had taught him what a transition is, and its pages on the Violin Concerto, that requiem built from a chorale and a folk tune, show the method and the gratitude in a single movement. He was sixty-five, the lectures were being disrupted, the Aesthetic Theory was unfinished, and he sat down to write, at full length and for the last time, about the music that had formed him. The choice of subject, at that moment, is itself the profile's evidence: when the philosopher's world closed in, he returned to the score, as he had begun.

## Los Angeles, the damaged life, and the book of aphorisms

In 1941 Adorno moved to Los Angeles, joining the colony of German exile that had gathered in Pacific Palisades and Brentwood, Thomas Mann a few streets away, Bertolt Brecht and Hanns Eisler within arguing distance, Schoenberg himself across the city at the university. The California years were, on the surface, improbable: the Viennese composer manqué and Frankfurt dialectician sunning himself, badly, in the capital of the entertainment industry he was anatomising, dependent on Institute funds and research contracts, collaborating with Eisler on a study of film music, and sitting for the research programme on anti-Semitism whose largest fruit, The Authoritarian Personality, appeared in 1950 from the Berkeley group with which he worked. Beneath the surface they were the most productive years of his life. Two books of the first rank belong to them, and a third, the one by which most readers know him, was written at a desk within sight of the Pacific he never learned to love.

The first of the two, written with Horkheimer, was the Dialectic of Enlightenment, circulated in mimeograph in 1944 and published in Amsterdam in 1947. It argued that the Enlightenment project of liberating reason from myth had reversed into its opposite: reason narrowed to calculation and control, enlightenment itself becoming a mythology that administers the world it promised to free, with the culture industry chapter supplying the analysis of mass culture as the system's smiling face. That book is treated at length in its own study in this series, [the dialectic of enlightenment guide](/2015/07/01/dialectic-of-enlightenment-guide/), and the disambiguation discipline of this profile is to say what a profile must and no more: that the co-authored book's cold grandeur is unthinkable without the Los Angeles vantage, the direct, daily inspection of a civilisation in which amusement had become an industry and the industry had become an environment. The second book is his alone, and it is the door through which most readers should enter.

Minima Moralia, subtitled Reflections from Damaged Life, was written between 1944 and 1949 and published in 1951. He composed it for Horkheimer's fiftieth birthday and then continued it past the occasion into a book of some one hundred and fifty three aphorisms in three dated parts, the years of their writing standing at the head of each part like entries in a log. Its form is its argument. Against the philosophical treatise, whose system he held to be complicit with the administered totality it mirrored, he set the fragment: short prose pieces, from a paragraph to a few pages, on gifts and tact, on hotel rooms and door handles, on the impossibility of dwelling, on generosity, on the etiquette of a life conducted among ruins, each fragment exact, each self sufficient, none subordinated to a master argument that would betray them by explaining them. The title invokes and inverts the Magna Moralia of the Aristotelian tradition: great ethics shrunk to the smallest scale, morality sought not in doctrine but in the micrology of everyday conduct, because in a false whole the truth, if it survives anywhere, survives small. Its most famous sentence, that wrong life cannot be lived rightly, is not a counsel of despair but a refusal of the consolation that private decency can exempt anyone from a damaged totality. It is also, not incidentally, the most readable great book of philosophy of its century, the place where the musical method's virtues, the aphorism as chamber music, the detail carrying the whole, operate without the defensive fortifications of the systematic works.

### Why is Minima Moralia written in fragments?

Because its subject is a whole too damaged to be mirrored by a system. The aphorism lets each observation, a greeting, a gift, a hotel corridor, stand in its own light, exact and unsubordinated, the way a chamber movement holds a single texture without symphonic machinery. The fragment is not modesty. It is the form honesty takes under his premises.

The aphorisms themselves deserve a slower inventory than profiles usually allow, because the book's reputation for quotable gloom conceals its architecture. The dedication to Horkheimer speaks of a melancholy science and of the teaching of the good life withdrawn into the observation of the damaged one, and the three parts, dated 1944, then 1945 to 1947, then 1949, trace the arc from the war's end through the freezing of the postwar settlement. Certain pieces have become canonical for good reason. The meditation on dwelling argues that the modern house, the hotel, the functional flat have made genuine habitation impossible, and that the honest person lodges in the knowledge of that impossibility rather than in the kitsch of restored interiors. The piece on gifts watches the act of giving decay under exchange into strategy and embarrassment, and salvages, from the decay, an ethics of attention. The reflection on tact elevates a dying social grace into a model of cognition itself: tact as the capacity to approach another without either intrusion or indifference, to know the distance a person requires, which is precisely the relation his philosophy demands toward every object. Running beneath the famous pieces is a stratum of autobiography unusual in his work, the childhood at Amorbach, the mother and the aunt, the memory of a bourgeois world whose destruction the book neither mourns in the manner of nostalgia nor celebrates in the manner of progress. Nostalgia and progress are, for the exile writing these pages, the same lie in two tenses, and the aphorism, which refuses to develop, which simply exhibits, is his defence against both. That is why the book, alone among his works, has never needed a defence of its difficulty. Its difficulty is the difficulty of experience itself, distributed into pieces small enough to be honest.

The exile books posed the question that shadowed the rest of his career, the question of register. The German works, written in a German of extreme density and deliberate syntactic resistance, and the American projects, written to the specifications of foundations and journals, differ in more than language, a difference the FAQ of this profile takes up directly. What matters here is that Los Angeles fixed the mature Adorno: the thinker of the administered world, of enlightenment's self destruction, of the damaged life and its micrology, a Marxist whose Marxism had survived the loss of every expected agent of redemption and had converted itself, in consequence, from a philosophy of history into a discipline of attention. The war ended. The Institute's remnant prepared its return to a destroyed Frankfurt. And the exile, who had become an American resident of a decade's standing, whose books were appearing in Amsterdam and whose name in Germany was a rumour from before the deluge, had to decide whether the return was a homecoming or a second exile. He decided, with Horkheimer, to go back.

## The Berkeley study: the authoritarian character, measured

One exile enterprise stands apart from the books and complicates, usefully, the portrait of the anti-empirical mandarin: The Authoritarian Personality, published in 1950, the large collaborative study of prejudice directed from Berkeley in which Adorno served as a leading author alongside the psychologists Else Frenkel-Brunswik, Daniel Levinson and R. Nevitt Sanford. The study's instrument, the F-scale, attempted to measure the potentially fascist character structure by indirect questionnaire items, on the hypothesis that ethnocentrism, submission to authority, aggression toward out groups and a cluster of related dispositions form a syndrome rooted in character rather than in opinion, and therefore detectable beneath the surface of respectable answers. The Institute's anti-Semitism research programme, of which the study formed part, was for Adorno no excursion into an alien discipline. It was the empirical counterpart of his theoretical conviction that fascism had not been an opinion adopted by voters but a deformation of the person, prepared in the family, the school and the workplace, and that a critique which could not describe the deformity in measurable instances had not finished its work.

His own contributions to the volume, the long interpretive chapters on the interview material, show the musical method operating inside an empirical machine without surrendering to it. Where the scales counted, he listened: to the interviewees' clichés, to the slips where the stock phrase failed and something personal, frightened or tender broke through, to the difference between the convinced ideologue and the merely conventional respondent mouthing a script. The distinction he drew there, between the person who has made prejudice his own and the person who performs it as social small change, anticipates his postwar German studies and remains live in prejudice research. The study attracted methodological criticism, some of it justified, on acquiescence bias in the scale's construction and on the pathologising frame; Adorno, characteristically, absorbed the technical criticism more readily than the philosophical, conceding instrument faults while defending the syndrome's reality. The episode matters to the profile for a simple reason: the man who had collided with Lazarsfeld over the innocence of instruments spent his American decade helping to build one, and the collision and the construction together mark the real position, which was never that measurement is ideology, but that measurement without theory is blind in the specific way that serves the existing order, by counting its symptoms and certifying them as nature.

## Doctor Faustus, and the quarrel over a novel

The strangest literary episode of the exile involves a novel he did not write. Thomas Mann, composing Doctor Faustus between 1943 and 1947 in the same Pacific Palisades streets, constructed his hero, the composer Adrian Leverkühn, as a figure of the German catastrophe, and for the novel's musical substance, the descriptions of works, the theory of strict composition, the twelve-tone method attributed to Leverkühn as his demonic invention, he drew heavily and privately on Adorno, who read drafts, supplied analyses and served, in the novelist's own phrase to others, as his adviser and helper in the musical chapters. Mann's diaries and his later account of the novel's genesis acknowledge the debt handsomely. The acknowledgment came, however, only after a wound. Schoenberg, alive, in Los Angeles, and alert to slights, read the novel as the theft of his method by a fictional syphilitic, and said so in print with fury; Mann added a postscript to later editions recording that the twelve-tone technique was the intellectual property of Arnold Schoenberg, and the two old men glared at one another across the exile's drawing rooms while Adorno stood at the junction of the quarrel, the actual author of much of the prose that had caused it, unmentioned in the first edition and embarrassed in all directions.

The episode is more than gossip, because it displays the method in an unguarded setting. Mann, the great ironist, needed a musical intelligence inside the novel that could think composition from within, and he recognised that the age's supply of that intelligence, in exile and within reach, was the young critic from Frankfurt. The Leverkühn analyses, the reading of the late Beethoven quartets, the account of strict style as both liberation and curse, are Adorno's Philosophy of New Music wearing the mask of fiction, and Mann's genius was to know that the mask improved the circulation of the thought. Adorno's own conduct through the affair, loyalty to Mann, deference to Schoenberg, silence about himself, shows the private man beneath the polemicist: a person of considerable personal courtesy, capable of watching his best ideas travel under two other men's names and of filing the grievance, mostly, under the comedy of exile. Biographers who stress the resentment he did feel, recorded in letters, complete rather than contradict the picture. The thinker of the non-identical had been treated, by literary history, as an identical unit of ghost labour, and his protest, when it came, was aimed at the filing system as much as at the men.

## The exile colony: Brecht, and the arguments of the emigration

The Los Angeles exile was a village, and its arguments belong to the biography because they kept the method exercised on live opponents. Bertolt Brecht, settled in Santa Monica, maintained with Adorno a relation of mutual respect curdled by mutual diagnosis: Brecht regarded the Frankfurt theorists as brilliant emigrés analysing a catastrophe from the safety of endowed desks, and said so in his journals with a playwright's economy; Adorno regarded Brecht's didactic theatre as the committed art whose message could be detached and administered, and said so in print with a dialectician's thoroughness. The two men, who agreed on the enemy and on little else, circled each other for years through intermediaries, Benjamin's ghost presiding over the non acquaintance, since Benjamin had been Brecht's collaborator and Adorno's intimate and the triangle had never resolved while its third point lived. The exile's other households supplied gentler material. The salon of Salka Viertel in Santa Monica, the screenwriter hostess whose Sundays gathered the emigration's musicians and writers, gave the theorist of the culture industry his weekly seat inside its human reality, among the studio employees and refugee virtuosi whose company complicated, as company does, the abstractions of the working week. He was not a recluse in exile, whatever the later caricature requires. He was a sociable, funny, gossipy member of a doomed and brilliant colony, whose members' posthumous papers keep recording, with a consistency that amounts to evidence, that the mandarin was delightful at lunch.

The colony's great collective experience, the House Un-American Activities Committee's descent on Hollywood in 1947, touched his circle directly, Eisler summoned, Brecht testifying and departing for Europe within days, and Adorno's own position, a resident alien with a Marxist bibliography, was precarious in ways the published work never advertises. His response to the climate was characteristic: no public heroics, no recantations, a quiet renewal of his teaching and research posts, and the completion, in those very years, of the aphorisms that anatomise a society's demand for loyalty oaths of the soul. The profile records the episode because it completes the American education. He had now observed, at close quarters and in sequence, a fascist state, a liberal market democracy, and that democracy's machinery of suspicion, and the mature work's refusal to rank the administered worlds into a simple good and evil is the deposit of the sequence. Lesser exiles chose a camp. He chose the analysis, and the choosing is the politics his critics found missing.

## Film music with Eisler, and the suspicion of the screen

The film work of the exile years is the least read of his American productions and the most prophetic. Composing for the Films, published in 1947 under Hanns Eisler's name alone, with Adorno's authorship restored only in the German edition of 1969, grew out of a Rockefeller funded project on film music in which the two exiles, the pupil of Schoenberg and the pupil of Berg, subjected the Hollywood score to the analysis their training made inevitable. The book's findings read like a checklist of everything the industry preferred not to know about itself: the leitmotiv degraded into a name tag for characters the audience might otherwise have to watch, the illustrative doubling in which the music tells the viewer what the image is already showing, the standardised moods laid under dialogue like carpet, the whole apparatus arranged so that the ear is spared every effort and the film's shocks arrive pre-digested. Against the practice the book sets a counter programme drawn from the new music: music that comments rather than illustrates, that contradicts the image where contradiction tells the truth, that treats the film as material for composition rather than as a customer for upholstery. Eisler's practical experience in the studios and Adorno's theory of listening fuse in the book into something rare in either man's bibliography, a reform programme, specific, technical and addressed to practitioners.

His suspicion of the screen itself, as distinct from its music, ran deeper and has worn worse, and the profile should mark the difference. The culture industry chapter of the co-authored book treats film as the paradigmatic medium of the administered consciousness, its realism a training in adjustment, its cutting a drill in the acceptance of the given. In the essay on film transparencies of 1966, however, the older man returned to the medium with a noticeably loosened grip, conceding that film's photographic basis and its collective production harboured possibilities his earlier account had foreclosed, that a cinema which refused illustration and psychology might yet achieve the estrangement he demanded of art. The concession is of a piece with the poetry qualification and with the Stravinsky palinode, and the pattern across all three matters for the portrait: on the objects he had judged earliest and most absolutely, film, Stravinsky, the lyric after the camps, he is found, in the final decade, reopening the file. The dogmatist of the anthologies is hard to square with the documentary record of the revisions, and a reader choosing between the two portraits should choose the record.

## The poetry remark, and the qualification nobody quotes

No sentence of Adorno's has had a stranger career than the one he wrote in 1949, in the essay Cultural Criticism and Society, later collected in the volume Prisms: the sentence rendered in English as the claim that to write poetry after Auschwitz is barbaric. Stripped of its essay, whose subject was the compromised position of cultural criticism itself in a society that had produced the camps, the sentence escaped into the culture as a free standing prohibition, quoted by people who had read nothing else he wrote, deployed as a ban on art after atrocity, as a piece of Germanic presumption, as a slogan for curricula and a target for editorials. It became, in the most literal sense his theory could have wished or dreaded, a culture industry product: a standardised unit of Adorno, endlessly reproduced, its identifying tag intact, its context discarded. The entry in this profile exists to restore the two facts the reproduction discarded. The sentence was written about the barbarism of a culture that could proceed as if nothing had happened, culture critic included in the indictment. And its author returned to it in print, in his major philosophical work of 1966, and qualified it in terms that effectively withdraw the prohibition while keeping the problem it named.

### Did Adorno really say no poetry after Auschwitz?

He wrote in 1949 that writing poetry after Auschwitz is barbaric. In 1966 he returned to the remark and wrote that perennial suffering has as much right to expression as a tortured person has to scream, which withdraws the prohibition while keeping the difficulty it named.

The qualification deserves the slow reading the slogan never gets. In the Meditations on Metaphysics that close Negative Dialectics, he takes up his own sentence as an object, the way his method takes up any object, and subjects it to the pressure of the particular it had flattened. Suffering, the passage argues, is not an abstraction available for prohibition; it is the condition of persons, and its right to expression is as unconditional as the scream of the tortured, which no theory may license itself to forbid. Poetry after Auschwitz had in any case been written, by Paul Celan above all, whose work Adorno knew and honoured, and the existence of the poems refuted the ban in the only court his philosophy recognised, the court of the object itself. What survives the qualification is not a permission slip. It is the difficulty the original sentence was gesturing at with a bluntness that betrayed it: that art after the camps works under a permanent suspicion, the suspicion that form consoles, that beauty arranges, that to give suffering a shape is already to begin making it bearable for the wrong people, the spectators. Every serious artist since has worked inside that suspicion whether they know its source or not. The sentence that named it was a provocation in an essay about criticism's bad conscience; its author, given seventeen years and a major book in which to reconsider, chose correction over consistency, and printed the correction where the specialists would find it and the slogan makers would not.

### Why is the qualification so seldom quoted?

Because the slogan is portable and the qualification is not. A prohibition fits a headline, a syllabus, an editorial; a dialectical correction, which keeps the problem and drops the ban, must be read in its paragraph or not at all. The reproduction of the remark without its retraction is an exact instance of the standardisation he spent his life describing.

The episode belongs in a profile for a further reason: it is the cleanest demonstration available of how to read him, and of how not to. Read as slogan, he is a censor with a famous sentence. Read in the movement of the texts, the sentence of 1949, the reconsideration of 1966, the poems of Celan standing between them as evidence no honest theory could override, he is something more interesting and more demanding: a thinker whose propositions are moments in an argument with himself, whose contradictions are frequently the record of the object correcting the concept, and who must therefore be followed the way a performance is followed, in time, or not understood at all. That requirement is, once again, the musical method, now visible as a demand on the reader rather than a habit of the writer. Critics who find the requirement self serving are entitled to their suspicion. The texts make it anyway.

## Negative dialectics and the ethics of the remainder

The major philosophical statement of the postwar years, Negative Dialectics, appeared in 1966, when its author was sixty-three, and it gathers everything the formation had prepared. Its target is named identity thinking: the operation, so basic to cognition that it usually escapes notice, by which thought identifies an object with the concept under which it files it, and treats the filing as knowledge. To identify a tree with the concept tree, a person with a function, an event with a category, is necessary; no thought proceeds without it. Adorno's claim is that it is never sufficient, and that the insufficiency is not a technical residue but the moral centre of philosophy. Every concept leaves a remainder, the object's own particularity, what it is beyond its classification, and he calls that remainder the non-identical. A civilisation organised by exchange, in which unlike things are made equivalent so they can be traded, trains identity thinking into a social reflex and then into a metaphysics. The camps, in the book's darkest meditations, stand at the end of that road: the reduction of persons to specimens of a category, carried out with the filing system's own serenity. Philosophy's task, as the book defines it, is the disciplined refusal of that serenity: to use concepts, unavoidably, while keeping faith with what the concepts omit, to think against identity from inside identification itself.

### What is the non-identical?

The non-identical is what an object is beyond the concept that classifies it: the remainder every identification leaves behind. Thought cannot proceed without concepts, and concepts cannot exhaust their objects, and he makes that gap the conscience of philosophy. In musical terms it is the note the analysis cannot absorb, sounding on after the system has closed over it.

The book is dialectical in the Hegelian inheritance and negative in its refusal of Hegel's destination. In Hegel, contradiction is the engine that drives thought to a reconciliation in which the opposed terms are preserved and lifted into a higher unity; the system's final chord resolves. Adorno takes the engine and declines the chord. His dialectic moves by determinate negation, the criticism of a concept by its own standards and its object's resistance, but it grants no synthesis the authority to declare the movement finished, because every achieved unity is a new identity claim, and every identity claim reoffends against the non-identical. The result is a philosophy in permanent, principled unrest, whose negativity is not a mood but a method: being negative, in the sense the phrase is so often misunderstood, has nothing to do with complaint. It names the refusal to let the false whole pass itself off as true by adding one's affirmation to it. The whole is the false, runs the aphorism from Minima Moralia, inverting Hegel's dictum that the true is the whole, and negative dialectics is that inversion worked out at the scale of a system that refuses, on principle, to be one.

Why this should be so hard to summarise is a question the FAQ below answers in its own terms; here the biographical point is enough. The method that Vienna taught, the constellation against the system, the detail that resists the whole, the refusal of the easy cadence, had found its final philosophical form, and the form was recognised as such by its author, who called the book, without false modesty, the thing he had to say. Around it the late work arranged itself: the lectures on metaphysics and on moral philosophy, the essays on Beckett and on the endgame of drama, the polemic against the jargon of authenticity in Heideggerian existentialism, and, continuous with all of it, the music writing, which never paused, the Mahler monograph of 1960, the introduction to the sociology of music of 1962, the essays toward a theory of musical reproduction that his students edited after his death. The unity of the late work is the unity of a single ear applied, without respite and without synthesis, to everything the postwar republic put in front of it.

The architecture of Negative Dialectics repays a paragraph of its own, since even sympathetic readers often miss that the book is built like the music its author analysed. After the introduction, which performs the method on the concept of philosophical experience itself, three large movements develop the argument through models, his term for concrete constellations in which the abstract claim must prove itself on resistant material. The first model, freedom, works through Kant's doctrine of the will and the modern experience of unfreedom, arriving at the position that freedom survives, if anywhere, as the capacity to perceive one's own determination, a formulation that stands as far from liberal spontaneity as from determinist resignation. The second model, world spirit and natural history, confronts Hegel's philosophy of history with the thesis that history and nature are not opposites but each other's sediment, that what presents itself as natural is frozen history and what presents itself as historical progress carries nature's blind compulsion inside it. The third movement, the Meditations on Metaphysics, written under the pressure of the camps' aftermath, asks whether metaphysical experience, the experience that life might be more than its mere continuation, remains possible after the failure of every system that promised it, and answers, in the book's most quoted movement, that it survives precisely in the refusal to let the administered world be the measure of the possible. The poetry qualification stands in these meditations, in its proper place, not as a retraction of a slogan but as a load-bearing element of the argument about suffering's right to expression.

The book's reception completed the paradox of his career. In Germany it installed him as the philosophical authority of the era, the name a generation of students cited the way an earlier one had cited Heidegger. In the English speaking world its translation arrived late, in 1973, four years after his death, by which time the slogans attributed to him had long circulated without it. The delay mattered. Anglophone readers met the culture industry chapter and the Minima Moralia in the nineteen sixties and seventies largely through anthologies and secondary accounts, while the systematic statement that disciplined the slogans, that forbade the pessimism attributed to him from hardening into doctrine, waited in German. Much of the English language image of Adorno, the mandarin of despair, is an artefact of that publishing sequence, and the rehabilitation of the subtler figure in later scholarship is, among other things, the story of the translations catching up with the texts. A profile that stresses editions and their dates is not pedantic. In this case the editions are the biography of the reputation.

## The administered world

One phrase from the corpus has passed into general use so thoroughly that its origin is forgotten, and a profile should restore the receipt. The administered world is his name for the social totality of the twentieth century's second half: a world in which the spheres that once stood partly outside the circuit of production and control, education, leisure, the inner life, the countryside, the weekend, have been drawn into administration, planned, supplied and supervised by the interlocking bureaucracies of state, corporation and culture industry, until the space in which a person might be unadministered shrinks to the dimensions of a hobby. The phrase is not a synonym for totalitarianism, and its force depends on the distinction: administration in his usage is the form domination takes when it no longer needs terror, when it rules by provision and by the organisation of needs themselves, so that the administered experience their administration as service and their conformity as preference. The concept gathers the whole career into a single designation. The radio studies watched administration colonising listening; the authoritarian studies watched it forming character in the family; the postwar German essays watched it managing the past. What the concept refuses is the liberal consolation that the private sphere remains a reserve of freedom by definition. The private, in the administered world, is itself administered, decorated with the signs of individuality the way the standardised product is decorated with pseudo individual detail, and the shrinking of the unadministered remainder is the process his micrology tracks, aphorism by aphorism, in the damaged life.

The concept has been charged with totalising exaggeration, and the charge is the standard objection in its most precise form: a world described as seamless administration leaves no seam from which resistance could begin, and the description, however accurate as diagnosis, disarms as politics. His answer, given across the late essays, is that the seams are exactly where his method looks for them, in the non-identical remainders that administration cannot file: the unassimilated artwork, the unadjusted impulse, the memory that refuses the official version, the detail of experience that the questionnaire cannot hold. Administration is total as a tendency, not as an achievement, and the difference between the tendency and the achievement is the space in which thought, art and the occasional refusals of ordinary people continue to occur. Whether that answer is a politics or the description of why he had none is the question the students put to him in the flesh, and the profile has already reported it without adjudication. What can be said for the concept is that the decades since have administered a great deal more of the world in precisely his sense, by provision rather than by terror, and that the phrase he coined for the process remains the shortest accurate name for it.

## Adorno and Freud: the exaggerations that are true

Psychoanalysis runs through the corpus as a second native language, and its place in the formation deserves the record. The young Frankfurt intellectuals of his circle absorbed Freud as they absorbed Marx, as a matter of course; what distinguished Adorno's absorption was its orthodoxy under pressure. Where the neo-Freudians of the exile generation revised the doctrine toward optimism, softening the drives into interpersonal styles and the unconscious into a reservoir of health, he defended the scandalous core: the libido theory, infantile sexuality, the repetition compulsion, the entanglement of civilisation with renunciation that Freud's late cultural writings had stated without consolation. The famous aphorism from Minima Moralia, that in psychoanalysis nothing is true except the exaggerations, is usually quoted as a witticism. It is a methodological charter. The exaggerations, the points where Freud's theory overshoots the evidence of the clinic, are for Adorno the points where the theory registers the truth of the social whole inside the individual case, the exaggeration being the form accuracy takes in a false world. The essay on sociology and psychology of 1955 erected the charter into a programme: the two disciplines must be kept separate precisely so that their antagonism can be thought, the psyche obeying laws that society exploits but does not simply write, the family serving as the agency in which authority is first internalised and therefore the first school of both obedience and refusal.

The clinical edge of this loyalty appears in the authoritarian studies, where the F-scale's items translate Freud's characterology into questionnaire form, and in the late lectures on metaphysics and morals, where the superego returns as the internalised voice of the administered world. It also explains his distance from the therapeutic culture that America was inventing around him in the exile years, the adjustment psychology whose ideal of the healthy personality he read, with Freud's pessimism at his back, as the demand that the patient reconcile himself to the conditions that had made him ill. A psychoanalysis that cures by adaptation had, on his account, simply changed sides. The position cost him allies among the analysts and consistency among his own: the defender of Freud's hardness never underwent a training analysis, kept the couch at the distance of the text, and would have answered, one suspects, that the distance was the homage the theory exacted from a man whose instrument was the concept. Whether the answer suffices is a genuine question for his biographers. The loyalty itself is not in question, and it completes the formation's triad: Marx for society, Freud for the soul, and the Viennese score for the method by which both were read.

## His Marx: what he took, and what he refused

Since this series is a history of Marxism and not only of a man, the profile owes a systematic paragraph on the inheritance. What Adorno took from Marx was, first, the critique of the commodity form as the master key: the analysis, in the opening chapter of Capital, of a social order in which relations between people assume the character of relations between things, and in which equivalence, the exchange of unlike labours as equals, is the founding fiction that is also a fact. Identity thinking, in the late philosophy, is that fiction raised to a metaphysics: the concept's claim to exhaust its object is the exchange principle at work in the mind, and the non-identical is the use value, the particular, that the equivalence discards. Second, he took the method of immanent criticism, the procedure Marx perfected against the political economists, of judging a social order by the standards it professes, freedom, equality, property, and exhibiting the distance between the profession and the practice. Third, he took the refusal of the consolation that ideas float free of the material life that produces them, the insistence that consciousness is socially mediated in its finest fibres, which his sociology of music applies to the last vibrato.

What he refused is equally definite. He refused the philosophy of history that orthodox Marxism had distilled from the inheritance: the sequence of modes of production marching toward a guaranteed destination, the proletariat installed by theory in the role of identical subject object of history, the party as the role's authorised understudy. He refused, with the Soviet experiment in full view, the identification of socialism with the administered state, and his scattered remarks on the Eastern bloc treat its order as a variant of the same administered world, a judgement he shared with few Western Marxists of his prominence and printed without flinching. He refused, finally, the demand that theory justify itself by immediate practice, the demand he met in its gentlest form from Marcuse and in its harshest from the students, and his late address to the German Sociological Congress of 1968, which opened by asking whether the society confronting them was late capitalism or an industrial society indifferent to the name, condensed the refusal and the loyalty into a single performance: the categories of Marx retained because the society had retained the realities, the eschatology discarded because the society had discarded nothing so thoroughly as its promises. The comparison with the classical doctrine is drawn out in full in the study of [critical theory against classical Marxism](/2015/09/01/critical-theory-vs-classical-marxism/), and the profile's task is only to fix the temperamental point: he held the inheritance the way he held a score, as a text to be realised against its own letter where the letter had died, which his orthodox contemporaries called betrayal and his readers have mostly called fidelity.

## The return: Frankfurt, the chair, the second fame

He came back to Germany in the autumn of 1949, to a Frankfurt of rubble and black markets, where the university was rebuilding and the Institute, re-established with municipal and American support, resumed its work under Horkheimer's direction. The return was not inevitable and was not universally understood, by the exiles who stayed in America or by the Germans who received him. His answer, given in essays and interviews across the following years, was characteristically double. Germany was the country of the catastrophe, and also the country whose language he thought in; the United States had saved his life and had also shown him, at Princeton and after, the shape of a future he distrusted. A thinker of the object follows the object, and his object, the damaged culture whose sediment he read better than any living analyst, was here. In 1957 he took the chair of philosophy and sociology at Frankfurt; in 1958 he succeeded Horkheimer as director of the Institute, and held the post for the rest of his life. The Institute under his direction became a centre of empirical as well as theoretical research, conducting the group experiments and attitude studies by which West German society was invited, often against its will, to examine what it had been and what it remained.

The two decades of the return made him, to his own evident ambivalence, a public figure of a kind the exile never was. His radio talks and television appearances carried an idiom of daunting abstraction into ordinary German households; his interventions in the educational debates, his essay on the theory of half education, his broadcasts on working through the past and on education after Auschwitz, made him the Federal Republic's licensed admonisher, a role he performed with courtesy and iron. At the university he was a magnetic lecturer and a demanding Doktorvater, the teacher of a generation of German sociologists and philosophers, among them Jürgen Habermas, his assistant in the late nineteen fifties, whose path away from the master's pessimism toward a theory of communication is traced in the article on [Habermas and the turn from Marx](/2015/09/15/habermas-and-marxism/) in this series. The positivist dispute of the nineteen sixties, opened at Tübingen in 1961 in his exchange with Karl Popper over the logic of the social sciences and prolonged in print for a decade, fixed his public image as the dialectical pole of German social thought, the anti-positivist in chief. It was in these years that the international reception began in earnest, translations multiplying, the name becoming a citation, the works becoming a syllabus. He had become, in his sixties, an institution, and the knowledge of what institutions do to thought was the most Adornian knowledge he possessed.

### What changed when Adorno returned to Frankfurt?

Everything external and nothing internal. The exile became a professor, then director of the rebuilt Institute, a broadcaster heard in ordinary households, the republic's licensed admonisher on education and the unmastered past. The method did not soften for the audience. The radio talks are models of lucidity; the books kept their resistance, and the fame rested on both.

The Institute's empirical programme of those years deserves its own sentences, because it confounds the caricature in the same way the Berkeley study did. The Group Experiment of the early nineteen fifties, in which discussion groups of ordinary Germans were led onto the subjects of guilt, the war and the Jews and their talk analysed for the ideology sedimented in its evasions, produced findings of a bleakness that no armchair could have supplied: the past unmastered, the defences intact, the admissions rehearsed. Adorno's methodological reflections on the study are a small classic of their kind, alert to the difference between what a subject says to a questionnaire and what a group reveals when it thinks it is merely talking. The studies of the nineteen sixties, on political attitudes, on the universities, on the afterlife of the authoritarian syndrome in the republic's classrooms, continued the double programme, scales and listening together, to the end of his directorship. If the Princeton collision had taught him the blindness of instruments without theory, the Frankfurt decades taught the obverse lesson, which he also published: theory without the humiliation of contact with what people actually say becomes a mandarin's game, and he had not returned to Germany to play it.

One shadow falls across the success story, and a profile must let it fall. The Federal Republic he admonished was also the republic that restored, in its judiciary, its universities and its ministries, a striking proportion of the personnel of the regime that had expelled him, and his public role existed by tolerance of a society whose continuities he documented better than anyone. He knew it. The essays on the meaning of working through the past are, among other things, the record of a man auditing the terms of his own employment by the unrepentant. That he stayed, and spoke, and was heard, is the biographical fact; that the hearing changed the society in the ways he demanded is a claim the record supports only in part, and he would have been the first to grade the claim accordingly. The melancholy of the late interviews, the professorial courtesy stretched over an unappeased judgement, is the sound of that partial success, heard by the finest ear of the age, registering precisely how much of the chord was false.

## Working through the past: the essays of the return

The public Adorno of the Federal Republic is unimaginable without one essay of 1959, The Meaning of Working Through the Past, broadcast and then printed, in which he took the republic's favourite phrase for its relation to the Nazi years and submitted the phrase itself to analysis. Working through, he argued, had come to mean drawing a line under the past, an administrative closing of the account, and the drawing of the line was itself a symptom: the past was to be mastered, in the psychoanalytic vocabulary he borrowed from Freud, only in the sense in which a neurosis is mastered, by the defences that keep it operative. The essay's evidence is not rhetorical. It cites the survival of the regime's personnel, the polling on guilt and on the Jews, the rhetoric of a political class that spoke of the catastrophe as a misfortune that had befallen Germany among others, and it draws the conclusion his Institute's group experiment had prepared: that the conditions which produced fascism, the social and characterological soil, persisted beneath the democratic forms, and that democracy's survival therefore depended on an enlightenment about its own preconditions rather than on ceremonies of closure. The companion charge, against a fashionable existentialism of guilt in which everyone confessed in general so that no one need answer in particular, completes the essay's pincer movement.

These essays and broadcasts made him, in the judgement of many contemporaries and of the historians since, one of the architects of the Federal Republic's eventual confrontation with its past, the slow and contested process by which the murder of the European Jews moved from the margin of public memory to its centre. The claim should be filed with the care he taught. The confrontation's other architects were prosecutors, journalists, a younger generation of historians, and eventually the students of 1968, whose accusation against their parents' silence he would have recognised as his own curriculum returned to sender. His specific contribution was lexical and moral at once: he supplied the terms in which evasion could be named as evasion, and he refused, on air and in print, the consolations of the balanced judgement where the balance itself was the evasion. A society gets the admonisher it can tolerate, and the Federal Republic tolerated him because the admonition arrived in the idiom of its highest culture and from a chair of its own university. He knew the bargain's terms and said so, in the melancholy register of the late essays, which is why the public role never inflated into a public vanity.

One document of the public years has had a second career of its own and earns a separate mention. In April 1967, invited to Vienna by the Austrian students' association, he delivered the lecture Aspects of the New Right-Wing Extremism, an analysis of the conditions under which fascist movements recruit in a formally democratic society whose economic and psychological soil remains prepared for them. The lecture, preserved as a recording and published half a century later to a reception no one predicted, argues that the survival of the preconditions matters more than the survival of the parties: monopoly concentration, the fear of social descent, the authoritarian character formed in the old family and the new workplace, and a propaganda technique he anatomises in detail, the pseudo concreteness of the demagogue who never argues a system but exhibits a personality, offers the movement as a spectacle of national renewal, and keeps every commitment vague enough to be all things to the frightened. Listeners who meet the lecture as a period piece usually finish it in a less comfortable mood. Its distinctions, between the fascism of the old style, with its doctrine and its terror, and the newer variant working through democratic forms with democratic slogans, were drawn from the Federal Republic of 1967 and from nothing else; the uses later readers have made of them belong to those readers. For the profile the lecture's importance is biographical and methodological at once. It shows the author of Negative Dialectics, in the last years, doing in public and in plain language the work his theory prescribed, reading a political formation the way he read a score, by its smallest links, and declining, as always, to confuse the diagnosis with a reassurance.

## The teacher: lectures, broadcasts, education after Auschwitz

A side of Adorno that the printed works conceal is the pedagogue, and the concealment is a loss, because the pedagogy is where the mandarin image dissolves fastest. His Frankfurt lecture courses, on aesthetics, on Kant and Hegel, on sociology's classic texts, drew audiences far beyond the enrolled, and the transcripts show a teacher of unusual patience, unfolding the hardest arguments at the speed of the slowest serious listener, returning week after week to the same knot from different sides until the room heard it. The seminars were the forge of a generation: the assistants and doctoral students of the Frankfurt years, Habermas among them, and the visiting Americans and returning exiles who passed through the Institute's rooms, encountered there a standard of reading, the text pressed until it confessed, that marked careers on three continents. And beyond the university stood the broadcasts. His radio talks for the Hessian station, on education, on the taboos of the teaching profession, on the meaning of working through the past, carried the idiom of the Institute into households that would never open Negative Dialectics, and they did so without dilution, by the simple expedient of treating the listener as the adult the argument required.

The most famous of these broadcasts, Education after Auschwitz, delivered in 1966, states its demand in its opening movement: that the premier requirement placed on all education is that Auschwitz not happen again, and that every debate about curricula is idle beside that requirement. The talk then asks the question his whole work had prepared: what forms the character that obeyed, and what formation might produce, instead, the self determining person who can refuse? His answer descends, characteristically, from the abstraction to the nursery and the schoolyard, to coldness, the socialised incapacity to identify with another's suffering, which he names as the substrate the educators must work against, and to the strengthening of self reflection as education's only real content. Critics have called the talk the most humane thing he wrote, and the description, which would have embarrassed him, is difficult to dispute. It is also the document that most plainly refutes the portrait of the aesthete indifferent to practice. Here is a practical programme, addressed to teachers, parents and administrators, derived rigorously from the theory and stripped of the theory's armour, delivered in the plainest prose of his career to the largest audience he ever addressed. The man who would not give the students a slogan gave the schoolteachers a task, and the difference between the two refusals is the whole of his politics in miniature: no identities, no transfigurations of the moment, only the slow formation of persons capable of not complying.

## The partnership with Horkheimer

Behind the individual works stands a collaboration so complete that intellectual historians treat its products as jointly authored even when one name stands on the title page, and a profile must give the partnership its own account. Max Horkheimer, born in 1895, the son of a Stuttgart textile manufacturer, brought to the alliance what Adorno conspicuously lacked: institutional genius, the ability to endow, house, fund and defend a research programme across four decades and three countries, and a philosophical temperament pitched toward the architectonic where Adorno's was pitched toward the fragment. Adorno brought the sharper instrument. From the nineteen twenties, when the two began the conversations that Horkheimer's household in Kronberg hosted like a standing seminar, through the Institute's exile and return, the division of labour held with remarkably little friction for men of such force: Horkheimer set the programme and protected the space, Adorno filled the space with work of a density that the programme alone could never have commissioned, and each supplied, in argument, the resistance the other's thought required. The dedication of Minima Moralia to Horkheimer is the alliance's public monument, and the co-authored Dialectic of Enlightenment is its intellectual one, a book whose authorship is genuinely dual, drafted in conversation across a Los Angeles decade until the voices interleave beyond separation.

The texture of the collaboration is preserved in its settings. In the Frankfurt years the two households met on weekends at Horkheimer's house in Kronberg in the Taunus hills, where the conversation ran for days over the manuscripts in progress, the Institute's politics, the music Adorno played and the books both men were reading, a standing seminar with the character of a family council. In the Los Angeles years the setting shrank to two desks and a daily exchange of drafts, the chapters of the joint book passing back and forth until, as both men testified, neither could assign the sentences. Gretel Adorno and Maidon Horkheimer, the wives, were the collaboration's infrastructure and its first audience, a fact the title pages omit and the letters everywhere confirm. Intellectual history, which prefers its thinkers solitary, has never known quite what to do with a masterpiece of two voices, and the Dialectic of Enlightenment's resistance to attribution is the record's own verdict: the partnership was not a sum of two careers but a third thing, an institution of two persons, which survived exile, triumph and the return because each man regarded the other's judgement as the court in which his own work had first to be acquitted.

The partnership had its shadow side, and honesty in a profile extends to shadows that fall on other people. Horkheimer's caution, his protective instinct toward the Institute and toward his own position in the postwar Federal Republic, repeatedly damped initiatives that Adorno's judgement favoured, most consequentially the publication plans for Benjamin's legacy, where Horkheimer's reluctance and Adorno's editorial zeal pulled against each other for years. Adorno, for his part, accepted a subordination in the Institute's public hierarchy that his work had long outgrown, and accepted it with a loyalty that younger colleagues sometimes read as the alliance's price. Both readings miss the texture visible in the correspondence: two men who had survived the century's worst together and had concluded, not sentimentally but as a finding, that the collaboration was the form in which each did his best work. When Horkheimer retired to Montagnola in Switzerland, the letters between Frankfurt and Ticino continued the seminar by post, and Adorno's succession to the directorship in 1958 changed the letterhead without changing the circuit. The Frankfurt School, as an institution, is unthinkable without either man; as a body of thought, it is the record of their difference, the architect and the musician, arguing one argument for forty years.

## The jargon, the quarrel with Heidegger, and the positivist dispute

The postwar Adorno fought on two fronts at once, and the symmetry instructs. Against the logical empiricist inheritance in social science, the front opened at Princeton and formalised in the positivist dispute of the nineteen sixties, he defended the rights of theory, interpretation and the whole against a method that recognised only the measurable part. Against the existential ontology that dominated postwar German philosophy, the front of The Jargon of Authenticity, published in 1964, he defended the rights of the concept, of mediation and of society against a language he prosecuted as ideology in verbal form. The jargon, his name for the Heideggerian idiom and its theological and literary derivatives, was in his analysis a vocabulary of consecrated words, rootedness, encounter, concern, decision, whose glow of profundity functioned to exempt its speakers from the obligations of argument: the words themselves performed the authenticity they merely named, and the performance sanctified a posture of inward depth that left every external arrangement untouched. The polemic is his most purely enjoyable book and his most purely German one, a connoisseur's demolition conducted with the weapons of style.

The quarrel beneath the polemic was the deepest in German philosophy of the century, because the antagonists agreed on the diagnosis and split on the cure. Heidegger too had arraigned the modern world of calculation, equipment and idle talk; Adorno read the arraignment as a brilliant description whose remedy, the turn to Being and to the rooted self, reinstalled in metaphysical dress exactly the identity thinking, the reverence for what is closest and most one's own, that the description had exposed. His counter claim, developed in Negative Dialectics and in the lectures, is that the primal, the immediate, the authentically owned are themselves products of mediation, and that a philosophy which worships them has mistaken the scar for the skin. The quarrel was never joined in person; the two men never debated, and Heidegger's silence about his admirer turned prosecutor is itself a document of the period. Posterity has distributed its sympathies variously, and the Anglophone reception long treated the polemic as provincial spite. Read now, the Jargon looks less like spite than like an early field manual for a permanent condition: the manufacture of depth effects by vocabulary, a culture industry of the seminar room, which its author's ear, trained on the manufacture of feeling in music, was uniquely built to detect.

## Benjamin, and the argument between friends

No profile of Adorno is complete without the figure who stands at the other pole of his intellectual life, Walter Benjamin: the friend he met in 1923, the writer whose work he championed, funded where he could, criticised with a severity that still startles readers of their correspondence, and mourned from 1940, when Benjamin died at the Spanish border fleeing the Gestapo, with a grief that issued, after the war, in Adorno's editorial labour to bring the friend's writings before the public. Their argument is the most instructive in the Frankfurt School's history because it was conducted between equals in affection and unequals in judgement, and because its object was precisely the matter of this profile: what mechanical reproduction, mass culture and the decay of aura had done to art and to the possibility of experience.

The dispute's famous documents are letters. In March 1936, responding to the artwork essay in which Benjamin greeted the reproducibility of film as a solvent of aura and a school of new perception, Adorno wrote the sentence that defines their difference: both, he said, are torn halves of an integral freedom, to which, however, they do not add up. High art and mass art, the autonomous work and the reproduced commodity, were for him not thesis and antithesis awaiting a celebrant but two poles of a single catastrophe, each marked by the stigmata of the order that produced both, and he refused Benjamin's hope that the newer pole was secretly the progressive one. Two years later, over Benjamin's study of Baudelaire, the criticism sharpened into the charge that the work stood at the crossroads of magic and positivism: motifs assembled by fascination, theory asserted rather than mediated, the materialist invocation doing the work that only immanent analysis could do. The charge was unjust in its severity and correct in its diagnosis often enough to have fuelled seventy years of commentary, and the profile of Benjamin in this series, [walter benjamin and mass culture](/2015/08/01/walter-benjamin-marxism-profile/), gives the other side of the correspondence its due. What belongs here is the character of the critic. Adorno's severity toward the writer he valued most was not rivalry, though it has been read as rivalry by readers who prefer their intellectual history melodramatic. It was the musical method applied to friendship: the particular cherished, the false cadence refused, the friend treated as an object worthy of the full pressure of thought rather than the relaxation of loyalty. One may judge the price too high; the letters cost Benjamin pain in years when pain was plentiful, and Adorno's later remorse is documented. But the argument itself, about whether the new media educate perception or administer it, has outlived both men and structures the debate about every platform built since, which suggests that the severity was in the service of something real.

The friendship had a third document, less quoted than the letters of 1936 and 1938 and in some ways the most revealing, because it concerns the great unfinished work through which posterity chiefly knows Benjamin: the Arcades project, the vast study of nineteenth century Paris assembled from quotations and commentary in the convolutes Benjamin carried into exile. Adorno read the exposés of 1935 and 1939 as the Institute's stipend holder and as the friend charged with the project's welfare, and his responses, admiring, exacting, at moments exasperated, pressed the same demand the Baudelaire letter would press: that the assembled material be mediated by theory rather than exhibited in the faith that the exhibition argues by itself. Benjamin's defenders have read the pressure as the Institute's orthodoxy disciplining a more original mind; Benjamin's own letters answer with a gratitude and a resistance so finely balanced that the correspondence remains the best school in which to learn what intellectual friendship, conducted at full seriousness, actually costs and yields. Adorno's postwar labour for the friend's memory, the edition of the writings of 1955 prepared with his wife, the essays that introduced Benjamin to the German postwar reader, the custodianship of the manuscripts that had survived through the Bibliothèque Nationale and the Institute's own removals, discharged the debt in the only currency he trusted, editorial work. That the two profiles in this series sit side by side is the series' own judgment: the argument was never settled, because it was never meant to be, and each man's account of the other is the indispensable commentary on his own.

## The colleagues: Fromm, Marcuse, and the internal borders

The Frankfurt School is habitually presented as a single doctrine with several signatures, and the profile of its most famous member should correct the presentation from inside, because Adorno's relations with the school's other leading figures define his position as much by friction as by alliance. The rupture with Erich Fromm came earliest and cut deepest into method. Fromm, the Institute's psychoanalyst in its Frankfurt and early exile years, revised Freud toward a social psychology of character types and interpersonal relations; Adorno came to regard the revision as a liquidation, the removal of the drive theory's hardness in favour of an adjustment friendly humanism, and the campaign he conducted in correspondence and in the Institute's internal politics contributed to Fromm's departure at the end of the nineteen thirties. The quarrel fixed a permanent feature of his thought: the defence of Freud's most unpalatable doctrines, the death drive, the primacy of sexuality, the pessimism about civilisation, against every attempt to make psychoanalysis respectable, on the ground that the unpalatable parts were the true ones, the registration in theory of what the administered optimism could not admit. With Herbert Marcuse the relation was warmer and the disagreement, in the end, more public. The two men shared the exile, the Institute and the critique of one dimensional administration avant la lettre; they divided over the political conclusions, Marcuse greeting the liberation movements of the nineteen sixties as the theory's missing subject, Adorno withholding the greeting in the name of everything his work had learned about movements. The exchange of letters in the summer of 1969, in which Marcuse pressed the case of the students and Adorno restated the defence this profile has reported, is the school's internal border drawn in its founders' hand, and its courtesy, two old men refusing to lie to each other for the sake of the obituaries, does both of them honour.

Around these two axes the wider circle arranged itself in degrees of distance from his standard: Franz Neumann and Otto Kirchheimer, the political scientists of the exile, whose analyses of the Nazi state he respected and whose empiricism he thought insufficiently philosophical; Leo Löwenthal, the sociologist of literature, a friend from the Frankfurt youth who survived every quarrel; Friedrich Pollock, the economist and Horkheimer's oldest ally, the Institute's administrator, whose theory of state capitalism supplied the economic frame within which the cultural analyses moved. The profile of one man cannot do the circle justice, and the school's collective article in this series carries the institutional story. What the internal borders establish for this profile is the negative space of the figure: Adorno was the school's extremist of method, the member for whom every dilution, of Freud, of Marx, of the musical material, registered as a falsification, and the extremism, which cost the school its unity and its founder friendships, is the same quality that kept its central texts from dissolving into the period's commonplaces. Schools survive by their moderates. They are remembered by their extremists, and the remembrance is not always unjust.

## No fear of the ivory tower: the public man in the last decade

A few months before his death he gave an interview to Der Spiegel, Germany's news weekly, under the title his answers earned: no fear of the ivory tower. The exchange is the best short introduction to the public Adorno that exists, because the interviewers pressed exactly the charges his reputation carried, remoteness, pessimism, the refusal of practice, and the answers, delivered with a courtesy that does not concede an inch, state the whole position as plainly as he ever stated it. Theory, he told them, is itself a form of practice when it changes consciousness, and the demand that every thought prove itself in immediate action is a demand that thought cease at the moment it becomes inconvenient. He declined the title of the student movement's father and declined, with equal care, to disown the children: the movement had taken from his work what it needed, and a teacher does not grade his influence by its obedience. Pressed on his own situation, the professor in the tower, he answered that he had never regarded the tower as a refuge, that the university's distance from the day's urgencies was the condition of the only service it could render the day, and that he intended to go on rendering it. The interview appeared in May 1969. Its author cancelled his lectures that term and was dead by August, and the document survives as the closest thing the record holds to a self portrait at the easel of the press: the mandarin at bay, courteous, funny in the driest register, and entirely unrepentant, a combination his obituarists on both sides of the barricade found, in their different ways, impossible to improve on.

## The students, the occupation, the last year

The final episode must be reported, as the brief for this profile requires, factually and without partisanship, giving both the students' grievance and his position, and adjudicating neither. By the late nineteen sixties Adorno had become, for the radicalised student movement in West Germany, a double figure: the author of the works from which the movement had learned its critique of the administered world, and a professor of the university that administered it, an Ordinarius of the establishment his books indicted. The movement's own theorists had passed through his seminars; one of its most gifted leaders, Hans-Jürgen Krahl, was his doctoral student. When, in the wave of occupations that followed the emergency laws debates and the shooting of the student Benno Ohnesorg by police in 1967 and of the movement's spokesman Rudi Dutschke in 1968, students occupied the Institute for Social Research at the end of January 1969, it was Adorno, as director, who called the police to clear the building, and some seventy six students were arrested. He gave his reasons in the language of the distinction his work had always maintained: that theory and practice are not identical, that an occupation is not an argument, and that the director of an institute who permits its seizure has not advanced critique but abolished the institutional condition of it. The students' answer, delivered in leaflets and in the movement's press, was that the distinction itself was the ideology: that a theory of emancipation which calls the police on the emancipators has confessed its function, and that the master's dialectic, at the decisive moment, resolved into the dean's telephone.

The confrontation moved from the institute to the lecture hall in the spring term of 1969. On 22 April, as he began a lecture, students disrupted the proceedings; a group of women students pressed toward the lectern with flowers and bared their breasts, an action designed, in the idiom of the moment, as provocation and mockery at once; leaflets declared that Adorno as an institution was dead. He left the hall and cancelled his lectures for the remainder of the term, and he did not resume them. In July he appeared as a witness in proceedings arising from the occupation, and his testimony, measured and unreconciled, restated the distinction on which he had acted. To Marcuse, who wrote to him in sympathy with the students and reproached him across the Atlantic, he replied with the full statement of his position, including the sentence that the thought which cannot be translated into practice without remainder is not thereby refuted, since the remainder is where thought keeps its truth. It is the last public argument of his life, and it reproduces, in biographical form, the structure of everything he wrote: the refusal of the identity between concept and act, maintained at the moment when maintaining it cost him most.

In August 1969 he travelled with his wife to Switzerland, to Zermatt and then to Visp, carrying the manuscript of the Aesthetic Theory on which he had been working through the crisis, writing to his publisher and his students that the book would be finished by the spring. On 6 August he died in Visp of a heart attack, two months short of his sixty-sixth birthday. The students' movement produced, in the days after, obituaries of real grief alongside the leaflets, a confusion that remains the most accurate verdict available on the episode. He had taught the generation that disrupted him to distrust every institution including his own, and the distrust, arriving on schedule and in his own lecture hall, was neither the refutation his enemies wanted nor the vindication his friends claimed. It was the non-identical, returning in person. The aftermath has its own record, and completeness requires it. The Frankfurt students' organisations issued obituaries that mingled the grief with the quarrel in proportions that varied by faction; the university held its memorial; the Institute continued under its staff while the directorship passed, in the fullness of the institution's own history, toward the generation of his assistants. Krahl, the student leader whose argument with the master had been the confrontation's intellectual honour, died the following year, in 1970, and the double death closed the episode for its protagonists while leaving it open for everyone else. Adorno's own last letters, written from Switzerland in the days before the heart attack, plan the autumn lectures, correct proofs, and speak of the Aesthetic Theory as a thing of the coming spring. There is no valedictory sentence in them, no summing up, and the absence has the rightness his method would have demanded: the work does not conclude, it stops, and the stopping is not a meaning.

The essays of that last summer give the defence its considered form, and a factual report should register them alongside the telephone call and the cancelled lectures. In Marginalia to Theory and Praxis, written in 1969 and published after his death, he argued that the demand for the immediate unity of theory and practice had itself become an ideology, a pseudo-activity that mistakes the gesture of action for its substance and punishes the thought that insists on knowing what it is doing; genuine practice, he held, requires theory's distance as its conscience, and the impatience that denounces distance as betrayal repeats, inside the movement, the administered world's own hatred of everything that does not function. In the short piece Resignation, broadcast in the same period, he answered the charge that critical thought ends in passivity by inverting it: the uncompromising thinker, who refuses to let his thought be terrorised by the demand for usefulness, is not the resigned one; resignation belongs to those who trim their perception to what the moment permits. Krahl, his own student and the occupation's most intelligent leader, conducted the movement's side of the argument at a level that honoured the seminar both had shared, maintaining that the master's theory, taken seriously, indicted the master's institution, and the exchange between them, in print and in the courtroom where Adorno's testimony helped fix the legal record of the occupation, has the quality, unbearable to both parties' partisans, of a family quarrel conducted in a shared language. The family's patriarch died in the middle of it, and neither side has finished the sentence since.

### What did Adorno say in his own defence?

That theory and practice are not identical, and that the difference is not an excuse but a condition of truth. An occupation was not an argument; a director surrendering the institute abolished the ground on which critique stood. To Marcuse, the sharper point: a thought refusing translation into immediate action keeps its fidelity to what action would betray.

## Aesthetic Theory, and the shape of the whole

The book he carried to Switzerland appeared in 1970, edited from the manuscript and its layered revisions by his widow Gretel Adorno and by Rolf Tiedemann, under the title Aesthetic Theory. It is his largest single work and his most demanding, a treatise on art that proceeds, true to its author, without the scaffolding of a system: long paratactic movements in which the concepts, enigma, mimesis, shudder, truth content, constellation again, circle the artworks and one another, and the argument accumulates as a chord accumulates, by the pressure of simultaneous notes. Its claims can be indicated here only in the manner of a profile. Art, for the late Adorno, is the social antithesis of society: made of society's materials, by society's labour, inside society's markets, and yet, in the works that count, organised against society's principle, the principle of exchange and equivalence, by a logic of form that preserves what the principle destroys. The shudder before a great work, the involuntary tremor that precedes understanding, is his name for the subject's registration of the non-identical, the moment in which the classified world admits something it did not file. Aesthetic Theory is unfinished in the trivial sense that its author died with revisions in progress, and finished in the only sense its method permits, since a theory of art that resolved into doctrine would have falsified its object at the final bar.

The companion essay on commitment, written in 1962 against the doctrine that art must enlist, shows the aesthetics at its most combative and clarifies a misunderstanding as persistent as the poetry slogan. Sartre's committed literature and Brecht's didactic theatre are examined with respect for their intentions and refused on their results: the work that delivers a message, he argued, delivers the audience to the message's administration, and the political content, detachable as a slogan, becomes indistinguishable from the culture industry's own products, opinion with orchestration. Autonomous art, the work that refuses every function, performs by that refusal the politics available to art at all, keeping open a space in which experience is not yet administered. The formula has been misread as aestheticism, the doctrine of art for art's sake, by readers who miss its dialectical second half: the autonomous work is political because it is social through and through, made of society's material, and its refusal is a social act aimed at society. Picasso's Guernica, which the essay treats as the century's test case, succeeds not by the legibility of its protest but by the violence its construction does to legibility, horror rendered as form rather than as information. The position left him, characteristically, without a camp: the committed found him quietist, the aesthetes found him a sociologist, and the work continued in the cleared ground between the camps, which is where he had always said the truth would have to live if it lived at all.

### Why did his last book stay unfinished?

He died with the revisions in progress, in August 1969, the manuscript layered with insertions and reworkings that his widow and his student Tiedemann edited into the published text. The unfinished state is also, unusually, appropriate to its doctrine: a theory holding that artworks and truths alike refuse completion could hardly demand of itself a sealed and final form.

Stand back from the sequence and the shape of the life has the clarity of a form he would have recognised. A musical formation supplied a method. The method, transposed to philosophy, produced a critique of identity and of the culture that identity administers. Exile supplied the two great laboratories, fascism and the market, in which the critique was tested. The return supplied an institution, a public, and a final collision with the generation the critique had educated. Through every phase the distribution of his labour kept its early proportion: more pages on music than on any other subject, the philosophy forever returning to the score for its standard of what thinking, at its most honest, sounds like. That proportion is the simplest statement of this profile's claim. Adorno is misread as a philosopher who had views about music. He is better read as a musician whose instrument became philosophy, and whose philosophy never stopped being marked, in its virtues and its failures alike, by the training that made it.

## The essay as form: a defence of his own genre

Late in the nineteen fifties he published the defence of his own practice that readers of the systematic books most need, the essay on the essay as form that opens the first volume of Notes to Literature. The essay, as he describes and performs it, is the heretical genre among the forms of thought: it refuses the treatise's deductive order, begins in the middle of its object, proceeds by constellation rather than by derivation, and accepts the fragmentary and the provisional as the price of fidelity to experience. Its concepts are not defined at the gate but clarified in their use, the way a theme is clarified by its development. Its unity is the unity of a movement, not of a system. The apologia is transparently also a self portrait, and it arms the reader with the author's own account of why the books look as they do, an account that relocates the difficulty from temperament to doctrine. A thought that suspects every closed system as a small administered world will build its houses accordingly, with the doors left open and the load bearing walls made of prose rhythm, transitions and returns, rather than of numbered propositions. The essay as form is where the musical method becomes self conscious at the level of genre, and it is not an accident that he placed the piece at the head of the literary volumes, as an overture is placed: everything the reader needs to know about how to listen is stated there, once, in advance, in the confident expectation that most readers will skip it.

## The literature essays: Beckett, Kafka, and the paratactic sentence

The writings on literature, gathered in the four volumes of Notes to Literature that appeared across the nineteen fifties and sixties, are the chamber music of the corpus, and for many readers they have become the preferred entrance, ahead even of the aphorisms. The essay on Samuel Beckett's Endgame, which he called the most significant work of the postwar stage and to whose author he intended to dedicate Aesthetic Theory, reads the play's clipped exchanges and exhausted rituals as the exact register of a world in which meaning has been withdrawn and the forms of drama persist as its wreckage, and it practises what it praises: an interpretation that stays inside the work's desolation without redeeming it into a message. The notes on Kafka, written for the Prisms volume, take the prose's dreamlike literalness as a photographic negative of the administered world, each gesture exact and each context withheld, so that the reader experiences the estrangement that the sociology describes from outside. The Proust essay, the Hölderlin lecture on parataxis, the studies of Eichendorff, of George, of the essay form itself in the famous apologia that opens the Notes, extend the same loyalty: the work treated as a monad, its social truth locked in its formal decisions, the critic's task defined as the patient unlocking.

The Hölderlin lecture of 1963 deserves a sentence of its own in a profile organised by method, because it is where the key term of his prose was theorised in public. Reading the late hymns, he named their syntax paratactic: clauses set side by side without the subordinating machinery that ranks thought into masters and servants, so that meaning arises from adjacency and resonance rather than from command. He had been writing that way for decades. The lecture is the moment the compositional practice announces itself as doctrine, with a poet dead a century and a half as its witness, and it arms the reader to hear the late philosophy's long unspooling sentences for what they are: not failures of organisation but an ethics of organisation, a refusal to let the syntax commit the violence that the argument exists to protest. Whether the refusal justifies the labour it imposes is the standing question of his reception, and it has been pressed in the objection section of this profile. What the literature essays establish beyond dispute is the range of the ear: the man could hear a sentence by Beckett the way he heard a bar of Berg, and there are not five critics of the century of whom that can be said.

## The typology of listening, and the sociology of music

In 1962 he published the Introduction to the Sociology of Music, built from radio lectures, and its opening chapter contains the most quoted piece of applied method in his whole corpus: a typology of musical listening, eight modes arranged from the expert, who follows a work's structure as a whole in motion, down through the good listener, the culture consumer for whom music certifies education, the emotional listener who uses it as a trigger for feelings, the resentment listener who polices correct performance, the listener for entertainment who hears music as acoustic wallpaper, and the musically indifferent. The typology has been quarrelled with for sixty years, and the quarrels usually miss its genre. It is not a ranking of persons by worth, though its author's manners sometimes let it sound like one. It is a set of ideal types in the Weberian ancestry, instruments for thinking about the relation between a society's organisation and the capacities of attention it cultivates or corrodes, and its empirical content lies in the claim of tendency: that the industrial distribution of music presses listening downward through the types, from structure toward stimulus, whatever the listener's intentions. Concert life since, with its programming of excerpts, its relaxation formats, its playlist logic, has run the experiment the typology predicted, and the results are not obviously in the typology's disfavour.

The sociology of music as a whole, lectures, essays and the posthumous fragments on musical reproduction, is the part of the corpus where his two competences, the composer's and the sociologist's, fully fuse, and where posterity has been kindest to him. Musicians read the Beethoven fragments and the Mahler book without needing the philosophy. Sociologists read the listening types without needing the scores. The general reader, standing between the two readerships, can watch in these pages what the profile has called the musical method operating with the least friction: the object known from inside, the categories drawn from the object's own behaviour, the social totality appearing, as he always promised it would, in the smallest technical detail, the vibrato of a violin section, the cut of a broadcast symphony, the applause that arrives before the final chord has died because the audience has been trained to consume endings. His projected theory of musical reproduction, the study of performance itself as interpretation, remained unfinished at his death, and its surviving fragments, on the pianist's touch, on Glenn Gould's Bach, on the x-ray picture a great performance gives of a work's structure, suggest the shape of the book the century lost when the lectures in the hall at Frankfurt were cancelled in the spring of 1969.

The typology has a defect its author half acknowledged, and fairness requires it on the record beside the jazz verdict. A ladder of listening that descends from the expert is drawn from the concert hall's own self image, and it cannot hear what it was not built to hear: the communal, participatory modes in which music is not an object of attention at all but a medium of assembly, work, worship and dance, modes in which the categories of structural hearing do not apply because no structure is being offered to a listener. His sociology of music knew these modes existed; the ethnographic chapters glance at them; the ladder has no rung for them, because the ladder measures everyone's distance from the Viennese ideal. The defect is the jazz failure generalised, the formation's limit drawn as a map of the world, and the most useful way to read the typology now is the way he taught his students to read Hegel: as a true map of a territory, whose borders the cartographer mistook for the borders of the earth. Read so, it remains what it was in 1962, the sharpest instrument ever built for describing what industrial distribution does to attention, provided the reader supplies, from outside the instrument, the attention the instrument could not describe.

## The letters: the second corpus

Posterity has been given, since his death, a second corpus beside the works, and no modern profile may omit it: the correspondence, thousands of letters, edited volume by volume, with Horkheimer, with Benjamin, with Thomas Mann, with Marcuse, with his publishers, with his students, and, most unexpectedly moving, with his parents. The letters to his mother and father from the American exile, written in a German kept deliberately simple out of tenderness for readers whose world had shrunk to a Frankfurt flat and then to a London exile of their own, report the supermarkets, the weather, the price of oranges, the progress of the work, with a son's discipline of reassurance that the parents' danger made heroic. They are the standing refutation of the coldest version of his reputation: the warmth is unmistakable, and it is administered, in his own sense, with a tact his essay on tact could have used as its exhibit. The professional letters conduct the century's business at his level: the quarrels over editions, the negotiations with Suhrkamp, the assessments of colleagues delivered with a candour that friendship survived because the candour was reciprocal. And the late letters, to Marcuse in the summer of the confrontation, to his publisher from Visp in the last week of his life announcing the Aesthetic Theory's approaching completion, close the corpus with an abruptness that no editor could soften. He wrote, his widow's editorial note to the final volume in effect records, until the day the writing stopped, and the stopped sentence is the biography's true ending, more exact than any peroration a profile could compose.

## Reading him after his death: translation, reception, the two Adornos

The posthumous history of the work repeats, at the level of reputation, the edition dependency that governed Gramsci's afterlife in the cluster this series has just traversed, and the parallel is worth a section because it disciplines everything a reader thinks they know about him. There are, in effect, two Adornos abroad in the culture. The first is the German author: the aphorist of 1951, the essayist of Prisms, the professor of the broadcasts, the systematic philosopher of 1966, whose corpus stood complete in his language within a year of his death and whose image at home, contested and admonished, never lost the ballast of the full shelf. The second is the translated author, assembled by the accidents of publishing: the culture industry chapter arriving as a detachable unit in anthologies of media studies, the Minima Moralia in E. F. N. Jephcott's translation of 1974 becoming a cult book of the Anglophone humanities, the systematic works following slowly and out of order, Negative Dialectics in 1973, Aesthetic Theory not until 1984 in its first English version, the music writings trailing decades behind, some untranslated into the next century. Each receiving culture constructed the thinker its shelf permitted: the French met the aesthetician, the Americans the media theorist of the administered society, the British the aphorist and the melancholy Marxist of the New Left, whose journals mediated him to a generation that read the Dialectic of Enlightenment beside the situationists and heard, in the doctrine of total administration, a warrant for strategies he would have disowned.

The material basis of the repair deserves naming, since in his case the basis is unusually concrete. The collected writings in German, assembled under Rolf Tiedemann's editorship in twenty volumes, established a citable corpus; the posthumous writings followed in their own series, lecture by lecture and fragment by fragment, the Beethoven book that never was, the theory of musical reproduction, the radio studies, the Current of Music, until the corpus available to a reader now exceeds, by a wide margin, what any contemporary of the author could have read. The Frankfurt archive holds the manuscripts, the library with its marginalia, the correspondence files, and administers the whole as a working research collection rather than a shrine. The consequence for the reputation is still unfolding. Every decade's newly published stratum has revised the portrait in the direction this profile has argued, away from the anthology figure and toward the working musician philosopher, and there is no reason to think the revision complete, since the lectures on aesthetics and the remaining correspondence continue to appear. An honest citation of Adorno is therefore a dated citation, a report on which Adorno the present state of the editions permits, and the requirement, once again, is the one his method imposes everywhere: check the edition before quoting the fragment.

The scholars who repaired the picture did so by the slow method he would have prescribed, returning to the objects: the letters edited, the lectures transcribed, the radio manuscripts recovered, the compositions recorded, the Nachlass in Frankfurt opened to a research that continues. Their cumulative finding can be stated as the profile's finding at larger scale. The despairing mandarin of the anthologies recedes; in his place stands a more various and more demanding figure, a working musician and working sociologist whose pessimism was a finding about a historical situation and not a temperament's perfume, whose comedy, visible in the letters and the broadcasts, the slogan had edited out, and whose relevance to a century of platforms and feeds he did not live to see rests not on prophecy but on the portability of his central question: what is the industrial organisation of culture doing to the capacities of the people it supplies? That the question was first asked of dance bands and radio symphonies is the accident of his lifetime. That it survives translation into every successor medium is the measure of the method's grip on its object.

## The objection at full strength

A profile that has corrected misreadings all the way through owes its reader, at the end, the objection that is not a misreading: the strongest case against its subject, stated as its makers would state it. Here it is. Here is a body of work that condemns almost the whole of modern culture, the films, the songs, the broadcasts, the magazines, the amusements by which hundreds of millions of people furnish their inner lives, and condemns it from a position of extraordinary privilege: the only child of a wealthy household, formed in the most exclusive musical culture in Europe, sustained in exile by an endowed institute, installed after the war in a chair from which he lectured the republic on its taste. The canon he defends is narrow and forbidding, the late quartets, the Second Viennese School, Beckett, Kafka, a few dozen works whose difficulty functions, whatever the theory says, as a password at the door of a club. The politics is a politics of refusal: no programme, no party, no strategy, no account addressed to anyone who asks the vulgar and necessary question what is to be done, only the assurance that the whole is false and that correct consciousness consists in not affirming it. Marxism, in this rendering, has been boiled down to its vocabulary: the commodity form supplies the metaphors, the critique of political economy supplies the prestige, and what issues is aristocratic cultural criticism in the costume of the left, a mandarin's lament for a world of cultivated inwardness that mass society has flooded, dressed in the terminology of the movement that promised to emancipate the masses he could not forgive for their pleasures. The jazz essays are not, on this reading, a local failure. They are the truth of the whole enterprise leaking at its weakest joint: the moment when the mandarin met the people's music and heard only noise to be ranked.

A second objection runs alongside the first and should be entered separately, because it comes from readers who share his aims. The work, for all its talk of suffering and solidarity, has almost nothing to say about half the species: women appear in the corpus as figures inside analyses of the family, of the novel, of the authoritarian character, but rarely as thinkers, artists or subjects in their own right, and the canon of defended art is, in this respect too, the canon of a Frankfurt drawing room. Feminist scholars of the next generation took the tools and corrected the hand that passed them, arguing that the dialectic of enlightenment's own narrative, reason's domination of nature rehearsed first in the domination of women, required the master's categories to be turned on the master's blind spots, and that the critique of identity, faithfully practised, must notice the identities its author never thought to question. The correction is live in the scholarship and it is conducted, at its best, in his own method, which suggests the method's reach exceeds the man's application of it. A profile should state the limitation as plainly as the jazz failure, and for the same reason: the works that survive are the ones whose instruments can be lifted out of the hands that first held them, and his survive that lifting unusually well, which is the opposite of a refutation.

The answer this profile gives begins by identifying what the central argument actually claims, since the objection, for all its force, habitually aims at the man and the tone rather than at the claim. The claim about standardisation is structural, not evaluative. It says that under industrial conditions, cultural goods are produced to a schema that predetermines the whole, decorates the parts with pseudo-individual difference, and trains perception to consume the schema as experience; and that this claim is true or false as a description of a mode of production, entirely independent of whether the describer summers in Amorbach or in a tenement. It can be tested, and much of it has been confirmed by the industry's own handbooks, which teach the schema as method. The claim about difficult art is likewise functional before it is evaluative: the works he defends are defended as the places where the schema does not rule, where form still registers what administration excludes, and the defence attaches to the function, not to the social address of the audience, which is why the same writer could find the culture industry's logic inside a prestige product and authenticity inside a despised one, and why he insisted, against the club reading of his canon, that a folk ballad stands outside his critique altogether. On the political charge, the record is mixed in ways the objection flattens. He offered no programme; the refusal was principled, argued from the fate of every programme the century had bureaucratised, but principled refusal and practical emptiness are not as different in their consequences as the principled would like, and the students of 1969 were not wrong to feel the difference closing on them. What can be conceded without reserve is the tone. The contempt that surfaces in the jazz essays surfaces elsewhere too, in flashes, in the treatment of readers, of audiences, of the ordinary consolations of ordinary people, and at those moments the argument about standardisation, which is a defence of those people's capacities against the industry that stunts them, is betrayed by a voice that sounds as if it despises the stunted. The tone frequently exceeds the argument. A fair reader keeps both findings: the structure stands, and the voice, at its worst, does the structure's enemies' work for them.

## The Adorno entry points: where to begin

The practical question remains, for the reader this profile addresses: where, in a body of work of this size and difficulty, should a beginning be made? The Adorno entry points, set out in the table below, arrange the major works by what each argues, how hard it is, what it asks the reader to bring, and whom it suits, so that the choice can be made by the reader's purpose rather than by the prestige of the hardest book. The governing advice is the advice his own pedagogy implied. Enter where the method is most pleasurable, in the aphorisms; proceed to the essays, where the concepts appear one at a time on concrete objects; approach the systematic books last, with the ear trained; and keep the music writing beside the philosophy throughout, since the philosophy's key is cut there. The reader who reverses the order and begins with Negative Dialectics unassisted is performing the equivalent of learning harmony from a treatise before hearing a chord: possible, heroic, and not the author's design, whatever his reputation for forbidding doors.

| Work | What it argues | Difficulty | What it requires first | Who it suits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Minima Moralia | Morality and experience under a damaged whole, in aphorisms | Low to moderate | Nothing but attention | Every reader; the natural door |
| Prisms | Cultural criticism against itself; essays on music, literature, the poetry remark | Moderate | A tolerance for essays | Readers who think in essays |
| Philosophy of New Music | Schoenberg as progress, Stravinsky as restoration, in musical material | High | Score literacy helps greatly | Musicians and music students |
| In Search of Wagner | The leitmotiv as proto advertising; Wagner as prophet of mass culture | Moderate | Some Wagner, some patience | Opera readers, sceptics of Wagner |
| The Jargon of Authenticity | Existentialist language as ideology in verbal form | Moderate | A passing acquaintance with Heidegger | Readers suspicious of uplift |
| Introduction to the Sociology of Music | Twelve modes of listening, from expert to entertainment listener | Moderate | Concert going experience | Sociologists, teachers of music |
| Negative Dialectics | Identity thinking, the non-identical, metaphysics after the camps | Very high | Hegel, Kant, and the earlier books | Philosophers; the summit, not the start |
| Aesthetic Theory | Art as society's antithesis; form, enigma, the shudder | Very high | The whole shelf, ideally | Readers who have lived with him |

Readers differ, and the table is a servant, not a syllabus. The student of the Frankfurt School's place in the wider Marxist tradition will want, alongside whichever of these is chosen, the maps supplied elsewhere in this series: the account of the field in [marxist cultural theory](/2020/06/01/marxist-cultural-theory-guide/), and the institutional frame within which the works were produced. The reader whose interest is the music should reverse the table's emphasis entirely, begin with the Mahler book and the essays on Beethoven and Berg, and treat the philosophy as the commentary the music eventually required. The reader with an hour should take a single aphorism of Minima Moralia and read it the way the author read a chord, slowly, against its neighbours, until it gives up the whole it contains. There is no wrong door except the door of prestige, the decision to start at the summit because the summit is famous. He despised that decision in his students and forgave it in everyone else, and the forgiveness, like the aphorisms, is part of the record.


Two further doors stand open for the reader who has finished the table's circuit and wants the voice rather than the system. The lectures, published in growing numbers from the Nachlass, on metaphysics, on moral philosophy, on aesthetics, on the problems of teaching, preserve the classroom manner that the books suppress: the argument unfolding at speaking speed, the examples drawn from the day's newspaper and the term's reading, the jokes, which exist and are better than the reputation allows. And the letters, above all the exchange with Benjamin and the reports to his parents from America, supply the register no treatise admits, the man at his desk in the evening, tired, funny, frightened for his family, exact about money and tender about music. A reader who has taken the aphorisms and the essays and then reads a volume of the lectures and a volume of the letters owns the corpus in the proportion its author lived it, and may approach the two great systematic books, when ready, as the scores of a music already heard in performance.

## Coda: the grave, the prize, and the standing question

He is buried in the Frankfurt main cemetery beside Gretel, who survived him by twenty four years and spent the first of them as his editor, and the grave, visited by students who leave stones and scores, has become a minor station of the city's intellectual geography. The city of Frankfurt endowed a prize in his name in 1977, awarded at intervals to figures in philosophy, music, film and theatre, a civic arrangement he would have read with the double vision he brought to every honour: gratitude for the intention, suspicion of the institution, and a footnote, unwritten, on prizes as the administered world's way of filing its critics under culture. The archive of his papers in Frankfurt, the editions of the letters and lectures still appearing, the recordings of the compositions, the broadcasts preserved: the apparatus of a classic is fully installed, and would have interested him chiefly as evidence for his own thesis that a society neutralises its negations by enshrining them. The enshrinement has not neutralised this one yet. The questions he asked, of the song on the radio and the concept in the seminar, of the slogan and the symphony, remain the questions a reader puts to a feed, a platform, a syllabus and a self, and the method by which he asked them, the descent into the particular, the constellation, the refusal of the final chord, remains available to any reader willing to pay its price in attention. That willingness is the only membership the work has ever required, and the door, as the aphorisms promised, is the one that was never locked.

## For students and exam candidates

Build your answers on the formation before the doctrines. An examiner can tell within a paragraph whether a candidate knows that Adorno was a composer trained in Vienna, because that single fact reorganises everything else: negative dialectics stops sounding like a slogan and becomes a procedure, and the culture industry argument reveals its roots in the radio studies and the fetishism essay. Learn the entry points table in this profile as a map of the corpus, and be able to argue, for any major work, what it claims, how hard it is and what it presupposes. Two corrections win marks wherever they are relevant: the poetry remark must always be given with its 1966 qualification attached, and the jazz essays must be assessed, not defended. Keep a reading log of the aphorisms and the essays as you work through them; the study notes companion at [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) is designed for exactly that accumulation, and the series capstone, [the Frankfurt School exam guide](/2015/10/15/frankfurt-school-exam-answers/), shows how the examinable questions on this cluster are built and marked.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The productive seminar on Adorno begins with a recording and a page of Minima Moralia in the same hour, because the juxtaposition teaches the thesis of this profile before any lecture can: the ear and the argument are one training. Set the fetishism essay beside a session of close listening, structural and distracted in turn, and let the group discover the typology of listening from its own behaviour before naming it. The jazz essays make an exemplary exercise in critical reading precisely because they fail by his own standards; asking a group to specify where the method is abandoned, and what evidence would have corrected the verdict, teaches immanent criticism better than a success could. For the examinable concept at the centre of the school's syllabus, pair this profile with [the culture industry explained](/2015/07/15/culture-industry-explained/), and reserve the confrontation of 1969 for the final meeting, when the group knows enough of the work to hear both the students' grievance and the professor's answer as positions inside one tradition rather than as a scandal to be gossiped about.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Three cautions govern responsible citation. First, never quote the poetry remark without its qualification; the retraction stands in the Meditations on Metaphysics of Negative Dialectics, and reproducing the slogan alone repeats the standardisation this profile describes. Second, treat the correspondence as a primary source of the first rank: the letters to Benjamin, to Horkheimer, to Marcuse and to his parents carry arguments, dates and retractions that the published works presuppose, and the Benjamin letters of 1936 and 1938 are the indispensable documents of the artwork dispute. Third, attend to the editorial condition of the corpus. Aesthetic Theory is a posthumous construction by Gretel Adorno and Rolf Tiedemann from a layered manuscript; the music writings, the radio studies and the Current of Music materials reached print piecemeal over decades; and the English translations, principally those of E. B. Ashton in one generation and Edmund Jephcott in the next, differ in ways that matter for quotation. The exile archive, the Frankfurt Nachlass and the Institute's records remain the ground against which every journalistic shortcut should be checked.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was Theodor Adorno?

Theodor Adorno, born Theodor Ludwig Wiesengrund in Frankfurt in 1903 and dead in 1969, was a German philosopher, sociologist and trained composer, and with Max Horkheimer the leading figure of the Frankfurt School. He studied composition in Vienna with Alban Berg, wrote music criticism all his life, and spent the Nazi years in exile, in Oxford and then in the United States, where he worked on the Princeton radio project and, in Los Angeles, wrote Minima Moralia and, with Horkheimer, the Dialectic of Enlightenment. Returning to Frankfurt in 1949, he took the chair of philosophy and sociology, directed the rebuilt Institute for Social Research, and published Negative Dialectics in 1966. His posthumous Aesthetic Theory appeared in 1970.

### Q: Was Adorno primarily a philosopher or a musician?

The question assumes a separation his life refuses. By output, music was his largest single subject across five decades: reviews, essays on composers, a sociology of music, studies of Beethoven, Wagner, Mahler and Berg. By reputation and by institutional position, philosophy claimed him, and the systematic works are philosophical in address. The resolution offered by this profile is that the philosophy is best understood as the transposition of a musical training: composition with Berg taught him to hear relations rather than statements, to trust the detail that resists the whole, and to refuse resolutions that the material has not earned. Called to choose, he would have resisted the choice itself, and the resistance is the answer.

### Q: Did Adorno really say no poetry after Auschwitz?

He wrote the sentence in 1949, in an essay on cultural criticism, and the sentence in its usual rendering holds that writing a poem after Auschwitz is barbaric. What the slogan omits is the sequel. In Negative Dialectics, published in 1966, he returned to the remark directly and corrected it: suffering that does not end, he argued, has the same claim to expression as the scream of a tortured human being, so it may have been wrong to forbid the poems. The correction does not dissolve the problem the sentence had named, that art after the camps works under suspicion of consoling where it should accuse. It withdraws the prohibition and keeps the difficulty, and any honest citation gives both halves.

### Q: What is negative dialectics?

Negative dialectics is the name of his mature method and of the 1966 book that states it. Dialectics, in the inheritance he takes from Hegel, means thinking through contradiction, allowing a concept to be tested against its object and against itself. Negative names the refusal of the Hegelian destination: no synthesis is permitted to close the movement and declare the contradiction reconciled, because every reconciliation achieved in thought reoffends against what the concepts have left out. The procedure is determinate negation, the criticism of a position by its own standards and its object's resistance, continued without the guarantee of arrival. It is a discipline of non-closure, designed to keep thought faithful to the remainder that systems discard.

### Q: What is identity thinking?

Identity thinking is his term for the basic operation by which thought equates an object with the concept under which it is classified, and then mistakes the classification for knowledge of the thing. To call a person by a function, an artwork by a genre, an event by a category, is indispensable and unavoidable; no cognition proceeds without it. His charge is that the operation conceals its own remainder, everything in the object that the concept does not capture, and that a society organised by exchange, where unlike things are rendered equivalent for trade, trains this concealment into a metaphysics. Philosophy's obligation, on his account, is to perform identification while remembering and honouring what it excludes, the non-identical.

### Q: Why did Adorno criticise jazz?

He criticised jazz from within a theory of musical material and of the entertainment industry, and the criticism is the weakest part of his work. What reached him in nineteen thirties Germany was chiefly commercial dance-band music, arranged and regularised for social dancing, and he generalised from that commodity to the entire form: syncopation as mechanical decoration over an unchanging beat, improvisation as a stock of interchangeable formulae, the soloist as pseudo-individual garnish on a standardised chassis. The description fits much of the dance-band product. It does not survive contact with the central tradition, the improvising masters of New Orleans, Chicago and Kansas City, whose art consists precisely in the non-interchangeable invention the theory ruled out. The essays proceed from category to object, the reverse of his own method, and their tone is contemptuous. The criticism of them is largely justified.

### Q: What is Minima Moralia?

Minima Moralia, published in 1951 with the subtitle Reflections from Damaged Life, is his most accessible book: some one hundred and fifty three aphorisms in three parts, written in American exile between 1944 and 1949 and dedicated to Horkheimer. Its subjects are deliberately small, gifts, tact, dwelling, generosity, the etiquette of intellectual life, and its wager is that a damaged whole can be read in its smallest cells more truthfully than in any system built to mirror it. The title inverts the great ethics of the Aristotelian tradition: morality at the smallest scale, because the smallest scale is where life is actually conducted. Its most quoted sentence, that wrong life cannot be lived rightly, refuses the consolation that private decency exempts anyone from a false totality.

### Q: What happened between Adorno and the students?

In the late nineteen sixties the radical student movement, which had learned much of its critique of the administered world from his books, turned on its teacher as an institution of that world. At the end of January 1969 students occupied the Institute for Social Research, and Adorno, as its director, called the police; some seventy six students were arrested. In April 1969 his lecture was disrupted, women students pressed to the lectern with flowers and bared breasts, and leaflets declared Adorno as an institution dead. He cancelled his lectures and did not resume them. His defence rested on the distinction between theory and practice: an occupation, he held, is not an argument, and surrendering the institute would abolish critique's institutional ground. He died that August, with the quarrel unresolved.

### Q: Was Adorno an elitist?

The charge survives because it is half earned. His canon of defended works was narrow and difficult, the late Beethoven quartets, the Second Viennese School, Beckett and Kafka, and his prose can flash a contempt for ordinary pleasures that his own argument, which casts ordinary people as the culture industry's victims rather than its villains, should have forbidden. Against the charge stands the structure of the critique: standardisation, not popularity, is its object, and it falls on a prestige product manufactured by formula as readily as on a hit song, while a folk ballad made for its occasion falls outside it entirely. The fairest verdict distinguishes the argument, which is about production, from the tone, which frequently exceeds the argument and gives the charge its evidence.

### Q: Where should you start reading Adorno?

Start with Minima Moralia, where the method works at aphorism scale and the pleasures are immediate, a few pieces a day, read slowly. Move next to the essays, in Prisms and the musical collections, where single concepts meet concrete objects, a composer, a poem, a jargon. Keep the music writing in parallel throughout, since it is the method on its home ground and the philosophy's key. Take the co-authored Dialectic of Enlightenment when the culture industry argument is wanted whole, and leave Negative Dialectics and Aesthetic Theory for last, when the ear has been trained by everything else. The entry points table in this profile grades the major works by difficulty and by what each presupposes, so the order can be adjusted to the reader's purpose.

### Q: How does the musical method shape Adorno's prose arguments?

It shapes them at the level of construction. A passage of his prose behaves like a passage of the music he was trained in: its meaning is carried by relations among its elements rather than by extractable statements, so that a sentence means what its neighbours, its qualifications, its deliberate dissonances make it mean. Concepts are arranged in constellations around an object instead of being stacked under a thesis, and elements are set side by side, paratactically, so that their tensions sound rather than being marched into subordination. Endings refuse the final cadence; an argument closes when the constellation has been sounded, not when a conclusion has been bagged. Readers who excerpt him for positions therefore reliably falsify him, not from malice but because the unit of his thought is the movement, not the proposition.

### Q: What should a reader separate between the entry points into Adorno?

Separate the three registers in which he wrote, because each obeys a different contract with the reader. The music writing is concrete, technical and verifiable against scores, and it is the method on home ground. The aphoristic and essayistic writing, Minima Moralia above all, compresses the same method into chamber forms that reward slow rereading and forgive an unprepared reader. The systematic books, Negative Dialectics and the posthumous Aesthetic Theory, demand the philosophical tradition as a prerequisite and punish skimming with genuine incomprehension. Confusing the registers produces the standard disappointments: readers who meet the systems first conclude he is fog, and readers who meet only the aphorisms conclude he is a sage of quotable gloom. He is a worker in all three registers, and the entry points table grades them separately for that reason.

### Q: Why is negative dialectics so hard to summarise fairly?

Because a summary is an identity claim, and the method exists to police identity claims. To summarise is to equate a movement of thought with the set of propositions it passes through, discarding the qualifications, reversals and self corrections in which, for Adorno, the thinking actually occurs. A fair summary therefore has to do something summaries are not built to do: it must report not only what positions were taken but what each position was prevented from settling into, which objections were absorbed and which remainders were left deliberately standing. Anything shorter converts the method into the doctrine it refuses to be, a set of portable negations, pessimism with footnotes. The difficulty is not a defect of exposition added to a summarisable core. It is the core, behaving as designed.

### Q: How did the exile writings differ from the German ones?

In language, in genre and in the position of the writer. The exile years produced the aphorism book, the co-authored treatise circulated first in mimeograph, the collaborative empirical study of the authoritarian personality, memoranda for a radio project, film music theory with Eisler: work addressed to American institutions, foundations and fellow exiles, often pragmatic in form and sometimes, in the empirical projects, written to specifications he privately resisted. The German writings before and after exile are the essays and treatises of a university man addressing a national culture in its own densest idiom, the prose of the Zeitschrift essays and of the postwar books, uncompromising in syntax because its first audience was the German tradition itself. The continuity underneath the difference is the method; what exile changed was the laboratory, adding the market's administration of consciousness to the fascism he had fled.

### Q: What separates Adorno's aesthetics from his sociology?

A division of labour within one method. The sociology, including the sociology of music, studies how artworks and tastes are produced, distributed and consumed inside a society organised by exchange: institutions, industries, audiences, the training of listening. The aesthetics asks the prior and stranger question of what an artwork is when it succeeds: a construction of society's own materials that nevertheless refuses society's principle, preserving in its form the experience of what exchange discards. Sociology can explain the culture industry completely and still not touch the truth content of a single quartet; aesthetics without sociology floats into the idealism he spent the Kierkegaard book criticising. The two are held apart so that each can correct the other, the sociologist recalled to the object's specificity, the aesthetician to the object's conditions.
