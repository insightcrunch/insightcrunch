The wedding day in Romeo and Juliet is named twice, and the two names cannot both be true. On the Monday night of Act 3, in a scene that begins with a father asking what day it is, Capulet appoints Thursday: Paris confirms the day of the week, Capulet reaches first for Wednesday, corrects himself, and settles the matter with a formula he repeats like a seal, "O' Thursday let it be" (3.4.20). On the Tuesday evening of Act 4, delighted by a repentance that is entirely feigned, the same father annuls his own appointment in a single sentence: "I'll have this knot knit up to-morrow morning" (4.2.24). To-morrow morning, counted from Tuesday, is Wednesday. Between those two sentences the whole machinery of the catastrophe is re-timed. The Friar's plan, designed on the Tuesday of Act 4 Scene 1 for a Thursday wedding, assumes a dose drunk on Wednesday night and a waking on Friday. The advance steals the dose's night, pulls the waking back to Thursday, and leaves a letter describing the old timetable travelling toward Mantua while the new timetable executes itself in Verona. Thursday is the day the wedding was appointed. Wednesday is the day the wedding was enacted, prepared for, dressed for, and found impossible over a daughter's apparently dead body. The play marries Juliet to Paris on neither day, since the ceremony never happens, but every intention, preparation, and consequence in the text obeys Wednesday, and Thursday survives only in the mouths of characters who have not yet heard that the calendar has been torn up by the man who wrote it.

That verdict has to be earned from the day-references themselves, because the play is unusually generous with them and unusually careless, or unusually cunning, about their agreement. Few Shakespearean plots let an audience count the days at all. This one names Monday outright, names Wednesday twice in opposed senses, names Thursday so often that the word becomes a drumbeat in the second half, and then stages a wedding morning that belongs to the second name, not the first. The argument of the pages that follow is that the contradiction is a decision before it is a discrepancy. Capulet makes the appointment in haste, in grief, minutes after his nephew's corpse has been carried out of the street, and he unmakes it in haste, in delight, minutes after his daughter kneels and performs the obedience he demanded. The same hand holds the pen both times. Reading the date change closely, scene by scene and line by line, shows a father governing his household by pronouncement, a friar building a rescue on a timetable he does not control, and a playwright who understood that in a five-day tragedy a single day is not a detail of the scenery. A single day is a quarter of the world.

## Monday Night in Act 3: Thursday Is Appointed, Wednesday Refused

The appointment scene is short, late, and tired, and every one of its day-references is spoken aloud. Act 3 Scene 4 opens in the small hours after the worst day of the play: Tybalt dead in the afternoon, Romeo banished by the Prince's sentence, Juliet weeping in a chamber upstairs, and Capulet receiving Paris in a house that has not yet gone to bed. "Things have fall'n out, sir, so unluckily / That we have had no time to move our daughter" (3.4.1-2), Capulet begins, and the apology contains the scene's whole problem in miniature. Time is the thing the household has run out of. Paris presses his suit anyway, and Capulet, who in Act 1 had asked the same suitor to wait two summers and let Juliet ripen at leisure, surrenders the leisure in a dozen lines. The surrender deserves a pause. The father of Act 1 Scene 2 was a man who counted in years, who told Paris that his child was a stranger to the world and that the marriage could wait upon summers. The father of Act 3 Scene 4 counts in days, and the compression of his unit of measure, from summers to days, is the first sign that grief has already begun to govern the calendar before the calendar is even named. The series' examination of [how old Romeo and Juliet are, and what the age question does to the marriage market around them](/2022/02/18/how-old-are-romeo-and-juliet-age-question/), sets out the stakes of that earlier patience; the patience is what Tybalt's death spends.

Capulet's first instinct, once the surrender is made, is Wednesday. He turns to his wife with the errand half formed: acquaint Juliet with "my son Paris' love, / And bid her, mark you me, on Wednesday next" (3.4.16-17), and then the sentence breaks against a fact the speaker does not possess. "But, soft! what day is this?" (3.4.17). The question is the only one of its kind in the play, a householder of Verona asking a guest to tell him what day of the week it is in his own house, and Paris supplies the play's single fixed point: "Monday, my lord" (3.4.18). Everything else in the tragedy's chronology is arithmetic hung on that answer. Sunday, the day of the feast and the first meeting, is never named by any character and is reconstructed backward from it. Tuesday, the day of the advance and the dose, is never named either and is reconstructed forward from it. Friday, the day of the intended waking, is never named at all. The named days of the play are Monday, Wednesday, and Thursday, and two of the three collide.

Given the anchor, Capulet performs the calculation in public and gets two answers in four lines. "Monday! ha, ha! Well, Wednesday is too soon, / O' Thursday let it be: o' Thursday, tell her, / She shall be married to this noble earl" (3.4.19-21). The laugh is worth dwelling on. A man who did not know the day of the week a moment earlier is suddenly confident enough to judge one date too soon and another date fit, and the grounds of the judgment are never given, because there are no grounds beyond the feel of the thing: Wednesday is too soon the way a coat is too thin, by inspection. Thursday is chosen as a compromise between a dead nephew unburied in the audience's mind and a suitor standing impatient in the hall, and the doubled formula, "O' Thursday let it be" spoken twice within three lines, performs the settling of a question that the speaker had not known how to frame thirty seconds before. The haste of the lovers is the play's famous subject. The haste of this scene is quieter and structurally identical: a decision about a date taken at speed, on incomplete information, by a man certain that pronouncing a thing is the same as arranging it.

### Why did Wednesday come first out of Capulet's mouth in Act 3 Scene 4?

Wednesday came first because haste, not calculation, was doing the talking. Speaking before he knew the day of the week, Capulet reached for the earliest date his tongue could find, and only Paris's answer, that the day was Monday, forced the arithmetic that made Wednesday too soon and Thursday the compromise.

The scene closes by ratifying the compromise from the suitor's side. Paris, offered Thursday, answers with the wish that gives the appointment its emotional temperature: "My lord, I would that Thursday were to-morrow" (3.4.29). Capulet seals the exchange a final time, "o' Thursday be it, then" (3.4.30), and sends his wife to prepare Juliet "against this wedding-day". Three fealties to Thursday are sworn in one short scene, by the father who appoints it, the suitor who wishes it nearer, and the mother sent to announce it. An audience keeping count has been given a date with the firmness of a contract. What the scene has also given, less visibly, is the demonstration that the contract's author treats dates as emanations of his own mood. Wednesday was refused on Monday night not because a council sat, or a church was consulted, or a bride was asked, but because the word felt soon in a tired man's mouth. A calendar kept on such terms can be rewritten the moment the mood changes, and the play will not wait long to change it. The appointment is real, sworn, and repeated. It is also, from the moment of its making, exactly as durable as Capulet's temper, which is to say durable until Tuesday evening.

Paris's part in the scene completes the picture of a calendar kept by courtesy rather than by record. This is the suitor who, in Act 1, accepted a postponement measured in summers without a murmur, who came to the feast as a guest and danced attendance on a household whose daughter he had barely addressed. Patience was his established character, and the play takes care to establish it, because patience is what the appointment scene spends. Offered a wedding three days off, after two years of encouraged waiting, Paris does not ask for the register, the banns, or the church's convenience. He wishes the day nearer. The wish is conventional gallantry, and Capulet's sealing of it is conventional hospitality, and between the two conventions a real date is fixed with all the permanence of a toast. Nobody in the scene writes anything down. Nobody consults the church whose morning is being disposed of, Saint Peter's, named a scene later as if the parish were a family possession. The tragedy's Verona is a city of spoken instruments: vows spoken in a garden bind for eternity, sentences spoken in a street exile for life, and dates spoken in a hall at midnight govern a household's week, until the same voice speaks again. A calendar with no archive, kept in the memory of a man who had to ask what day it was, is the appointment's true character, and Thursday's authority, for all its triple sealing, is the authority of an utterance, no stronger than the next utterance from the same mouth.

## Thursday Repeated: A Household Swears by a Date

Act 3 Scene 5 is the announcement scene, and Thursday is its refrain. The dawn parting between the married lovers gives way to Lady Capulet at the chamber door with the news carried up from the night before: "Marry, my child, early next Thursday morn / The gallant, young, and noble gentleman, / The County Paris, at Saint Peter's Church, / Shall happily make thee there a joyful bride" (3.5.113-116). The phrasing is ceremonial, almost a proclamation, and its precision matters. The day is named, the church is named, the groom is named, and the bride is addressed as the one person in the arrangement who has not been consulted. Juliet's refusal detonates the scene, and the detonation is conducted in the same vocabulary of dates. When Capulet enters and learns that his daughter will not be grateful, his rage keeps returning to the appointment like a tongue to a broken tooth: the wedding stands "Thursday next", the bride will go "to Saint Peter's Church" on that day or be dragged there, and as the threats escalate the date contracts into a snarl of nearness, "Thursday is near" (3.5.192, in the Folger numbering). A date chosen as a compromise has hardened, within twelve hours of stage time, into the instrument of a father's honour. To move it, after this scene, would be to lose it, and Capulet's whole performance of mastery depends on the appointment being immovable.

The repetition does dramatic work beyond characterization. By the end of Act 3, Thursday has been spoken by Capulet in council, by Lady Capulet in announcement, by Juliet in horrified echo, and by Capulet again in rage, and an audience has heard the word often enough to carry it into Act 4 as a fixed expectation. Fixed expectations are the raw material of this tragedy's irony, because the audience is repeatedly placed ahead of the characters, knowing the marriage to Romeo that makes the Paris wedding bigamy, knowing the vial that makes the death a sleep. The date is about to join that list of things the audience must hold in two versions at once. There is also a quieter asymmetry to notice. Thursday belongs to the public play: it is the day of announcements, proclamations, and threats, the day as the household performs it for suitors, servants, and itself. The rival day, when it comes, will belong to the private play, spoken in a kneeling girl's feigned repentance and a father's delighted aside, and it will override the public day without any proclamation at all. Verona's calendar has two registers, the heralded and the whispered, and the whispered one wins.

One more feature of the announcement scene deserves emphasis, because the advance scene will reverse it. In Act 3 Scene 5, everybody in the Capulet house knows the wedding day. The mother announces it, the father enforces it, the Nurse carries messages around it, and even the bride's refusal is a refusal of a named and dated thing. Knowledge of the date is, for one scene, perfectly distributed. The tragedy of Act 4 will be built by degrading that distribution: the Friar will keep designing for Thursday after Thursday has been cancelled, Paris will keep expecting Thursday until a messenger reaches him offstage, and the one person who acts on the true date, Juliet herself, will act on it alone, at night, with a vial and a knife. The play's catastrophe is often described as a failure of messages. The date change shows the same failure in its purest form, a fact of the highest importance, changed by the one man entitled to change it, and communicated to almost nobody whose plans depended on it.

## The Friar's Calendar in Act 4 Scene 1: Wednesday Is To-Morrow

Act 4 Scene 1 is the planning scene, and it is dated from its first line. Paris arrives at the Friar's cell to arrange the ceremony, and the Friar's greeting is a protest about the calendar before it is anything else: "On Thursday, sir? the time is very short" (4.1.1). Paris answers with the play's most economical account of where haste lives in this household: "My father Capulet will have it so, / And I am nothing slow to slack his haste" (4.1.2-3). The suitor named here as a son, by a father not his own, locates the speed in Capulet's will and confesses his own eagerness to feed it. When Juliet enters and Paris takes his leave of the girl he believes to be his bride, his farewell is a date and a promise fused together: "Juliet, on Thursday early will I rouse ye" (4.1.42). The rousing he imagines is the bridegroom's morning privilege, the ceremonial waking of the bride on her wedding day. The Friar then repeats the appointment to Juliet as the sentence she has come to escape: she must, "and nothing may prorogue it, / On Thursday next be married to this County" (4.1.48-49). Four speeches into the scene and its planning, Thursday has been affirmed by the priest, the groom, and the reluctant bride's own hearing. The plan the Friar is about to build is built, necessarily, on the only wedding day anybody has given him.

The plan's timetable is stated with a precision that invites counting, and counting is where the trouble starts. "Hold, then; go home, be merry, give consent / To marry Paris: Wednesday is to-morrow: / To-morrow night look that thou lie alone" (4.1.89-91). The sentence is the plan's date-stamp, and it fixes three facts at once. The day of the planning is Tuesday, since Wednesday is to-morrow. The dose is to be drunk on Wednesday night, the night of to-morrow, in a chamber cleared of the Nurse. The bridegroom's rousing, which Paris has just promised for Thursday early, will find not a bride but an apparent corpse, since the liquor works within the night. The Friar then gives the sleep its measure, in the number the whole series of source studies has already weighed: "in this borrow'd likeness of shrunk death / Thou shalt continue two and forty hours, / And then awake as from a pleasant sleep" (4.1.105-107). Forty-two hours from a Wednesday-night dose expires on Friday afternoon. The bride found dead on Thursday morning will be carried to the Capulet vault, the Friar's letters will have had their day and more to reach Mantua, and Romeo, warned and instructed, will be waiting at the tomb when the sleeper wakes on Friday, in a Verona that has finished mourning her. The design is not foolish. On the calendar the Friar was given, the design has slack in it, a full day's grace between the funeral and the waking, and the slack is the plan's margin for the ordinary frictions of roads, gates, and messengers. The full anatomy of the scheme, its promises and its machinery, is set out in the series' scene study of [Act 4 Scene 1 and the Friar's desperate potion plan](/2023/10/20/act-4-scene-1-potion-plan/); the concern here is narrower, the dates the scheme assumes and the moment those dates stop being true.

### What does the Friar's "Wednesday is to-morrow" fix beyond the dose's night?

The line fixes the planning scene itself on Tuesday, and with it the plan's entire frame of reference. Because Wednesday is to-morrow when the Friar speaks, the dose belongs to Wednesday night, the discovery to Thursday morning, and the waking to Friday. Every date the plan contains is computed from that one orientation, granted hours before the orientation is destroyed.

For the frame of reference is destroyed the same evening, in the scene that follows, and the destruction never reaches the planner. The letters the Friar promises, "I'll send a friar with speed / To Mantua, with my letters to thy lord" (4.1.124-125), are written to the Tuesday design: they describe a Juliet who will be found dead on Thursday and who will wake on Friday, and they send Romeo to a Friday appointment at the vault. No second letter will ever be written to correct the first one's dates, because no scene in the play shows the Friar learning that the wedding has moved. He will attend the funeral on Wednesday morning and draw his own conclusions from a corpse that has arrived a day ahead of his pharmacology. He will spend the last act reacting to a calendar he did not make. Criticism of the Friar tends to prosecute his plan as reckless, a desperate device from a man meddling past his competence. The date evidence refines the charge. The plan, as designed on Tuesday for Thursday, was proportioned to its timetable, with a day's slack held against misfortune. What was reckless was not the arithmetic. What was reckless was building on Capulet's calendar as if Capulet's calendar were a law of nature, when the play had already shown, in the appointment scene itself, that the calendar was a mood with dates attached.

## Tuesday Evening in Act 4 Scene 2: The Knot Knit Up To-Morrow Morning

Act 4 Scene 2 is the shortest scene in the tragedy's second half and the most consequential per line. Juliet comes home from the Friar's cell with a performance to give, and the scene is usually read for that performance, the kneeling, the apology, the begging of pardon for a disobedience she does not feel. The performance is superb and it matters, because its completeness is the trigger. A partial repentance, a sullen compliance, a daughter merely worn down, would have kept the Thursday appointment in place as an instrument of surveillance, the father watching the days off like a jailer. What Capulet receives instead is total submission, theatrically perfect, and his response to total submission is not gratitude, or suspicion, or even relief. His response is acceleration. "Send for the County; go tell him of this: / I'll have this knot knit up to-morrow morning" (4.2.23-24). The knot metaphor deserves its weight: a knot knit up is a thing tied fast, finished, made incapable of slipping. Capulet advances the wedding for the same reason a man pulls a knot tighter when the rope finally comes into his hands. He has his daughter back, and he will have the marriage done before she can be lost again.

The mechanics of the advance repay a slow reading, because the play stages the change of date as carefully as it staged the appointment. First, the order is given to servants and messengers, not to the bride: the County is to be sent for and told, the household is to be set in motion, and the machinery of cooks and provisions that Act 4 Scene 4 will show at full stretch is started by this sentence. Second, the one objection the change receives is raised and buried inside the scene. Lady Capulet, who announced Thursday with such ceremony a day earlier, protests on the only ground she can see: "No, not till Thursday; there is time enough" (4.2.42, in the Folger numbering). Her objection is logistical, a housekeeper's objection, about food and furnishing and the shortness of the notice, and it is answered by her husband's bustle rather than by argument. Third, the bride herself, the person whose body the date governs, says nothing about the date at all. Juliet's silence in the scene is usually read as the silence of the accomplished liar. It is also the silence of the only person present who knows what the new date costs, who has a vial in her possession measured for a different night, and who cannot trade that knowledge for a protest without detonating the entire design. The date moves through the room like weather, announced by the master, queried by the housekeeper on grounds of catering, and absorbed in silence by the girl it will kill.

### Whose haste actually moves the date in Act 4 Scene 2?

The hand on the calendar is Capulet's alone. Juliet's feigned repentance is the occasion, but the advance is not her request, her hint, or her design: the sentence that moves the wedding is her father's, spoken to servants about a third party, in the grammar of command. Delight merely signs what mastery dictates.

The transformation of the father across the two scenes is the character study the date change performs, and the series' portrait of [Lord Capulet, from loving father to furious tyrant](/2022/12/02/lord-capulet-character-analysis/), traces the full arc of it. The calendar adds a dimension that portraiture alone can miss. In Act 1, Capulet's time was generous and his power relaxed inside it: two summers of waiting cost him nothing, because a thirteen-year-old daughter unmarried was no threat to his house. In Act 3, with Tybalt dead and the feud's costs suddenly itemized in blood, time became threat, and the appointment of Thursday was power reasserting itself over a household that had slipped out of his control into grief and disobedience. In Act 4, with obedience theatrically restored, time becomes appetite. The advance to Wednesday is not policy and not panic. It is a man enjoying the sensation of a house that moves when he speaks, and moving it one day faster than necessary purely to feel the speed. Paris had wished Thursday were to-morrow, as a lover's flourish. Capulet makes Wednesday to-morrow, as a master's fact. The wish and the fact are the tragedy's two kinds of haste standing side by side: the suitor's haste is rhetoric, and harms nobody; the father's haste is executive, and kills his daughter within two days.

## Impulse or Mechanism: What Kind of Thing Is the Advance?

The brief for any account of the date change poses a fork. Either the advance is a piece of character, the impulsive gesture of a choleric father whose temper the play has established from the first scene's reach for a crutch and a sword, or it is a piece of mechanism, a dramatist's device for tightening a plot whose machinery requires the catastrophe to arrive before any message can overtake it. The criticism named in the pages around this question divides along exactly that fork, and adjudicating the division is the quickest route to the verdict the whole article defends. Susan Snyder, in The Comic Matrix of Shakespeare's Tragedies (1979), reads the play's machinery as comedy's machinery running in a world that has stopped forgiving it. The eager father hurrying a wedding belongs to the stock furniture of romantic comedy, where the senex who blocks a marriage and the arrangements that rush one are bent, by the genre's contract, toward a feast and a dance. On Snyder's account, the advance is a comic device photographed at the moment of its failure: the same bustle that fills a comedy's fourth act with cooks and music here fills a house with preparations for a funeral, and the device itself, not any single temper, carries the irony. Coppélia Kahn, in "Coming of Age in Verona", reads the same gesture from inside the household's politics. For Kahn, Capulet's government of the marriage is the tragedy's social engine, a patriarch disposing of a daughter as the instrument of his house's standing, first at leisure, then in grief, then in the flush of restored command. On her account the advance is character all the way down, or rather it is power, which in this house is the same thing as character: the date moves because the father's authority requires a fresh proof of obedience, and a wedding performed ahead of its own announcement is the purest available proof that the house moves at his word.

The editorial tradition stands slightly apart from both, counting days. René Weis, editing the play for the Arden Shakespeare's third series, treats the day-references as a coherent and deliberate scheme, five days from the Sunday feast to the Thursday dawn, with the advance written into the scheme as one of its load-bearing members: the references agree with each other too systematically, and the advance is prepared by too many subsequent scenes, for the seam between Thursday and Wednesday to be a nod. Against that confidence stands an older editorial habit, visible in the chronology notes that generations of editions have printed, of conceding a single discrepancy in the potion's arithmetic and treating the play's time as theatrical rather than calendrical wherever the two diverge. The older habit is not foolish, since the waking does arrive ahead of its forty-two hours, as a later section shows. The disagreement, properly stated, is not whether the numbers all reconcile, since they do not, but what kind of fact a date is in this play: a entry in a ledger the dramatist kept faithfully, or a pressure the dramatist applied and relaxed as the scenes required.

The adjudication this article reaches is that the fork is false, and that the date evidence shows why. Consider what a mere slip would look like. A dramatist nodding over his calendar writes Thursday in one scene and Wednesday in another, and no other line in the play notices. The advance in Act 4 is the opposite of unnoticed. Lady Capulet objects to it on the record. Capulet repeats it as an order to servants and a promise to the groom's messenger. Juliet registers it in soliloquy, alone, with a knife laid by as insurance against the morning it creates. The preparation scene spends a whole night of stage time exhibiting its cost in labour, and the discovery scene converts its feast in front of the audience. A device registered by five separate speeches, in four separate scenes, by characters with opposed interests, is not a slip and not merely a mechanism either. It is a dramatized decision, written the way this play writes all its fatal decisions, as a speech act by a person whose position lets the speech act bind other people's lives. Snyder is right about the machinery the decision rides on, and her comic matrix explains why the bustle feels festive even as it darkens. Kahn is right about whose hand is on the machinery, and her patriarch explains why the decision is taken in delight rather than in need. Weis is right that the dates are meant to be counted, and the older editors are right that the counting eventually outruns the clock. The advance is an impulse that the plot then honours with the full rigour of a mechanism, and that double nature, a father's whim executed with a machine's fidelity, is the tragedy's whole theory of how Verona kills its children.

One further editorial consideration sharpens the adjudication, and it concerns the direction in which an audience's sympathy is steered by each account. If the seam is a slip, the play's maker is at fault and the play is diminished by exactly the size of the error: a great machine with a loose screw, admirable in spite of its calendar. If the seam is a decision, the fault migrates inside the fiction, to the character whose office made the decision binding, and the play is enlarged by exactly the size of the migration: the calendar becomes one more instrument, like the feud, the banishment, and the plague, by which Verona's arrangements for its young are converted into their deaths. Criticism that protects the dramatist by blaming his arithmetic purchases tidiness at the price of the tragedy's meaning, since a Romeo and Juliet whose dates merely wobble is a play about young people destroyed by weather. A Romeo and Juliet whose dates are a father's instruments is a play about young people destroyed by government, which is the harder, colder, and more durable work, the one that survives its competition on the page and the stage alike. The evidence of registration, five speeches in four scenes all keyed to the change, is what licenses the colder reading, and the colder reading is the one the production history rewards whenever a staging lets the audience hear the date move.

## One Vial, One Night: The Dose Moves to Tuesday

The cost of the advance is paid first and most precisely in Act 4 Scene 3, in a chamber, at night, by a girl doing arithmetic she cannot say aloud. Juliet dismisses the Nurse, and her soliloquy begins by confronting the possibility that the Friar's mixture might not work at all. "What if this mixture do not work at all? / Shall I be married then to-morrow morning? / No, no: this shall forbid it: lie thou there" (4.3.21-23). The knife she lays beside her is an insurance against a wedding she names by its new date without emphasis, to-morrow morning, and the quietness of the reference is the scene's most telling detail. No soliloquy protests that the morning has been moved. No aside explains to the audience that the plan has changed. Juliet simply prices the new date into her contingencies, the way a person who has lived inside Capulet's calendar all her life prices in its mutability without surprise. She drinks that night, Tuesday night, a full day before the Friar's Wednesday-night appointment with the vial, because the wedding she must be found dead before is no longer Thursday's. The dose has not changed. The liquor, the measure, and the forty-two hours are what they were in the cell. What has changed is the night available for drinking, and the change propagates through every figure the plan contains.

Follow the propagation, since the play invites it and the criticism of the plan often skips it. Under the Tuesday design, the dose drunk on Wednesday night sleeps through Thursday's discovery and Friday's waking, and the Friar's letters travel inside a timetable with a day of slack. Under the enacted calendar, the dose drunk on Tuesday night must be discovered on Wednesday morning, mourned and buried on Wednesday, and woken from on Thursday, since the enacted action closes at Thursday's dawn inside the delivered five-day frame. The forty-two hours, counted from a late Tuesday dosing, expire on Thursday afternoon, and the tomb scene, which gathers its deaths in the night that ends that Thursday, spends most of them: the enacted waking falls short of the spoken number by hours rather than by the day that looser accounts sometimes claim, and the shortfall is the residue the editorial chronologies have always flagged. The more serious casualty is not the count but the letter. The letter that leaves the Friar's cell on Tuesday describes, in whatever words, a design whose waking falls on Friday. Even delivered perfectly, even carried by a messenger immune to plague and quarantine, it would have sent Romeo to the vault a day late for the sleeper Verona actually produced, or kept him waiting in Mantua while Thursday's events ran without him. The advance falsifies the message before the message fails. Scholarship on the letter tends to prosecute the quarantine that stopped it, as if the road were the plan's only broken bridge. The date evidence shows a second bridge already down behind the first: the plan's paper described a calendar that its author's own parishioner had cancelled within hours of the writing.

### Why does Juliet not protest the new date in Act 4 Scene 3?

Protest is the one currency she has spent the afternoon counterfeiting. Her repentance was a performance whose value lay in its completeness, and any objection to the date, from a girl who has just sworn total obedience, would devalue the performance and reopen the Thursday question on the worst possible terms.

There is a further, quieter point to register about the knife line, because it corrects a habit of reading the soliloquy as pure terror. Juliet's fears in the scene are famous: the mixture might be poison, she might wake early and suffocate, she might go mad among the bones of her ancestors. The date line stands apart from the fears because it is not a fear. It is a scheduling fact, handled with the competence of a project manager: if the instrument fails, the deadline it was bought against arrives to-morrow morning, and a second instrument lies ready. The girl who handles a moved deadline with a contingency plan is not the passive victim of the household's calendar. She is its most fluent reader, the only character who recalibrates instantly and correctly when the date changes, faster than the Friar will, faster than Paris can, and she recalibrates alone because fluency of that kind, in that house, cannot be shared. The tragedy is not that nobody in Verona can keep count of the days. The tragedy is that the one person who keeps count perfectly is fourteen, female, and forbidden to publish her arithmetic.

## Wednesday Morning: The Wedding That Becomes a Funeral

Act 4 Scene 4 is the advance rendered as labour. The Capulet house has been awake all night converting itself from Thursday's timetable to Wednesday's, and its master is discovered in the middle of the conversion, apron-handed and hoarse with orders: "Come, stir, stir, stir! the second cock hath crow'd, / The curfew-bell hath rung, 'tis three o'clock" (4.4.3-4). The hour matters because it is evidence. A household that had kept Thursday would have slept; the preparations would have spread across Wednesday at a tradesman's pace, and the third hour of the morning would have found Verona dark. Instead the scene is all motion at an hour when motion is indecent, servants sent for logs and dates and quinces, the Nurse dispatched to wake the bride, and Capulet boasting of his own wakefulness as a point of honour, a man who has sat up all night for lesser cause and will not be sent to bed by a wedding. The scene is comic in texture and the comedy is Snyder's point made visible: this is the fourth act of a festive comedy, the bustle before the feast, except that the audience carries knowledge the bustlers lack, and every basket carried across the stage is being carried toward a funeral. The advance is what put the house on this footing. Wednesday morning exists as a scene at all because Tuesday evening's sentence demanded it, and the scene's energy is the energy of a calendar being obeyed at ruinous speed by people who believe they are obeying it for a celebration.

Act 4 Scene 5 then performs the conversion the whole sequence has been approaching. The Nurse's discovery, the parents' entrance, the groom's arrival with music and flowers for a rousing that becomes a keening: the morning Paris promised in the Friar's cell arrives on schedule and finds its purpose inverted. He had said, on Thursday early will I rouse ye, and he does rouse the house on the wedding morning, a day earlier than his promise, to stand among the mourners of the bride he came to wake. Capulet's great speech of conversion, in which every article prepared for the feast is renamed for the grave, is the father's calendar collapsing in his own mouth: the wedding cheer becomes funeral food, the solemn hymns become dirges, the bridal flowers serve for a buried corse. What the speech never does is name the day. Grief in this scene has no use for Wednesday or Thursday, and the absence is itself a kind of evidence. Dates belong to intention, to plans and appointments and the will that makes them. Once intention is void, the calendar that organized the play's second half simply stops being spoken, and the final act will name no weekday at all. The tragedy's remaining hours are dated only by darkness and dawn, as if the week itself had been cancelled along with the wedding.

The morning has a coda, and the coda is dated too. After the lamentations, Peter detains the musicians who were hired for the wedding and begs a merry dump, a tune, some scrap of the festivity they were engaged to provide, and the exchange curdles into the scene's famous squabble over silver sounds and heart's ease. The musicians are the advance's humblest witnesses. They were not engaged for Thursday in any sense that survived Tuesday evening; they were re-engaged, like the cooks and the quinces and the groom, for a morning created by a single sentence, and they stand in the hall at dawn with instruments tuned for a feast, negotiating their dismissal from a funeral. Comedy of that kind is sometimes treated as relief, a valve opened after intensity. In the calendar reading it is evidence of another order: proof that the new date propagated all the way down the household's economy, to day labourers hired by the morning, before it failed. A whim at the top of a house becomes wages, provisions, and tuned strings at the bottom within hours. The advance was not a private resolve. It was a public instrument, executed by dozens of hands, and its public character is what makes its reversal impossible by the time anyone with knowledge is awake to attempt one.

It is worth stating plainly what the morning proves, since the article's verdict depends on the proof. The discovery, the funeral preparations, the procession to the vault, and the Friar's attendance all take place on the morning Capulet created in Act 4 Scene 2, the morning after the Tuesday of the advance. No character arrives expecting Thursday. No servant protests that the wedding is a day early. Paris, who had the strongest contractual interest in Thursday, appears on Wednesday without a syllable of surprise, because the messenger Capulet dispatched in the advance scene, "go tell him of this", has done its work offstage: the groom has been re-dated like everyone else. The enacted calendar, in other words, is not a critic's construction imposed on a reluctant text. It is the calendar the entire household, the groom, the church's musicians, and the priest all act on, unanimously and without comment, from the moment the advance is spoken. Thursday, by contrast, is acted on by nobody after Act 4 Scene 1. It persists as a word in old speeches, a dead letter of a date. The distinction between the appointed day and the enacted day is not a nuance laid over the play by tidy scholars. It is the difference between what Verona says and what Verona does, and the play's catastrophe happens in the gap.

## The Waking Re-Timed: Thursday Dawn and the End of the Week

The delivered chronology of the whole action, Sunday's feast to Thursday's dawn across five calendar days, is the frame the series' timeline study, [how long the action takes, from Sunday to Thursday](/2030/07/26/how-long-does-the-action-take/), establishes in full, and the wedding question lives inside that frame as its most stressed joint. The last act spends the advanced calendar to its end. Balthasar rides to Mantua with news of a funeral that happened on Wednesday, the funeral Thursday was never given the chance to host. Romeo buys poison and rides back inside a night the play declines to date. In the Friar's cell, in Act 5 Scene 2, the planner finally confronts the wreck of his timetable: the letter has never left Verona, stopped by a quarantine in a house of suspected plague, and Lawrence's response prices the waking by the enacted calendar rather than by the one he designed: "Within this three hours will fair Juliet wake" (5.2.22, in the Folger numbering). Three hours, spoken on the day after the funeral, means a Thursday waking, the advanced schedule's waking, not the Friday waking of the cell speech in Act 4. The Friar has silently re-based his expectations on the new calendar, as everyone else in Verona has, and the re-basing is never acknowledged by him or by anyone. The plan's author has stopped believing his own dates without noticing that he has stopped.

That silence is the deepest evidence in the play for how the date change should be judged. A slip, repeated and absorbed until even its victim plans around it, behaves in performance exactly like a decision. Editors who print chronologies have therefore been driven to a characteristic compromise, and it is worth describing accurately rather than mocking. The standard chronology keeps both truths in separate columns: the day-references are tabulated scene by scene, the appointment and the advance both honoured, and a bracketed note concedes that the potion's forty-two hours and the tomb scene's arrival do not perfectly agree, the one discrepancy in an otherwise precise accounting. The compromise is honest, and its honesty points at the right conclusion. The day-names agree with each other completely, once the advance is admitted as an event inside the fiction rather than an error outside it: Monday appoints Thursday, Tuesday advances Wednesday, Wednesday discovers, Thursday wakes and dies. The only figure that refuses the reconciliation is a duration, forty-two hours, spoken by a character whose information was already obsolete when he acted on it. The play's calendar is consistent. The play's clock, in one speech, is not, and the inconsistency belongs to the Friar's plan, not to Capulet's week.

### Why do editions annotate the seam instead of removing it?

Annotation is the only tool that fits the evidence, because there is nothing to emend. The day-names stand in the early texts without a rival reading an editor could choose instead: Thursday is genuinely appointed in Act 3 and genuinely superseded in Act 4. Altering a name to harmonize the week would delete the mechanism to tidy the arithmetic.

Performance faces the same evidence with fewer options. A production that plays Act 4 Scene 2 in full, objection and all, gives its audience the experience the text designs: the small shock of recognition that a date sworn three times in Act 3 has been unsaid in one line, and the dawning arithmetic of what the line costs. A production that trims the scene, as many do under the pressure of the act's length, keeps the catastrophe and loses its cause, and audiences leave such performances blaming fate, or the plague, or a friar's incompetence, for what the full text assigns to a father's delighted sentence. The advance also resists the most common staging shortcut, the dreamy timelessness in which the lovers float outside any week at all. Verona in the text has a week, a working week with named days and a church appointed for one of them, and the lovers die inside it on schedule, killed less by timeless passion than by a timetable revised once too often. Directors who restore the calendar, letting a household visibly re-time itself overnight, recover the tragedy's least sentimental truth: the instrument of death was an appointment book.

## Two Summers to Three Days: The Shrinking of a Father's Time

The appointment of Thursday cannot be weighed without the appointment Capulet refused in Act 1, because the two scenes are the same negotiation photographed at different temperatures. In Act 1 Scene 2, Paris sues for Juliet and is told to wait: she has not seen the change of fourteen years, let two more summers wither in their pride before she is thought ripe to be a bride. The measure of the refusal is agricultural, seasonal, almost geological: summers, ripening, pride of seasons. Capulet in that scene is a landlord of time, rich in it, spending none. Between that scene and Act 3 Scene 4 lie a feast, a death in the street, and a banishment, and the landlord returns to market as a bankrupt. Thursday, appointed on Monday night, is three days away. The interval between a father's first answer and his second is the measure of what the feud has cost his house: a unit of patience that shrank from two summers to seventy-two hours without any speech in the play remarking the shrinkage. The audience is left to remark it, and the remark matters for the advance, because the advance is the shrinkage continued by one more step. A man whose patience has already collapsed from years to days is not making a new kind of decision when he collapses it from days to hours. He is continuing an established trajectory at its established acceleration, and Act 4 Scene 2 surprises only the characters. To the audience that remembers Act 1, the advance is the third term of a sequence, two summers, three days, to-morrow morning, each term a fraction of the last, and the sequence's limit, by the mathematics the play has taught, is the zero hour of Act 4 Scene 5, a wedding with no time left in it at all.

The sequence also disciplines a common misreading of the appointment scene, in which Thursday is treated as Capulet's cruelty and Wednesday as his caprice, two unrelated vices. The text's own arrangement argues otherwise. Thursday was already the product of the same force that produced Wednesday: the conversion of a daughter's marriage into an instrument for managing a household emergency. Tybalt's death created the emergency, a house in mourning, a daughter weeping in a way her father misread as grief for her cousin, a suitor whose suit could be converted into good cheer by decree. Thursday was the emergency measure, dressed in the compromise formula of a man who still believed he was being moderate, refusing Wednesday as too soon. Wednesday is the same measure with the dressing removed, taken when the emergency appeared to lift and the father's pleasure in command replaced his fear of scandal as the governing emotion. Cruelty and caprice are both present, but they are one trait under two weathers: the belief that a date in his house is a function of his feeling about the house. The play gives that belief a name in Paris's mouth, in the cell, before either scene of Act 4 has run: her father, the suitor says, in his wisdom hastes our marriage. Wisdom is flattery, and the play knows it, but the flattery is precise about the location of the haste. It has one address in Verona, and every date in the tragedy is posted from it.

## Paris Re-Dated: The Groom Who Hears the Sentence Secondhand

Paris is the advance's most instructive victim, because he is the only principal whose consent to the new date is never staged. Trace his itinerary through the day-references and a pattern emerges that the busier scenes conceal. On Monday night he receives Thursday as a gift and wishes it were to-morrow. On Tuesday, at the Friar's cell, he behaves as Thursday's owner, arranging the ceremony, enduring the Friar's protest about the shortness of the time, and taking leave of Juliet with the rousing promise that fixes his expectation in the audience's ear. He exits the play's knowledge at that point. The advance, when it comes, is communicated to him by an errand: Capulet's order to send for the County and tell him of this is the last the text says of the matter, and Paris's re-dating happens entirely offstage, in the custody of a messenger whose name and errand no scene records. When he reappears, in Act 4 Scene 5, he is standing in a house of mourning on Wednesday morning with his musicians and his flowers, fully converted to the new calendar, mourning a bride whose wedding day he has accepted at second hand. The groom of Verona learns his own wedding day the way Verona learns everything in this play that matters, by report, and the report, for once, arrives. The contrast is structural and bitter. The one message in the tragedy that is delivered accurately and on time is the message that advances the catastrophe. The Friar's letter miscarries; Capulet's errand flies.

The flowers Paris carries deserve their sentence in the argument, because they follow him to the tomb and complete his calendar. In Act 5 Scene 3 he comes to the Capulet vault at night to strew the bride's grave with flowers and to perform, in his own phrase for the rite, a nightly observance of grief. The wedding flowers of Wednesday morning have become grave flowers by Thursday night, and Paris, who never consented onstage to the day that killed his bride, dies defending her grave against the man he believes has come to dishonour it. His dying request is to be laid beside Juliet in the tomb, a marriage in death granted by Romeo's hand within moments of the request. The itinerary gives the date change its final irony. Paris wished, on Monday night, that Thursday were to-morrow, and the wish was the only one in the play granted in full and on schedule: a day was indeed removed from his waiting. The removal cost him the bride, the wedding, and his life, in that order, and the groom who was re-dated by messenger is buried by the calendar he never got to read. Criticism sometimes dismisses Paris as furniture, a name in a marriage contract. The day-references restore his outline: he is the tragedy's purest example of a man whose life is scheduled by other people's sentences, appointed by a father, promised by a priest, advanced by an errand, and ended by a misunderstanding, without one scene in which a date is ever put to him as a question.

## Two Weddings in One Week: The Marriage Made and the Marriage Appointed

Verona schedules two weddings for Juliet in the same week, and their days mirror each other with a precision the date debate should not waste. The real wedding, the marriage to Romeo in the Friar's cell, is performed on Monday afternoon, arranged that morning, consented to the night before, and witnessed by a priest and, in a sense the play insists on, by God. The appointed wedding, the marriage to Paris at Saint Peter's Church, is decreed on Monday night, some hours after the real one has already taken place, and is aimed first at Thursday and then at Wednesday by a father who does not know he is scheduling a bigamy. The sequence is the tragedy's central irony expressed as a calendar. Every subsequent naming of a wedding day in the play is false in law before it is anything else: Thursday is the day appointed for a ceremony that could not validly occur on any day, since the bride is a wife. The haste that appoints and advances the Paris wedding is therefore haste in the service of an impossibility, and the play's time pressure, often admired as pure acceleration, is more exactly described as a household racing toward an appointment that its own secret has already voided. Romeo and Juliet's Monday marriage is the fixed fact. Thursday and Wednesday are two versions of a fiction, the second version merely closer.

The mirror sharpens when the two weddings' preparations are compared. The real wedding has no preparations at all in the festive sense: a cell, a priest, a rope ladder for the night, a bride who walks to her marriage from a morning's errand. The appointed wedding consumes the household: cooks, quinces, logs, musicians, a church, a groom's flowers, a father's sleepless night. Verona spends its substance on the wedding that cannot happen and spends nothing on the wedding that did, and the advance increases the spending precisely as it decreases the possibility, since each hour removed from the timetable is an hour in which the truth might have surfaced and did not. There is a sense in which Capulet himself half-knows the proportion is wrong. His Act 3 appointment was accompanied by an apology for the haste, a plea that the times had fallen out unluckily; his Act 4 advance is accompanied by no apology at all, only by orders. The fiction's second version is more confident than its first, and confidence, in this play's accounting, is the most expensive substance a father can buy. By Wednesday morning the household has spent a night's labour, a feast's provisions, and a daughter, on a ceremony whose impossibility was sealed before the first date for it was ever named, in the same cell where the rescue plan would later be dated with equal futility. The calendar and the sacrament fail together, and the wedding question, pursued honestly, ends at that joint failure: Wednesday or Thursday hardly matters to the validity of the marriage, and matters entirely to the timing of the deaths, which is why the play, a tragedy and not a treatise, keeps its eyes on the days.

## The Days Nobody Names: Sunday, Tuesday, and Friday

A close reading of the wedding day should also inventory the days the play declines to name, because the pattern of naming and silence is part of the design. Three weekdays are spoken aloud in the tragedy: Monday, anchored by Paris's answer in Act 3 Scene 4; Wednesday, spoken first as a refusal and then as a plan's orientation; and Thursday, spoken by nearly everyone until the advance strands it. Three weekdays are never spoken at all. Sunday, the day of the street brawl, the feast, and the first meeting, is reconstructed by editors counting backward from Monday, and its anonymity suits it: the day of love's beginning belongs to a part of the story before calendars, a holiday outside the working week of appointments that will kill the lovers. Tuesday, the day of the plan and the advance and the dose, is the tragedy's busiest day and its most silent, named by no character even while every character acts inside it. The omission has a logic an audience can feel. Tuesday is the day on which Verona's fate is actually decided, in a cell, a hall, and a chamber, in speeches about other days: the Friar speaks of Wednesday and Thursday, Capulet of to-morrow, Juliet of to-morrow morning. The day itself goes unnamed because nobody in it is looking at it; every eye is fixed on a future morning, and the present day, the only one in which rescue was possible, passes without a name like a servant nobody thanks.

Friday completes the pattern with the tragedy's saddest silence. Friday is the day the plan was aiming at, the day of the intended waking, the morning after the bridegroom's rousing that was to find a corpse and the afternoon that was to restore a living girl to a waiting husband in a vault. No character ever speaks the word. The Friar, who computed it, does not name it in the cell, preferring the relative arithmetic of to-morrow and two and forty hours. Romeo, who was to keep the appointment, never hears of it. By the time Friday could have arrived, the play has been over for a day, its stage filled at Thursday's dawn with the bodies of the young and the apologies of the old. The unnamed Friday hangs over the tragedy's ending as the ghost of the calendar that should have been: a day with a waking in it, a day of explanations and a journey to Mantua, a day the audience is left to imagine precisely because the text withholds its name. Shakespeare's naming pattern, read whole, is a map of the tragedy's knowledge. The days that are named are the days men appoint and announce. The days that go unnamed are the day love begins, the day the fatal decisions are taken, and the day the rescue was due. Verona can name its intentions fluently. Its realities pass in silence, and the wedding question, Wednesday or Thursday, sits exactly on the border between the two kinds of day: one name spoken by authority until it stopped being true, and one name enacted by a household until it could not be unsaid.

## Verdict: Thursday Was Appointed, Wednesday Was Enacted, and the Difference Kills

The question the title poses has a two-part answer, and both parts are true in different registers. In the register of intention, appointment, and sworn public fact, the wedding day is Thursday: appointed on Monday night with a doubled formula, announced on Tuesday morning as proclamation, sworn to by the groom in the Friar's cell, and built into the only rescue plan the play contains. In the register of action, preparation, and consequence, the wedding day is Wednesday: ordered on Tuesday evening, objected to and sustained in the same scene, prepared for through a sleepless night, dressed for by a groom who arrives with flowers, and voided over an apparently dead bride before the church bells of Saint Peter's can be rung for it. The two registers cannot be reconciled into one calendar, because the plan that mediates between them was written for the first and executed inside the second. A dose measured for Wednesday night is drunk on Tuesday night. A letter describing a Friday waking travels toward a Thursday event. A friar prices a waking by a timetable he never agreed to change. The seam between the days is not a blemish on the tragedy's surface. The seam is where the tragedy's force is concentrated, the narrow place where a father's delight, a daughter's silence, a priest's obsolete arithmetic, and a suitor's fulfilled wish all pass through a single renamed morning. Capulet asked what day it was because he did not know. The play's answer, delivered across two acts, is that the day is whatever he says it is, and that a household, a church, and a rescue can all be killed by a calendar with that property. Wednesday is the wedding day, because Wednesday is the day Verona obeyed.

The distinction travels beyond the seminar room, because the wedding day is one of the facts about the play that general knowledge gets confidently wrong. Reference summaries, study aids, and examination answers repeat Thursday with the serenity of the characters who swore to it, and the repetition is understandable: Thursday is the day the play says most often, and a reader moving at speed remembers sayings. The enacted day rewards the slower reader, the one who tracks what a household does after its master speaks, and the discipline the distinction teaches is the discipline the whole tragedy demands. Again and again in this play, the spoken instrument and the enacted fact diverge: a marriage spoken in a cell is realer than a wedding announced on a staircase, a death reported in Mantua is falser than a sleep witnessed in a vault, a pardon promised by a prince arrives behind a corpse. Learning to answer "Wednesday, because the question is about the event; Thursday, because the question is about the appointment" is learning to read the play's deepest habit, the habit by which Verona's words keep perfect faith with each other and none at all with the world.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which day does Paris believe is his wedding day when he leaves the Friar's cell in Act 4 Scene 1?

Thursday, without a shadow of hesitation. Paris opens the scene by arranging the ceremony for Thursday, answers the Friar's protest about the shortness of the time by laying the haste on Capulet's will, and takes his leave of Juliet with the promise that fixes his expectation in the audience's hearing: "Juliet, on Thursday early will I rouse ye" (4.1.42). Nothing in the scene warns him that the appointment is fragile, and nothing in his manner suggests a man quoting a provisional date. He believes in Thursday the way a bridegroom believes in a contract his future father-in-law has sealed three times. The advance that cancels his belief happens in the following scene, in a house he has left, and reaches him afterward by messenger. His Thursday is therefore the measure of the information problem the whole act is built on: the groom's certainty is perfect, current, and obsolete within hours, and the play lets the audience watch a man walk out of the story's knowledge while remaining entirely inside its danger.

### Q: What does the Friar's phrase "Wednesday is to-morrow" prove about the day on which Act 4 Scene 1 takes place?

The phrase proves that the planning scene falls on Tuesday. When the Friar tells Juliet to give consent to marry Paris and adds "Wednesday is to-morrow: / To-morrow night look that thou lie alone" (4.1.90-91), the sentence only makes sense spoken on the day before Wednesday, and every other reference in the scene locks into place around that orientation. Paris has just promised to rouse the bride on Thursday early, the wedding day the Friar protests is very short in coming, and the dose is assigned to the night between the planning and the wedding, which the phrase identifies as Wednesday night. Editors building the play's chronology treat the line as the plan's date-stamp because it is the one moment where a character states the present day by relation rather than leaving it to inference. Its evidentiary value runs in both directions: it dates the scene, and it dates the design, proving that the rescue was engineered for a Thursday wedding by a man standing in Tuesday, with Wednesday night as his chosen ground.

### Q: If the wedding had stayed on Thursday, on which night was Juliet meant to drink the potion?

Wednesday night, the night immediately before the appointed wedding. The Friar's instructions in Act 4 Scene 1 are sequenced with care: consent is to be given on the Tuesday of the planning, the chamber is to be cleared of the Nurse on to-morrow night, and the vial is to be drunk in bed that same night, since "Wednesday is to-morrow" and the bridegroom is to come rousing on Thursday early. The sleep's forty-two hours would then run from Wednesday night to Friday afternoon, carrying Juliet past the Thursday discovery, the Thursday funeral, and a full day of mourning in the vault, with Romeo warned by letter and waiting at the waking. The design's elegance is easy to miss once its replacement is familiar. The dose was never meant to be drunk on the night of its own planning, and Juliet was never meant to act on the scheme within hours of hearing it. A clear night separated the conspiracy from its execution, a night in which second thoughts, messages, and corrections all remained possible. The advance abolished that night entirely.

### Q: On which night does Juliet actually drink the potion, and which of her own lines shows the new date already priced into her thinking?

She drinks it on Tuesday night, the night of the planning itself, within hours of receiving the vial. The line that proves she has absorbed the new date is her contingency question in Act 4 Scene 3, spoken alone over the mixture: "What if this mixture do not work at all? / Shall I be married then to-morrow morning? / No, no: this shall forbid it: lie thou there" (4.3.21-23). The wedding she fears surviving to attend is to-morrow morning's, which on the Tuesday of the soliloquy is Wednesday, the advanced day, and the knife she lays beside her is the insurance she buys against that morning. Nothing in the speech protests the change, expresses surprise at it, or wastes a syllable wishing Thursday back. The date has been revised by her father between her return home and her bedtime, and her soliloquy treats the revision as settled fact while it agonizes over everything else, poison, suffocation, and madness among the bones. The line is the audience's confirmation that the enacted calendar has replaced the appointed one inside the only mind that could have challenged it.

### Q: What morning was Paris appointed to rouse Juliet, and on what morning does he arrive at her house instead?

He was appointed to rouse her on Thursday morning, and he arrives instead on Wednesday morning. The promise is made in the Friar's cell with a bridegroom's confidence: "Juliet, on Thursday early will I rouse ye" (4.1.42), imagining the ceremonial waking of a bride by the man she is to marry that day. The advance moves the appointment a full day nearer, the messenger reaches him offstage, and Act 4 Scene 5 opens on the substituted morning, with Paris entering a house of preparation turned to mourning, flowers in hand, to stand among the lamenters over the body of the girl he came to wake for a wedding. The rousing he performs is therefore the tragedy's bitterest kept appointment: the groom does come early, does come to the bride's chamber door, and does wake the household, exactly as promised, on the wrong day and for the opposite ceremony. His punctuality survives the date change intact. Only the meaning of the morning has been inverted, from the first hour of a marriage to the first hour of a funeral.

### Q: Does any scene show the Friar being told that the wedding day has changed?

No scene shows it, and the absence is one of the play's quietest structural facts. The Friar designs the rescue in Act 4 Scene 1 for a Thursday wedding, dispatches his letter toward Mantua on that design, and is next seen in Act 4 Scene 5 presiding over a funeral that his own timetable says should not be happening for another day. Between those appearances, no servant, no Capulet, no Juliet sends him word that Capulet has advanced the ceremony. He is left to infer the change from its result, a corpse arriving ahead of his pharmacology, and his conduct at the funeral, calming the parents and hurrying the body to the vault, is the conduct of a man recalculating in public while concealing that he is recalculating at all. By Act 5 Scene 2 his expectations have been silently re-based on the new calendar, since he prices the waking a matter of hours away. The plan's author thus learns of his plan's obsolescence the way Verona learns everything fatal in the play, late, indirectly, and without any scene in which the information is honestly handed over.

### Q: Which errand carries the news of the advance to Paris, and why does its success matter beside the Friar's failed letter?

The errand is Capulet's own order in Act 4 Scene 2: "Send for the County; go tell him of this" (4.2.23), spoken in the same breath as the sentence that moves the wedding. A servant is dispatched to inform the groom that his bride has repented and that his wedding has been brought a day nearer, and the errand evidently succeeds, since Paris appears on Wednesday morning correctly re-dated, with musicians and flowers, expecting the ceremony the new calendar assigns him. Its success matters because it is the one message in the tragedy's second half that is delivered accurately, promptly, and to the right person, and it is the message that advances the catastrophe. Set beside the Friar's letter, stopped in a quarantined house and never delivered at all, the pattern is exact and bleak. Verona's channels work. What fails is not communication in the abstract but beneficial communication: the news that would rescue is the news that miscarries, and the news that destroys travels at the speed of a father's delight. The date change is the clearest case, since its transmission can be timed against the letter's failure inside the same act.

### Q: Do modern editors change any of the play's day-names to make the week agree with itself?

No editor of standing changes a day-name, because there is nothing in the early texts to change to. The names stand in the second quarto and the Folio without a rival reading that would let an editor choose Thursday where Wednesday is printed, or Wednesday where Thursday stands, and the apparent contradiction is not a corruption of the words but a sequence of events inside the fiction: a date appointed, a plan built on it, and a date advanced past it. Editorial energy therefore goes into chronology rather than emendation. Editions tabulate the action day by day, mark the appointment in Act 3 Scene 4 and the advance in Act 4 Scene 2, and confine their disagreement to notes about the potion's forty-two hours and the tomb scene's arrival. The restraint is itself an interpretation, and a sound one. An editor who harmonized the week by altering a name would be deleting the tragedy's mechanism to tidy its arithmetic, converting a father's dramatized decision into a printer's error. The seam stays in the text because the seam is the play, and the notes exist to teach readers to see it working.

### Q: What work does the sleepless night of Act 4 Scene 4 do for the date question?

The scene is the advance made visible, and its work is evidentiary before it is comic. A household keeping the Thursday appointment would have slept through the Tuesday night and spread its preparations across Wednesday at a tradesman's pace. Instead the stage fills before dawn with a master who has been up all night, calling the hour himself: "the second cock hath crow'd, / The curfew-bell hath rung, 'tis three o'clock" (4.4.3-4), while servants hunt logs and quinces and the Nurse is sent to wake the bride. Every basket carried across that stage is proof that the new calendar has been obeyed at the level of labour, by people who had no vote in setting it and no doubt about its authority. The scene also fixes the discovery morning beyond argument. The preparations exhibited are for a wedding that morning, mounted at ruinous speed because the morning was created a day early, and the audience watches the cost of one sentence in Act 4 Scene 2 being paid in other people's sleep. Remove the scene and the advance remains a line. With it, the advance is an economy.

### Q: What happens to Paris's wish that Thursday were to-morrow?

The wish is granted, almost in its own words, and the granting destroys the wisher. On Monday night, offered Thursday as his wedding day, Paris answers with a lover's flourish: "My lord, I would that Thursday were to-morrow" (3.4.29). He means that eagerness would gladly skip the interval. On Tuesday evening Capulet removes a day from the interval, not for Paris's sake and without Paris in the room, and by Wednesday morning the groom's waiting is indeed over, a full day sooner than appointed. No other wish in the play is fulfilled in its own terms, or so promptly. Romeo wishes for death and receives it late and indirectly; Juliet wishes for night and receives a night that ends the marriage; the Friar wishes for reconciliation and receives statues. Paris alone gets the precise thing he asked for, a wedding day pulled toward him by twenty-four hours, and the fulfilment costs him the bride, the ceremony, and within two more days his life at her grave. The play's arithmetic of desire could hardly be stated more cleanly: the interval Paris wanted skipped was the interval in which he might have lived.

### Q: Why is Thursday the right answer to some questions about the wedding and the wrong answer to others?

Thursday is the right answer wherever the question concerns intention, appointment, or belief, and the wrong answer wherever the question concerns event. Asked what day Capulet first set, what day Lady Capulet announced, what day Paris expected in the Friar's cell, or what day the rescue plan was designed to defeat, the answer is Thursday in every case, and any other answer is simply incorrect. Asked on what morning the household prepared, the groom arrived with flowers, the bride was found, or the funeral set out for the vault, the answer is Wednesday, and Thursday becomes the error. Most confusion about the wedding day comes from treating the two families of question as one. Students inherit the appointment because the appointment is what the characters keep saying, and the saying is louder than the doing. The text, read with its scenes in order, separates the registers cleanly: Thursday is the day of the play's speeches about the wedding, Wednesday is the day of the play's actions around it, and the tragedy is generated precisely where the speeches and the actions stop agreeing with each other.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch date-change note to argue that Wednesday is the enacted wedding day?

An essay should use the note as an evidence ladder, climbing from appointment to enactment one dated speech at a time. The first step establishes the appointment honestly: Thursday was sworn in Act 3 Scene 4, announced in Act 3 Scene 5, and confirmed by the groom and the Friar in Act 4 Scene 1, so the essay concedes the full strength of the opposing answer before displacing it. The second step isolates the superseding act, Capulet's order in Act 4 Scene 2, and shows it meeting every test of an enacted decision: it is given to servants, objected to on the record, communicated to the groom, and priced into the bride's soliloquy that night. The third step follows obedience rather than language, pointing to the sleepless preparations of Act 4 Scene 4 and the discovery morning of Act 4 Scene 5, where the entire household acts on Wednesday without a dissenting voice. The conclusion the ladder supports is precise: Thursday names the wedding as intended, Wednesday names the wedding as staged, and an essay that keeps the two verbs separate can hold both facts without contradiction.

### Q: On the Friar's original timetable, on what day was Juliet supposed to wake, and what day does the tomb scene reach instead?

She was supposed to wake on Friday, and the tomb scene reaches her on Thursday. The original timetable is computed from the cell speech in Act 4 Scene 1: the dose assigned to Wednesday night, the sleep measured at two and forty hours, and the waking therefore due on Friday afternoon, with Romeo summoned by letter to be present in the vault when the sleeper stirred. The enacted calendar runs a day faster at every point. The dose is taken on Tuesday night, the discovery and funeral fall on Wednesday, and the final night at the vault, with Paris, Romeo, and the Friar converging on the body, belongs to Thursday, closing at that day's dawn. The plan's Friday thus never occurs inside the play at all. It exists only as an appointment in a dead letter and an expectation in its author's head, and the girl who was to wake into a rescue wakes instead into the aftermath of two suicides. An essay on the tragedy could take the missing Friday as its emblem: the day the design promised, which the advance quietly deleted from the week.

### Q: How does the Wednesday Capulet rejects on Monday night become the Wednesday he commands on Tuesday evening?

The day does not change; the father's feeling about his household changes, and the day follows the feeling both times. On Monday night Wednesday is refused because grief is governing the house: a nephew lies dead, a daughter is weeping, and even a hurried father can feel that a Wednesday wedding, two days after the funeral meats, would advertise the indecent haste Paris is urging. Thursday is chosen as the respectable face of an indecent decision, the soonest day that can be called not too soon. On Tuesday evening the government of the house passes from grief to delight. Juliet has knelt, apologized, and performed the obedience whose absence had humiliated him, and the recovered sensation of mastery is sweet enough to spend. Wednesday is commanded because the obstacle that made it too soon was never calendrical; it was the appearance of disorder in his house, and the appearance has been cured by a performance. The rejected day returns wearing the authority of the man who rejected it, and nobody in the room, least of all its author, remarks that the week has been rewritten by the same hand in opposite directions on consecutive evenings.

### Q: Does the advance make the Friar's plan impossible, or only unforgiving?

Only unforgiving, and the distinction is the tragedy's exact measure of blame. Nothing in the advanced calendar prevents the plan from working as far as its mechanics go. The vial can be drunk a night early, and is. The discovery, funeral, and vault all proceed on the substituted morning without exposing the sleep as a sleep. A letter delivered in good time, even one written to the old timetable, would have brought Romeo to Verona inside a window the advanced schedule still leaves open, and the Friar, standing at the vault as he intended, could have managed a waking displaced by hours. What the advance removes is not feasibility but slack. Under the Thursday design, the plan holds a full spare day against the ordinary failures of roads, gates, quarantines, and human error, and a single failure can be absorbed. Under the Wednesday enactment, the spare day is gone, the letter's dates are stale, the waking has moved, and the first failure in the chain meets no margin anywhere behind it. The plan dies of the second misfortune because the advance had already spent its allowance for the first.

### Q: What is lost when a production cuts or compresses the date change in Act 4 Scene 2?

The catastrophe loses its cause, and the audience is left blaming the wrong agents. Act 4 Scene 2 is short, domestically quiet, and often trimmed under pressure of the act's length, yet it contains the single sentence on which the tragedy's timetable turns, together with the objection that proves the sentence was heard and the silence that proves its cost could not be spoken. A production that cuts or hurries the exchange still shows a wedding moved, in effect, but it no longer shows the moving as a decision: a father's delight, a mother's logistical protest waved aside, a bride absorbing the news without a word. The audience of such a performance tends to distribute blame afterward among fate, the plague that stops the letter, and the Friar's rashness, because those are the causes left visible on the stage. The full text assigns the initiating cause closer to home. Keeping the scene entire restores the play's own accounting, in which the machinery of mischance operates only inside a window that one man's sentence had already narrowed past safety, and restores as well the scene's chill: the most consequential line of the act is delivered as a pleasantry.

### Q: Which weekdays does the play name aloud, and which are left for editors to compute?

Three weekdays are named aloud: Monday, Wednesday, and Thursday. Monday is confirmed by Paris in Act 3 Scene 4, in answer to the play's only direct question about the day. Wednesday is named twice in opposed senses, refused by Capulet in the appointment scene and used by the Friar in Act 4 Scene 1 to orient the plan, "Wednesday is to-morrow". Thursday is named by nearly every character concerned in the wedding, from the appointment through the announcement to the groom's promise in the cell. The remaining three days of the action are computed, not spoken. Sunday, the day of the brawl, the feast, and the first meeting, is inferred by counting back from Monday. Tuesday, the day of the plan, the advance, and the dose, is inferred by counting forward from it. Friday, the day of the intended waking, is inferred from the Friar's forty-two hours and never arrives. The pattern is a map of the tragedy's knowledge: authority names the days it appoints, while the days on which everything actually happens to the lovers pass unspoken.

### Q: Why does the play let Thursday be sworn to so many times before it is cancelled?

The repetitions build the weight the cancellation must carry. A date mentioned once could be altered without cost, and its alteration would register as housekeeping. Shakespeare instead has Thursday sworn by the father in council, proclaimed by the mother on the stairs, echoed by the bride in horror, promised by the groom in the cell, and protested by the priest as very short, five separate oaths across three scenes, until the word carries the full pressure of public expectation. Only then is it cancelled, in one delighted sentence, in a scene where almost nobody present understands what has been spent. The structure mirrors the play's larger handling of vows. The lovers' marriage is sworn once, briefly, in a cell, and holds against everything. The wedding day is sworn five times, ceremonially, in public, and holds against nothing, because the man who swore it kept the power to unswear it and exercised the power the moment obedience pleased him. Repetition in the appointment scenes is therefore not redundancy. It is the sound of a household mistaking volume for permanence, and the cancellation scene is quiet because permanence was never in the volume to begin with.

### Q: What does the word "to-morrow" mean in turn, as the Friar, Capulet, and Juliet each use it in Act 4?

The word names the same Wednesday three times, and changes its nature at each speaking. In the Friar's mouth in Act 4 Scene 1, to-morrow is a unit of design: "Wednesday is to-morrow" orients the plan, and to-morrow night assigns the dose, the word working as an instrument of rescue in a timetable built to save a life. In Capulet's mouth in Act 4 Scene 2, to-morrow is a unit of command: "I'll have this knot knit up to-morrow morning" spends the same day as a wedding, the word working as an instrument of mastery in a timetable built to display obedience. In Juliet's mouth in Act 4 Scene 3, to-morrow is a unit of dread: "Shall I be married then to-morrow morning?" prices the day as a deadline against which a knife must be laid by, the word working as the measure of her isolation. One word, one date, three incompatible futures, and the sequence of its speakings is the tragedy's argument in miniature. Verona's calendar is a single day fought over by three wills, and the will with the least power is the one the day finally kills.
