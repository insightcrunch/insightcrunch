Act 2 Scene 4 is the play's great scene of wordplay under pressure: a street morning in Verona where Mercutio's wit runs at full stretch while a written challenge from Tybalt hangs over every exchange, and where the Nurse's entrance converts the sport into an errand that ends with the wedding arrangements made. The scene's verdict can be stated at the outset. The comedy is not a pause in the tragedy but its dress rehearsal in a lighter key, because the same instruments that make the morning funny, the duel of words, the fencing vocabulary, the insult traded as entertainment, are the instruments that will draw blood in Act 3 Scene 1. Mercutio performs the duel here with puns; Tybalt will perform it with a rapier; the scene's design insists that the two performances are one art at two temperatures. René Weis, in the Arden Third Series edition used for the quotations in this article, prints the scene from the 1599 second quarto, and its proportions deserve notice before any single joke is glossed: the fencing satire, the pun contest, and the Nurse's business divide the scene into three movements, and the threat announced in the first movement is still unpaid when the curtain falls on the third.

The morning-after placement matters. Romeo has spent the night at the Friar's cell being married in prospect and is expected home by friends who know nothing of Juliet. Benvolio opens with the practical question of where Romeo has been, and Mercutio answers with the scene's first joke at the lover's expense: "Without his roe, like a dried herring. O flesh, flesh, how art thou fishified!" (2.4.37 to 38). The gloss is precise. A roe is a fish's eggs and, by sound, the first syllable of Romeo's name stripped of its final vowel in the older pronunciation of the name's second half; a lover without his roe is a fish out of its element and a man out of his desire at once. The wit is diagnostic before it is cruel: Mercutio reads the absence as lovesickness because lovesickness is the only condition his philosophy stockpiles remedies for. Everything the friends believe at the scene's opening is wrong, and the wordplay's brilliance is built on that wrongness, since Romeo's transformation, when it arrives, will be misread as a cure by the very man whose system cannot imagine a wedding.

The scene's position in the act sharpens the design. Act 2 moves between enclosures: the orchard wall at night, the Friar's cell at dawn, the orchard again for the parting. Scene 4 is the act's one sustained stretch of open street in daylight, and the play uses the exposure deliberately. Secrets made in the enclosures are carried through the street by people who do not know them, and the street's inhabitants, the friends, price everything they see by the street's own measures: reputation, readiness, wit. The Chorus at the act's head has already filed the official summary, old desire on its deathbed, new affection gazing at a new heir, in substance; the street scene is where that summary is tested against the evidence of behaviour, and the street gets the evidence wrong with complete confidence. A reader measuring the scene's function should therefore resist the anthology habit of treating it as a detachable comic turn between the balcony and the wedding. Remove it, and the challenge arrives in Act 3 unannounced, Mercutio's death loses its priced context, and the Nurse's errand loses the resistance that proves her competence. The scene is load-bearing, and its load is carried in jokes.

## Tybalt's challenge in Act 2 Scene 4: the letter nobody reads

The challenge enters the scene as talk before it enters as fact, and the form of its arrival is the first piece of evidence for the scene's argument. Benvolio reports it in a single reported sentence: "Tybalt, the kinsman of old Capulet, hath sent a letter to his father's house" (2.4.6 to 7). No document is produced, no servant carries it on, and no character reads a line of it. Mercutio supplies the interpretation before the evidence: "A challenge, on my life" (2.4.8). The exchange that follows is two men reconstructing a text neither has seen, and the reconstruction is confident because the form is conventional. A challenge in the duelling culture of the period was a written cartel whose phrasing followed manuals; the friends can complete the letter from its genre alone. The scene thereby establishes a Verona in which the deadliest communications circulate as common knowledge while the life-saving ones, a friar's letter in Act 5, travel in sealed secrecy and fail. The audience is handed the threat in the scene's first minute and then asked to watch ninety lines of comedy performed underneath it, which is the scene's central tension stated as staging.

### What does Benvolio's guess about Romeo's answer get wrong?

Benvolio's guess assumes the old Romeo, whose honour would oblige him to answer a challenge in kind (2.4.9 to 11). The guess is wrong because the married Romeo will answer with a refusal grounded in a kinship Benvolio cannot know exists, and the refusal, not compliance, is what draws Mercutio into the fight.

Benvolio's error is the scene's kindest irony: the one prediction made in the scene that sounds like prudence is the one the plot will invert most completely, because the challenge's danger was never that Romeo might fight. The danger is that Romeo might not.

Mercutio's response to the news performs the scene's thesis in miniature. Asked whether Romeo is man enough to meet the challenge, he answers with a mock lament for a dead man: "Alas, poor Romeo, he is already dead, stabbed with a white wench's black eye, shot through the ear with a love song, the very pin of his heart cleft with the blind bow-boy's butt shaft" (2.4.12 to 15). The wounds are erotic, the weapons are Cupid's, and the register is the fencing report parodied in advance: stabbed, shot, cleft. The individual weapons gloss precisely. The white wench's black eye is the beloved's gaze weaponized, the dark eye of the pale mistress in the period's beauty code, and its stabbing makes looking a blade. The love song through the ear makes music the second projectile, the serenade as artillery. The blind bow-boy is Cupid, whose butt shaft, the blunt practice arrow used at the butts, and whose pin, the centre peg of the archery target, complete the equipment: Romeo's heart has been used for target practice by a blind child with training weapons. The catalogue's comedy depends on the disproportion between the arsenal and the injury, a sighing fit dressed as a casualty report, and its cruelty depends on the report being, in the only sense the speaker cares about, accurate: the patient is, socially, dead, absent from company, useless to the feud, a name the friends conjure rather than a man they meet. Mercutio narrates his friend as a casualty of love in the vocabulary of the duel, one speech before he narrates Tybalt as a duellist in the vocabulary of fashion. The two portraits are built to be compared, and the comparison is the scene's buried argument: in Mercutio's telling, love and the rapier are rival marksmen competing for the same body, and Romeo's heart already has one bolt in it. When the real challenge is answered in the square, the bolt that kills Mercutio will be struck under Romeo's arm, and the parody's geometry, a friend wounded through the lover's defence, will have been rehearsed here as a joke about a love song.

The challenge also fixes the scene's clocks. Tybalt's grievance dates from the feast; the letter has had a night to travel; Romeo has been absent the same night on the errand that makes the challenge obsolete. Three timelines, the feud's, the letter's, and the marriage's, run side by side through the scene, and only the audience can see all three. The wordplay that fills the stage is therefore never idle: every exchange the friends spend on wit is time in which the unanswered letter ages and the secret wedding approaches its hour. A scene that dramatizes waiting disguises itself as a scene of pure display, and the disguise is the point, since Verona's young men experience their own danger as entertainment precisely because the paperwork of violence moves slower than its performance.

The letter's address repays a second look. Benvolio's report places it at the father's house, not in Romeo's hand, and the destination is the code's logic made postal. The grievance behind the challenge is a household grievance, an uninvited Montague at a Capulet feast, and honour in this world is a household asset held in common, so the cartel is served on the corporation rather than the man. Romeo's absence from home all night, the fact Benvolio's opening questions circle, is therefore more than a lover's irregularity: the house's answer to a formal challenge is being delayed by an empty bed. The friends' joking about where Romeo has been is, in the code's accounting, joking about an unanswered summons, and the comedy's first movement is shadowed by a procedural default nobody names. When the answer finally comes in the square, it will come from the man in person and in the wrong genre, a declaration of love where the code required a cartel, and the household form of the original letter helps explain the square's confusion: Tybalt addressed a house and received a bridegroom.

## The prince of cats: how Mercutio's fencing satire works

Mercutio's portrait of Tybalt is the most quoted passage in the scene and the one whose mechanics reward the closest glossing, because every term in it is drawn from the fencing manuals the portrait pretends to admire. Benvolio asks the practical question, "And is he a man to encounter Tybalt?" and Mercutio answers with the catalogue: "More than Prince of Cats. O, he's the courageous captain of compliments. He fights as you sing prick-song, keeps time, distance, and proportion. He rests his minim rests, one, two, and the third in your bosom; the very butcher of a silk button, a duellist, a duellist, a gentleman of the very first house, of the first and second cause. Ah, the immortal passado, the punto reverso, the hai!" (2.4.18 to 25). The speech is a syllabus disguised as a compliment. Prick-song is music sung from written notes, pricked on the page; a fighter who keeps time, distance, and proportion is performing combat as notation, executing rests like a singer counting pauses, minim rests being the brief silences of the score. The compliment curdles term by term: precision becomes mechanism, mechanism becomes pedantry, and the pedantry is lethal, since the third rest lands in a bosom. Mercutio's satire holds both judgments at once, which is why it survives as the play's sharpest piece of threat assessment: the fashionable style is ridiculous and it kills.

### How does the fencing portrait turn compliments into a death sentence?

Each compliment converts, on inspection, into a count in an indictment. The captain of compliments commands the ceremony that obliges a man to fight over graded insults. The butcher of a silk button places a blade within a button's width. The portrait's grammar is praise; its arithmetic is a casualty list.

Mercutio, who will die of the style he mocks, is reading his own death warrant aloud in the accent of a fashion review, and the gentleman of the first and second cause has mastered the manual's taxonomy of quarrels, the ranked causes for which the code demands satisfaction.

The Italian terms carry the satire's cultural charge. Passado, the forward thrust of the Italian school; punto reverso, the reversed or backhanded point; hai, the cry of the hit, from the Italian for the second person of having, the fencer's announcement that the touch has landed: the vocabulary imports a whole technology of honour from the fencing treatises circulating in London, Vincentio Saviolo's manual of honour and honourable quarrels chief among them. The duellist, a word the period was still naturalizing, names the new professional of the code. Mercutio's mockery is ethnographic as much as personal: Tybalt is dangerous because he has outsourced his courage to a system, a book of arithmetic by which quarrels are computed and touches are counted. Against that system the scene will shortly set two rivals, Mercutio's improvisation and Romeo's refusal, and Act 3 will grade all three. The satire's precision about the manual matters because the manual is what makes Tybalt predictable; a fighter by the book can be read like a book, and Mercutio's tragedy in the square is that he reads the style correctly and underestimates the man, mistaking the syllabus for the whole of its student.

The cat completes the portrait's logic. Prince of Cats reaches into the Reynard beast tradition, where Tibert the cat is a prince among animals, proud, ceremonious, and quick with his claws; the name Tybalt echoes the cat's, and the allusion lets Mercutio grant his enemy a mock heraldry. Romeo's dry interjection about the nine lives sets up the portrait's coda: a cat's lives are a stock to be spent, and Mercutio promises to take one of them and, in the ribald arithmetic that follows, beat the remaining eight. The joke's violence is exact. What begins as heraldry ends as a threat to spend a life, delivered in the idiom of animal husbandry, and the audience that laughs is laughing at a scheduled killing described in advance by its eventual victim's closest friend. The full study of the nickname and its honour code belongs to the Tybalt articles in this series; here the portrait's local function is enough: it prices the threat so the comedy that follows can be measured against a known cost.

The compliments deserve their own gloss, since the word has faded since the period used it as a technical term. In the duelling literature, compliments are the formal courtesies of the quarrel: the set phrases of address, deference, and defiance with which gentlemen conducted hostility without surrendering polish. A captain of compliments is thus a master of ceremonies for violence, a man whose courtesy is not the opposite of his aggression but its uniform. Saviolo's treatise on honour and honourable quarrels, Englished in the middle of the last decade of the century, codified exactly this world, grading the lies that demand satisfaction and prescribing the forms by which satisfaction is sought. Mercutio's portrait assumes an audience fluent in that codification, able to hear a syllabus where a stranger hears a compliment. The music terms work the same way. Prick-song, song sung from the pricked or written part, was the period's ready emblem of art held to rule, and the minim rest, a silence of fixed length counted inside the measure, makes the fighter's pauses part of his notation. Tybalt does not merely fight well, on this account; he fights literately, and literacy in a killer is the portrait's coldest joke, since a literate opponent's next move is always on the page for those who have read the same book.

## The challenge as literature: what a cartel said without being read

The friends' instant reconstruction of the unread letter deserves a fuller account, because it reveals how literary Verona's violence is. A cartel, the written challenge of the duello, was a formal instrument with a settled anatomy: the grievance recited, the lie given or the satisfaction demanded, the place and weapons left to arrangement. Its phrasing was taught by the same literature that taught the fencing, and its recipient was expected to answer inside its forms, step by graded step. The first and second cause of Mercutio's portrait belong to that anatomy: the manuals ranked quarrels by the quality of the affront, the lie direct standing highest among the causes that oblige a gentleman to fight, with lesser imputations graded beneath it, so that a combatant's honour could be computed from the rank of the insult he had received. A gentleman of the very first house, in Mercutio's phrase, is a master of that computation, a man who can price an affront to its cause the way a money changer prices coin. The satire's deepest cut is therefore bookkeeping again: Tybalt's courage is an accountancy, and the challenge he has sent is a ledger entry, which is why it can be read without being opened. Everyone in the street knows the form because the form is the culture.

The answer had its literature too, and the scene's talk assumes it. A challenge answered in kind produced a correspondence, cartel answering cartel, each document narrower than the last, until the parties met with seconds to witness the arithmetic settled in person. The correspondence's etiquette is what Benvolio predicts and what Mercutio's portrait presupposes: a paper war with rules of engagement, conducted between houses through their principals, legible to every gentleman in the street. Romeo's marriage has removed him from the correspondence's jurisdiction without notifying its other party, and the scene's comedy of the unread letter has this further layer: the letter is addressed to a correspondent who, in the code's terms, no longer exists, the bachelor son of the house having become, at dawn, a Capulet's husband. The square's catastrophe begins as a jurisdictional error, a summons served on a man whose status changed before the ink dried, and the scene plants the error in its first ten lines under cover of a joke about a challenge on Mercutio's life.

That shared literacy cuts both ways, and the scene is honest about the comfort it provides. Because the challenge is conventional, it feels manageable: a known form, a graded quarrel, an answer due in kind. Benvolio's confidence and Mercutio's satire are both purchased with that familiarity. The tragedy will collect on the familiarity's blind spot, since the answer the challenge receives in the square is composed in no manual: a refusal grounded in a secret marriage, an interposition by the satirist himself, a wound struck under a friend's arm. None of those moves exists in the literature the street has read. The scene's first movement thus establishes the play's documentary tragedy in miniature. Verona is a city that reads its violence fluently and its love not at all, and the unread letter, perfectly understood by everyone, is the emblem of the fluency: the one text in the scene that requires no gloss is the one that kills.

## Prose against verse: the street's element in Act 2 Scene 4

The scene's register is its final piece of self-description. From Benvolio's first question to the Nurse's last courtesy, Act 2 Scene 4 runs in prose, unbroken, the longest sustained stretch of unversified talk in the play's first half. The distribution is meaningful. Verse in this play belongs to the enclosures and the elevations: the lovers' shared sonnet at the feast, the balcony's exchanges, the Friar's couplets of doctrine. Prose belongs to the street, the servants, the market of reputation, and Mercutio is prose's laureate, a speaker whose genius requires the unmetered line the way the fencer requires the open floor. The scene's comedy is therefore formally distinct from the balcony scene's rapture in a way an audience feels before it can name: the night scene climbed, this scene circulates. Nothing in the street rises; everything in it moves sideways, from speaker to speaker, sense to sense, errand to errand. Even the scene's one approach to lyric, Mercutio's hare song, is a catch, a form built for circulation among voices. When Romeo steps out of the prose into his aside with the Nurse, the talk stays prose and changes temperature, proof that the register was never the warmth's source: the street's element can carry an oath as faithfully as a jest, and the scene's argument about instruments is completed by its medium. Wit, satire, logistics, and judgment all run in the same unversified current, and the current's direction, from sport toward business, is the scene in one sentence.

## The wit duel in Act 2 Scene 4: how the pun contest works

Romeo's entrance transforms the scene's engine. He arrives with a greeting, "Good morrow to you both. What counterfeit did I give you?" (2.4.46), and the word counterfeit, a false coin and a false portrait at once, announces the new game: the friends will trade in debased and genuine currency of wit, and the trading has rules. Mercutio's first sally sets the exchange's terms with the slipper joke: "The slipper, sir, the slipper," answered by Romeo's "Pardon, Mercutio, my business was great" in substance, and Mercutio's gloss, "That's as much as to say, such a case as yours constrains a man to bow in the hams" (2.4.48 to 52). The glossing must be exact. A slipper is owed when a dancer's shoe slips; bowing in the hams is the curtsy of apology; the joke converts Romeo's absence into a social debt and his excuse into a bodily stoop. Romeo does not protest the debt. He pays in the same coin, and the duel is joined on the principle the whole exchange will obey: every thrust is a word made to carry two senses, and every parry is the second sense turned back on the first speaker.

The duel's central rally runs on the goose. Mercutio charges Romeo with having given him the slipper; Romeo answers that the slipper is not for a single goose; Mercutio caps it: "Thou hast most kindly hit it" and spins the bird through its senses, goose as fool, goose as prostitute in the slang of the period, goose as the tailor's iron in the trade sense, until Romeo cries the rally's rule aloud: "Nay, good goose, bite not" (2.4.63 to 78, in substance across the exchange). Bite is the rally's hinge word: the goose bites, the wit bites, the audience is being taught the exchange's scoring system in real time. A point is scored when a word's hidden sense bites the opponent; a rally ends when one player calls the bite by name. Romeo's "A most courteous exposition" and Mercutio's counter about the bauble in a hole extend the scoring into bawdy, where the senses multiply fastest, and the exchange's speed is itself the datum: these are not decorations on the friendship but its native sport, played at a tempo that excludes every bystander. The full anatomy of the bawdy system, its puns catalogued and its editors weighed, is set out in [Mercutio's bawdy wordplay and its sexual wit](/2022/09/16/mercutio-bawdy-wit-wordplay/); the present scene study needs the duel's mechanics, which are those of the fencing school translated into talk: measure kept, touches counted, the hit announced by the man who receives it.

### Why does Romeo answer the duel's pressure with a pun instead of a protest?

The pun is the scene's proof of recovery, and Romeo knows the examination he is sitting. A protest would concede that the jesting wounds; a pun denies the premise by outbidding it. Each rally survived buys back his standing in the company whose membership fee is fluency.

In Act 1 his wit failed every test Mercutio set, his answers coming back as sighs and oxymorons; here the first sally is met in kind within a line. The duel is therefore not Romeo enduring Mercutio's sport but Romeo rejoining a guild.

The rally's exhaustion is as scripted as its energy. Romeo attempts the exit the duel's etiquette allows: "Here's goodly gear!" and the appeal to business, "my business was great," returns in earnest as he tries to close the exchange and be gone to his errand. Mercutio refuses the close with the duel's last invention, the catalogue of what Romeo's silence would mean, and Romeo's final parry is the scene's most efficient joke, the oath by his pump: "By my troth, I am ..." in substance, swearing by the shoe whose single sole Mercutio has already taxed, so that the oath's furniture is the rally's own debris (2.4.95 to 100). The exchange ends not when the wit runs out but when the plot recalls its player: Romeo's business, the wedding hour, is the one opponent Mercutio's sport cannot outlast. Its closing images deserve notice, since the duel signs off in worn leather. Romeo's oath by his pump, the light dancing shoe of the feast, is taxed by Mercutio on the shoe's single sole, and the jest that answers is taxed in return as single-soled, thin stuff worn through at the tread. Wit in these final rallies is figured as footwear consumed by use: soles thin, jests wear out, the company's sport has a mileage. The metaphor is the scene's quietest piece of self-knowledge. Everything in the morning is being spent, time, patience, the letter's grace, Mercutio's credit with his friend, and the duel's own imagery keeps converting expenditure into footwear and coin, the two currencies a young gentleman carries on his person. When Romeo finally exits to his errand, the account the scene has been keeping can be closed: the wit has been spent to the sole, and what remains is business. The duel's formal lesson is the one the fencing portrait taught in reverse. Tybalt fights by a book and the book is mocked; the friends fight without one and the improvisation is glorious; yet both contests end by appointment, the one with a letter, the other with a wedding, and Verona's appointments have a way of collecting their debts in the same square.

Eric Partridge, whose Shakespeare's Bawdry assembled the period's sexual vocabulary sense by sense, supplies the method this scene demands of its readers: the puns are not smut scattered over a clean text but a system with rules, in which the bawdy sense is usually the operative one and the innocent sense is the cover that lets it travel. Read by Partridge's rule, the duel's scoring becomes visible: Romeo wins his rallies precisely when his second senses bite hardest, and Mercutio's delight is the delight of a master watching a pupil's covers grow thinner as his thrusts grow honester. The wordplay critic's contribution is to make the scene legible as competition rather than decoration, and the legibility changes the scene's temperature: a contest has a loser in waiting, and the contest's loser, in the square two scenes later, will be decided by the other syllabus.

The duel's opening word, counterfeit, sets its economy before the goose takes over. Mercutio's charge, delivered as Romeo enters, is that Romeo gave the company a counterfeit the night before: a false coin passed in the dark, the sighing effigy of a lover instead of the genuine Romeo. A counterfeit in the period is both a forged coin and a portrait, an image claiming a value its substance lacks, and the charge frames the whole exchange as an assay. The friends will test the metal of the returned Romeo ring by ring, pun by pun, and certify him sound only when the ring is true. The slipper debt is the assay's first instrument: the dancer who slipped owes the company a forfeit, and Romeo's plea of great business is an attempt to pay in excuse what the guild demands in wit. Mercutio's refusal of the excuse, his gloss of the case as one that constrains a man to bow in the hams, is the guild rejecting paper payment. From that refusal the rally structure follows necessarily: every subsequent exchange is Romeo minting genuine coin under pressure, and the celebration, when it comes, is the assay office's stamp.

The wild-goose rally is the duel's most instructive passage for the same reason, since it preserves a concession and its instant withdrawal. The wild-goose chase, in the phrase's first sporting sense, was a horse race in which riders followed a leader's unpredictable course as geese follow the flock's head, so the term names a pursuit whose path the pursuer does not choose. Romeo cries the match in the idiom of spurs and switching; Mercutio answers that if Romeo's wits run the wild-goose chase, Mercutio has done, for Romeo has more of the wild goose in one of his wits than Mercutio has in his whole five (2.4.65 to 70, in substance). The concession is a feint with the score inside it: Mercutio yields the rally by declaring Romeo's wit too wild to follow, which is surrender phrased as a compliment on the opponent's abundance. Five wits, the period's inventory of the senses, are staked against one of Romeo's and found lighter. The duel's bookkeeping could not be plainer, and its lesson for the square is exact: Mercutio's concessions are attacks in retreat's clothing, a habit the fencers in Act 3 will meet again when his jesting covers a drawn sword.

The duel's vocabulary, gathered up, reveals the scene's third language, neither fencing nor bawdy but trade. The goose itself carries a workshop sense beside its animal and slang senses: a tailor's goose is the heavy smoothing iron with which seams are pressed, and the bird's progress through the rally, from fool to prostitute to iron, is a progress through the street's occupations. Around it the scene keeps reaching for the lexicon of the counter and the shop: counterfeit coin at the assay, the fee simple of a life in property law, the drudge's labour priced in delight, the ladder ordered like a length of cordage from a supplier, the bride appraised as wax from a caster's bench. Verona's young men may imagine themselves in a romance or a fencing school, but their talk keeps the city's real grammar, buying, valuing, owing, delivering. The observation sharpens the scene's verdict about its hero of wit. Mercutio's genius is translational: he can render any subject, love, honour, death, a friend's recovery, into the street's commercial idiom at speed, and the rendering always diminishes the subject to its price. The one subject in the scene that resists translation is the wedding, which is never priced in his hearing at all, and its exemption from his idiom is the measure of its secrecy and of its worth.

## Sociable again: what Mercutio believes he has cured

The duel's verdict is delivered by its referee, who is also its champion. Mercutio's celebration is the scene's most analyzed speech in miniature: "Now art thou sociable, now art thou Romeo; now art thou what thou art, by art as well as by nature. For this drivelling love is like a great natural that runs lolling up and down to hide his bauble in a hole" (2.4.83 to 86). The gloss runs on natural, the period's word for an innocent or fool, and on the bauble, the fool's sceptre and the phallus in one prop. Drivelling love made Romeo a natural, a lolling idiot hiding his bauble; wit has restored him to art, to the fashioned self of company. The speech is a diagnosis and a discharge certificate in one breath, and both are wrong in the same way: the transformation Mercutio celebrates is real, but its cause is the one fact withheld from him. Romeo is not cured of love. He is married into it, and the fluency Mercutio applauds is the fluency of a man whose desire has been answered rather than abandoned. The scene's irony is structural: the physician certifies the recovery at the exact moment the patient's condition has become, by the physician's own philosophy, incurable.

Joseph Porter's reading of Mercutio, in his study of the character, gives this misdiagnosis its psychology. Porter's Mercutio is a performer whose wit is driven rather than free, escalating under pressure and most brilliant when his hold on Romeo is most threatened; the bawdy, on that account, is a defence worked at speed, an attempt to keep the friend inside a philosophy of appetite that the friend's happiness keeps escaping. The celebration speech fits Porter's evidence closely. Its energy is relief performed as triumph: Mercutio has his sparring partner back, and the certificate's extravagance measures the fear it discharges. Against Porter stands the formal account associated with Harry Levin, for whom the friends embody the play's high and low registers and the wit is architecture, the low style's set piece in the design. The disagreement matters for this scene because the two accounts predict different performances: Porter's Mercutio is playing to win something, Levin's is filling a structural office. The scene's own evidence, weighed in the verdict section below, favours Porter on the duel's behaviour, since the wit does not stop when its structural office is complete; it overflows its office, pursues Romeo past his exits, and transfers itself, still hungry, to the Nurse. A set piece ends. This wit looks for new opponents.

Benvolio's two interventions frame the celebration with the scene's only accurate reporting. His first, "An I were so apt to quarrel as thou art, any man should buy the fee simple of my life for an hour and a quarter" (2.4.26 to 27), prices Mercutio's quarrelsomeness in property law: the fee simple, outright ownership, of his life could be had cheap because its tenant is always advertising for a fight. The joke is the scene's quietest and its most prophetic, since Mercutio's life will in fact be sold short in a quarrel not his own, inside roughly the span Benvolio names. His second intervention is the exit line that clears the stage for the Nurse: the friends withdraw on the observation that the errand approaches, and the scene's first movement closes with the wit's champion still talking. Benvolio's function in the scene is the chronicler's: he asks the questions that elicit the portrait, keeps the accounts of the duel, and files the one prediction, the fee simple, that the tragedy will honour to the letter. The friend who survives the play is the one whose wit was bookkeeping.

The celebration's vocabulary of art and nature carries a proverb tradition the scene expects its hearers to know. Art, in the period's pairing, is everything acquired: skill, fashioning, practice, the self as made. Nature is everything given: birth, instinct, the self as found. To certify Romeo what he is "by art as well as by nature" is to declare the made self and the born self reconciled, the performer and the person one. The drivelling lover failed the pairing from the nature side: a natural, in the word's other sense, is one in whom nature never met art at all, the fool whose bauble, sceptre and phallus in a single prop, is hidden in holes because its owner has no fashioned use for it. Mercutio's diagnosis is thus a complete anthropology in a dozen words, and its confidence is its flaw. Romeo's fluency in the scene is indeed art recovered, but the recovery's engine is a nature Mercutio's scheme cannot file: a desire that has been answered and solemnized, which behaves like health in company and like secrecy in fact. Romeo lets the misdiagnosis stand. He corrects nothing, explains nothing, and pays for the friendship's comfort with the friendship's ignorance, a bargain whose final accounting the tomb will present.

Underneath the celebration sits the scene's governing factual error, which the audience is never allowed to forget: Mercutio believes the patient is still Rosaline's. Every prescription in the scene is written for the old case. The fishified opening prices a lover dried out by an unresponsive mistress; the conjuring jokes of the previous night, recalled in the friends' talk, were addressed to a Romeo pining in an orchard for a woman who has already been replaced in his biography. Mercutio never updates the file. His philosophy has no form for a love that succeeded, since success, in his system, is appetite satisfied and discharged, not a sacrament performed at dawn in a friar's cell. Romeo's silence protects the fact, but the silence is also a kind of agreement to be misread, and the scene lets the audience watch the cost accumulate: the friend is delighted, the delight is genuine, and it is addressed to a man who no longer exists. When Mercutio dies in the square still uninformed, the exclusion completed here becomes permanent, and the curse he speaks there is addressed to houses, not to the secret, because the secret was never given to him to curse or to bless.

What the celebration cannot see is the scene's second secret, which is that Romeo's fluency has a destination. Every rally he wins shortens the time to the Friar's cell; the wit is a bridegroom's escort, not a bachelor's relapse. Romeo's attempts to leave, twice begun and twice parried, are the scene's submerged plot asserting itself against its surface, and the audience watches a man try to keep an appointment while his best friend, in perfect ignorance and perfect love, detains him with the finest talk in the play. The character study of Mercutio as the friend who steals the play is pursued in [the full account of Mercutio's character and magnetism](/2022/08/19/mercutio-character-analysis/); within this scene, his theft is narrower and sadder: he steals minutes from a wedding he will never hear of, and spends them proving a cure that never happened.

## The Nurse enters: fan, sail, and the scene's second movement

The entrance is staged as a sight gag with a soundtrack. Weis prints the direction for the Nurse and Peter entering, and Mercutio greets the apparition as a ship: "A sail, a sail!" (2.4.101), Benvolio topping it, "Two, two, a shirt and a smock" (2.4.102). The gloss is costume. The Nurse's headdress and veil, spread by the wind of her walk, read as canvas; the shirt and smock are the male and female garments in the period's slang, so the pair of servants is mustered as a fleet of laundry. The joke's mechanism is the duel's mechanism transferred: a word with two senses, sail as canvas and sail as dress, bites on contact. What changes is the target's equipment. Romeo could return the bite; the Nurse, arriving on an errand whose dignity is her whole capital, must absorb the sport without the guild's fluency, and the scene's register drops from tournament to street teasing. The wordplay's ethics are exposed by the transfer: the same art that read as friendship between equals reads as rank exercised on a servant, and the scene knows it, since Romeo's part in the second movement will be to repair what the first movement's champion inflicts.

Mercutio's formal greeting to the Nurse extends the exposure: "Good ye, good ye" answered by her "God ye good e'en," and his leap on the phrase, " 'Tis no less, I tell you, for the bawdy hand of the dial is now upon the prick of noon" (2.4.105 to 108). The dial's hand and its bawdy sense need no expansion beyond Partridge's rule: the operative sense is the operative joke, and its cruelty is its accuracy about the hour, since the scene's clocks, the wedding at midday's approach, are the Nurse's errand itself. Her reply is the movement's pivot and her best self-defence: "Out upon you, what a man are you!" (2.4.109), a verdict rather than a parry, and Romeo's gloss on his friend, "One, gentlewoman, that God hath made for himself to mar" (2.4.110), is the apology the guild owes and cannot phrase as one. The Nurse's counterstroke is the fan: "Peter, my fan" (in substance at 2.4.111), and the request deserves its gloss. A fan is a lady's instrument; demanding it reasserts the gentlewoman's rank Romeo's phrase has just granted her, cools the face the sport has heated, and gives her hands the business of dignity while she recovers the errand. The scene's smallest prop does the work of a rebuttal.

### Why does the Nurse answer the mockery by calling for her fan?

The fan is the one weapon her station issues her, deployed with a tactician's timing. Speech has failed, since every phrase is converted to bawdy before it lands; the fan converts the contest back to objects, where her rank is legible, and buys the seconds in which Peter can be ordered forward.

The crowd of gentlemen can then be faced down by procedure rather than wit. Her exit line in the exchange completes the recovery: "God ye good morrow, gentlemen" (2.4.113, in substance), the courtesy restored, the errand intact. The fan is not a surrender flag. It is a standard raised.

Peter's part in the movement is the scene's quietest joke and its sharpest piece of class observation. Ordered to stand before her fan to hide her face, he answers the order's logic with a servant's literalism, and when Mercutio's sport is at its height his dagger stays in its sheath until the gentlemen are gone; Weis's text gives him the scene's closing beat of the movement, the drawn weapon flourished at an empty street. The timing is the satire: the one drawn blade in a scene full of fencing talk is drawn too late, at nobody, by a servant protecting a mistress whose danger was verbal. The scene thereby keeps its accounts straight. Talk of duels fills the morning; an actual weapon appears once, in farce, after the risk has passed; and the audience is being trained, without a word of instruction, in the gap between Verona's rhetoric of violence and its timetable, a gap that will close without warning in the square.

Mercutio's exit music completes the movement's evidence. His song for the departing Nurse, the catch about an old hare grown hoar, in substance "an old hare hoar is very good meat in Lent" (2.4.129 to 133), glosses in three layers. The hare is meat; hoar is grey with age; Lent is the season when flesh is forbidden, so the song prices an aged appetite as delicacy available out of season, a compliment to the Nurse's supposed experience wrapped in an insult about her years. A catch, moreover, is a round, a song built for several voices entering in turn; Mercutio sings at a retreating back in a single voice, the guild's chorus reduced to its soloist performing for an audience that has stopped listening. The form enacts the movement's verdict before the aside states it: the wit's social world is emptying. Romeo is already turning toward the errand, Benvolio has withdrawn, and the champion of company is left serenading a servant's exit. What the song cannot know is that its singer has two scenes of talk left in the play, and that the next crowd he performs for will be reaching for swords while he is still in the middle of the performance.

The Nurse's recovery of the scene is completed by her management of the audience of gentlemen. She asks for Romeo by description, "I pray you, sir, can you tell where young Romeo is?" in substance, and endures the identification jokes, the young Romeo against the old, the r and the dog's name, with a patience that is itself information: this is a woman who has priced male wit before and knows its market rate in delay. Before the hinge comes the identification exchange, a small masterpiece of the scene's method. The Nurse asks for young Romeo, and Romeo, presenting himself, answers with a riddle of seniority: he is the youngest of that name, for fault of a worse, in substance (2.4.115 to 120). The gloss runs on fault as want or lack: no worse Romeo exists to take the name's bottom place, so the speaker holds it by default, a self-deprecation engineered to be topped. Mercutio tops it on schedule, asking whether the worst is meant, and the Nurse's verdict, that Romeo speaks well, in substance, awards the exchange to courtesy over sport. The miniature matters because it is the scene's one contest the Nurse judges rather than suffers: presented with the guild's game in its polite form, she can score it, prefer the gentleman's version, and move the business forward. Her competence was never in question. Only her fluency in the bawdy register was, and the scene is careful to show the difference between a woman out-talked in one dialect and a woman outclassed in none.

Her phrase for her errand, that she would speak with Romeo "in secret" in substance, is the movement's hinge, since secrecy is the one currency the street cannot counterfeit. The gentlemen withdraw on it. Mercutio's exit song, the bailiff ballad of "an old hare hoar" (2.4.129 to 133), pursues her with a final volley about age and appetite, and its placement is the movement's verdict on its champion: the wit that could not stop now sings at a departing back, winning a contest the other party has already left. The full account of the Nurse's character, her comedy and her class position, is given in [the Nurse's character study of comedy, class, and betrayal](/2022/11/11/the-nurse-character-analysis/); the scene study's concern is the transfer of power the entrance performs, from the guild of talkers to the woman with the message, effected without her winning a single rally.

## The aside in Act 2 Scene 4: secrecy, coin, and the ladder

Romeo's first act in the movement is repair performed as stage management: he draws the Nurse aside, out of the sport's range, and the aside's privacy is the scene's first honest space. What he gives her there is not wit but logistics. The instructions, delivered in the scene's longest prose speech, deserve quotation at their operative points: "Nurse, commend me to thy lady and mistress. I protest unto thee ..." and then the arrangements, that Juliet is to contrive to come to shrift at the Friar's cell that afternoon, and that "there she shall at Friar Laurence' cell be shrived and married" (2.4.165 to 175, in substance across the speech). The register change is the meaning. The man who has spent ninety lines proving he can play now speaks in imperatives and timetables, and the prose that carried the duel carries the wedding with the same fluency, as if the scene were demonstrating that its hero's restoration is not to the guild but to purpose. The Nurse's interjections inside the speech, her praise of Juliet interrupting the logistics, keep the errand human: the message she carries home will be a story, not a memorandum, and its telling is already rehearsing itself in her asides.

The coin is the aside's delicate point. Romeo's offer of payment, and the Nurse's protest before acceptance, conduct a negotiation about what kind of transaction this is: a servant's fee for an errand, or a friend's honour for a trust. Weis's text gives Romeo the offer and the Nurse the demurral, her protest that she will not take a penny, in substance, followed by her acceptance on the errand's terms. The exchange matters because it prices the secrecy. A message bought can be resold; a trust accepted under protest cannot, and the scene makes Romeo insist and the Nurse resist so that the audience registers the bond being formed as something other than wages. Her demand that follows, for the means of the wedding night, the cords and the ladder, is practical to the point of carpentry: "I am the drudge and toil in your delight, but you shall bear the burden soon at night" (2.4.180 to 181, in substance). The drudge's joke is the aside's bawdy made honest, appetite acknowledged inside the arrangements rather than mocked outside them, and it lands kindly because the speaker owns it.

Inside the instructions, the Nurse's interjections perform the errand's second function. Romeo speaks in logistics; she answers in praise, breaking in on the timetables with her lady's virtues, and the interruptions are not noise in the channel. She is already composing the report she will carry home, testing its phrases aloud, finding the places where Juliet's name brightens the errand's prose. A messenger who merely repeats delivers words; this messenger rehearses delivery, and the difference is the difference between the scene's two documentary systems. Tybalt's letter was written once and left to work alone, its meaning fixed at sealing. Romeo's message is workshopped in transit by a carrier with editorial rights, and it will arrive improved, warmer, and later than its sender planned, which is the whole of the next scene in embryo. The aside thus contains the delay's cause inside the delivery's success: the same garrulous fidelity that makes the Nurse trustworthy makes her slow, and the scene registers both in a single trait.

The ladder promise closes the aside with the scene's third timetable. Romeo undertakes that his man will bring the cords "within this hour" in substance, a servant's errand nested inside the Nurse's errand nested inside the Friar's appointment, three delegations deep. The scene's structure of messages can now be drawn whole: Tybalt's letter travels to a house and waits unanswered; Romeo's answer travels by word of mouth through a servant and arrives within the hour; the Friar's letter, later, will travel by a friar and arrive never. Act 2 Scene 4 is the play's one scene in which every message moves at the speed its sender intends, and the wit that fills its delays is the sound of a machine, for once, working. The map section below sets the movements side by side; the point for the aside is that Romeo's competence here is the competence the tragedy will spend. He can run an errand network flawlessly at noon. By the tomb, the same network's failure will be total, and the contrast is prepared in this scene's sunshine.

The shrift device inside the instructions is the errand network's passport, and its design shows Romeo thinking like a conspirator of some skill. Juliet is to come to the Friar's cell that afternoon under the errand of confession: shrift is the one journey a Capulet daughter may make beyond the house without a chaperon's suspicion, since the confessional is private by sacrament and its destination is a churchman's room rather than a lover's. The cover is perfect because it is nearly honest; Juliet will indeed be shrived at the cell, in the same visit that marries her, and the Nurse can repeat the errand's public version in the Capulet kitchen without uttering a falsehood. Romeo's instructions, dividing the message into its public layer and its sealed layer, are the scene's second piece of documentary craft, the benign twin of the challenge letter in the first movement. Tybalt's document says one thing and means a killing; Romeo's errand says one thing and means a wedding; Verona's paper and spoken traffic alike run on double senses, and the pun duel, in which every word has carried two meanings all morning, has been the audience's training in exactly the literacy the plot requires.

### What does the nine o'clock promise ask the audience to listen for?

Juliet named the hour in the balcony scene: at nine she would send. The Nurse's entrance is that promise arriving in person, and Romeo's undertaking, that his man will be with her within the hour, sets a second clock against the first for the audience to hold.

The pair of clocks deserves holding. The bride's morning has been spent waiting; the bridegroom's hour will be spent delivering. Every later timetable in the play, the wedding moved forward, the potion's forty-two hours, the letter's lost day, is measured against this scene's pair of kept appointments, the standard of punctuality the plot will degrade by degrees.

## The warning: how the Nurse tests the bridegroom she has just mocked

The scene's final movement reverses its power relations a second time. The gentlemen gone, the fee settled, the ladder promised, the Nurse keeps Romeo for one more speech, and its opening is a credential: she will tell him what her lady said of him, and the telling begins with the other suitor. Paris is introduced as the control in the experiment, "a man, young lady, such a man as all the world, why, he's a man of wax" (2.4.192 to 193, in substance), wax being the sculptor's perfection, a figure cast without a flaw and without a pulse. The compliment is the test's first half. The second half is the warning itself, aimed at the one injury her lady cannot survive: if Romeo means to deal double with Juliet, to lead her, in the Nurse's phrase, into a fool's paradise, the dealing will be very gross kind of behaviour, in substance, and the phrase's legal colouring, gross as in manifest and actionable, gives the servant's warning a magistrate's edge (2.4.195 to 205, in substance). She has spent the scene being priced as a bawd by the sport; she ends it sitting in judgment on the bridegroom's intentions, and Romeo submits to the examination without a pun. The duel's champion never managed that submission. The errand's bearer extracts it in a dozen lines.

Romeo's answer is the scene's most consequential piece of plain speaking: "Nurse, commend me to thy lady and mistress. I protest unto thee ..." (2.4.206), the protestation of honest meaning offered in the formula of an oath. The word protest carries its period weight, a solemn declaration before witnesses, and the witness is a servant whose testimony the bride will trust above any document. The scene's economy of proof completes itself here. Tybalt's challenge needed no reading because its genre vouched for it; Romeo's honesty needs a person because no genre can vouch for a heart, and the person it gets is the one the morning's sport treated as a sail, a smock, and an old hare. The exit choreography underlines the reversal. The Nurse leaves as she was announced to leave, with Peter ordered before her carrying the fan, in substance, the servant preceding his mistress with her standard unfurled. The prop that served as her shield in the sport's worst minutes now processes ahead of her like a banner at a review, and the street that greeted her as a sail sends her off as a small fleet in good order. Stage pictures of this kind are the scene's silent scoring system: she entered as a joke's raw material and departs as a personage with an escort, and not one line of dialogue has been spent on the transformation. The warning's ending turns judgment into adoption: the Nurse's praise of Juliet, her lady's beauty and her worth as the sweetest serving girl in the city in substance, blesses the match she has just policed, and Romeo's exit line, "What wilt thou tell her, Nurse? Thou dost not mark me" answered by her catalogue of his attention, closes the scene on the errand's human freight. She leaves for home with the fan, the fee, the ladder promised, and a story whose delay in the telling belongs to [the next scene, where the Nurse keeps Juliet waiting](/2023/08/25/act-2-scene-5-nurse-delays-news/); this scene's business ends with the message delivered to its carrier intact, the last fully successful transmission in the play.

Romeo's closing courtesy completes the examination's paperwork. His commendation, "Commend me to thy lady and mistress" (2.4.206, in substance across the leave-taking), sends formal respects to the bride through her examiner, a bridegroom's respects composed in the idiom of a visiting card. The formula matters because it is the scene's first speech from Romeo addressed, through an intermediary, to Juliet herself: all morning his talk has been spent on the friends, the errand, and the arrangements, and only at the scene's end does a sentence travel from him toward his wife. It travels correctly, too, by the approved carrier, under the seal of the Nurse's new office as confidante, with the examiner's blessing as its frank. The street scene that began with a letter delivered to the wrong generation ends with a message delivered to the right woman by the right hands, and the contrast is the morning's whole education in how Verona's communications succeed: not by document, not by genre, but by a trusted person walking a message home.

The warning is also the scene's only speech aimed at the future, and the distinction organizes the whole morning in retrospect. The friends' talk is retrospective or speculative: a night unaccounted for, a letter already sent, a swordsman already described. Mercutio's art, for all its speed, faces backward, restoring an old Romeo, pricing an absent enemy, curing a finished sickness. The Nurse's warning faces forward. Its grammar is conditional and consequential, if the dealing should prove double, then the paradise is a fraud and the behaviour gross, a servant drafting the future's indictment in advance and asking the bridegroom to sign his innocence beneath it. Romeo's protest is the signature. The scene thereby hands its last movement to the only speaker whose business is what happens next, and the tragedy's own bias is rehearsed in the handover: Verona's talkers describe the world brilliantly and alter nothing, while its carriers, whose talk is timetables and warnings, decide what the world will contain by Friday.

The warning also completes the scene's argument about registers. Three kinds of speech have now been tested as instruments. Wit, the duel's, proves brilliant at restoring a friend and useless at everything else, since it cannot read a letter, carry a message, or examine a conscience. Satire, the portrait's, proves accurate about the threat and powerless against it, a diagnosis that changes no outcome. Plain speech, the Nurse's warning and Romeo's oath, proves to be the only register in the scene that moves the plot: the wedding happens because this exchange happened. The scene's comedy keeps auditioning the talkers for the role of agent and keeps rejecting them in favour of the woman they mocked, and the pattern is the play's in miniature, since the tragedy will likewise be decided not by its finest speakers but by its carriers, a friar with a letter, a servant with a report, an apothecary with a drug. Act 2 Scene 4 is where the audience is taught, under cover of laughter, whose hands the story is actually in.

The warning's two governing phrases repay the glossing the brief for this scene demands. A man of wax is a figure cast in a mould, perfection without pulse: wax takes any impression and holds no warmth of its own, so the Nurse's praise of Paris is, in her own idiom, a reservation, the suitor appraised as an object whose finish is its whole recommendation. The phrase also measures the distance between the two appraisals of Paris the play supplies, the mother's catalogue of his beauties in the marriage talk and the servant's wax, delivered here as a control against which Romeo is to be weighed and found warmer. The fool's paradise, her name for the state Juliet would inhabit if Romeo's dealing proved double, is the period's phrase for a bliss founded on error, happiness whose ground is a mistake about the facts. Its precision is legal as much as moral: the paradise is a fraud, its tenant deceived, its landlord liable. When the Nurse adds that such dealing would be gross behaviour, in substance, the adjective borrows the magistrate's register, gross as manifest and actionable, and a servant's warning acquires the weight of a charge read in court. The scene's champion of wit never speaks with consequences attached. Its mocked servant, in her last speech of the scene, speaks in nothing else.

The map also explains the scene's notorious performance problem, its pace. Played at the speed its jokes invite, the morning becomes a revue and the errand an interruption; played for the errand, the jokes curdle into delay. The fuller the scene stands, the clearer its verdict: Verona's tragedy is not caused by a failure of talk but by talk's success at everything except the tasks that matter.

Two minor figures frame the scene as its record-keepers, and the map's rows are steadier for naming them. Benvolio opens the morning with questions and closes its first movement with the fee-simple valuation, the friend as ledger, entering every item at its price and surviving to file the accounts in later scenes. Peter closes the second movement with a drawn dagger and an empty street, the servant as parody ledger, entering the scene's violence at its farcical rate. Between the two bookkeepers the morning's real transactions occur, and the symmetry is the scene's last joke about itself: a morning devoted to pricing, of lives, of wit, of suitors, of errands, is witnessed at both ends by the two characters whose function is to keep the books. The tragedy that follows can be read as the audit of this morning's accounts, every price called in, every valuation tested, and the scene's comedy is the sound of a city entering figures it will not enjoy having audited.

## Porter, Partridge, Weis, and the verdict on the scene's wit

The critics divide on what the wit is, and the division decides how the scene should be played and taught. Joseph Porter's Mercutio is a study in driven performance: the wit escalates because its owner is defending a philosophy and a friendship at once, and its brilliance is the sound of pressure. Eric Partridge's contribution is methodological and, for this scene, indispensable: the bawdy is a system with recoverable rules, and the reader who restores the operative senses recovers the duel's scoring, its winners, and its stakes. Susan Snyder's comic matrix offers the structural alternative to Porter: the scene is comedy's machinery running true to form, the restored lover, the teasing of the go-between, the arrangements advanced under laughter, and its wit is the genre's rather than the man's. René Weis, editing the scene, prints the fuller second-quarto text and annotates the fencing terms against the duelling literature; Jill L. Levenson's Oxford text likewise keeps the fuller scene, and the editorial agreement on fullness is itself a judgment, since the scene's length is its argument: a shorter 2.4 would be a pleasanter scene and a lesser preparation.

Brian Gibbons, editing the play for the Arden Second Series, adds the structural observation that the scene's prose abundance is itself a datum: the play's first half invests in comedy at a scale the second half will liquidate, and 2.4 is among the largest single deposits. On Gibbons's account the wit's quantity, not only its quality, is the preparation, since an audience that has banked this much laughter experiences the square's turn as a fortune reversed rather than a mood changed. The observation supports Snyder's machinery at the level of the whole play while leaving Porter's man intact at the level of the scene, and it explains why the scene's length has survived every editorial regime: cutting the deposit reduces the bankruptcy.

The adjudication turns on the duel's behaviour after its work is done. Snyder's machinery explains the scene's placement and its outcomes, the lover restored to company, the errand advanced; but machinery does not explain overflow, and this wit overflows every boundary the machinery sets. It pursues Romeo past two attempted exits. It transfers itself, unspent, to a servant who never enlisted. It sings its last volley at a departing back. Porter's driven performer predicts exactly that overflow, since a defence that has secured its object does not stand down while the threat, a new philosophy embodied in a happy friend, remains in the room. The verdict therefore follows Porter on the wit's nature and Snyder on its office: the scene is comedy's machinery, worked by a man whose need to work it is the tragedy's first symptom. Partridge's restoration is the precondition of seeing either claim, since the driven quality is audible only when the puns' operative senses are restored and the duel can be heard as a contest with something at stake.

The editors add a documentary vote to the same verdict. The earliest performance text, the first quarto, compresses this scene's sport while keeping its plot joints, which is the record of a playing company deciding that the talk was the expendable layer. Weis and Levenson, printing the fuller second-quarto scene, decide the opposite for readers, and the scene's internal evidence supports the editors against the players: the aside's effect depends on the duel's length, since Romeo's plainness registers as a changed man only after his fluency has been exhibited past the point of need, and the Nurse's competence registers only against the volume of sport she absorbs. Compression keeps the story and loses the measurement. The disagreement between the quarto witnesses is thus the oldest form of the Porter and Snyder dispute, the players voting for machinery, the editors for the man, and four centuries of fuller texts have settled the question in Porter's direction without settling the performance problem the players were solving.

That verdict returns the scene to its thesis. Act 2 Scene 4 poises wit against violence not as opposites but as phases: the fencing satire prices the violence, the pun duel rehearses its forms, the teasing spends its ethics, and the plain speakers inherit its plot. The scene is the play's sunniest hour and its most complete warning, and the two are the same fact, since Verona's danger was never that its young men lacked brilliance. The danger is that brilliance was their substitute for every other instrument, reading letters, keeping hours, examining consciences, until the morning a servant with a fan accomplished in twenty lines what all the wit in Verona could only describe.

The scene's last word on its own method belongs to its structure. It begins with a letter that nobody reads and ends with a message that everybody in the scene works to deliver; it opens with a swordsman described and closes with a bridegroom examined; its first movement prices a life in a joke about property and its last movement prices a marriage in a warning about fraud. Every element of the tragedy is present in the morning, filed under comedy. The audience leaves the scene laughing, in possession of the challenge's terms, the portrait's syllabus, the friendship's limits, and the errand's success, which is to say in possession of everything except an excuse, later, to call what happens in the square a surprise.

Read as a control experiment, the scene is the play's most exact piece of engineering. Act 3 Scene 1 will reassemble its elements one by one: a challenge outstanding, Mercutio performing for a crowd, Romeo declining the expected part, a servant class watching from the edges, the heat of a Verona day. Every variable is held constant except one, the presence of the antagonist in the flesh, and the experiment's result is the difference between a morning of wordplay and a noon of funerals. Act 2 Scene 4 exists so that the result can be measured rather than merely witnessed: the audience has seen the machine run safely and can name, part by part, what changed. That is the final service the wit performs for the tragedy it cannot prevent. It teaches the spectators how the killing will work while persuading them, one last time, that it will not.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Why does Tybalt's challenge reach the audience as household news rather than as a letter read aloud on stage?

The challenge arrives as report because its contents are a formality its genre guarantees. Benvolio states the delivery in one sentence, that Tybalt has sent a letter to Romeo's father's house (2.4.6 to 7), and Mercutio names the genre instantly: "A challenge, on my life" (2.4.8). A written cartel followed the duelling manuals so closely that two friends can reconstruct it unread. Staging the news as talk rather than as a document also serves the scene's design: the threat must hang over ninety lines of comedy without ever taking the stage, so that the audience watches the wit perform underneath a sentence of death that nobody present has bothered to open.

### Q: What do Mercutio's fencing verbs, the passado, the punto reverso, and the hai, reveal about the duelling manuals?

The three terms are a syllabus in miniature, imported from the Italian fencing school whose treatises circulated in London. The passado is the forward thrust delivered with a pass of the feet; the punto reverso is the reversed, backhanded point; the hai is the fencer's cry at the touch, announcing that the hit has landed. Mercutio strings them together as a chant (2.4.24 to 25) because that is how the manuals taught: vocabulary first, the foreign terms worn as credentials. The satire's bite is that fluency in the terms is presented as a substitute for courage, a gentleman of the first and second cause being a man whose honour has been alphabetized.

### Q: Why does Mercutio's portrait turn Tybalt's fighting into arithmetic, and why is the reckoning an insult rather than praise?

The reckoning is an insult because arithmetic leaves nothing to judgment. Mercutio's Tybalt fights as singers sing prick-song, keeping "time, distance, and proportion" and resting his "minim rests, one, two, and the third in your bosom" (2.4.19 to 22). Every term converts skill into mechanism: the rests are counted, the measure is kept, the touch arrives on schedule like a note in a score. Praise of such fighting is praise of a machine, and the insult deepens with the compliment's accuracy, since a fighter computed by rule can be read by rule. Mercutio's contempt is for a courage that has been outsourced to a system, even as the system's lethality is granted in the same breath.

### Q: Which of the cat's nine lives does Mercutio promise to spend first, and what happens to the promise in Act 3 Scene 1?

Mercutio promises to take one of the nine lives and, in the ribald arithmetic of the exchange, to dry-beat the remaining eight (2.4.29 to 31, in substance). The promise is a spending plan delivered as heraldry, the Prince of Cats being granted his full stock of lives the better to bankrupt him. Act 3 Scene 1 collects the debt from the wrong account. Mercutio spends his own single life in the square, killed under Romeo's arm by the very fighter whose lives he had budgeted, and the nine-lives joke is recalled by its inversion: the cat keeps his lives, the accountant loses his one. The scene's comedy is priced by that recall every time the portrait is reread.

### Q: Why does the wordplay duel keep returning to the goose, and what does the bird let the friends say?

The goose is the duel's hardest-working word because its senses cover the whole contest. A goose is a fool, which scores the insult; a goose is a prostitute in the period's slang, which scores the bawdy; a goose is a tailor's pressing iron, which scores the trade joke; and a goose bites, which names the sport itself. Romeo's cry, "Nay, good goose, bite not" (2.4.78, in substance), is the rally's rule spoken aloud: the bite is the point scored. The bird lets the friends conduct aggression, desire, and judgment inside one syllable, and its returns chart the duel's rising temperature better than any stage direction.

### Q: What happens to the duel each time Romeo tries to end it, and why does Mercutio refuse the close?

Each attempted close is converted into a new rally. Romeo pleads his great business; Mercutio glosses the plea as a curtsy and charges interest on it. Romeo swears by his pump; Mercutio taxes the shoe's single sole and spins the oath into the wit-worn exchange. The refusals are the scene's evidence for the driven quality Joseph Porter assigns the wit: a game whose purpose was restoring Romeo to company has achieved that purpose by the celebration speech, yet its champion plays on, because the playing is the point for him. The duel ends only when the plot removes its other player, Romeo's exit being an appointment kept rather than a contest conceded.

### Q: Why does Romeo refuse to explain his business to Mercutio, and what does the refusal protect?

Romeo refuses because the business is a marriage that Mercutio's philosophy would convert into sport before it could become safety. The secret is hours old, its other parties are a friar and a Capulet, and its exposure in a street, in jest, would arm every listener. The refusal protects the wedding's timing as much as its fact: the ceremony is set for that afternoon at the Friar's cell, and Romeo's errands, the Nurse, the ladder, the shrift, must run in sequence without the feud's intelligence service, which Mercutio's loose brilliance would unintentionally staff. His silence is the scene's one act of operational discipline, and its cost is the friendship's exclusion from the truth.

### Q: Why does Mercutio summon the absent Romeo with drinking songs and a fishmonger's cry instead of love poetry?

The summons is a parody cure administered in the wrong pharmacy. Mercutio's catalogue of the missing Romeo runs through tavern catches and a fishwife's street cry in substance, the registers of appetite and trade, because his philosophy holds that the lover's sickness is a posture to be shouted down rather than a feeling to be answered. Love poetry is the disease's own language, and Mercutio will not prescribe the disease. The comedy carries the act's saddest datum: the summons is delivered over a man who is not absent at all but married, and the cure's noise is loudest at the moment its diagnosis has expired.

### Q: How does the scene divide its morning between prose wit and the errand that ends it?

The division is a descent of registers with an ascent of consequence. The first movement is talk about absent persons, a letter, a lover, a swordsman, conducted in prose sport. The second movement admits a present person with business, and the prose changes function: greetings curdle into teasing, teasing into repair, and the aside replaces the rally as the scene's working form. By the warning, prose has become examination and oath. The scene's shape argues that wit is what Verona's gentlemen do with a morning, while errands are what its servants do with the plot, and the handover between the two is the scene's true plot.

### Q: What makes the pun duel a contest with turns, stakes, and a declared winner rather than random joking?

The duel obeys the fencing school's etiquette translated into talk. Turns alternate and answers must take up the opponent's exact word, as the slipper, the goose, and the pump each pass from hand to hand gathering senses. Stakes rise because each new sense risks the bawdy, and a player whose cover sense collapses has been touched. The winner is declared by the loser himself: Mercutio's celebration, "Now art thou sociable, now art thou Romeo" (2.4.83), is a referee's verdict awarding Romeo his name as the prize. The contest's formality is the point Mercutio's satire of Tybalt has already taught: Verona scores everything, and friendship is one of its scoring systems.

### Q: Why does the Nurse's entrance change the scene's target from an absent swordsman to a present woman?

The entrance changes the target because sport requires a body, and Tybalt's body is elsewhere. Mercutio's wit has been exercised on phantoms, a letter, a reputation, a missing lover, and phantoms cannot be wounded. The Nurse arrives with a face, a costume, and an errand, and the guild's art, deprived of its equals, spends itself on the available target. The change exposes the sport's ethics in a way the duel could not: against Romeo the bite was a membership test, against the Nurse it is rank used as entertainment. The scene registers the difference through Romeo, whose second-movement work is repair, drawing the target out of range and converting the sport's damage into business.

### Q: What insult does the sail exchange build out of a single costume object?

The sail insult builds a ship out of a headdress. Mercutio's cry, "A sail, a sail!" (2.4.101), reads the Nurse's veil and head-cloth, spread by her walk, as canvas under wind, and Benvolio's topping line, "Two, two, a shirt and a smock" (2.4.102), musters the servant pair as male and female garments in the period's slang. One object thus supplies vehicle, crew, and cargo: the woman is freighted as laundry and propelled as shipping at once. The insult's efficiency is its cruelty, since costume is the Nurse's rank made visible, and the joke strips the rank by renaming its cloth.

### Q: When does Peter finally draw his dagger, and why is the timing funnier than the threat?

Peter draws after the gentlemen have gone, flourishing the weapon at a street from which the danger has departed. The timing is the joke's whole mechanism: a bodyguard's courage that matures only in safety is a certificate of the threat's unreality, and the scene's accounts are kept straight by it. A morning saturated in fencing vocabulary produces exactly one drawn blade, produced late, by a servant, against nobody. The farce is also a measurement the audience carries into Act 3, where a blade will be drawn early, by a gentleman, against somebody, and the gap between the two timings is the distance between Verona's rhetoric of violence and its practice.

### Q: Why does Romeo draw the Nurse aside before he mentions any payment at all?

The aside comes first because the message must be established as a trust before it can be priced as a service. Romeo's instructions concern a secret marriage, a friar's cell, and an afternoon appointment; spoken in the street's hearing they would be gossip within the hour. Privacy also changes the Nurse's office: addressed alone, she is a confidante being sworn, not a servant being hired, and her conduct of the errand afterward follows the higher office. When the coin is finally offered, it lands on a bond already formed, which is why her protest against taking it can be believed and why his insistence reads as honour rather than purchase.

### Q: Why does the Nurse keep her errand unspoken while Mercutio remains on stage?

The errand stays unspoken because Mercutio's presence converts every utterance into material. A message about Juliet, delivered within his hearing, would be versified, bawdified, and repeated across Verona before the Nurse reached the Capulet gate; his wit is a broadcast system with no off switch, as the scene's first movement has spent ninety lines proving. The Nurse's silence is professional judgment, the same instinct that makes her ask for Romeo by description and endure the identification jokes without correcting them. She prices the room before she spends the secret, and the scene marks her as the only person in it who does.

### Q: What does Romeo's promise that his man will bring the cords within the hour add to the scene's clocks?

The promise adds a kept appointment to a play of broken ones. Juliet's nine o'clock messenger has arrived; the Friar's afternoon cell is booked; Romeo now undertakes a delivery inside sixty minutes, by a servant, of the tackled stair by which he will climb to the wedding night (2.4.175 to 181, in substance). Three clocks are thus wound in one scene and all three will strike on time, the last occasion in the tragedy when delegation works exactly as designed. The audience is being given a standard of punctuality in sunshine so that the later failures, a moved wedding, a quarantined friar, a lost day, can be measured against it.

### Q: Why does the Nurse's warning to Romeo end in praise of Juliet rather than in a threat?

The warning ends in praise because its purpose is adoption, not deterrence. The Nurse has examined the bridegroom for double dealing and received his protested honesty; the examination's natural close is the examiner's verdict on the match itself, delivered through the bride's worth. Her praise of Juliet, her beauty, her sweetness, her value above the city's other daughters in substance (2.4.207 to 213), tells Romeo the price of the trust he has been granted in the only currency he cannot counterfeit, a mother's inventory spoken by the woman who raised the goods. Threat would have kept him a suspect. Praise makes him a debtor, and debt binds longer.

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch wit-and-threat map, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch wit-and-threat map is the table in this article that sets the scene's ten movements side by side, tracking what the wit performs in each against what the threat does meanwhile. Its pattern is an exchange rate: the rows where the talk is finest, the portrait, the duel, the celebration, are the rows where nothing is accomplished, while the plot advances in the plain rows of timetables, coin, and oath. The threat column never sleeps; the letter waits, the hour approaches, the square is being prepared. The map exposes Act 2 Scene 4 as a scene whose comedy and whose danger are not alternating moods but simultaneous accounts, kept in different registers by different classes of speaker.

### Q: How do the early texts differ on this scene's fullness, and what do Weis and Levenson print?

The second quarto of 1599 gives the scene at full stretch, the complete fencing catalogue, the whole pun duel, the Nurse's business entire. The first quarto of 1597 compresses the scene's sport, as it compresses the play throughout, keeping the plot's joints and trimming the talk. René Weis in the Arden Third Series prints the fuller text and annotates the fencing terms against the duelling literature; Jill L. Levenson's Oxford edition likewise presents the full scene. The editorial agreement matters because the scene's length is its meaning: trimmed, the morning is a pleasant interlude; entire, it is a preparation, and the fuller text is the one in which the wit's overflow, the datum the critics argue over, can be measured.

### Q: How can a student use the wit-and-threat map in an essay on comedy and danger in the play?

The map gives an essay its structure and its evidence at once. Its rows supply a sequence any argument can follow, from the reported challenge through the portrait and duel to the aside and the warning, and its two columns supply the thesis: that in this play comedy is not relief from danger but danger's rehearsal in a lighter register. An essay can test the pattern against Act 3 Scene 1, where the duel of words becomes a duel of swords with the same contestants' friends keeping score, or against the Nurse's later scenes, where the plain register inherits the plot again. The map's discipline, one movement per row, one claim per column, keeps the argument anchored in the scene's text rather than in general impressions of Mercutio's brilliance.
