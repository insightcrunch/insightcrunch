Most books cross the proof stage on tiptoe. By the time galleys are pulled, the composition is supposed to be finished, and the writer's job is to catch the errors the typesetters introduced: a dropped letter, a misspelled name, a paragraph out of order. Fitzgerald treated the galley proofs of The Great Gatsby as a composition surface instead. The revisions he made on the proofs were substantial. They were not corrections. They were decisions, and they included the reworking of Gatsby's biography and the reshaping of the middle chapters of the novel, the sixth and the seventh, where the book's architecture bends and then holds.

That claim needs a little unpacking, because it sounds like a story about heroics. It is really a story about evidence. The surviving galley record, together with the pre-proof text and the apparatus of the scholarly editions that collate them, shows Fitzgerald still rewriting in the printing house. James L. W. West III's 2019 Cambridge variorum edition makes the proof record legible in a way earlier editions could not, and the 2000 Cambridge edition of Trimalchio preserves the text as it stood before the galley-stage rewriting began. Between those two documents sits the proof record: the changes Fitzgerald wrote onto the sheets, the passages he recast, the biography he rethought, the chapters he rearranged. This article is a complete account of that record.

The namable claim, stated plainly at the start because everything in this article serves it, is this: a novel was still being rewritten on the proofs. Fitzgerald's substantial galley-stage revisions show the book taking final shape late, the changes to Gatsby's biography and the middle chapters demonstrating that the published novel was secured at a stage where most books are merely corrected. The significance is twofold. For readers, it changes the picture of how finished Gatsby was when the typesetters set it, which changes what it means to call the novel perfect. For students of textual scholarship, the proofs are the crucial witness: they reveal which parts of the novel Fitzgerald still regarded as negotiable, and therefore which parts of the finished book carry the deepest decisions.

## What a Galley Proof Is, and Why Fitzgerald's Were Unusual

A galley proof is an intermediate printing: the text of a book set in type and printed on long, unbound sheets so the author and the publisher's readers can check it before it is paged into final form. In the normal life of a book, the galleys arrive after the writing is done. The typesetter has translated the manuscript into metal, and the proof sheets carry whatever the typesetter got right and got wrong. The author's job at this stage is custodial: find the misprints, mark the corrections, return the sheets. Compositors expect a certain amount of authorial change, and they charge for it, but the contract of the stage is clear. The composition is settled. The corrections are small.

Fitzgerald violated that contract, and the violation was expensive in every sense. The galleys of the novel then called Trimalchio reached him in the opening months of 1925, and the surviving record shows him rewriting on them rather than proofreading them. This was not a handful of sharpened sentences. The apparatus of the later scholarly editions records changes at a scale that breaks the normal categories of proof correction: passages rewritten, narrative material relocated, the account of Gatsby's past rethought. The distinction matters because the two kinds of change look the same on a proof sheet. A correction and a revision both appear as handwriting over print. Only the extent, the pattern, and the judgment the handwriting expresses tell them apart. The handwriting on Fitzgerald's proofs expresses judgment, not housekeeping.

There is a physical dimension to this that students of textual scholarship should keep in mind, because it constrains what kind of change was possible. Galley sheets are long and narrow. A revision that fits in a margin is easy; a revision that replaces a paragraph requires squeezing new text into cramped space, sometimes on separate sheets, sometimes with the order of material renumbered. Fitzgerald's changes were of the kind that fought the format: the rewriting of the biography material and the reworking of the middle chapters required him to reorganize sequences, cut and restore, and recast passages whose new shape had to be indicated by hand. The effort itself is evidence of intent. Nobody expends that effort to fix typos.

The normal workflow of the stage is worth describing in full, because the abnormality of Fitzgerald's use of it is only visible against the norm. In the ordinary course, the author reads the galleys against the manuscript or typescript, marks the compositor's errors, answers the publisher's queries, and returns the sheets. The publisher's own proofreaders have already been over the text, so the author's pass is the second line of defense, not the first. The queries the author answers are small: a doubtful spelling, an ambiguous punctuation, a name that appears in two forms. The author is not expected to improve the book at this stage, because the book is supposed to be finished. The entire apparatus of the galley stage, the queries, the allowances, the deadlines, assumes a settled text. Fitzgerald's sheets are the record of a writer who declined the assumption and used the apparatus for a different purpose.

### What drove Fitzgerald to treat the proofs as a composition surface?

Fitzgerald rewrote at the proof stage because the gap between the novel submitted and the novel wanted had become visible, and the proofs were the last instrument that could close it. Dissatisfied with the pre-proof telling of Gatsby's past and the pacing of the middle chapters, he used the sheets before him rather than asking for a fresh setting.

That answer implies a comparison that is worth stating. Most writers experience the galley stage as a narrowing: options close, the text hardens, and change becomes correction by default. Fitzgerald experienced it as a last widening, a final chance to make structural decisions. The difference is not a matter of temperament alone. It reflects the fact that the novel's weakest passages, as he saw them, were structural rather than verbal: the biography needed rethinking, not rephrasing, and the middle chapters needed reshaping, not polishing. Proof sheets are a poor tool for structural revision, which is exactly why the scale of what he accomplished on them marks the depth of the need.

## The Circumstances: Distance, Deadlines, and the Last Instrument

The proof sheets of the novel then called Trimalchio reached Fitzgerald far from the Scribner's offices, in the opening months of 1925, and the geography of that moment shaped what he did with them. A writer correcting proofs within reach of the publisher works under one set of pressures; a writer correcting proofs across an ocean works under another. The sheets had to travel. Every round of correction cost weeks. The deadline for the spring publication stood fixed, and the machinery in New York waited on the author's marks. In that situation the economical choice would have been restraint: catch the misprints, approve the setting, let the book go. Fitzgerald chose the expensive alternative, and the circumstances make the choice legible. The distance that made restraint economical is the same distance that made the proofs the last instrument. There would be no second look, no office visit to repair what the sheets got wrong. Whatever the novel was going to be, the marks he made on those sheets would decide it.

The surviving correspondence between Fitzgerald and his editor shows a writer who understood the position he was in. He knew the galleys were the last point of control, and he wrote about the book with the urgency of a man who had run out of stages. None of this requires inventing private motives from the letters; it requires only reading them as what they are, the business record of a production. The production record shows the author treating the proof stage as the final composition surface. The letters show him pressing the editor on the same points the sheets show him pressing: the past, the middle, the ending. The correspondence and the handwriting agree, and the agreement is the evidence. The scholar who works the proof record should read the letters beside the sheets, not to supply motives the sheets lack, but to confirm that the revision campaign was deliberate rather than incidental.

There is a lesson in the circumstances for anyone who studies revision. Writers do not revise in the abstract. They revise inside constraints: deadlines, distances, formats, costs. The constraint of the galley stage is closure. Everything about the proof sheet says the text is finished, and the writer who revises anyway is arguing with the format, not only with the text. Fitzgerald's argument with the format was a long one, written in the margins of sheets that were supposed to be read once and returned. The student who wants to understand the proof revisions must understand the circumstances, because the circumstances are what turn the revisions from a curiosity into a decision. Anyone can rewrite a manuscript. Rewriting a set text, across an ocean, against a deadline, is a different kind of act, and it leaves a different kind of evidence.

The deadline deserves its own attention, because it sharpens the meaning of the lateness. The novel was scheduled for the spring of 1925, and the schedule was not flexible in the way a manuscript deadline is flexible. A book's publication date engages printers, binders, advertisers, and booksellers, and moving it costs money and standing. Fitzgerald knew this. He also knew that the changes he wanted, to the biography, to the middle chapters, to the closing, were changes that a compositor would have to set fresh, at the publisher's expense and against the clock. The proof sheets he returned carried those changes anyway. That is the measure of how inadequate he found the pre-proof text in the places he reworked. A writer does not spend that kind of capital, the publisher's money and the schedule's margin, on passages he regards as adequate. The circumstance of the deadline is therefore not background. It is part of the proof that the revision was substantial, because substantial revision under deadline pressure is the one kind of proof work nobody does by accident.

The distance had one more effect worth naming. Working alone with the sheets, Fitzgerald was his own editor, his own proofreader, and his own compositor's adversary. There was no editorial conference in which a colleague might talk him out of a large change or talk him into a small one. The decisions on the sheets are his alone, unmediated, which gives the proof record a particular purity as evidence of authorial intention. The biography rewritten, the middle reworked, the closing revised: each of these was Fitzgerald's call, made in solitude, written by hand, and sent across the ocean to become the book. The circumstances isolate the agency. That is why the proof record answers the question of what Fitzgerald wanted with unusual directness. The sheets show a writer alone with his novel at the last moment of control, and they show what he did with the moment.

The solitude of the proof work also shaped its tempo. Without an editor at his elbow, Fitzgerald could work at the speed of decision, moving from passage to passage as the sheets suggested the next problem. The proof record shows this tempo in the consistency of the campaign: the biography, the middle, and the closing reworked in a single sustained effort, not in scattered bursts. A writer interrupted by conferences and second opinions revises differently, in fits, with the marks showing the hesitations of collaboration. These sheets show no such hesitation. They show a writer who had thought the book through, who knew what needed changing, and who used the solitude to change it. The circumstances of distance, which made the revision expensive, also made it concentrated, and the concentration is part of what the record preserves.

## The Cost of the Changes: Why Proof Revision Is Expensive

Proof revision is expensive in the most literal sense, and the literal sense matters, because it converts the scale of Fitzgerald's changes from a literary judgment into a financial fact. When an author alters set type, the compositor must reset the altered lines, and the publisher bills the author for changes beyond a contracted allowance. A corrected misprint costs a line or two of resetting. A rewritten paragraph costs the paragraph. A reorganized chapter costs the chapter, and then some, because the resetting cascades through the surrounding pages. Fitzgerald's proof changes were of the cascading kind. The reorganization of the biography and the reworking of the middle chapters required fresh setting on a scale that no publisher's allowance was designed to absorb. The bill for the revision was the bill for a second composition, paid in the currency of the printing house.

The economics of the stage explain why proof revision of this scale is rare, and why its rarity is evidence. Publishers discourage it, compositors resent it, and schedules punish it. The contract of the galley stage, that the composition is settled and only errors remain, is not a literary convention. It is a business arrangement, and breaking it has a price. Most writers respect the arrangement because the price is real. Fitzgerald paid it. That payment is one of the strongest pieces of evidence this article can offer for the substantiality of the changes, because it is evidence that does not depend on anyone's reading of the handwriting. Whatever the sheets show, the fact that the author was willing to bear the cost of showing it tells the scholar that the changes mattered to him. Cost is a kind of testimony, and in this case it testifies to seriousness.

The cost also clarifies the distinction between correction and revision in a way that pure textual analysis cannot. A correction is cheap. It restores what was intended, and the resetting it requires is minimal. A revision is expensive. It imposes a new intention on set type, and the resetting it requires is extensive. When the scholar sorts the changes on Fitzgerald's sheets, the expensive ones are the revisions almost by definition: the rewritten biography, the relocated middle-chapter material, the recast transitions, the revised closing. These are the changes that cost, and they are the changes this article counts as the proof-stage revision. The cheap changes, the caught misprints and restored words, belong to the correction record, and they are not the subject of this article. The subject is the expensive handwriting, the handwriting that paid to change the book.

There is a further economic point, subtler but worth stating. The cost of proof revision is not only the compositor's bill. It is the risk. Every change made on a proof sheet is a change made without the safety of the manuscript stage, where a false step can be revised again. On the proofs, the revised text goes more or less directly to the page, and an error introduced at this stage is harder to catch and harder to repair. Fitzgerald was therefore gambling as well as spending: gambling that his late decisions about the biography, the middle, and the closing were right, with no further round of revision to save him if they were wrong. The gamble paid off, in the judgment of the novel's readers across the decades since, but it was a gamble all the same. The proof record is the record of a writer betting the book on his last decisions. That is the human meaning of the economics, and it is what makes the sheets worth studying as more than a curiosity of production.

The economics also explain a feature of the proof record that might otherwise puzzle the student: the unevenness of the revision. Fitzgerald spent his proof-stage capital where he judged the need greatest, and the sheets show the spending concentrated on the biography, the middle chapters, and the closing, with other passages left comparatively untouched. An even distribution of changes would suggest a writer polishing everything; a concentrated distribution suggests a writer solving specific problems. The concentration is the signature of the campaign. It tells the scholar where Fitzgerald thought the novel was weakest, which is information no manuscript can give with the same force, because the manuscript stage spreads revision across the whole text. The proof stage, with its costs, forces the writer to choose, and the choices are visible in the unevenness. Read the distribution of the marks, and the priorities of the revision emerge without any need to invent them.

## Gatsby's Biography: The Centerpiece of the Proof Revisions

The biography of Gatsby, the account of the young man from the North Dakota farmlands who became the figure on the Long Island lawns, was the single largest object of Fitzgerald's proof-stage attention. Chapter six, which carries the concentrated version of that account in the published novel, was substantially reworked on the galleys. The pre-proof text, preserved in the Trimalchio version printed by the 2000 Cambridge edition, contains a fuller, differently ordered telling of Gatsby's past than the one readers know. The published text carries the version Fitzgerald secured on the proofs: a biography restructured, its proportions altered, its narrative placement reconsidered, its relationship to the chapter around it changed.

What kind of reworking was this, exactly? The record supports description in kind. The biography material was reorganized, passages were recast, and the ordering of the account was revised so that the past arrived in the chapter with a different weight and a different rhythm than before. The apparatus of the 2019 variorum edition shows the proof layer as the layer where these changes entered, which is the textual fact that matters: whatever the biography looked like before the proofs, it was on the proofs that it became the biography the published novel contains. The change was not the addition of a fact about Gatsby's life. It was the rethinking of how the novel tells the past, which is a larger and more consequential decision than any single detail of the life itself.

Why did the biography demand this late labor? Because Gatsby's past is the novel's structural problem. The book must explain a mysterious figure without destroying the mystery, must give Nick a past to narrate without turning the narrator into a biographer, must make the invented self believable while keeping the original self legible underneath. Those are competing requirements, and getting their balance right is a matter of proportion, placement, and timing, which are exactly the qualities a proof-stage revision can address. Fitzgerald saw that the biography as first set did not balance those requirements to his satisfaction, and he used the sheets to rebalance it. The result is the chapter six that readers inherit: a biography that tells enough and withholds enough, arriving at the point in the novel where the withholding has become a question the plot itself is asking.

The pre-proof telling of the past, the version the typesetters set from the Trimalchio text, gave the biography a different weight in the novel than the published version gives it. Described in kind, the earlier telling was fuller and arranged on different principles, and its relationship to the chapter around it distributed the novel's attention differently. The proof revision did not add facts to the life. It redistributed the telling: the order in which the past arrives, the proportion of the chapter it occupies, and the point at which the disclosure interrupts the present action. Those three variables, order, proportion, and placement, are the whole of the revision, and they are also the whole of the difference between a biography that sits inside a novel and a biography that drives it.

Consider the problem from the novel's side. The Great Gatsby is narrated by a man who was not present for most of what he reports, and the past he reports is the past of a man who invented himself. The biography therefore has to do double work: it has to supply the facts of the life, and it has to preserve the quality of invention that makes the facts matter. A telling that leans too far toward fact turns Gatsby into a case history. A telling that leans too far toward invention turns him into a rumor. The published chapter six holds the balance, and the proof record shows that the balance was struck on the sheets. The pre-proof version, whatever its virtues, did not hold it to Fitzgerald's satisfaction. The proof revision is the record of a writer adjusting the weights until the scale settled.

The placement question deserves particular attention, because it is the kind of decision that only the proof stage could make in this production. The biography could have been told earlier, later, or in pieces, and the novel's effect changes with each choice. By the time the proofs arrived, the surrounding chapters were set, and the question of placement had to be answered within the set text. Fitzgerald's answer, written on the sheets, was to give chapter six the concentrated telling the published novel carries, positioned where the mystery of Gatsby has become the novel's central question. The placement is a proof-stage decision, and it is one of the most consequential in the book, because it determines when the reader learns what Nick learned and therefore how the rest of the novel is read.

## Chapters Six and Seven: The Middle of the Novel Reworked

The middle chapters of a novel are where structure is won or lost, and in The Great Gatsby the middle is the stretch where the biography, the confrontation, and the acceleration of the plot toward the disaster all converge. Fitzgerald's proof revisions addressed this stretch with the same substantiality they brought to the biography. Chapters six and seven were reworked on the galleys: material was relocated, passages were rewritten, and the pacing of the approach to the novel's climax was reconsidered. The brief that governs this article names these chapters as the textual anchor alongside the biography, and the documentary record supports the pairing. The biography is the deepest single change; the middle-chapter reworking is the broadest.

To understand why the middle needed revision, consider what chapters six and seven have to do. Chapter six must deliver the past while the present of the novel is still moving; chapter seven must stage the confrontation between Gatsby and Tom and set the machinery of the catastrophe in motion. Between the two lies the point where the novel's two registers, the lyric and the dramatic, have to join without a seam. That joining is a matter of architecture, not of sentences, and Fitzgerald used the proofs to work on it. The reworking he performed included the relocation of material between positions, the rewriting of passages that carried the transitions, and the adjustment of pacing so that the approach to the climax arrived at the right pressure. Described in kind, these are exactly the operations the proof record shows.

There is a temptation, when reading about proof-stage revision, to imagine the writer fiddling: a word here, a comma there, the author's anxiety given physical form. The middle-chapter revisions resist that picture. The changes to chapters six and seven were choices about what goes where and how much weight each position carries, which are compositional decisions of the first order. That Fitzgerald made them on the proofs, where every change cost more than it would have cost on typescript, is the measure of how wrong he thought the middle was in the setting. The published chapters six and seven are the record of a late, expensive decision that the novel's center had to be rebuilt.

The architecture of the middle can be stated as a sequence of obligations. Chapter six must slow the novel enough to deliver the past without stopping it. Chapter seven must then accelerate without breaking the lyric spell the earlier chapters have cast. Between them, the novel must turn its attention from who Gatsby was to what Gatsby wants, and the turn must feel like a deepening rather than a change of subject. That is a demanding design, and the pre-proof arrangement of the material did not satisfy it. The proof reworking addressed the obligations one by one: the past was repositioned so that the slowing felt earned, the transitions were rewritten so that the acceleration felt continuous, and the turn from biography to desire was re-paced so that the deepening read as inevitable.

The relocation of material is the operation that most clearly marks this work as revision rather than correction, and it is worth understanding exactly what relocation means on a proof sheet. To relocate material, the author must mark a passage for removal from one position, indicate its new position, and rewrite the seams at both ends so that the removal and the insertion do not show. On a galley sheet, where the text is already set in order, this is laborious. The author's hand must do the work of a compositor's rearrangement, and must do it clearly enough that the compositor can execute it without introducing new errors. Fitzgerald performed this labor on the middle chapters. The published order of the material in chapters six and seven is not the order in which the typesetters set it. It is the order Fitzgerald imposed on the sheets, and the imposition is visible in the apparatus as a proof-layer fact.

### Why was the middle of the novel still open on the proofs?

The proof revisions relocated material, rewrote transitional passages, and adjusted the pacing of the run toward the climax, making chapters six and seven compositional decisions rather than corrected settings. The record places this work at the proof layer: reordered sequences, recast transitions, and the biography rebalanced against the confrontation. The published middle is the version the proofs secured.

The larger point is that the middle of the novel, like the biography, carries its final authority from the lateness of its revision. Readers sometimes treat the architecture of a novel as though it were decided when the plot was invented, with the late stages adding only finish. The Gatsby proofs contradict that picture. The architecture of the middle was still being decided on the galleys, which means the finished novel's structure is a proof-stage achievement. That is a stronger claim than that Fitzgerald revised late. It is the claim that the novel was structurally incomplete until the proofs, and the proofs completed it.

## A Model Reading: The Biography Across Three Layers

The three-layer view is the instrument this subject requires, and the biography of Gatsby is the passage on which to learn it. The three layers are the pre-proof text, the proof sheets, and the published novel. The pre-proof text is preserved in the Trimalchio version printed by the 2000 Cambridge edition. The proof sheets are the layer of handwriting, collated by the apparatus of James L. W. West III's 2019 variorum edition. The published novel is the result. The model reading sets the three side by side and asks, at each point of difference, which layer the difference entered and what the difference decided. The exercise is the whole of textual scholarship in miniature, and the biography is the ideal text for it because its proof-stage transformation is the most substantial in the novel.

Begin with the pre-proof telling. Read the account of Gatsby's past as Trimalchio gives it, and attend to three variables: the order in which the life is told, the proportion of the chapter the telling occupies, and the placement of the disclosure within the novel's present action. Note the arrangement without judging it. The task at this stage is description, not evaluation. The scholar's first duty is to see what is there, and the pre-proof telling is there in full, a complete alternative to the version readers know. It is the baseline, and the baseline must be understood on its own terms before the comparison begins, because a comparison that caricatures the earlier state proves nothing about the later one.

Turn next to the published telling, the chapter six biography as the novel carries it. Attend to the same three variables: order, proportion, placement. The differences from the pre-proof telling will be visible at once, even described in kind rather than quotation. The past arrives in a different sequence. It occupies a different share of the chapter. It interrupts the present at a different point, with a different effect on the surrounding action. None of these differences is a correction. Each is a decision about how the novel tells its central mystery, and the decisions are legible in the finished text to any reader who knows to look for them. The published biography is the answer. ## The Hand on the Sheets: Reading Proof Marks as Decisions

A proof sheet carries several kinds of marks, and the textual scholar reads each kind differently. There are deletions, where the author's hand strikes through set type. There are insertions, where new wording is written between the lines or in the margin with a caret to show its place. There are transpositions, where numbered marks reorder passages. And there is marginal rewriting, where a whole passage is recast in the white space of the sheet, sometimes continued on a separate slip, with instructions to the compositor about what it replaces. Fitzgerald's sheets carry all four kinds, and the biography and middle-chapter revisions are rich in the fourth, the most laborious kind. The kind of the mark is itself evidence: marginal rewriting is the mark of a writer rethinking, not the mark of a writer tidying.

Reading the marks as decisions requires a particular caution. It is tempting to read a deletion as a rejection and an insertion as an embrace, to turn the handwriting into a diary of the writer's feelings about his own text. The discipline forbids this. A deletion shows that the author removed the passage, not why. An insertion shows that he added the wording, not what he felt about the wording it replaced. The scholar's business is the decision, which the mark records, and not the motive, which the mark does not. The Gatsby sheets reward this restraint, because the decisions they record are large enough to matter without any motive supplied: the biography reorganized, the middle relocated, the closing revised. Those are facts about the text. What Fitzgerald felt while making them is not on the sheets, and the honest scholar leaves it off the record.

The marks also show the order of decision, which is a subtler kind of evidence. Where the handwriting layers, where an insertion is itself corrected, where a transposition is renumbered, the scholar can see the author changing his mind in the course of the proof work. This is revision within revision: not only the decision to rewrite the biography, but the decisions made inside the rewriting, as one formulation replaced another on the sheet. The apparatus records these sub-layers where they are legible, and the variorum's collation is the instrument for seeing them. The student who learns to read the layering learns the most advanced skill the proof record teaches: the reconstruction of a writer's working sequence from the physical evidence of the hand. It is detective work, and like all detective work it requires the discipline of not concluding more than the marks support.

One more caution belongs here, and it concerns the most common misreading of proof marks. A heavily marked sheet does not necessarily show a passage in trouble, and a clean sheet does not necessarily show a passage that satisfied the author. The distribution of marks reflects where the author chose to work, not where the text was weakest in any absolute sense. Fitzgerald left some passages of the novel nearly untouched on the proofs, and the untouched passages include some of the most admired in the book. That does not mean those passages were perfect in his eyes. It means he spent his proof-stage capital where he judged the need greatest: the biography, the middle, the closing. The economy of the marks is the economy of the revision campaign, and it should be read as a statement of priorities. What he marked is what he still wanted to decide. What he left alone is what he was willing to let stand.

## The Complication: Proofs Are Not Only for Typos

The complication this article must address is the common assumption that proofs are for typos. It is a reasonable assumption, because it is true of almost every book. The galley stage is where errors are caught, and the corrections an author makes there are normally small, verbal, and custodial. The assumption becomes an error only when it is generalized into a rule about what proofs can show, and the Gatsby proofs are the case that breaks the rule. Fitzgerald's sheets show that the proof stage can be a revision stage, that a novel can still be taking shape where most novels are merely being checked, and that the evidence of late revision is not the presence of typos but the presence of rethought passages.

The distinction between correction and revision is the hinge of the whole argument, so it deserves a careful statement. A correction fixes an error in the realization of a settled intention: the typesetter dropped a word, and the author restores it. A revision changes the intention itself: the author reconsiders what the passage should do, and rewrites it to do the new thing. The handwriting looks the same. The difference is in what the change does to the text. Corrections leave the design alone. Revisions alter it. The proof changes to Gatsby's biography alter the design: they change how the novel tells the past, which is a change of intention, not of accuracy. The proof changes to the middle chapters alter the design: they change the order and pacing of the novel's center, which is again a change of intention. By that test, the Gatsby proofs are a revision record, and the typo assumption fails.

The test of intention can be applied by any reader, and it is worth practicing on a concrete case. Take the reorganization of the chapter six biography. As a correction, it would have to be the repair of some error in the pre-proof telling: a misprinted name, a dropped line, a transposed paragraph. But the pre-proof telling was not erroneous. It was a complete, coherent account, set accurately from the typescript. What Fitzgerald changed was not its accuracy but its design: the order, the proportion, the placement. No error explains that change. Only a new intention explains it. The test works because it forces the question the misconception avoids: what would the error have been. Where no error can be named, the change is a revision, and the proof record of this novel is full of changes for which no error can be named.

Why does the misconception persist, and why does it matter? It persists because it is true often enough to feel like a law, and because the labor of proof correction is invisible in the finished book: nobody reads a novel and sees the typos that were caught. It matters because it determines what questions a reader asks of textual evidence. The reader who assumes proofs are for typos will read the Gatsby proof record as a list of caught errors and miss the biography rewritten, the middle reworked, the closing revised. The reader who understands that proofs can be revision surfaces will ask the right question: what did the writer still want to change when change was most expensive.
