## Religion and Pilgrimage Imagery in Romeo and Juliet: The Verdict in Brief

The religion and pilgrimage imagery in this play is not decoration laid over a love story, and it is not a blasphemy the play smuggles past its audience. It is a working system: a complete sacred register, with its own shrine, its own pilgrim, its own sacrament, its own confessor, and its own liturgy of touch, prayer, sin, and pardon, which the lovers build in the space of a single sonnet and then maintain, under worsening pressure, for the whole length of the tragedy. The verdict argued through this article is that the sacred register ennobles the love rather than accusing it, because the worship inside the system is mutual, answerable, and sacrament-seeking: Juliet corrects Romeo's devotion as often as she receives it, the pilgrim's prayer is answered by the saint, and the private faith insists on the Church's own marriage rite to seal what it feels. The idolatry charge, pressed by the period's theology and by the moralising tradition that descends from Arthur Brooke's preface to the source poem, fails against the text: the lovers never mistake each other for God, and the play reserves its genuine idolatry for the feud, an honour cult that demands and receives human sacrifices in the street. What kills the lovers is not their faith but its homelessness. Their religion has rites and no institution, a confessor and no congregation, a sacrament performed in a cell and a congregation of two; when the letter fails and the friar flees the tomb, the faith has no structure left to catch them. The sacred imagery therefore does double judgement work. It measures the love upward, against the highest vocabulary the culture owned, and it measures Verona downward, against the worship the city actually practises.

That double work begins at the Capulet feast, where the system is founded in fourteen lines, and it continues everywhere the lovers' story is told in sacred terms: in a friar's cell at dawn, where grace is predicated of herbs and of a bride in the same conversation; in an orchard, where a vow sworn by the blessed moon is refused and a marriage demanded instead; in a chamber at dawn, where faith is located in heaven and a husband on earth; in a churchyard, where the parents who forbade the worship end by commissioning golden images of the worshipped. A reading that stops at the famous kiss, as the anthology habit does, mistakes the founding rite for the whole religion. The pages that follow restore the cluster: the pilgrim conceit read as a system rather than a scene, the sacred images catalogued across all five acts, the critics who have treated the register as a courtly game, as a cluster of light, and as a Christian problem set against one another and adjudicated, the friar's compromised office examined, the Catholic setting weighed against the Protestant audience that first heard the lines, and the machinery of shrift, vows, and sacrament traced through the plot. The sacred register is the play's second language, and it is spoken fluently by more characters, in more situations, than the lovers' two mouths.

## The Pilgrim at the Shrine: How the Sacred Register Is Built

The founding exchange at the feast (1.5.92-105, in René Weis's Arden Third Series edition, the text quoted throughout this article) is usually excerpted as a jewel of courtship, and its close reading belongs to the kiss itself. What matters for the system is its architecture. Romeo's opening gambit, "If I profane with my unworthiest hand / This holy shrine, the gentle sin is this: / My lips, two blushing pilgrims, ready stand / To smooth that rough touch with a tender kiss" (1.5.92-95), assigns every role the religion will need. Juliet is the shrine, the consecrated place where the sacred resides and toward which devotion travels. Romeo is the pilgrim, the traveller whose devotion is defined by distance, effort, and arrival. Sin enters at once, not as an afterthought but as the currency of the exchange: the touch is a profanation, the kiss a fine paid for it, and the whole economy of offence and pardon that the Church administered through penance is miniaturised into a flirtation. The conceit is often praised for its daring, the erotic charge of sacred words put to profane use, but its structural achievement is greater than its daring. In four lines the play establishes a sacred geography (a shrine, set apart, approached with reverence), a sacred personnel (saint and pilgrim), a sacred physics (touch transmits both sin and blessing), and a sacred economics (sins are incurred, fined, purged, and returned). Every later use of the register in the play is a withdrawal from this founding deposit.

The lore the conceit draws on was concrete, not vague. A pilgrim was a person under a vow, travelling to a shrine where a saint's relics were housed, in order to touch, kiss, or be sprinkled at the holy place, to leave an offering, and to carry home a badge or a token as proof of the journey completed. A palmer was a pilgrim of the senior kind, one who had made the Jerusalem journey and carried the palm branch that gave the order its name. Shrines were touched because holiness in the period's devotional practice was transmissible by contact: the faithful pressed hands, rosaries, and cloths against reliquaries and tombs so that the virtue of the place would pass into the object and the body. Romeo's apology for a touch, and his request to substitute a kiss for the touch his hand has taken, are precise applications of that physics. The hand has profaned by contact without licence; the lips, figured as pilgrims, seek the contact that blesses rather than offends. When Juliet answers, "Good pilgrim, you do wrong your hand too much, / Which mannerly devotion shows in this; / For saints have hands that pilgrims' hands do touch, / And palm to palm is holy palmers' kiss" (1.5.96-99), she does not merely accept the metaphor. She corrects its theology. The touch was never a profanation, because the shrine is made for touching; devotion of the mannerly kind is the shrine's purpose, not its violation. The saint in this system is not a remote intercessor but a participant who knows the rite better than the pilgrim does and instructs him in it.

That correction is the system's constitution, and it is why the register cannot be read as one-sided worship of a passive idol. Across the fourteen lines the lovers speak a sonnet jointly, quatrain answering quatrain, and the sacred terms pass back and forth like a ball: shrine, pilgrims, sin, saints, palmers, prayer, faith, despair. Romeo proposes; Juliet disposes. His second quatrain presses the logic of prayer toward the kiss, "O, then, dear saint, let lips do what hands do; / They pray; grant thou, lest faith turn to despair" (1.5.102-103), and her couplet-line holds the rite at its proper station, "Saints do not move, though grant for prayers' sake" (1.5.104). The saint grants by stillness, the pilgrim receives by approach, and the kiss, when it comes, is framed as a prayer's effect taken rather than a liberty seized. Then the register performs its neatest trick: sin, purged by the kiss, is discovered on the saint's lips and demanded back, "Sin from thy lips? O trespass sweetly urged! / Give me my sin again" (1.5.108-109), and Juliet closes the liturgy with the driest line in it, "You kiss by th' book" (1.5.109). The book in question is double, the prayer book and the manual of courtship, and the joke only works because the system is already complete enough to have a book. A metaphor improvised for a compliment does not generate its own rubrics in ninety seconds. This one does, because Shakespeare is not decorating a compliment with sacred words but staging the foundation of a faith, with doctrine (touch transmits), rite (palm to palm), clergy (the saint who instructs), and scripture (the book) all in place before the Nurse interrupts. The kiss exchange itself, its speeches apportioned and its pilgrimage lore glossed line by line, is the subject of [the first kiss: pilgrims and palmers' hands](/2023/07/14/first-kiss-pilgrim-imagery-analysis/); the system that exchange founds, and its career across the whole play, is the subject here.

### Where does the play's sacred language appear outside the lovers' own scenes?

The sacred register appears in the mouths of the Friar, the Nurse, both households' parents, Paris, and the Prince, in scenes from which both lovers are absent. Verona is a Catholic city whose speech is stocked with the faith's vocabulary; the lovers' distinction is that they use that vocabulary deliberately, as a system, while the city uses it as weather.

The Friar blesses, warns, and absolves in it; Capulet rages in it, threatening to drag his daughter to church; the Friar's consolation at the false funeral speaks of heaven's will; the Prince's closing judgement speaks of a scourge laid by heaven on the fathers' hate. The register's spread across speakers is the evidence that Shakespeare built the lovers' faith out of the common stock of Verona's religion, heightening a civic idiom into a private liturgy rather than importing an exotic one.

The distinction matters because it locates the play's irony. Verona is saturated with the forms of the faith: a friar is the city's confidant of choice, confession is the standing errand, church is where brides are taken and bodies are mourned. Yet the city's actual worship, the cult that organises its loyalties and demands its offerings, is the feud. The sacred register in the lovers' mouths is therefore not an escape from Verona's religion into fantasy; it is a repatriation. They take the vocabulary the city spends on processions and funerals and spend it on each other, and the play measures both expenditures. When the Friar, greeting Romeo's news, exclaims "Holy Saint Francis! what a change is here!" (2.3.35), the order's founder is invoked as a casual oath in the cell of a Franciscan who is about to break his church's discipline for the lovers' sake. The line is a joke, and it is also the system's signature: in this play, sacred language is always doing two jobs at once, the devotional and the human, and its speakers are always slightly at cross purposes with their own words. The lovers alone speak the register with full knowledge of what it commits them to. That is the first reason the imagery ennobles rather than decorates.

## Pilgrims, Palmers and Shrines: The Devotional World the Words Came From

The conceit's precision becomes visible only against the practice it quotes, because pilgrimage in the centuries before the Reformation was not a metaphor but an industry, a discipline, and a geography. The great destinations formed a graded map of holiness: Jerusalem first, where the palmer's branch was earned; Rome, with its apostles' tombs and its jubilees; Santiago de Compostela in Galicia, where the apostle James lay and where the scallop shell became the pilgrim badge of badges; and the national shrines, Canterbury for Thomas Becket, Walsingham for the Virgin's house, each with its roads, its hospices, its offerings, and its seasons. A pilgrimage began with a vow and a blessing of scrip and staff, proceeded on foot as an act of penance as much as travel, and ended at the shrine with a sequence of acts the feast scene reproduces in miniature: approach, confession, offering, and contact. The pilgrim touched the shrine, kissed the reliquary, pressed beads and cloths against the holy place to carry its virtue home, left money or wax or a votive image in payment of the vow, and received a badge, a leaden token stamped with the saint's sign, as proof of completion and protection for the road back. Every element of that sequence is present, transposed, in fourteen lines of dialogue at a dance: the approach under apology, the touch debated and licensed, the kiss as contact with the holy, the sin as a fine to be paid and then purged, the saint instructing the pilgrim in the correct form of the devotion. Shakespeare's audience did not need the sequence explained, because the sequence was the common inheritance of European devotion, still legible in the 1590s from ruins, badges, place names, and grandparents' stories, even where the practice itself had been suppressed.

The palmer stood at the top of this hierarchy, and Juliet's promotion of Romeo from pilgrim to palmer inside the sonnet is a promotion in orders. The pilgrim was any traveller to a shrine; the palmer was the pilgrim who had completed the Jerusalem journey and carried the palm, in some accounts a professional or perpetual pilgrim, one whose life was the road between shrines. When Juliet answers Romeo's "pilgrims" with "palmers", she upgrades his devotion from the local to the ultimate destination, and she does it inside a correction, which is the scene's characteristic move: the saint knows the grades of devotion better than the devotee. The palm itself carried the meaning of victory and of paradise regained, the branch laid on the Jerusalem road and the leaf of the tree of life; to call the kiss a palmer's kiss is to locate it at the journey's end, in the holy place itself, rather than on the way to it. Romeo's answering pressure, from hands to lips, is a pilgrim's impatience to complete the rite in its fullest form, and Juliet's stillness is the shrine's answer: the holy does not travel; the traveller arrives.

Shrine devotion had its own theology of matter, and the play's imagery assumes it throughout. The saint's holiness was held to reside in the relic and to radiate from it, so that proximity and touch were channels of grace: the sick were laid near tombs, the faithful queued to kiss the reliquary's crystal, and objects touched to the shrine, brandea in the old terminology, became secondary relics, carrying the place's power into homes and onto bodies. This is the physics behind the exchange of sin across the kiss, in which a moral quantity is transferred by lip contact as if it were a substance, and behind the play's later life, in which a ring sent by the Nurse revives Romeo in the cell and a vial carried to a bedroom does the work of a sacrament in reverse. The lovers' world is one in which things mediate persons: hands, rings, letters, potions, statues. Protestant teaching had attacked precisely this mediation, insisting on a faith without channels, and the attack is what gives the imagery its second, polemical life in London. But inside Verona the physics is unchallenged and fertile, and the play exploits it with the confidence of a dramatist who knows his audience remembers how holiness was once believed to move. The pilgrimage conceit, read against its world, is therefore not a flight of fancy but a piece of applied theology, the lovers' first act of world-building: a sacred geography with Juliet at its centre, a road with Romeo on it, and a physics by which the road and the centre can meet.

Three features of the catalogue deserve emphasis. The first is the migration of the imagery from place to person to object. At the feast the sacred is located in a place, the shrine that Juliet is said to be; in the middle acts it is located in acts and offices, the holy act of marriage, the confessor's cell, the errand of shrift; at the end it is located in objects, a letter that arrives too late to be read as testimony, and two statues in pure gold. The faith begins as an address between living speakers and ends as artefacts in the keeping of the bereaved. The second feature is the imagery's survival of its speakers. The lovers die at the beginning of the final movement, yet the register does not die with them: the Friar's providence, the Prince's scourge, and the fathers' votive gold are all spoken after the tomb, by men who never shared the lovers' liturgy, in the vocabulary that liturgy made current. The third feature is the imagery's even spread between the lovers. Romeo founds the system, but the catalogue shows Juliet matching him image for image and exceeding him in theological precision: hers are the refusal of the moon oath, the statement of faith in heaven, and the division of the marriage between God's joining and the friar's sealing. A register sustained by both parties, in both voices, across five acts, is a structure of the play, not a habit of one character's speech. The light imagery that Shakespeare sets beside the sacred, in the lovers' inverted world of day and night, is traced in the companion study of [day and night: the lovers' inverted world](/2024/02/02/day-and-night-imagery-romeo-juliet/), and the two systems interlock: the saint is also the sun, and the shrine is also the east window. What the catalogue isolates is the doctrinal half of that double system, the half that speaks of sin, grace, prayer, and pardon, and its claim is that the doctrinal half is load-bearing.

One thread in the catalogue repays separate attention: prayer, the register's central act, keeps changing its address. At the feast, prayer is what lips do, and its answer is a kiss: the pilgrim prays, the saint grants for prayers' sake, and the prayer's effect is taken on the spot. In the cell the next morning, prayer has become petition to an officer, Romeo's "this I pray, / That thou consent to marry us" (2.3.67-68), and its answer is a sacrament scheduled for the afternoon. Thereafter prayer recedes as the plot darkens, displaced by its institutional cousins, confession, counsel, and absolution, until at the end no character is shown praying at all: the tomb scene contains narrative, judgement, and bargain, but no prayer over the bodies, as if the register's central act had been exhausted by its own history. The trajectory is quiet and exact. A faith that began by making prayer erotic ends by finding prayer impossible, and the catalogue's order lets the loss be seen: the word prayer belongs to the play's morning, and the afternoon belongs to law.

## Caroline Spurgeon and the Sacred Cluster: What the Count Shows

Caroline Spurgeon's Shakespeare's Imagery and What It Tells Us (1935) changed the study of this play by counting. Her method was to extract every image in a work, sort the images into clusters, and read the dominant cluster as the play's imaginative atmosphere, the medium in which its thought moves. For Romeo and Juliet her celebrated finding was the light cluster: the lovers are figured, by themselves and by everyone who describes them, in terms of light in darkness, sun, moon, stars, lightning, torches, and dawn, with a density unmatched elsewhere in the canon. The sacred imagery has usually been treated as a tributary of that finding, a few bright metaphors in the light cluster's stream, because Spurgeon's own emphasis fell on radiance. Yet her method, applied strictly, returns a verdict on the religious register that her emphasis understudied. A cluster, in her terms, is proved by recurrence across the whole work and across speakers, and by the images' tendency to complete and answer one another. The sacred terms pass every one of her tests. Shrine answers to pilgrim as pilgrim answers to saint; prayer in the first act is answered by shrift in the fourth and by the confessor's narrative in the fifth; the sin taken on the lips at the feast is the same coin as the sin the Friar names in rebuke, "O deadly sin! O rude unthankfulness!" (3.3.24), and the grace predicated of plants in the cell is the same word Romeo turns back on the Friar an hour later in stage time, "she whom I love now / Doth grace for grace and love for love allow" (2.3.81-82). Spurgeon's great insight was that Shakespeare thinks in image systems whose members gloss one another across thousands of lines. The sacred cluster is one of her systems, whether or not she paused over it: a complete devotional vocabulary, internally cross-referenced, running from the first act's shrine to the fifth act's scourge.

The count also settles a question of weight. A decorative metaphor appears where a speech needs colour and is not missed when it goes. The sacred register appears at every hinge of the plot. It is the language of the meeting (1.5), of the vow and its correction (2.2), of the marriage's arrangement (2.3) and celebration (2.6), of the crisis of conscience (3.5, 4.1), of the plot's machinery (the shrift errands of 3.5 and 4.2), of the catastrophe's interpretation (5.3). Remove it and the play loses not a colour but a connective tissue: the scenes would still occur, but the terms in which the characters understand what is occurring to them, terms of sin, grace, faith, heaven, blessing, and scourge, would be gone, and with them the play's own account of its events. Spurgeon's method has its documented critics, who argue that counting images cannot by itself prove a design, since a concordance cannot distinguish a pattern intended from a pattern assembled by the counter. The objection has force against a cluster that exists only in the count. It has none against this one, because the sacred cluster is not merely counted but staged: the play builds a shrine out of two actors' hands, performs a sacrament in a friar's cell, sends its heroine on errands of confession, and sets its final scene among tombs in a churchyard. The images are the actions. Where Spurgeon's method and the stage agree, the design is not in dispute.

Her method also catches the sacred cluster's entanglement with the play's imagery of physic, an entanglement the catalogue records in the Friar's double practice. Spurgeon's clusters were never sealed compartments; she showed images breeding across categories, and in this play the devotional and the medical grow together because both are administered by the same man. The Friar is physician to bodies, with his basket of herbs and his doctrine of poison and medicine, and physician to souls, with his confessional and his sacraments, and the play's language keeps the two practices in one vocabulary: Romeo describes himself and Juliet as two wounded people who need the Friar's physic; the potion is a sacrament administered as a drug; the poison Romeo buys in Mantua is a medicine taken against the disease of living. On the cluster method, this is not a pun but a system: grace in the play behaves like physic, infused, dosed, and capable of killing in the wrong measure, and the Friar's herb doctrine, grace and rude will contending in man as in plants (2.3.27-28), is the theology of both his practices at once. The sacred cluster, followed by Spurgeon's rules, thus proves larger than the feast that founds it: it includes the pharmacy.

There is a further dividend in her approach, and it concerns the idolatry question that the next sections take up. Spurgeon treated imagery as the playwright's thought below the level of statement, the stratum where a play's real valuations live. If the sacred register were the play's joke at the lovers' expense, a blasphemous game the text invites its audience to condemn, the cluster would be expected to decay as the tragedy deepens, to curdle into parody or punishment, as the bawdy cluster around Mercutio dies with Mercutio. It does the opposite. The register's most solemn uses are its latest: Juliet's "my faith in heaven" (3.5.205), her "God joined my heart and Romeo's, thou our hands" (4.1.55), the Friar's providence and the Prince's scourge in the tomb's audit. The imagery matures with the suffering instead of being discarded by it. On Spurgeon's own principles, that trajectory is the signature of a valuation the play endorses. The count, the staging, and the trajectory point the same way, and they frame the question the critics of the register's meaning then have to answer: if the sacred language is structural and endorsed, what exactly is its status, a courtly game played in earnest, or a faith the play itself believes in?

## The Religion of Love: C. S. Lewis and the Courtly Code

The strongest account of the register as a game comes from the tradition C. S. Lewis anatomised in The Allegory of Love (1936). Lewis showed that the courtly love of the troubadours and their heirs operated as a deliberate counter-religion: it had its god, its service, its humility, its martyrdoms, and its rewards, and it borrowed the Church's vocabulary wholesale, addressing the lady as a saint, figuring the lover as her worshipper and suppliant, and treating her favour as a species of grace. Lewis's crucial insistence was on the code's formality. The religion of love was, in his account, a system of manners raised to a metaphysic, a game whose rules everyone understood, in which the sacred vocabulary signified devotion by convention rather than belief by confession. The lover who called his lady a saint was not committing idolatry, because the trope was legible to its audience as the period's highest compliment, translated into the only superlative register available. Petrarch stands behind Shakespeare as the code's great Italian master: the Canzoniere worships Laura in language drawn from the liturgy, dates the first sight of her to Good Friday, the day of the Crucifixion, in the church of Sainte-Claire at Avignon, and builds a calendar of anniversaries in which erotic and sacred time are made to coincide. When Romeo casts himself as a pilgrim and Juliet as a shrine, a 1590s audience formed on the sonnet tradition heard a known dialect. The pilgrim conceit is Petrarchan currency, and part of its first effect is recognition: the audience knows this religion, has read its hymns, and can grade the performance, as Juliet does when she marks the kiss as executed by the book.

The code reached Shakespeare's audience freshly stocked. The English sonnet sequences of the 1590s, Sidney's Astrophil and Stella above all, had naturalised the religion of love for the London ear: Astrophil worships Stella in language borrowed from devotion, complains of his service, and treats her disdain with the patience of a martyr, while the sequence's wit depends on the reader's knowing that the creed is a courtly instrument. Andreas Capellanus, whose De Amore codified the tradition centuries earlier, had given the religion its laws and its church of love, with commandments, judgements, and a clergy of experienced lovers. A playgoer of 1595 had therefore heard the pilgrim conceit many times before the feast scene, in sonnets read and songs sung, and heard it as a known pleasure, the pleasure of watching a familiar liturgy performed well. Shakespeare's stroke, visible only against this saturation, is to treat the liturgy as if it were binding. His pilgrim does not go home to write a sonnet about the shrine; he goes to a friar. The audience's recognition of the code is thus turned against the audience's expectations, and the comedy of the feast, Juliet grading the performance by the book, becomes the tragedy's first act in retrospect: the book's rules, followed in earnest, lead to the cell, the potion, and the tomb.

Lewis's account explains the conceit's polish, and it explains the comedy inside the sacred exchange, the wit with which Juliet corrects the rite and caps it. It does not explain what the play does with the code after the feast. A game has no consequences outside itself, but Shakespeare submits the religion of love to the jurisdiction of the actual Church and watches the two systems collide. The lovers do not rest in the trope: within hours the pilgrim is in a real friar's cell, asking for a real sacrament, and the saint is stipulating that the devotion be cashed out in marriage or not at all, "If that thy bent of love be honourable, / Thy purpose marriage, send me word tomorrow" (2.2.145-146). The court's religion, in Lewis's telling, was precisely a substitute for marriage, an adulterous devotion that flourished in the space marriage left empty; Shakespeare's lovers invert the tradition by dragging the trope into the sacrament, demanding that the metaphor produce a wedding. The code's own logic is thereby broken from inside. A compliment that insists on becoming a marriage is no longer a compliment, and a game whose players invoke a real confessor, a real rite, and real vows has stopped being only a game. The play knows the difference and marks it in the Friar's astonishment: his "Holy Saint Francis!" (2.3.35) is the sound of the Church's officer discovering that the courtly religion has arrived at his door asking to be made official.

The adjudication between Lewis and the text therefore runs in the text's favour, with a concession. Lewis is right about the register's origin and its first effect: the vocabulary is inherited, conventional, and legible as compliment, and no account of the imagery that ignores the Petrarchan code can explain its fluency. He is wrong, or rather his account is incomplete, about the register's fate in this particular play, because the drama treats the convention as raw material for a working faith rather than as a finished performance. Shakespeare's originality is not the invention of the sacred conceit, which the sonneteers had worn smooth, but its institutionalisation: he gives the religion of love a minister, a sacrament, a discipline of confession, a doctrine of grace, and a martyrology, and then tests the completed system against the pressures of a hostile city. The test is tragedy, but the system under test is real within the fiction. That distinction, between a trope the audience recognises and a faith the characters inhabit, is the hinge on which the idolatry question turns: a trope cannot be idolatrous, since nobody believes it, but a faith can, and the play has deliberately raised the stakes from trope to faith. The charge must now be heard in its strongest form.

## The Editor's Evidence: The Pilgrim Sonnet in the Early Texts

The text of the lovers' liturgy reaches the modern reader through editorial hands, and the editorial record is itself evidence for the system's importance. The play survives in the 1597 first quarto, a shorter and rougher text generally treated by editors as a reported version of the play in performance, and in the 1599 second quarto, the fuller and more authoritative text on which René Weis's Arden Third Series edition, the text quoted throughout this article, is based. The pilgrim exchange is present in both. That fact matters more than any variant within it. A reported text, assembled from memory under the pressures of the playhouse, preserves what performance made unforgettable, and the shared sonnet survived the reporting intact in its structure and nearly intact in its wording: the shrine, the blushing pilgrims, the palmers' kiss, the purged sin are all there in 1597, in a version of the play that elsewhere compresses, transposes, and garbles. The liturgy was already, within two years of the play's first performances, the scene an audience carried out of the theatre. Editors from the eighteenth century onward, printing the second quarto's fuller text, have treated the exchange as a set piece whose wording is to be preserved with special care, and the modern lineation that lets the sonnet be counted, fourteen lines apportioned between two speakers, is an editorial gift the criticism of the register silently depends on.

Weis's editorial choices frame the imagery in two further ways. The first is his retention of the second quarto's readings at the points where the sacred vocabulary is sharpest, so that the text's devotional terms stand unmodernised in their strangeness: the gentle sin, the palmers, the ghostly confessor are glossed in his commentary rather than smoothed in his text, and a reader of the Arden page meets the Catholic machinery as machinery, with notes explaining the confessor's office and the palmer's palm to an audience for whom both are archaeology. The second is the apparatus's quiet demonstration of how much of the register is stable across the tradition. Where the quartos differ in the love scenes, they differ in connective tissue, in the prose of servants and the phrasing of transitions; the sacred set pieces, the sonnet, the marriage scene's holy church, the cell's doctrine of grace, hold their wording across both texts with a consistency that suggests set speeches, learned and kept. An imagery system that survives textual transmission undisturbed, in the roughest reported version and the fullest authoritative one alike, is not an accident of any single printing. The editors' evidence therefore corroborates the critics' from an independent direction: the register was load-bearing in the playhouse before it was load-bearing in the study, and the article's argument from recurrence rests on a text whose recurrence is documentary, not conjectural.

## Ennoblement or Idolatry: The Charge the Imagery Has to Answer

The charge, stated at full strength, is this. In the theology of the period, worship belonged to God alone; the veneration of saints, which Catholic practice defended as honour paid to God's friends and as requests for their intercession, was distinguished from the worship due to the Creator by the careful scholastic terms latria and dulia, but Protestant polemic collapsed the distinction and named the whole economy of shrines, images, pilgrimages, and saint-invocation idolatry. The English Church's articles condemned the invocation of saints and the worshipping of images; the shrines of England, Canterbury and Walsingham among them, had been dismantled two generations before the play; pilgrimage itself was abolished as a practice. Against that background, a young man who calls a girl a shrine, figures his lips as pilgrims, takes his sin from her mouth, and treats her hand as a relic to be kissed is not merely complimenting her in an available idiom. He is performing, in miniature and in reverse, exactly the devotional acts the reformed church had forbidden toward saints, and he is performing them toward a person met an hour earlier at a party. The moralising tradition that descends from Arthur Brooke's preface to The Tragical History of Romeus and Juliet (1562) read the play's world in something like these terms: Brooke warned his readers against the lovers' "dissolute desire" and against the "superstitious friars" who abetted it, presenting the story as a caution in which unlawful love and corrupt clergy destroy the young. On the Brooke reading, the sacred register is the disease, not the diagnosis: the lovers perish because they have made a god of each other and a confessional of their conspiracy, and the Friar's complicity is the Church's corruption made visible. Roy Battenhouse's Christian reading of the tragedies gives the charge its scholarly form: within a providential order, the lovers' self-worship is a disorder of love, a good thing loved in the wrong way and the wrong measure, and the catastrophe is the order reasserting itself. The disagreement to be adjudicated is therefore sharp. Either the sacred imagery is the play's ornament for a devotion it approves, or it is the play's evidence against a devotion it condemns.

The evidence, weighed scene by scene, acquits the lovers and convicts their city. Begin with the theology itself, which the play handles with surprising exactness. The lovers' worship, for all its daring, observes the very distinction the idolatry charge depends on: nothing in the register asks Juliet to do what only God can do. She is asked to grant a kiss, as a saint grants a prayer, by stillness and favour; she is never asked to forgive sins in her own right, to save a soul, or to receive the worship of the heart's ultimate allegiance, and when the question of allegiance arises Juliet is explicit about its address. Her marriage, she tells the Friar, was God's act before it was the Friar's: "God joined my heart and Romeo's, thou our hands" (4.1.55). The line is theologically precise. God joins hearts; the minister joins hands; the saint of the feast has become a wife who assigns the sacrament to its proper agents and herself to neither the priest's place nor God's. Likewise Romeo, at the height of the conceit, figures his sin as purged by a kiss, but the play never lets the figure harden into a claim that Juliet absolves; the absolution machinery of the plot, such as it is, belongs to the Friar, and its failure is the plot's tragedy. What the lovers practise is veneration raised to the pitch of love, dulia in the scholastic sense, honour and intercession and the kiss of peace, and the play's Catholic Verona provides the doctrinal shelf on which that practice sits without scandal. The scandal exists only for the audience's other ear, the Protestant one, and the play's use of that double hearing is examined in the next section but one. As a charge against the characters, within their world, idolatry does not stick.

### What is the difference between veneration and worship in the period's theology?

The distinction was between latria, the adoration owed to God alone, and dulia, the honour lawfully paid to saints, whose prayers were sought as intercession rather than adoration. A pilgrim kissing a shrine honoured the saint and, through the saint, God; reformed polemic answered that the distinction collapsed in practice.

The play's lovers stay on the veneration side of the line in everything they ask and grant: Juliet is entreated as an intercessor and a presence, never petitioned as a saviour, and her own speeches assign the joining of hearts to God. The idolatry charge therefore describes what a hostile observer might call the imagery, not what the imagery, read closely, does.

The positive evidence for ennoblement is stronger still, and it is Juliet who supplies it. Twice she intervenes to purify the register when Romeo's use of it threatens to become mere worship. At the feast she corrects the pilgrim's premise, teaching him that the shrine's holiness is a relation, not a property: saints have hands, pilgrims' hands touch, and the holiness is in the meeting. In the orchard she polices the oath, refusing the moon as a warrant, "O, swear not by the moon, th'inconstant moon, / That monthly changes in her circled orb, / Lest that thy love prove likewise variable" (2.2.109-111), and substitutes the only sacred instrument she trusts, the marriage rite itself. A worshipped idol does not revise the liturgy, reject inadequate oaths, and demand a sacrament. Juliet behaves throughout as the faith's theologian rather than its object, and the play's deepest sacred line is hers, spoken in despair to the Nurse and through her to no one: "My husband is on earth, my faith in heaven; / How shall that faith return again to earth, / Unless that husband send it me from heaven / By leaving earth?" (3.5.205-208). The conceit of the feast has become a metaphysics of marriage: faith is a substance that can be located, displaced, and lost, and the speech weighs it with a precision no merely decorative imagery sustains. Battenhouse's disorder charge, measured against such lines, mistakes the play's valuation. The disorder the play actually anatomises is located elsewhere, in a city where fathers treat daughters as assets, where honour is a god that receives Mercutio, Tybalt, Paris, and the lovers as its offerings, and where the Prince, surveying the sacrifices, can only name the mechanism in the lovers' own sacred vocabulary, heaven finding means to kill the fathers' joys with love (5.3.292-293). If any cult in Verona is idolatrous, demanding worship, forbidding questions, and eating its devotees, it is the feud. The lovers' faith is the play's counter-church, and its imagery is the standard by which the reigning idolatry is judged.

## The Friar's Office: A Church of One Minister

Every faith needs its clergy, and the lovers' faith has exactly one minister, whose compromised office is the system's strength and its fatal weakness. Friar Laurence is a Franciscan, a member of the order founded by the saint whose name he swears by, and the play loads his office with every function the private religion requires. He is confessor: Juliet greets him as her "ghostly confessor" (2.6.20), the period's term for the priest who hears the soul's account, and Romeo treats the cell as a place where hearts are opened and physic prescribed. He is celebrant: he performs the marriage, blessing it in advance with a prayer that heaven smile on "this holy act" (2.6.1) even as he hastens it through in a side room with no witnesses, no banns, and no parents. He is theologian: his herb soliloquy lays down the system's doctrine of creation, in which the same earth is womb and tomb and the same plant holds medicine and poison, "In man as well as herbs, grace and rude will" (2.3.27). He is, in the end, the faith's failed evangelist and its surviving witness, the man who must narrate the whole design to the Prince because no one else knows it entire. The irony the brief requires is exact: a friar, vowed to poverty, chastity, and obedience, becomes the enabler of a clandestine marriage, a dispenser of a feigned-death potion, and a plotter of elopements, and he does it all in the language of his office, blessing, warning, absolving, and providing. His motives, as he states them, are pastoral and civic at once: the marriage may turn the households' rancour to pure love (2.3.91-92). The Church's sacrament is conscripted as an instrument of public peace, and the lovers' private faith is, from its minister's side, a piece of social engineering. The lovers believe in the rite; the Friar believes in its effects. That gap in the sanctuary is where the tragedy enters.

His ministry to Romeo in the cell after the banishment shows the office at its most characteristic, a priesthood translated into accountancy and Stoic counsel. He rebukes the despairing bridegroom in the register's own terms, "O deadly sin! O rude unthankfulness!" (3.3.24), naming ingratitude as the sin of a man who has been given Juliet alive, Tybalt dead by his own hand, and a prince's mercy in place of the law's death. Then he audits the case, counting the blessings in a pack: the bride lives, the enemy is dead, the sentence is exile and not execution. The consolation is real and it is recognisably a confessor's technique, the examination of a conscience by enumeration, but its scripture is philosophy, the "sweet milk" of adversity he promises as armour against despair (3.3.54-55). The lovers' faith, in its crisis, is thus pastored by a wisdom that is only partly its own: the Friar comforts with Seneca where the system would prescribe prayer, and plans where the system would prescribe patience. None of this is villainy; it is the sound of a humane man improvising a theology of management for a catastrophe his office was never designed to hold. But the improvisation is diagnostic. A church whose minister reaches for philosophy and logistics at the moment of trial is a church whose own resources have run out, and the lovers, who never doubt the faith, are left to be failed by its administration.

His language, examined against theirs, shows the difference. The lovers' sacred register is constitutive: it creates the relation it names, shrine and pilgrim, saint and suppliant, husband and faith. The Friar's sacred register is administrative: it manages, schedules, warns, and files. He speaks in blessings and in proverbs, in the sententious couplets of a man accustomed to being quoted, and his holiest utterances are conditionals, heaven smiling on the act if the act is rightly used, violent delights warned against their violent ends. Even his doctrine of grace is a doctrine of balance, of powers held in check, learned from plants. None of this makes him a hypocrite, and the play does not treat him as one; the Prince's final description of him as a holy man whose word the letter confirms is the city's considered verdict, and his narrative in the tomb is accepted as the truth of the matter. What it makes him is a church reduced to a single conscientious official, and a church of one cannot bear the weight a church of many distributes. When the crisis comes, the system's entire infrastructure is one man's legs between Verona and Mantua, one colleague's quarantine, one letter's delivery. The lovers' faith had sacraments, doctrine, and a minister; it had no redundancy, no hierarchy to appeal to, no congregation to shelter it, and no successor when its minister, at the tomb's mouth, hears the watch and runs. His flight is the system's institutional failure in a single stage direction, and his return under guard, to confess and to offer his old life to the rigour of the law, is its institutional confession: the minister submits the faith's whole history to the state's audit, because the faith has no court of its own.

### How does the Friar's sacred language differ from the lovers' sacred language?

The difference is between a register that constitutes and a register that administers. The lovers' sacred words make the thing they name: calling Juliet a shrine establishes the shrine, and their vows bind because the speaking is the act. The Friar's sacred words manage what already exists, blessing, warning, scheduling, and finally narrating.

His vocabulary is the same, grace, blessing, holy, heaven, but its grammar is conditional and procedural where theirs is present and performative. The play sets the two grammars side by side so that the faith's poetry and its bureaucracy can be told apart, and its catastrophe occurs in the gap between them.

Set against the other sacred voices of Verona, the Friar's office looks lonelier still. There is no bishop, no parish, no chapter, no second confessor; the city's church is one Franciscan with a basket of herbs. The play's ecclesiology is thus as compressed as its chronology: an entire religious establishment is concentrated into a single character, who must be theologian, confessor, celebrant, pharmacist, and postmaster, and who fails, in the end, at the post. Shakespeare's compression is usually discussed as a matter of days and hours, but it is just as much a matter of institutions. The lovers' faith is what a religion becomes when it is rebuilt for two people in five days by one overextended friar: intense, improvised, and structurally incapable of surviving a missed delivery. The imagery's verdict and the plot's mechanics meet here. The sacred register ennobles the love, the office fails it, and the play's judgement distributes itself accordingly, praise for the devotion, pity for its homelessness, and blame reserved for the city that made a church of two necessary and then made it impossible.

The system's second cleric underlines the point by contrast. Friar John, the colleague entrusted with the letter to Mantua, exists in the play for a single speech, and in it the institutional church appears in its other aspect: not the charismatic confessor improvising doctrine, but the order as a network, with brothers travelling in pairs, houses visited, and rules of quarantine that seal a city gate against a suspected plague. John's failure is not of the heart but of the system entire: he is stopped by searchers of the town, detained on suspicion of infection, and the letter, the faith's one piece of writing, never leaves Verona. Shakespeare gives the catastrophe an ecclesiastical mechanism as precise as any in the imagery: a church organised enough to quarantine its own messenger, and not organised enough to send another. Between Laurence's improvisation and John's detention, the lovers' religion exhibits both ways a ministry can fail, by doing too much and by being unable to move, and the sacred register's splendour in speech stands against the plain fact that its institution cannot deliver a letter twenty miles.

## Catholic Verona, Protestant London: The Setting's Double Hearing

The play's sacred machinery is Italian and Catholic: a friar of Saint Francis, confession and shrift, a shrine-centred devotion, saints addressed and pilgrims received. Its first audiences were Londoners of a reformed church, for whom friars were figures of a abolished order, confession to a priest was a rejected practice, shrines were dismantled monuments, and pilgrimage was a byword for superstition. The imagery therefore sounded in two keys at once, and any account of its effect that hears only one key is incomplete. To the ear formed by the sonnet tradition, the pilgrim conceit was the period's supreme compliment, and its Catholic colouring was part of the compliment's romance, the glamour of the older, richer devotional world that Petrarch's Italy preserved. To the ear formed by the pulpit, the same lines staged a young man performing forbidden devotions to a girl, coached by a friar of exactly the kind reformed polemic loved to expose, superstitious, meddling, and secretive. Shakespeare exploits the double hearing rather than resolving it. The play never cues its audience to laugh at the friar as a stage stereotype, the lecherous or drunken monk of the old interludes; Laurence is learned, well intentioned, and finally vindicated as a teller of truth. Neither does the play endorse the machinery without reserve: the sacrament is clandestine, the confession produces plots, and the shrift errands are lies told to parents. The double hearing is the point. A London audience could enjoy the lovers' faith as Italian romance while watching, with Protestant satisfaction, the confessional system fail to govern what it had blessed. Both pleasures are in the text, and the critics who insist on one, the romance readers for whom the imagery is pure elevation and the moral readers for whom it is pure warning, are each hearing half the chord.

The London ear had also been trained by the stage itself, where friars were stock figures with a long and mostly disreputable history in the interludes and moralities: meddlers, hypocrites, and go-betweens, useful for plots and ripe for exposure. Shakespeare's Laurence defeats the stock expectation point by point, learned where the type is ignorant, chaste where the type is lecherous, disinterested where the type is purchasable, and yet the plot still uses him exactly as the old plays used their friars, as the secret-keeper who makes an illicit union possible and then loses control of the secret. The audience's double hearing therefore has a theatrical as well as a doctrinal layer: Laurence is the good friar of romance trapped inside the plot function of the bad friar of farce. His final vindication, the Prince accepting his narrative and his holiness together, is the play overruling its own theatrical inheritance, much as its lovers overrule the courtly code by marrying. In both cases Shakespeare takes a form his audience thought it had classified, the stage friar and the love religion, and submits it to earnest use until the classification breaks.

The historical depth of the pilgrimage image sharpens the effect. By the 1590s, pilgrimage in England was not a living practice but a memory and a ruin: the great shrines had been destroyed in the 1530s, the roads to Canterbury and Walsingham no longer carried their crowds, and the palmer's branch survived as an emblem in heraldry and poetry rather than as a traveller's token. When Romeo calls his lips pilgrims and Juliet answers with the palmer's kiss, the words arrive with the patina of a vanished world, exquisite and slightly archaic, like the Catholic colouring of Verona itself. The archaism is functional. It removes the lovers' devotion from the jurisdiction of any church the audience actually attended, licensing the audience to admire a faith it would have condemned in its own parish, and it dates the devotion to the world's youth, as if such worship belonged to an older, more credulous, more splendid dispensation. Shakespeare's Verona is not a documentary Italy; it is England's Catholic past dressed in Italian names, and the sacred imagery is the past's language spoken by the young. The feud, by contrast, is dressed in no archaism at all: honour, insult, and the duelling code were live practices in the London streets, and the audience's recognitions there were contemporary. The play's two religions are thus set at different distances, the lovers' faith in the beautiful past, the city's idolatry in the immediate present, and the audience's sympathy is steered by the staging of time as much as by the content of the imagery. What is old is forgiven; what is current is judged.

The suppression had been thorough and its ruins were famous. At Walsingham the Virgin's house had been burned and its image carried to London for public burning; at Canterbury the shrine of Becket, once the richest in England, had been dismantled and the saint's name ordered erased from the service books; the great monastic houses whose guest halls had lodged pilgrims were dissolved and their roofs lead-stripped. A Londoner of the 1590s thus knew pilgrimage the way later ages know any abolished world, through fragments: a roofless chancel on a Norfolk road, a lead badge dredged from the Thames, a grandmother's rosary kept in a drawer. Shakespeare's sacred imagery trades on that knowledge. It is not antiquarian display; it is archaeology performed live, the audience watching a suppressed practice reconstituted for two hours by young actors in Italian dress, and finding, perhaps to its own surprise, that the reconstitution is beautiful. The lovers' faith could not have carried that charge in a culture where pilgrimage was routine, nor in one where it was forgotten. It needed a culture where the rite was lost, remembered, and officially condemned, which is to say it needed the 1590s, and the play's Verona is built to those specifications.

### Does any character in the play worship inside a church onstage?

No act of public worship is ever staged: no mass, no prayer in a church, no font, no altar. The church exists as a destination spoken of, the place Capulet will drag his daughter to, the churchyard where the tomb stands. Every sacred act the audience witnesses is displaced into private or improvised spaces.

A dance floor becomes a shrine, a cell becomes a chapel and a pharmacy, an orchard becomes a place of vows, a bedroom becomes a confessional of last speeches, a tomb becomes the marriage's completion. The displacement is the play's ecclesiology in action. Verona has churches; the drama has none, because the drama's subject is what happens to sacred practice when it leaves the institution and takes refuge in persons.

The Catholic setting performs one more service, and it is doctrinal. Within a Catholic frame, the lovers' story can be told in the vocabulary of sacrament and intercession without the play having to decide the Reformation's quarrels. The marriage the Friar performs is a sacrament in the old reckoning, a means of grace and not a contract only, and its sacramental character is what makes Juliet's later refusal of the Paris match a matter of conscience rather than preference: a second ceremony would not be a change of mind but a sacrilege, the sealing of a hand already sealed, as she tells the Friar in the play's most exact theological speech (4.1.55-59). A Protestant setting could not have given her refusal that ground; in Verona it is unanswerable, and the Friar, who should know, does not answer it but reaches for the potion. The setting, in short, is not local colour. It is the legal and doctrinal system inside which the lovers' faith can be a faith, with binding sacraments and a real confessor, and inside which its collision with the city's other cult can be measured. Strip the Catholic machinery away and the imagery collapses into compliment; with it, the compliment has consequences, and the consequences are the play.

## Shrift, Vows and Sacraments: The Private Faith at Work

A religion is known by its practices, and the lovers' faith has three: confession, vowing, and marriage. Each practice works in the play with a doubleness that the imagery keeps in view. Confession, the sacrament of penance, is the faith's most used and most abused instrument. Juliet's movements in the second half of the play are governed by shrift: she is sent, or sends herself, to the Friar's cell to confess, and the errand is at once genuine and a cover. When she goes to Laurence in Act 4 she goes in earnest, with a knife in her hand and a conscience in torment, resolving to confess a marriage and refuse a bigamy; the sacrament's machinery, secrecy, counsel, absolution, is the only machinery in Verona that can even address her case. When she returns home with a merry look, having begged her father's pardon as the Friar enjoined her (4.2), the confession has been spent as an alibi, its fruits counterfeited for the plot's sake. The Nurse's report that her lady comes from shrift with a merry look is the city's reading of the sacrament, a mood improved by a rite performed elsewhere. The play's judgement is in the doubleness: confession is the one practice through which the truth of the marriage could lawfully be spoken, and it is used, scene after scene, to keep the truth unspoken. A sacrament designed for disclosure becomes the plot's engine of concealment, and the inversion is not the lovers' hypocrisy but their circumstance. In a city where the truth would be answered with a hurdle and a forced bridegroom, the confessional's seal is the only privacy there is. The practice holds its dignity and loses its function, which is the story of the whole system in miniature.

Vowing, the faith's second practice, receives the play's closest ethical attention. Romeo arrives in the orchard ready to swear, and offers the moon as his sacred warrant: "Lady, by yonder blessed moon I vow, / That tips with silver all these fruit-tree tops" (2.2.107-108). The epithet is the sacred register at its most casual, holiness as adjective, and Juliet's refusal of it is the faith's first act of self-discipline: "O, swear not by the moon, th'inconstant moon, / That monthly changes in her circled orb, / Lest that thy love prove likewise variable" (2.2.109-111). She will not have the vow underwritten by a changeable heaven. Her other refusal in the same scene, of the name as a warrant for identity, is the subject of ['What's in a name?': the balcony's big question](/2023/08/04/whats-in-a-name-romeo-and-juliet/), and the two refusals are one discipline: nothing changeable, neither moon nor surname, may underwrite the bond. Her counter-proposal abolishes oath-taking in favour of the sacrament itself: let the purpose be marriage, and let word be sent by a messenger, an arrangement of acts rather than invocations. The exchange is the play's whole theology of language in thirty lines. Romeo's religion begins as invocation, sacred names called in to guarantee feeling; Juliet's maturity is to distrust invocation and demand institution, the vow embodied in a rite with witnesses, a minister, and a date. The lovers' later oaths keep her discipline. There is very little swearing by sacred things after the orchard, and what swearing remains is desperate rather than decorative, Romeo's defiance of the stars in Mantua (5.1.24) standing as the anti-vow, the moment the faith's language is turned against the heaven that has failed it. Even that line is instructive: a man who had no religion could not defy the stars with that bitterness. The defiance is a believer's, addressed upward, in the grammar of the faith the play built.

Marriage, the third practice, is where the private faith and the public church meet, and the meeting defines both. The lovers could have rested in their religion of compliment, exchanging sonnets at feasts and vows in orchards; the tradition of courtly devotion asked no more, and indeed forbade more. Instead they insist on the sacrament, on being made, in the Friar's phrase as he leads them out, two in one by holy church (2.6.36-37). The insistence is the play's boldest stroke in the imagery's long argument, because it reverses the usual direction of borrowing. The courtly religion borrowed the Church's words to dignify a love that stayed outside the Church's law; Shakespeare's lovers borrow the words and then present themselves at the Church's door, demanding the law. The sacred register, on this reading, is not the love's escape from institution but its application for admission, and the tragedy is that the institution can only admit them in secret, at dawn, in a cell, without the banns and witnesses that make a marriage a social fact. The rite is valid and the rite is useless, sacramentally complete and civically void, and everything that follows, the shrift errands, the potion, the letter, is the sound of that contradiction working itself out. The imagery of the feast promised a faith; the cell delivered a sacrament; the city refused both a home.

The potion is the system's counterfeit sacrament, and the play stages it with a liturgist's care. Juliet receives it from the Friar's hands in the cell, with instructions, conditions, and a promised effect, as a communicant receives the elements; she carries it to her chamber as to a chapel; she dismisses her attendants, consecrates the moment with a speech that rehearses her fears like a litany, doubts the minister who prepared it, wonders whether the mixture is poison, and drinks at last with a dedication, to Romeo. Every element of a rite is present, matter, minister, words, intention, and faith in the effect, and every element is inverted: the sacrament simulates death instead of conferring life, is received in secret from a church that would condemn it, and works, exactly as promised, to no one's salvation. The counterfeit matters to the imagery's argument because it marks the limit of the private faith's improvisation. A church of two can borrow the forms of devotion and even of marriage; when it begins to mint its own sacraments, it enters the territory where faith becomes machinery, and the play's judgement on the potion is the judgement on all the system's later history: it works, and its working is the catastrophe. The distinction between the wedding and the vial is the distinction the idolatry section drew in theology. The wedding asked the Church's rite to bless the love; the vial asks the love to bless its own rite. The first is veneration seeking order; the second is the system worshipping its own ingenuity, and the play punishes the second while vindicating the first.

## The Feud as Counter-Church: Verona's Other Religion

No verdict on the lovers' faith is complete until the rival faith has been described, because the play's Verona is a city of two religions, and only one of them is Catholic. The feud has the full apparatus of a cult. It has its sacred history, an origin no living character can remember, an ancient quarrel whose first cause is never stated, as befits a mystery. It has its commandments, the honour code's unwritten law that a Montague must answer a Capulet's insult and a Capulet a Montague's, on pain of a shame worse than death. It has its rituals, the bitten thumb, the drawn sword in the public square, the formal challenge and the regulated duel, performed before witnesses according to inherited forms. It has its priests and acolytes, the servants who open the play rehearsing its liturgy in bawdy catechism, and its young zealots, Tybalt above all, for whom the cult is a vocation. It has its sacrifices, offered on a schedule the play observes with care: Mercutio, Tybalt, Paris, and at last the lovers themselves, each death a fresh oblation that obliges the next. And it has its eschatology, the golden statues of the close, in which the cult, sated, commemorates its victims as saints. The Prince's three interventions are the state's attempts to suppress a rival church within the city, and their failure is the measure of the cult's hold: edicts are issued, fines threatened, and the square fills again within the act.

Read against this counter-church, the lovers' religion changes its significance. It is not an escape from Verona's religious life but a schism within it, a reformation in miniature, in which two young members of the cult's own congregations defect to a faith of their own founding, taking the city's Catholic vocabulary with them because it is the only sacred language available. The defection explains the cult's fury, which in the play is never proportioned to the offence of a secret marriage: Capulet's rage in the chamber scene is the rage of a priesthood betrayed, a father whose daughter has apostatised from the family's god, and his threats, to drag her to church, to let her hang, beg, starve, and die in the streets, are anathemas pronounced in the cult's name under cover of the Church's building. Paris, the cult's approved bridegroom, is its model communicant, regular in his devotions, punctual at the tomb with his flowers, and killed at last in the shrine he came to tend. The lovers, meanwhile, are the counter-church's martyrs, and the play observes the martyrology's forms: they die for a confession, the confession is published after their deaths by a witness, an inquiry is held, the persecutors repent, and images are raised. Shakespeare does not need to underline the pattern, because his audience knew how the stories of persecuted faiths end, in Rome as in Canterbury: with the cult of the victims supplanting the cult that killed them.

This is the deepest answer to the idolatry charge, and the essay tradition that worries over the lovers' worship rarely reaches it. The charge assumes that the alternative to the lovers' faith is the true faith, order against idolatry. The play shows the alternative on stage: the alternative is the feud, a working religion with sacrifices on its altars, and by comparison the lovers' church of two, with its corrected rites, its sacrament-seeking devotion, and its refusal to shed any blood but its own, is the play's standard of what worship should be. The imagery ennobles the love not by flattering it but by outbidding the rival cult at every point: better sacrifices, freely given; a better sacrament, honestly desired; a better clergy, however fallible; and a better end, since the lovers' faith produces statues and repentance while the feud's produces only graves. The sacred register is the scoring system of that contest, and its verdict is legible in the final scene, where the Prince, suppressing the cult at last, can find no language for justice but the lovers' own, a scourge of heaven and joys killed with love.

## The Tomb as Shrine: The Sacred Register at the End

The system's final movement returns it to its first image, a shrine approached by a pilgrim, with the terms inverted and darkened. Romeo comes to the Capulet tomb as he came to the feast, in the night, uninvited, bearing a light, to a place where Juliet lies; but the shrine is now a grave, the pilgrim carries a crowbar and poison, and the prayer's effect he takes is death. The play does not underline the parallel with any verbal echo, and none is needed: the staging rhymes. Paris, arriving before him with flowers for the bridal bed he means to strew nightly, performs the tomb's other rite, the votive offering at a shrine of the beloved dead, and dies defending the shrine against a violator, a pilgrim turned, in his own account, avenger of a desecration. Two devotees meet at the shrine in the dark, each convinced the other profanes it, and the shrine receives both. The sacred register's physics, touch transmitting blessing and offence, completes itself as violence. Inside, Romeo drinks to Juliet and dies with a kiss on his lips, the liturgy of the feast recited a final time in the place where all pilgrimages in the old world ended, at the tomb of the beloved. Juliet wakes to find the rite completed without her, and finishes it with the dagger, cheerfully, as a devotee might speak of a familiar office: the register's last note in her mouth is not horror but recognition. The faith's martyrology, promised by its vocabulary from the first sonnet, is fulfilled in the place the vocabulary always pointed to, since every shrine houses a dead saint, and to worship at one was always to commune with the dead. The imagery had been honest from the beginning about where devotion ends.

Juliet's waking completes the rite's logic with terrible fidelity. She wakes in the shrine she has become, finds the pilgrim dead beside her with the cup in his hand, refuses the minister's offer of a nunnery, the institution arriving at last with its orthodox refuge an hour too late, and performs the faith's last office herself, with the dagger she calls happy for its sheath in her breast. The priesthood of the system devolves, at the end, onto its saint, who administers her own last rite without benefit of clergy. Beside the bodies lies the faith's only scripture, the Friar's letter to Mantua, which had been written to explain the potion and now serves a colder purpose, corroborating a confession in the Prince's inquiry. A religion's book should be read by its believers; this one is read by its investigators. The letter's career, composed as pastoral care, delayed by plague, delivered at last as forensic evidence, is the sacred register's whole history in a single object: composed in faith, defeated by circumstance, and honoured in the audit.

What survives the devotees is the register, passing into the hands of the city that opposed it, and the passing is the play's final judgement on the imagery. The Friar's account is received as testimony, his providence, "a greater power than we can contradict" (5.3.242), accepted as the frame for the catastrophe. The Prince's sentence is delivered in the register's own terms, a scourge laid by heaven upon hate, joys killed with love (5.3.291-293), as if the state's justice could find no vocabulary but the lovers'. And the fathers, who had threatened church on a hurdle and counted their daughter an asset, end the play as founders of a cult: Montague will raise Juliet's statue in pure gold, that while Verona is known there shall no figure be set at such rate (5.3.298-301), and Capulet answers in kind for Romeo, calling the pair poor sacrifices of the fathers' enmity (5.3.302-303). The word sacrifice, arriving at the last, names what the feud had been doing all along, and the statues name what the lovers' faith had been doing: making images of the beloved and setting them up for a city's devotion. Verona will now do publicly, in gold, what it punished the lovers for doing in words. If the imagery was idolatry, the city has adopted the idolatry and gilded it; if it was worship, the city has at last joined the congregation, too late, at the tomb, which is where the play leaves its audience as well. The light system and the sacred system, companions throughout, are read together in the study of [light and dark imagery in Romeo and Juliet](/2024/01/26/light-and-dark-imagery-analysis/): the statues are the light imagery's last form, the lovers fixed at last as the bright objects the whole city agrees to look at. The verdict the imagery finally delivers is therefore double and settled. The love was ennobled by its faith, and the faith was vindicated by its enemies, who could find no higher honour for the dead than to build them the shrine they had been accused of imagining.

The churchyard scene also conducts, in form, the closest proceeding the period knew to a canonisation inquiry. A saint's cult was ratified by investigation: witnesses examined, a life narrated, miracles and sufferings attested, documents produced and compared. The Prince's audit follows the order with unwitting fidelity. The Friar, the faith's surviving minister, deposes to the whole history under threat of the law. The letter, the system's documentary relic, is produced and found to make good the witness's words, as a charter confirms a testimony. Balthasar and the watch give the external evidence; the bodies themselves, the lovers found in the posture of their rite, are the inquiry's mute exhibits. No verdict of sanctity is pronounced, because the proceeding is a secular court doing sacred work without knowing it, but the effects of canonisation follow in due order: a public narrative fixed, a feast of commemoration promised in the persons of the statues, and a city instructed to remember. The lovers' church of two ends, as persecuted churches in the period's memory so often ended, with its story told by its enemies' court and its images commissioned by its persecutors. The sacred register, which began as a compliment at a dance, completes itself as a cult ratified by the state.

### Why do the golden statues count as the sacred register's last image?

The statues complete the register because they repeat its founding act with the city, rather than the lover, as the maker. At the feast Romeo figured Juliet as a shrine; at the tomb the fathers, who never heard the conceit, commission images of both lovers in pure gold, at the highest rate of honour Verona can set.

The devotional image that Protestant polemic called an idol and Catholic practice called a votive offering is produced, in the end, not by the lovers' private faith but by the public grief of the feud's survivors. The register's vocabulary of shrine, saint, and sacrifice thus passes from the lovers' mouths into the city's stone and gold, and the imagery's long argument closes where it began, with figures of the beloved set up to be looked at, now with the whole of Verona for a congregation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch sacred-imagery catalogue, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch sacred-imagery catalogue is the table in this article that lists the play's principal religious and pilgrimage images in performance order, with speaker, sacred source, and dramatic work for each. The pattern it exposes is distribution: the imagery runs through all five acts and at least eight speakers, and it migrates as the play proceeds, from a place (the shrine Juliet is said to be), through acts and offices (the holy act of marriage, the ghostly confessor, the shrift errand), to objects (the undelivered letter, the statues in pure gold). A metaphor used once would show none of this. The catalogue makes the register's career visible at a glance, and the career is the argument: the sacred language is a system with a life of its own, outliving the lovers who founded it and passing, at the end, into the mouths and the goldwork of their enemies.

### Q: Why does the play keep using religious language long after the first meeting is over?

The play keeps using religious language because the lovers' situation keeps requiring it: every later crisis is a crisis of conscience, vow, sacrament, or confession, and the sacred register is the only vocabulary Verona owns for those things. The meeting founds the system in a sonnet, but the marriage needs a celebrant and a rite, the banishment needs a confessor's counsel, Juliet's refusal of Paris needs the doctrine that God joined her heart and the Friar her hands, and the plot's errands run under cover of shrift. The register also spreads beyond the lovers because the city speaks it natively: the Friar blesses and warns in it, the Prince judges in it, the fathers mourn and bargain in it. What begins as a courtship conceit becomes the play's general language for everything that binds, forbids, forgives, or condemns.

### Q: Does the religious imagery ennoble the love or accuse it of idolatry?

The imagery ennobles the love, and the play builds the acquittal on idolatry into the lovers' own speeches. Idolatry, in the period's theology, is worship given to a creature in God's place; the lovers never ask that of each other. Juliet is entreated as a saint is entreated, for favour and intercession, and she assigns the marriage's real authorship to God: "God joined my heart and Romeo's, thou our hands" (4.1.55). She also polices the register, correcting the pilgrim's rite at the feast and refusing the inconstant moon as an oath's warrant in the orchard, which no passive idol does. The play reserves its true idolatry for the feud, an honour cult that demands sacrifices and receives them in the street. The sacred language measures the love upward; the city's cult is what the measurement condemns.

### Q: Why is the lovers' minister a friar rather than a parish priest?

The lovers' minister is a friar because a Franciscan's office fits the plot's needs as a parish priest's could not: the friar is mobile, learned, semi-detached from household and parish structures, and available for private counsel in a cell that anyone may visit without remark. A parish priest belongs to a cure of souls, keeps registers, reads banns, and answers to a bishop; a wedding he performed would become public knowledge by design. Laurence, by contrast, can be approached at dawn, can marry the couple in his cell, can keep the secret, and can move between the two households as a trusted spiritual adviser to both. His order matters as well: the Franciscans were the period's emblem of pastoral intimacy, confessors and preachers to the laity. Shakespeare concentrates the whole church into this one portable figure, which is why his failure, when it comes, is total: there is no second minister to appeal to.

### Q: How would the sacred imagery have sounded to a Protestant London audience watching Catholic Verona?

It would have sounded in two keys at once, romantic and suspect, and the play exploits both. To an ear formed on the sonnet tradition, the pilgrim at the shrine was the supreme compliment, its Catholic colouring part of the glamour of the older devotional world preserved in Petrarch's Italy. To an ear formed by the pulpit, the same scene staged forbidden devotions, a young man performing toward a girl the acts that reformed teaching had condemned toward saints, coached by a friar of the kind polemic delighted to expose. Pilgrimage, confession to a priest, and the invocation of saints were all abolished or condemned practices in the audience's own church, set at a safe Italian distance. The audience could therefore admire the lovers' faith as romance while judging its machinery as superstition, and Shakespeare never forces a choice between the two hearings, because the tragedy needs both.

### Q: How does the sacred cluster work alongside the light cluster that Caroline Spurgeon made famous?

The two clusters work as one double system, each glossing the other: the sacred terms supply the doctrine and the light terms supply the radiance, and the lovers are described in both vocabularies at once. Spurgeon showed that light in darkness is the play's dominant imaginative atmosphere, the saint of the feast being also the sun of the orchard and the torch-bright presence at the ball. The sacred cluster explains what the light is for. Radiance in this play is not mere beauty; it is holiness made visible, the quality by which a shrine is recognised and toward which a pilgrim travels. Juliet's speech moves between the systems without a seam, refusing the moon as an oath in one breath and figuring her bounty as boundless in the next. Read together, the clusters describe a single perception, that the beloved appears as a consecrated brightness, and each system keeps the other honest: light stops the sacred from going abstract, and the sacred stops the light from going merely pretty.

### Q: What did C. S. Lewis mean by the religion of love in courtly tradition?

Lewis meant that courtly love, as the troubadours and their heirs practised it, was organised as a deliberate counter-religion, with the lady as its deity, the lover as her humble worshipper, service as its discipline, and favour as its grace. In The Allegory of Love (1936) he treated the system as a formal code, a game of manners raised to a metaphysic, in which sacred vocabulary signified devotion by convention rather than by belief: the lover who called his lady a saint was paying a legible compliment in the only superlative register the culture possessed, not confessing a creed. The account explains the polish and the wit of the feast's shared sonnet, whose lovers plainly know the rules and grade each other's performance. Its limit, for this play, is that Shakespeare's lovers break the code from inside, dragging the trope to a real friar and demanding a real sacrament, at which point the game becomes a faith with consequences the game never had.

### Q: Why does Rosaline's vow of chastity make her a parody saint before Juliet becomes a real one?

Rosaline is a parody saint because Romeo's devotion to her reproduces the forms of saint-worship with the substance removed: she is absent, silent, vowed, and unreachable, and his cult of her produces nothing but his own eloquent suffering. He reports that she has Dian's wit and is armed in proof of chastity (1.1.205-206), a votaress of a pagan goddess of virginity, and that she has forsworn to love, leaving him living dead inside the vow. The devotion is a monologue; the saint never answers, corrects, or grants. Juliet, by contrast, answers within four lines of being addressed as a shrine, corrects the rite, sets the terms, and converts the worship into a marriage. The contrast is the play's control experiment: the same sacred vocabulary, in one case a Petrarchan exercise performed before an empty shrine, in the other a living relation in which the saint speaks. Romeo's religion is false while it is one-sided and becomes true when it is answered.

### Q: Why does Juliet summon Phoebus and Phaethon in Act 3 Scene 2 while Romeo summons saints and shrines?

The difference marks the two lovers' distinct imaginative educations: Romeo's desire speaks the Church's language of shrine, saint, and sacrament, while Juliet's desire reaches, at its most urgent, for classical machinery, Phoebus's steeds, Phaethon's fall, and night as a sober-suited matron. Her epithalamium is a pagan invocation, calling on the sun god's horses to gallop the day away so that her wedding night can begin. The contrast is not carelessness. Romeo's liturgy is public and inherited, a rite learned from books and sonnets; Juliet's mythology is private and impatient, concerned with time rather than worship. When her need becomes desperate in earnest, in the cell scene of Act 4, her language turns theological with a precision his never reaches, dividing the marriage between God's joining and the Friar's sealing. The play thus gives its heroine two registers, classical for desire and doctrinal for conscience, and reserves its exactest sacred speech for her conscience.

### Q: What work does the word "grace" do in the Friar's herb speech and in Romeo's answer to him?

The word "grace" does the work of a hinge, joining the Friar's natural philosophy to the marriage plot in a single term that means both a power infused by God and a favour freely given. In the soliloquy the Friar predicates grace of creation itself: great is the powerful grace that lies in plants, herbs, and stones, and "in man as well as herbs, grace and rude will" contend for mastery (2.3.15, 27). Grace, there, is the good principle resident in things, set against the will that corrupts them. When Romeo enters and begs the Friar not to chide him, he answers in the same word: the lady he now loves "doth grace for grace and love for love allow" (2.3.81-82), figuring Juliet as a source of reciprocal favour. The echo binds the scenes. The doctrine of grace stated over the herbs becomes, within the hour, the doctrine of the marriage: a favour that must be reciprocated to remain grace at all.

### Q: Why does confession in this play keep producing secrets and plots instead of absolution?

Confession produces secrets and plots because the seal of the confessional is the only reliable privacy in Verona, and the lovers' marriage exists inside that privacy from its first hour. The sacrament of penance was designed for disclosure to God through a priest, ending in absolution; in the play its machinery is diverted to concealment from everyone else. Juliet's shrift errands move her between house and cell under a cover no parent questions; her real confession, the marriage she lays before the Friar in Act 4, cannot be absolved because it cannot be undone, only managed; and the Friar's own great speech in the tomb is a confession offered to a prince rather than a priest, seeking the law's rigour instead of pardon. The inversion is circumstantial, not cynical: in a city where the truth would be answered with a forced marriage, disclosure would be destruction. The sacrament keeps its dignity and loses its end, which is the history of the lovers' whole faith in one practice.

### Q: Why is marriage the one sacrament the lovers' private faith insists on receiving from the real church?

Marriage is insisted on because it is the point where the lovers' religion refuses to remain a religion of words, and because Juliet, its theologian, makes the sacrament the condition of everything else. The courtly tradition was content with compliment, service, and longing at a distance; these lovers demand the rite, with a minister, a formula, and an effect in law and conscience. Juliet sets the terms in the orchard: if Romeo's bent of love is honourable, his purpose marriage, he is to send word the next day (2.2.143-146). The insistence gives the private faith its dignity and its doom. A sacrament received makes the bond indissoluble, so that the Paris match becomes bigamy in conscience and resistance becomes a duty; but a sacrament received in secret makes the bond civically void, unknown to the households whose peace it was meant to purchase. The faith's central act is thus at once its warrant and its trap.

### Q: How does the play's sacred language treat the body: as an obstacle to devotion or as its instrument?

The sacred language treats the body as devotion's instrument, reversing the ascetic tradition in which flesh obstructs the soul's approach to God. The system's founding acts are touches: a hand profaning and then blessing, palm to palm as the palmers' kiss, lips doing what hands do in prayer. Holiness, in the lovers' rite, is transmitted by contact, exactly as at a shrine, where the faithful pressed hands and objects against the relic so that virtue would pass through matter. Juliet's correction of the pilgrim turns on this: the hand does not offend the shrine, because the shrine exists to be touched. Later the body keeps its sacramental role, hands joined by the Friar, a ring carried by the Nurse, a potion drunk, a dagger taken up as the last office. The play's theology of the body is incarnational throughout: the sacred is never reached by escaping the flesh, only through it, and the tragedy is staged as a series of touches that arrive, or fail to arrive, in time.

### Q: What does Juliet mean by locating her faith in heaven and her husband on earth in Act 3 Scene 5?

Juliet means that her marriage has split her existence between two jurisdictions, and her speech to the Nurse weighs the split as a theological problem. "My husband is on earth, my faith in heaven; / How shall that faith return again to earth, / Unless that husband send it me from heaven / By leaving earth?" (3.5.205-208). Faith here is her pledged troth, the vow that binds her, and she figures it as a substance that ascended with the marriage's heavenly ratification while its earthly partner lives banished in Mantua. The only reunion she can imagine, with Romeo dead to Verona and her father commanding a new bridegroom, runs through death: her husband would have to leave earth for her faith to descend from heaven. The conceit of the feast, faith turning to despair unless a prayer is granted, has hardened into a metaphysic. The speech shows the sacred register doing its most serious work, stating a conscience's dilemma in the vocabulary of two worlds.

### Q: How widely is the word "holy" shared among the play's speakers, and what does the spread show?

The word "holy" is spread across the play's whole society, attaching to a shrine, a kiss, a church, an act, a friar, and a saint, and the spread shows that the sacred register is Verona's common property rather than the lovers' invention. Romeo speaks it first, of the shrine and the palmers' kiss; Juliet answers inside the same word-field; the Friar blesses "this holy act" of marriage and is himself called holy by the Prince and by Juliet, who greets him as her confessor and later swears she was enjoined by holy Laurence; Saint Francis takes the word as an oath in his own friar's mouth. The lovers' originality is therefore not lexical but systematic: they take a word the city spends casually, as adjective and exclamation, and build it into a discipline. The spread also explains the ending, in which the Prince and the fathers can judge and mourn in sacred terms without learning a new language. The register was always theirs; the lovers merely used it in earnest.

### Q: How can an essay argue that the lovers build a church of two?

An essay can argue it by inventory rather than assertion, showing that the lovers' devotion acquires, in sequence, every element of an organised faith. The feast supplies the shrine, the saint, the pilgrim, and the rite of touch; the orchard supplies the discipline, when Juliet refuses a changeable oath and requires a durable covenant; the cell supplies the clergy, the doctrine of grace, and the sacrament, when holy church incorporates two in one; the later acts supply the faith's casuistry, its confessions, its doctrine of the soul's two homes, and its martyrology in the tomb. The essay should then concede the counter-case, that a church of two has no congregation, no succession, and no defence, and argue that the concession is the thesis: the play builds the church in order to measure what a faith loses when it is reduced to its minimum membership, and Verona's rival cult of honour, with its congregations and sacrifices, wins the city while losing the argument.

### Q: Why do the parents end the play building images of the lovers whose devotion they had tried to forbid?

The parents build the images because grief converts them to the devotion they suppressed, and image-making is the form their conversion takes. Montague vows to raise Juliet's statue in pure gold, at the highest rate Verona can set, and Capulet matches the offering for Romeo, calling the dead pair poor sacrifices of the fathers' enmity (5.3.298-303). The irony is exact: a culture that might have charged the lovers' mutual worship as idolatry ends by commissioning golden figures of them, the votive statues of a civic cult, to stand where citizens will look at them daily. The fathers do not retract their judgement in words, but their gold retracts it. What the lovers did in metaphor, figuring each other as shrine and saint, the city now does in earnest and at public expense, too late for the devotion to be answered. The statues are the sacred register's last and heaviest image: worship institutionalised at the moment it becomes useless.

### Q: Why does the sacred imagery gather at the play's moments of meeting and parting?

The imagery gathers at meetings and partings because those are the moments when the lovers' bond is made, renewed, or severed, and the sacred register is the bond's language. At the first meeting it founds the faith in a sonnet; at the orchard parting it is disciplined into a marriage covenant; at the wedding it receives the Church's seal; at the dawn parting it states the two-worlds problem of a husband on earth and a faith in heaven; at the final meeting in the tomb it completes itself as martyrology. Sacred language in this play is threshold language, spoken where states change: strangers become worshipper and saint, lovers become spouses, the living become the dead. Between thresholds the register rests, because between thresholds the bond is not in question. The pattern is itself an argument about the imagery's function: it is not the play's ornament but its instrument for marking, and solemnising, every passage of the lovers' estate.

### Q: How does the imagery read differently in a decade when pilgrimage itself had been abolished in England?

It reads as an archaism, beautiful and slightly unreal, and the archaism does political work. England's shrines had been dismantled in the 1530s, their relics scattered and their pilgrim roads emptied, so that by the 1590s a palmer's palm survived as a poetic and heraldic emblem rather than a traveller's token. Romeo's pilgrim compliment therefore arrived with the patina of a vanished dispensation: an audience could admire devotions it would have condemned in its own parish precisely because those devotions belonged to a closed chapter of the national past, dressed here in Italian names. The distance licenses sympathy. Set against the feud, whose honour code and street duels were contemporary London facts, the lovers' faith is placed in the beautiful past and the city's idolatry in the immediate present. The imagery's politics are chronological: what is old is forgiven, what is current is judged, and the pilgrim's obsolete palm becomes the play's safest radical gesture.

### Q: How does the balance of sacred language shift between the lovers across the five acts?

The balance shifts steadily from Romeo to Juliet, tracking the transfer of authority inside the marriage. Romeo founds the register at the feast, speaking its shrine, its pilgrims, and its doctrine of sin, while Juliet answers, corrects, and completes. In the orchard her corrections become legislation: she refuses the moon oath and imposes marriage as the devotion's form. From Act 3 onward the register's most serious utterances are hers, the faith-in-heaven speech of the chamber, the division of the marriage between God's act and the Friar's in the cell, the solitary resolutions of the potion night. Romeo's sacred speech, by contrast, thins into the defiance of the stars and the tomb's last kiss, while he acts on reports and arrives too late. The shift is the marriage's real history: the pilgrim built the shrine, and the saint became its theologian, its confessor's best penitent, and finally its martyr.
