Sampson and Gregory, two servingmen of the house of Capulet, speak the first words of Romeo and Juliet after the Chorus has withdrawn, and the choice is the play's first argument. Before Romeo sighs, before Juliet appears, before either father draws a sword, the tragedy opens in prose, at street level, with a pair of liveried men daring each other to start a fight neither can explain. The verdict of this close reading is plain: the opening exchange is not a comic warm-up bolted onto a love tragedy. It is a working model of the feud itself, built at the smallest scale the play affords, and its bawdy wordplay is the mechanism of the model. In the space of some seventy lines, a pun about coal becomes a pun about anger, a pun about precedence becomes a threat against women, and a bitten thumb becomes drawn swords. Language in this scene does not describe violence after the fact. Language manufactures violence, step by deniable step, and the men operating the machine are the lowest-ranking speakers in Verona.

That design matters because the familiar account of the play begins with the lovers and treats everything before the Capulet feast as preamble. The text disagrees. The Prologue has already named the subject as an ancient grudge breaking to new mutiny, and the first scene embodies the grudge in bodies that have no stake in it beyond a livery and a wage. Sampson and Gregory do not know why the houses hate. Neither man ever says what the quarrel is about, and the one line that touches the question, Gregory's observation that "the quarrel is between our masters and us their men" (1.1.20), assigns the ownership of the hatred upward and the labour of it downward. What the servants own is the performance: the swagger, the puns, the fencing vocabulary of honour. A close reading of their exchange, line by line, shows how completely the feud has soaked through the city's social fabric when even its instruments must improvise reasons to fight in real time.

The exchange also establishes the play's deepest linkage before the love plot exists to express it. The servants' talk slides without a seam from fighting men to possessing women, from cutting off heads to cutting off maidenheads, so that sex and violence share a single vocabulary from the play's first minute. Every later fusion of desire and death in the tragedy, the lovers' wedding night shadowed by Tybalt's corpse, Juliet's bridal bed becoming her grave, is present in embryo in a joke two servants make while waiting for someone to offend them. Line references in what follows are to Rene Weis's Arden Third Series edition of the play; other editions differ by a line here and there, and the glosses discussed are those the standard editions supply.

Consider the drop in register the audience has just experienced. The Chorus has spoken a sonnet, the most finished and aristocratic verbal object the period possessed, fourteen lines of rhymed verse promising two households alike in dignity. The Chorus withdraws, and the play's first embodied voices belong to men with no dignity to speak of, talking prose about coal. The fall from sonnet to slang is not a lapse in tone. It is the tragedy's method announced in its first minute: every elevated thing in this play will be shadowed by a low equivalent that tells the truth about it. The Prologue says ancient grudge; the servants show what an ancient grudge looks like at ankle height, which is two employees hoping for a fight before breakfast. An audience that registers the drop has understood the play's double vision, the high style and the low fact held in a single frame, and that vision will be required at every crisis from the balcony to the tomb.

The servants also solve, with unusual economy, the exposition problem that the Prologue's summary creates. A chorus can state that a feud exists; only dramatized behaviour can establish what kind of thing the feud is, how it feels from inside, what it rewards, and what it costs. Abstractly announced, the grudge might be imagined as a solemn inheritance, a matter of old wounds gravely remembered. The servants correct the imagination in seventy lines. In their hands the feud is neither solemn nor remembered. It is recreational, improvisational, and entirely present-tense, a game with lethal equipment. That correction governs everything the audience subsequently sees, because the great houses will present their hatred in the costumes of dignity, wounded honour, paternal authority, injured love, and the audience, having met the feud first in its servant form, will recognize the substance under every costume. The opening is therefore not merely where the play starts. It is the interpretive key the play hands its audience before locking the door.

## Why Romeo and Juliet begins with Sampson and Gregory

Opening low was a known resource of the early modern stage, and the playwright uses it with unusual precision here. A tragedy that will end with princes and gold statues begins with hired men in a public place, speaking the prose that the period's stage conventions reserved for the low-born, the comic, and the unbuttoned. The effect is not decoration. Beginning at the bottom of the household tells the audience, before any exposition can, that the feud is not an aristocratic ritual conducted by the principals alone. It is weather. It hangs over the street, it employs the street, and it will recruit anyone standing in the street, down to men whose names the great houses would not trouble to remember.

### Why does the play begin with servants rather than with the lovers?

The play begins with servants because the feud, not the love story, is its opening subject, and servants prove the feud's reach. Hatred performed eagerly by men who merely wear the colours shows a contagion that has passed from the masters into the whole body of the city.

The delay of the lovers is itself a structural statement. Romeo does not enter until the brawl has burned itself out and the Prince has legislated over its ashes, and when Romeo does enter he speaks of the fight in exhausted oxymorons, as an event already stale to him. The audience has seen what Romeo only reports. That ordering makes the servants' scene the evidentiary foundation of the play: every later claim about the feud's cost can be checked against the memory of two men spending a morning trying to provoke a bloodletting for sport. Susan Snyder, in The Comic Matrix of Shakespeare's Tragedies, reads the play's opening movements as belonging to the machinery of comedy, a world of servants, wordplay, and street scuffles that a comic plot would resolve in marriages and reconciliations. The reading is valuable because it names the register correctly. The scene is built like comedy. What the close reading adds is that its materials are already lethal, and that the lethality is not imported later but latent in the jokes.

There is also a theatrical practicality that serves the design. A full-stage brawl within minutes of the Chorus warms a yard of groundlings, advertises the company's fencing, and fills the platform with the colours of both houses before a single principal has been identified. Yet the playwright spends the calm before the brawl on something quieter and stranger than noise: a duet of puns between two men alone on stage. The audience is made to listen closely, to work at double meanings, before it is given anything to watch. The play trains its hearers in equivocation at the door, because equivocation, saying one thing and meaning two, is the skill the whole tragedy will punish. Sampson will bite his thumb and deny the aim of the gesture in the same breath; the habit of the deniable meaning starts here, in comedy, where it costs nothing yet.

The low opening had respectable theatrical ancestry. The period's stages habitually admitted clowns, porters, gravediggers in embryo, and servingmen whose prose set off the verse of their betters, and audiences expected the first minutes of a play to mix registers while the yard settled. What distinguishes this opening is the discipline of its descent. The servants are not a random sample of low life; they are the enemy's mirror image in waiting, Capulet men whose entire scene is a rehearsal for meeting Montague men, so that the low material is aimed from its first line at the play's central conflict. A gravedigger digresses. Sampson and Gregory converge. Every joke they trade narrows the distance between talk and the drawn sword, and the convergence is complete before a single principal has entered. The convention of the comic opening is honoured in form and weaponized in content, which is why the scene survives translation to stages and centuries where the convention itself is forgotten: its pleasures are structural, not topical.

There is a further practical intelligence in the choice. A new play by a young company dramatist, performed in an open-air theatre to a standing audience, needed its world built fast and its stakes made bodily. Verse exposition about an Italian feud risks remaining geography. Two servants in livery, filling the stage with the threat of an immediate fight, build Verona in the only way that finally matters: they show a city where violence is ambient, where the street itself is a contested instrument, and where the audience's own position, standing in a crowd watching men posture, uncomfortably resembles the position of Verona's citizens, who will shortly pour onto the stage with clubs. The yard becomes the piazza. By the time the Prince legislates, the audience has been standing inside the problem for a quarter of an hour, and the legislation sounds, correctly, inadequate.

The scene is also, in the plainest sense, an invention. The story reached the playwright through narrative versions, Arthur Brooke's long poem and William Painter's prose tale, and narratives begin by telling: a narrator recounts the feud, the families, the lovers, in summary. No servant duet opens those tellings, because a narrator has no need of one. A stage has every need. The decision to begin not with narration dramatized, not with the lovers or the fathers, but with two hired men inventing a quarrel in real time is the adaptation's founding act of interpretation. It declares that the feud will be studied behaviourally, from the bottom, in the present tense, as something people do rather than something that happened. Everything the tragedy later achieves in its great set pieces, the feast, the balcony, the duel, the tomb, is written in the knowledge this scene installs: that Verona's catastrophe is not a fate imposed on the city but a practice maintained by it, at every rank, for reasons its practitioners cannot state. The servants cannot state them first, and loudest.

## Carry coals: the opening pun and the price of honour

The first line of dialogue in the play is a refusal. "Gregory, on my word, we'll not carry coals" (1.1.1), Sampson announces, and Gregory answers, "No, for then we should be colliers" (1.1.2). The exchange looks like small talk and functions like a thesis. To carry coals was proverbial for submitting to insult and drudgery, the work and the humiliation assigned to the lowliest labourer; a collier, blackened by his trade, carried the period's associations of dirt and churlishness. Sampson's opening position is therefore not about coal at all. It is a declaration that his honour, a servant's borrowed honour, will not tolerate indignity. The tragedy of Verona begins with a man swearing that he will not be made to feel small.

### What does Sampson mean by refusing to carry coals?

Sampson means that he and Gregory will not swallow insults the way coal-carriers swallow filthy, degrading work. The proverb made coal-carrying a figure for enduring disgrace, and colliers were proverbially dirty and base. His refusal announces the scene's logic: honour defined as the point where slights get answered with steel.

The pun then begins to move, and its movement is the scene in miniature. Sampson glosses himself: "I mean, an we be in choler, we'll draw" (1.1.3), converting coal to collier to choler, the humour of anger, so that a word for fuel becomes a word for rage becomes a promise of drawn swords, all inside three lines. Gregory completes the chain by bending choler toward collar: "Ay, while you live, draw your neck out of the collar" (1.1.4), the collar being the hangman's noose, so that the promised swordplay is instantly shadowed by the gallows that punishes street killing. Rene Weis's notes to the Arden edition gloss the sequence patiently, and the glossing is the point: four lines into the play, the audience is already doing philology, tracking one sound as it mutates through trade, temper, and execution. The feud's violence, the scene proposes, is a verbal habit before it is a physical one. Men talk themselves into the posture from which drawing feels necessary.

Notice, too, who leads and who corrects. Sampson opens every boast; Gregory annotates it. Sampson's "I strike quickly, being moved" (1.1.5) draws Gregory's audit, "But thou art not quickly moved to strike" (1.1.6), and the pattern is set for the entire exchange: assertion, then deflation, then a larger assertion to repair the damage. The wordplay is therefore never ornamental duetting. It is a negotiation between a man performing courage and a man who knows the performance is a performance, and the comedy lives in the gap. When Sampson reaches for an enemy grand enough to justify the posture, he produces the scene's bleakest joke: "A dog of the house of Montague moves me" (1.1.7). Not Montague. Not a son of the house. A dog of the house, any creature wearing the wrong allegiance, is provocation enough. The feud's threshold for violence has fallen below the human.

Gregory's reply extends the verb chain that the editions gloss as the exchange's spine: "To move is to stir, and to be valiant is to stand. Therefore if thou art moved, thou runn'st away" (1.1.8-10). Move, stir, stand: the sequence begins as fencing logic and quietly acquires the bawdy undertow that will shortly surface, since stand in the period's slang carried the sense the glosses record for a man's readiness. Sampson caps it in kind: "A dog of that house shall move me to stand. I will take the wall of any man or maid of Montague's" (1.1.11-13). The pun has now travelled from coal to combat to the body, and the destination of the next movement is already visible. The wordplay is an escalator. Each man boards a joke at the level of trade and rides it, through honour, toward sex and steel, and neither man at any point chooses the destination. The chain chooses it, which is precisely how the scene understands the feud.


The opening phrase deserves one more look, because Sampson stakes it on an oath: "Gregory, on my word, we'll not carry coals." On my word is the formula by which a man pledges his credit, and a servingman has no other capital. Sampson's word is the one asset his rank leaves him, and he spends it in the scene's first breath on a promise to be ready for a fight. The economy is exact and pathetic. Men with land pledge land; men with names pledge names; a man with nothing pledges his word, and the pledge's object reveals what the word is for. Throughout the exchange, words are the servants' only wealth and their only weapons, which is why the scene lavishes such invention on them. The puns are not decoration on poverty. They are poverty, talking rich.

The choler link in the chain also carries a physiology that a period audience took as bodily fact. Choler, yellow bile, was one of the four humours, the hot and dry fluid whose dominance made a man choleric, quick to anger and quick to strike. To be in choler was not a metaphor for temper but a state of the body, as material as a fever. The pun therefore does quiet philosophical work: it relocates the feud from the will to the fluids. Sampson does not decide to be angry; his choler rises, as bile does, and the drawing of swords presents itself to him as a symptom rather than a choice. The whole tragedy will keep returning to this evasion, in lovers who blame stars, a friar who blames a greater power, and fathers who blame disobedience, and the first statement of it is a servant's pun. The feud's deepest alibi, that nobody is responsible because everybody is carried, is coined here at the bottom of the house, where the carrying is easiest to see.

One verb governs the whole chain and deserves its own accounting: draw. Sampson promises that in choler the pair will draw; Gregory answers with the neck drawn out of the collar; and the scene will end with the order to draw the tool and with swords drawn in earnest. Draw is the period's ordinary word for pulling a blade from its sheath, and the pun-work keeps it in suspension for seventy lines, drawn necks and drawn humours standing in for drawn steel until the steel itself is required. The effect is a kind of verbal striptease of violence, the act named and renamed until naming it no longer suffices. It also marks the exact difference between the servants' morning and the duel that ends the play's third act: Tybalt draws without a dozen puns of preparation, while Sampson must talk his sword out of its sheath across an entire scene. The feud's professionals are laconic. Its amateurs need an overture, and the overture is the scene.

The fencing vocabulary threaded through the boasts tells the same story from another angle. Move, stir, stand, strike, the swashing blow to come: the servants speak of fighting in the technical terms of the fencing schools, as connoisseurs speak of a craft they admire. Yet nothing in their talk suggests training, and Gregory's audit of Sampson's quickness suggests the opposite. Their expertise is spectatorial. They have watched fights, heard fights described, absorbed the vocabulary of the duello from the street, and mistaken the vocabulary for the skill. Verona is full of such amateurs of violence in the play that follows, fathers calling for swords they are too old to lift and young men whose honour outruns their judgement, and the pattern is set by these two: a city where the language of the duel is common property long before the competence is, so that any man can talk himself into a fight he has no business surviving.

## Take the wall: pavement, precedence, and the road to the maids

"I will take the wall of any man or maid of Montague's" is the boast on which the exchange turns from wordplay about honour to the scene's governing threat. In the street geography of the period, the wall side was the cleaner side, away from the channel of filth running down the middle of the road. To take the wall was to claim precedence, to force the other party into the gutter; to be thrust to the wall was to be publicly demoted. Sampson's promise is therefore total in scope and petty in substance. He will concede the gutter to no Montague, man or maid, which means he is prepared to begin a blood feud over pavement. The scene's measurement of the feud's value is exact: it is worth killing for, and it is worth, in itself, nothing at all.

Gregory, faithful to his office as auditor, immediately supplies the proverb that reverses the boast: "That shows thee a weak slave, for the weakest goes to the wall" (1.1.14-15). The wall is double-valued. It is the place of honour for the man who takes it and the place of shame for the party thrust against it, and the same image holds both, because precedence and humiliation are a single transaction viewed from opposite ends. Sampson accepts the reversal and drives it to its destination with a logician's calm: "True, and therefore women, being the weaker vessels, are ever thrust to the wall. Therefore I will push Montague's men from the wall and thrust his maids to the wall" (1.1.16-19). The syllogism deserves attention. A street proverb about precedence becomes a premise about women; the premise becomes a programme; and the programme assigns the men to combat and the women to a thrusting whose double sense the audience is left in no doubt about, because the next exchange spells it out.

This is the hinge of the close reading, and it is worth pausing on the direction of travel. The servants began with their own honour. Within twenty lines, the honour has been redefined as something done to other people's bodies, first male bodies in the street and then female bodies in the imagination. Nothing in the scene's world has changed; no Montague has appeared; the escalation is purely rhetorical, a chain of puns and proverbs tightening link by link. The playwright is showing his audience the feud's internal mechanics at a scale small enough to see whole. A grievance is unnecessary. A vocabulary is sufficient. Given the words wall, thrust, stand, and weak, two idle men can reason their way to sexual violence as a morning's entertainment, and the reasoning will feel to them like loyalty.

The gendered turn also fixes the servants' relation to the play that follows. The maids Sampson threatens are abstractions, Montague's maids, a category rather than persons, and the threat against them costs him nothing because it will never be collected. Verona's men rehearse the feud on women's bodies throughout the tragedy, in Capulet's disposal of Juliet, in the Nurse's counsel to abandon a husband, in Paris's courtship conducted entirely through a father. The opening exchange is where that habit is first displayed, in its cheapest form, as banter. What the great house will later do with contracts and threats, its servants do first with a proverb and a leer, and the continuity is the accusation. The feud does not trickle down from policy to the street. It is the same substance at every level, and the street version is merely the least disguised.

The weaker vessels phrase repays a pause, since Sampson borrows it from scripture. The description of the wife as the weaker vessel descends from the First Epistle of Peter, where husbands are instructed to dwell with their wives according to knowledge, giving honour unto the wife as unto the weaker vessel. In its source the phrase is attached to an instruction of care, however patriarchal its frame: the vessel is weaker and is therefore to be honoured, handled gently. Sampson's use inverts the ethic while keeping the words. For him the vessel's weakness is not a claim on gentleness but a licence for force, the reason women are ever thrust to the wall. The scene's method is visible in miniature: Verona's men quote the authorities they live under, scripture, proverb, the code of honour, and every quotation comes out bent toward violence. A culture is indicted not by what it says but by what its sayings are made to do in the mouths of its least reflective speakers.

Performance translates the wall without difficulty, although the street geography that grounded it has vanished. No modern audience needs the gutter explained once the bodies arrange themselves: one man shouldering past another on a narrow pavement, the jostle that is almost an accident and entirely a challenge, communicates precedence in a physical language every city still speaks. Directors routinely discover that the scene requires almost no updating, because its materials are the permanent furniture of male public life, the claimed pavement, the held stare, the insult disguised as banter among friends. That durability is itself evidence in the close reading's central dispute. Material that survives the loss of its original references, its proverbs, its street plan, and its slang is not topical garnish. It is structure wearing period dress, and the dress can change because the body under it has not.

The scope of the wall boast is easy to skate past and should not be: "any man or maid of Montague's". Before the maidenheads pun exists, before the talk turns overtly to women, Sampson's precedence claim already includes the maids, which means the sexual programme of the scene is not a late improvisation but part of the original stake. He will take the wall of the maids too, will rank above them in the street as a matter of house policy, and the ranking of women in public space is asserted as casually as the ranking of men. The feud's claim, stated in its first minutes, is total: it orders every encounter in the city, across rank and across sex, and it orders the women's encounters without consulting them, as a clause inside a boast between men. The tragedy's heroines will live inside that clause. Juliet's whole plot is the attempt of one maid of a great house to secede from an order in which her place at every wall has been assigned in advance, by men who, in the deepest sense, are not even her enemies but her own house's servants, rehearsing the assignment as a joke.

One syllable in Sampson's wall speech carries the whole weight of custom: "women, being the weaker vessels, are ever thrust to the wall". Ever is the operative word. The proverb does not describe an event but a permanence, the way things are done, always have been done, and by implication must be done. Custom is the feud's favourite alibi at every level of the play, the form in which inherited violence excuses itself from examination, and the servants speak its dialect perfectly. An ancient grudge, the Prologue calls the feud, and ancient is the same alibi wearing a grander word: old enough to be nobody's fault, old enough to be everybody's duty. Sampson's ever and the Chorus's ancient are the same syllable at different ranks, and the tragedy is the story of what it costs to keep honouring them.

## Maidenheads: the bawdy engine of the exchange

Gregory now names the ownership of the quarrel, "The quarrel is between our masters and us their men" (1.1.20), and Sampson answers with the exchange's most quoted escalation: "'Tis all one. I will show myself a tyrant. When I have fought with the men, I will be civil with the maids. I will cut off their heads" (1.1.21-23). Gregory pounces on the word: "The heads of the maids?" (1.1.24). Sampson delivers the pun the whole sequence has been approaching: "Ay, the heads of the maids, or their maidenheads, take it in what sense thou wilt" (1.1.25-26). Gregory caps it, "They must take it in sense that feel it" (1.1.27), and Sampson closes the circuit in open bawdy: "Me they shall feel while I am able to stand, and 'tis known I am a pretty piece of flesh" (1.1.28-29).

### What is the maidenheads pun, exactly?

The pun equates the heads Sampson threatens to cut off with maidenheads, the period's word for virginity, so beheading and rape become one joke with two senses. Gregory's prompt and Sampson's gloss make the equation explicit. The wordplay converts a threat of murder into sexual violence without changing its grammar.

Eric Partridge's Shakespeare's Bawdy, the pioneering glossary of the playwright's sexual language, treats formations like maidenheads as entries in a coherent and widely understood vocabulary, and the standard editions follow that work in glossing the passage without embarrassment: heads, maidenheads, feel, stand, flesh, and, in Gregory's next speech, tool and poor John all carry the second sense the scene requires. The editorial record matters to the close reading because it removes the protective fiction that the bawdy is a modern projection. Generation after generation of editors, collating the early texts and the period's usage, have recorded the same double senses, because the dialogue itself insists on them. Gregory's "They must take it in sense that feel it" is the playwright's own footnote, an instruction to the audience to take the words in the sense that is felt. A text that glosses its own smut is not being accidentally coarse. It is teaching its hearers where to look.

The sequence that follows completes the lesson. Gregory warns Sampson off the final step of self-praise: "'Tis well thou art not fish. If thou hadst, thou hadst been poor John" (1.1.30-31), poor John being the cheapest dried hake, a fish so low in the market that the name was proverbial for worthlessness, with the flesh and fish terms keeping the bawdy register running under the joke. Then, in the same breath, Gregory supplies the line that converts the whole conversation into action: "Draw thy tool! Here comes two of the house of Montagues" (1.1.31-32). Tool is the scene's perfect hinge word, the sword and the bawdy sense arriving in a single syllable, so that the drawing of weapons is phrased in the vocabulary of the threats that preceded it. The men do not change registers when the enemy appears. They continue the joke by other means.

Here the brief's complication must be confronted directly, because the maidenheads passage is where a warm-up theory of the bawdy goes to die. Warm-up bawdy is detachable: it raises a laugh, marks time, and leaves the play unchanged. This passage is load-bearing. It establishes that in Verona's feud culture, violence against men and sexual possession of women are conjugations of one verb, and it establishes the fact before the romance exists, so that when the romance arrives it enters a world already formatted by that grammar. Coppelia Kahn's reading of the feud, developed in her account of the play's masculine coming-of-age, argues that the feud functions as a proving ground on which Verona's males at every rank rehearse and certify their manhood, with women as the currency in which the proof is transacted. The servants' exchange is the purest evidence for that reading in the play, purer than Tybalt, because Sampson and Gregory have no inheritance at stake and no Juliet in view. Their masculinity is performed in a vacuum, for each other, out of words, and its content is still identical: dominate the men, take the women. The bawdy is not the sugar on the scene's pill. The bawdy is the pill.

The textual history of the passage reinforces the point about design. The first quarto of 1597 prints a markedly shorter play than the second quarto of 1599, the text from which modern editions descend, and the opening scene in the longer text gives the servants' duet room to complete its chain link by link. Editors print the fuller exchange because the fuller exchange is the argument: shortened, the scene still opens the play and still starts a brawl, but the syllogism shows its seams. A tradition that reaches for the long version, quarto by quarto and edition by edition, has quietly voted on the question this article is adjudicating. The bawdy duet is kept whole because cutting it costs comprehension, not merely laughs. What survives editorial selection over four centuries under commercial and moral pressure earns a presumption of necessity, and the maidenheads passage has survived expurgators, school boards, and acting editions that removed far more famous lines.

Partridge's contribution also deserves precision, because the glossarial work is sometimes mistaken for a merely antiquarian rescue of dirty jokes. Shakespeare's Bawdy proceeded on the conviction that the sexual senses form a system, that the playwright's bawdy is a language with its own consistency, available to any hearer in the original audience and recoverable by methodical collection. Applied to this scene, the method yields the chain this article has traced: coals and colliers in the register of labour, wall and thrust in the register of the street, heads and maidenheads in the register of the body, tool at the junction where the registers fuse. A system is not noise. The recovery of the scene's vocabulary is what allows its grammar to be read at all, and the grammar, once read, is unmistakably an argument about how the feud thinks.

Sampson's invitation, "take it in what sense thou wilt", can stand as the scene's charter of interpretation, and it cuts deeper than a wink at the yard. The line concedes that meaning in this world is chosen by the hearer, and therefore deniable by the speaker, and the whole tragedy will be conducted under that charter. Words in Verona habitually carry a sense for the street and a sense for the heart: a marriage that is a sacrament and a transaction, a potion that is a sleep and a death, a tomb that is a monument and a bridal bed. The servants announce the rule in its coarsest form, over a joke about virginity, because the coarse form is the honest one. Every refinement the play later achieves is built on the foundation these two lay while waiting for a fight: nothing here means one thing, and the second meaning is where the danger lives.

Two words in Sampson's tyrant speech carry more freight than their size suggests. The first is tyrant itself. "I will show myself a tyrant" is a servingman's fantasy of sovereignty, the dream of a man whose life is obedience imagining a condition in which his will is law, and the fantasy's content is telling: his tyranny will be exercised not over his masters, not over the city, but over the defeated men's women. Even in imagination, the servant's rebellion aims downward. The feud supplies him a licensed direction for the rage his station generates, sideways and down, never up, and the licence is the system's genius. A house that lets its servants play tyrant over enemy maids in fancy has purchased their loyalty at no cost to itself, and the purchase is renewed every morning in talk like this.

The second word is civil. "I will be civil with the maids" places courtesy and atrocity in a single breath, since the civility Sampson promises is the prelude to cutting off heads, maiden- or otherwise. The joke depends on the audience hearing civil as its opposite, and the scene returns to that inversion at its climax, where the fatal quarrel is transacted in sirs. Civility in Verona, the opening proposes, is not the antidote to the feud but one of its dialects: the polite forms are how violence makes appointments. The Prince's edict, when it comes, will be civil too, a formal instrument of law, and it will fail for the reason the servants have already exhibited. Forms regulate the feud's expression without touching its supply, and the supply is renewed wherever men gather with time to fill and a house's colours to defend.

## Bravado audited: Gregory as the deflator of Sampson

A close reading owes the pair one more discrimination, because the exchange is not a duet of equals. Sampson is the scene's fantasist, Gregory its accountant. Nearly every Sampson boast is met by a Gregory correction that prices it: Sampson strikes quickly, but is not quickly moved to strike; Sampson will stand, but the moved man runs away; Sampson is a pretty piece of flesh, but happily not fish. The comedy of the sequence is double-entry bookkeeping, bravado on one side and audit on the other, and the audience is trained by the rhythm to discount Sampson's currency at Gregory's rate. That training pays off at the thumb-biting, where Sampson's courage must at last be spent rather than described, and the exchange rate proves ruinous.

### Are Sampson and Gregory brave men?

The text answers that their courage is rhetorical rather than real. Sampson's boasts shrink at every test Gregory applies, and when enemies arrive his first instincts are legal, not martial: he wants the law on his side and bites his thumb only with a denial prepared in advance.

The distinction matters because it locates the feud's energy precisely. These are not villains and not heroes; they are employees amusing themselves with the firm's mythology during a slow morning. Their war talk is a perquisite of the livery, like the wall side of the street, something the great house's colours entitle them to enjoy. When Gregory later whispers the instruction that finally ignites the brawl, the whisper will come from the auditor, not the fantasist, and the scene's logic completes itself: the man who sees through the performance is the man who makes it real. Cowardice in this scene is not the opposite of the feud's violence. It is the violence's normal operating condition, the posture of men who want the pleasures of aggression, its status and its jokes, at a discount from its risks. Verona will supply grander versions of the same posture, in fathers who rage from behind their households and a prince whose penalties arrive after the bodies, but the template is struck here, in prose, by the two least important men in the city.

It is worth weighing the alternative staging tradition that plays the pair as straightforward clowns whose scene merely delays the tragedy, since the text resists it at one specific point. Clowns delaying a tragedy do not usually frighten themselves with the law. Sampson's aside, "Is the law of our side if I say ay?" (1.1.56), is the anxiety of a man calculating consequences in the middle of his own provocation, and it has no analogue in pure clowning. The servants know the Prince's peace exists, know a brawl has penalties, and engineer their insult for deniability anyway. That engineering, aggression with an alibi drafted in advance, is the feud's mature form, and it is why the scene's comedy curdles in the mouth. The laughter the pair raise is real, and the scene is built to make the audience notice what it has been laughing at: two men rehearsing a rape threat as a warm-up for a knife fight, and calling the rehearsal loyalty.


The pair's fencing with words deserves a formal name, because the scene gives it one in everything but terminology: it is flyting, the ritual exchange of insults as competitive sport, in which the victory goes to the man whose invention holds out longest. Gregory is the better flyter, and the text marks his superiority in the only currency that matters in the form, the last word. Sampson opens every subject; Gregory closes nearly every exchange, with the collar, with the runaway gloss on standing, with the fish. The pattern prefigures the scene's literal fencing with quiet exactness. In the verbal duel, Sampson lunges and Gregory ripostes; in the street duel to come, the same distribution of gifts will operate, except that steel keeps a different score. An audience charmed by the ripostes has been taught to admire the skill that the feud rewards, quickness in the service of offence, and the admiration is another of the scene's small traps.

There is also the matter of the audience inside the scene. Two men alone on a stage should not need to perform, yet Sampson and Gregory perform incessantly, for each other. Each is the other's groundling, laughing on cue, topping on cue, and the arrangement discloses something the feud requires at every level: a witness. Honour in this world is not a state but a rating, assigned by whoever is watching, which is why a kinsman within earshot can change the price of a comparison in an instant. The servants, lacking any other theatre, build one out of their own company and spend the morning inside it. Verona's great men will use larger stages, a feast hall, a public square, a tomb at night with the watch approaching, but the mechanism is identical and was patented here: no audience, no honour; no honour, no feud.

Even the boaster's name is a small irony the scene sets ticking. Sampson cannot help suggesting Samson, the strongman of the Book of Judges, the hero whose strength was a gift and whose downfall ran through women, and the suggestion measures the distance between the servant's self-image and his substance. The biblical Samson pulled a temple down on his enemies. This Sampson's feats exist entirely in the future tense and the conditional mood: he will take the wall, he will be civil, he will cut off heads, always after a victory that the scene is careful never to let him win. His namesake's strength failed through a woman's questioning; the servant's fails through his friend's. Gregory is Sampson's Delilah in the only register the scene allows, lulling each boast to sleep with a gloss and shearing it of force, and the comedy of the name is that Sampson never notices the haircut. A strongman audited into harmlessness by his own companion is the feud's perfect soldier: all appetite, no execution, until the system finds him witnesses and makes the appetite good.

## The bitten thumb: a spark with legal cover

The Montague servants, Abram and Balthasar, enter to men whose courage has been spent in advance on talk, and the exchange's final movement is a study in aggression trying to remain deniable. Sampson's first instruction is procedural: "My naked weapon is out. Quarrel, I will back thee" (1.1.33-34), and Gregory hears the gap in it at once: "How, turn thy back and run?" (1.1.35). Sampson's answer is the pair's whole philosophy in three words, "Fear me not" (1.1.36), which Gregory returns with interest: "No, marry, I fear thee!" (1.1.37). Then Sampson states the legal strategy plainly: "Let us take the law of our sides. Let them begin" (1.1.38-39). The fight must be the other party's fault. Honour requires a brawl; prudence requires that the brawl be attributable elsewhere; and the instrument that reconciles the two requirements is the gesture Sampson proposes next: "I will bite my thumb at them, which is a disgrace to them if they bear it" (1.1.42-43), after Gregory's preliminary frown has tested the water.

The thumb-biting itself, its history as an insult and its exact weight in the period's code of offence, is a subject with its own full treatment in the companion study [Biting the Thumb: The Insult That Starts a War](/2023/06/02/biting-the-thumb-insult-analysis/), and the close reading here concerns only the gesture's placement inside the servants' exchange. Placed there, its function is grammatical. It is an insult in the passive voice. It says everything and asserts nothing, so that when Abram demands, "Do you bite your thumb at us, sir?" (1.1.44), Sampson can retreat into syntax: "I do bite my thumb, sir" (1.1.45), and then, after the whispered consultation about the law, "No, sir, I do not bite my thumb at you, sir, but I bite my thumb, sir" (1.1.58). The distinction between biting a thumb and biting a thumb at someone is the smallest distance in the play, and Sampson hides his whole courage inside it. The scene's long lesson in equivocation, the punning habit of meaning two things with one word, reaches its practical application: a man can insult with a gesture and acquit himself with a preposition.

For the close reading, the essential point is that the thumb-bite fails at its purpose. It was designed to make the Montagues begin, and it does not. Abram's questions are as lawyerly as Sampson's answers, and the provocation stalls in a thicket of sirs, until the ignition has to be supplied from the Capulet side after all, by quieter and more deliberate means. The gesture's fame has somewhat obscured that failure. What starts the fight is not the famous insult but the whispered stage management that follows it, and the difference between the two is the difference between the feud as folklore and the feud as practice.

The thumb-bite also marks the scene's one descent below language, and the descent is instructive. For seventy lines the feud has been conducted in words, elaborate, deniable, funny words, and the insult that finally tests the enemy is wordless: a gesture, chosen because it cannot be quoted in evidence. Sampson's instinct is the instinct of the whole culture the scene anatomizes, to find the form of aggression that leaves no record. The failure of the gesture is therefore a small parable. Deniability works only while both sides cooperate in the fiction, and the Montague servants decline to cooperate; Abram's question forces the gesture back into language, where it must be owned or disowned, and Sampson's disowning costs him more standing than the insult gained. The feud's verbal economy has rules even its inventors cannot bend: a meaning, once challenged, must be paid for in one currency or the other, shame or steel.

It is worth noticing, too, how little time the scene gives the gesture itself. Productions linger on it, freeze it, build posters from it, but the text moves through the bite in a handful of exchanges and spends its real attention on the consultation and the retreat, the aside about the law and the grammar of aim versus act. The playwright's interest is not the spark but the fire precautions that fail: the law consulted, the syntax searched, the dignity salvaged at the price of the quarrel stalling. When ignition finally comes, it comes from a whisper and a two-word insult in plain speech, the least picturesque materials in the scene. The close reading takes the hint. In Verona, violence is not ignited by exotic gestures from a vanished honour code. It is ignited by bookkeeping about reputation, conducted in whispers, by frightened men.

The Montague pair deserve a sentence of their own, because the scene's symmetry depends on them. Abram and the servant with him, Balthasar in the play's later naming, are not innocents ambushed by Capulet aggression. Abram's questions are as procedural as Sampson's answers, each sir a small legal instrument, and his refusal of the quarrel, "Quarrel, sir? No, sir", is a position in the same negotiation rather than a rejection of the game. The feud's servants on both sides speak one language, observe one ladder of insult, and consult one law. Nothing in the encounter suggests that a different pair, drawn from either house, would behave differently, and that interchangeability is the bleakest datum the scene provides. The brawl needs no villains because the roles are pre-written, and any four servingmen in Verona could be fed into them with the same result, down to the whispered consultation and the fatal two words.

The consultation is staged as asides, and the staging is part of the meaning. On an open stage, in daylight, an aside is a fiction everyone present agrees to sustain: the audience hears what the enemy, four paces away, conventionally does not. Sampson's whispered question about the law is therefore public privacy, a secret shared with hundreds, and that doubleness is the feud's native element. Verona's whole honour system runs on such sustained fictions, insults that are not insults, duels that are not anyone's fault, a peace that everyone keeps by breaking it deniably. The aside is the perfect theatrical emblem of the system because it lets a character speak the truth of his fear while the fiction of his courage stands intact beside it. When the asides stop, the swords come out; the scene's real threshold is not the thumb but the moment the men run out of private channels and must transact in public, where the code permits no retreat. Language, the scene keeps demonstrating, is not the alternative to the feud's violence. It is the violence's preferred medium, abandoned only when it fails.

## Draw, if you be men: how the quarrel ignites

The ignition sequence repays a slow reading, because every line in it transfers responsibility to someone else. Gregory tries a fresh opening, "Do you quarrel, sir?" (1.1.59), and Abram declines it: "Quarrel, sir? No, sir" (1.1.60). Sampson then lays the trap of service: "But if you do, sir, I am for you. I serve as good a man as you" (1.1.61-62), a sentence engineered so that Abram must either accept equality with a servant of the enemy or dispute the comparison and give offence. Abram disputes it with two words, "No better" (1.1.63), and Sampson, stalled, offers a baffled "Well, sir" (1.1.64). At that moment Gregory sees reinforcements, "Say 'better'. Here comes one of my master's kinsmen" (1.1.65), and the aside deserves its reputation as the scene's most efficient line. Courage, in this economy, is a function of the audience. Sampson's valuation of his master rises exactly when a kinsman of that master comes within earshot, and his amended claim, "Yes, better, sir" (1.1.66), draws the only sentence in the scene that cannot be unsaid: "You lie" (1.1.67).

Sampson's response is the exchange's epitaph and its detonation in one: "Draw, if you be men. Gregory, remember thy swashing blow" (1.1.68-69). After seventy lines of talk, the definition of manhood that the whole conversation has been assembling is applied as an order. To be men is to draw. The swashing blow, the showy slashing stroke of the fencing schools, is invoked as a fond memory and a stage direction at once, and the swords come out. The full architecture of the brawl that follows, its widening circles of citizens, fathers, and at last the Prince, belongs to the scene-level study [Act 1 Scene 1: The Brawl That Opens Verona](/2023/05/26/act-1-scene-1-opening-brawl-analysis/); the close reading ends at the ignition, because ignition is what the servants' exchange was built to explain. Two men who wanted a fight they could disown got a fight they had to own, and the mechanism was not passion. It was bookkeeping: a comparison disputed, a kinsman approaching, a two-word accusation that made retreat more expensive than steel.

One more feature of the sequence should not escape notice, which is its courtesy. The ignition is transacted almost entirely in sirs. Sampson and Abram fence with honorifics while manoeuvring each other toward felony, and the politeness is not ironic decoration but part of the mechanism, since every sir raises the cost of the insult it carries by proving the insult deliberate and composed. Verona's feud, the scene proposes, is not a failure of manners. It is manners, weaponized: a code of precedence, address, and gesture so refined that any participant can find, inside its ordinary courtesies, a fully serviceable reason to kill. The servants have mastered the code completely. It is the only thing their masters ever taught them.

The comparison Sampson lays as his trap, "I serve as good a man as you", deserves isolation, because its grammar gives the whole system away. The servants do not compare themselves. They compare their masters, through themselves: the worth of the Capulet Sampson serves against the worth of the Montague Abram serves, with the servants as the measuring instruments. Identity at this rank is entirely derivative, a reflection of the house whose colours the speaker wears, and the quarrel's fuel is that derivative identity, since a slight to the comparison is a slight to the master and therefore to the servant's only self. Abram cannot let "as good a man" stand without demoting Montague; Sampson cannot withdraw it without demoting Capulet, and himself to nothing. The trap is perfect because it is sincere. Both men really are their masters for the purposes of the street, and the tragedy will apply the same grammar upward, where sons are their fathers' heirs and daughters their fathers' goods, with consequences no less mechanical.

The approaching kinsman concentrates the mechanism. Gregory's aside identifies reinforcements from his own side, "one of my master's kinsmen", generally taken by editors to be Tybalt entering, and the mere approach alters what Sampson can afford to say. Retreat before enemies is strategy; retreat before a kinsman of the house is betrayal, reportable at the servants' hall table. The feud polices itself through this internal spectatorship far more efficiently than the Prince polices it through edicts, since the watch is intermittent and the kinsman is already here. Verona's tragedy, the scene proposes, is not that its peace is underenforced. It is that its war is overenforced, by every participant upon every other, in a lattice of watching that no statute reaches. Sampson draws, in the end, because the cheapest available witness was his own side's.

## The feud at the bottom of the house

What, then, has the exchange established, before the brawl it ignites widens beyond the servants' control? First, that the feud requires no grievance in the present tense. Neither Sampson nor Gregory mentions an injury, a debt, an insult received, or any event whatsoever. Their casus belli is a category: the house of Montague, down to its dogs. The companion account of that hatred's origins and career, [The Feud: Why the Capulets and Montagues Hate](/2022/04/01/capulet-montague-feud-explained/), traces how the play withholds a cause from every speaker who might supply one; the servants' scene is where the withholding does its most damning work, because men at the bottom cannot even pretend to a policy reason. There is nothing under the livery but the livery.

### What does a servants' brawl prove about the feud?

It proves the feud is a culture rather than a dispute. A dispute has terms and a conceivable settlement; the servants display only allegiance, appetite, and a shared script of insult. When men who own nothing in a quarrel risk the gallows to perform it, the quarrel has become common property.

Second, the exchange proves that the feud is heritable downward through employment. Gregory's line about the quarrel belonging to the masters and their men is usually read as description, and it is also a contract: the men's part of the quarrel is the fighting, the masters' part is the owning. The servants of both houses are thus a standing militia that neither house has to raise, pay extra, or even instruct, since the script of provocation is common knowledge in the street. The wider study of that servant class and its place in the play's economy, [The Servants of Romeo and Juliet: Class From Below](/2023/05/12/servants-in-romeo-and-juliet-class/), follows the pattern through the households' other retainers; the opening scene is its founding exhibit, the moment the audience watches class position convert directly into violence without any order being given. No one commands this brawl. That is the horror the comedy is carrying. A system in which the lowest employees spontaneously manufacture a riot, as a way of passing the time and affirming their worth, has no off switch located in any individual's conscience.

Third, the scene fixes the feud's emotional economy. The servants are enjoying themselves. Their talk is inventive, companionable, and funny, and the fight is the morning's entertainment before it is the morning's emergency. Critics who stress the scene's gaiety as evidence of a comic opening are right about the affect and, on this reading, wrong about its implication: the pleasure is the diagnosis. A feud that its instruments experience as sport will outlive every edict, because suppressing it means suppressing the one arena in which a servingman can feel like a principal. The Prince will arrive with penalties presently, and the penalties will fail in the play's third act for reasons the first scene has already supplied.

Livery is the scene's silent premise, and it should be made loud for a moment. Sampson and Gregory are recognizable as Capulet men before they speak, by dress, by place, by the company they keep, and the same is true of the Montague pair who enter to them. The feud is worn. It sorts the street's population at sight into targets and allies, which is why provocation can begin from a frown across a square: the uniforms have already done the identifying, and the men inside them need only supply the occasion. Gregory's formulation, the quarrel between our masters and us their men, has its possessive backwards in the way that matters most. The men belong to the masters, grammatically and sartorially, and the quarrel belongs to the men only as labour belongs to the labourer, something carried for another's profit. They will bleed for it in the street while its owners are still at breakfast, and the play's first scene arranges for the audience to watch the carrying before it ever shows the owners, so that ownership itself is arraigned.

The absence of any order is the scene's final proof, and it generalizes beyond the servants. Tyrannies of custom do not require commanders; they require scripts, audiences, and small rewards, and Verona supplies all three at street level. The script is the proverb chain and the insult ladder. The audience is whoever is watching, a friend, a kinsman, the yard itself. The reward is the one Sampson named at the start, the feeling of not carrying coals, of being a man whose word is backed by a house. Against that economy the Prince will shortly deploy the only instrument law possesses, a penalty, and the play's later brawls will demonstrate the exchange rate between a death penalty and a morning's self-respect in a servant who owns nothing else. The demonstration begins here because it can only begin here, with the citizens of the feud who have the least to lose and therefore show its workings with the least concealment.

A word about the morning itself, because the scene is unusually specific about its conditions: two servants at leisure in a public place, unoccupied, in no hurry, with the day before them. The feud, in this presentation, is what fills empty time. Sampson and Gregory are not ambushed by hatred while about their business; hatred is their business while they wait for something better to do, and the escalation's pace is the pace of bored men amusing themselves, each joke raising the bid because the game must not be allowed to end. This is the social physics the Prince's edict will fail to change, since an edict can raise the price of brawling but cannot supply the street with another pastime, another source of standing, another story in which a servingman matters. The play returns to the physics at its crisis, when a hot afternoon, an idle square, and young men with nothing to do but stand about in companies produce the duel that kills Mercutio, but the law of the thing is stated first and plainest here: in Verona, unstructured time among liveried men tends toward blood, as reliably as water tends downhill.

## Warm-up or thesis: the verdict on the bawdy

The disagreement the brief requires can now be stated cleanly. On one side stands a glossarial tradition, descended from Partridge's necessary labour of recovery, in which the opening bawdy is the period's background noise: the groundlings expected smut from low characters, the playwright supplied it, and the scene's real work is exposition of the feud. On that account the puns are period costume, detachable in principle, as detachable as the hundreds of productions that have trimmed them for school audiences without the plot noticing. On the other side stands Kahn's structural account, in which the bawdy is the feud's native language, the medium in which Verona's masculine order thinks, so that the scene's sexual threats are not noise around the feud's signal but the signal itself, audible at the servants' rank with the least interference.

### Is the opening bawdy only a warm-up for the groundlings?

No. The bawdy carries the scene's argument, because its chain of puns moves in one direction only: from trade to anger, precedence, sexual threat, and drawn steel. A warm-up can be cut without consequence. Cutting the maidenheads exchange removes the moment where the feud's grammar is stated whole, and the escalation breaks.

The verdict goes to Kahn, with one refinement drawn from the text rather than from theory. The decisive evidence is the chain's directionality, which the glossarial account cannot explain and does not try to. Detachable decoration is orderless; this sequence is a proof. Coals must precede colliers, colliers choler, choler the collar; the wall must be taken before the weakest can go to it, and the weakest named as women before the maids can be thrust there; heads must be threatened before maidenheads can be punned out of them. Each joke is the premise of the next, and the conclusion of the syllogism is a drawn sword. A dramatist arranging warm-up noise does not build proofs. A dramatist stating a thesis in the only register his opening characters command builds exactly this: an argument in puns, conducted by men who do not know they are arguing, concluding in the action the argument was always about.

Snyder's comic-matrix reading survives the verdict as a friendly amendment. The scene is comic in construction, and its comedy is the delivery system rather than a rival explanation: the audience laughs its way down the chain and discovers, at the bottom, that it has assented to every premise of a rape threat and a street killing along the way. That complicity is the scene's final piece of engineering, and it is why the opening belongs at the front of a tragedy rather than a comedy. The lovers will later ask the audience for a sympathy as unguarded as the laughter the servants extract, and the play will collect on both debts in the tomb. Restoring Sampson and Gregory to the head of the play, where the text puts them, restores the feud as the first subject and the first cause: two men, a wall, a thumb, and a vocabulary in which love and violence were never separate subjects to begin with.


The expurgation record supplies the verdict with a historical control experiment. The Family Shakespeare prepared by Thomas Bowdler in the early nineteenth century announced its method in its own preface, the removal of those words and expressions which cannot with propriety be read aloud in a family, and passages like the servants' duet were exactly its quarry. For generations of readers, the play effectively began nearer the brawl than the puns, and the feud in that received version is correspondingly more mysterious: men fight, a prince legislates, and no one has shown the audience the linguistic machinery that made fighting the morning's default. Restore the duet, as modern editions and unexpurgated stages do, and the mystery dissolves into mechanism. The experiment has been run, across two centuries of reading, and its result supports the structural side of the dispute. The bawdy was cuttable only at the price of intelligibility, and a passage whose removal damages comprehension is, by definition, load-bearing.

One refinement of Kahn should be recorded before the section closes, in fairness to the glossarial tradition she is set against. The structural reading depends at every step on the recovered senses: without Partridge's lexicon and the editors' notes, the chain cannot be followed, and the feud's grammar remains a rumour. The two traditions are therefore not equals competing for one chair. Glossing is the instrument; structure is the finding. Where the glossarial habit errs is in stopping at the instrument, cataloguing the senses as curiosities of a bawdier age and leaving the escalation they perform unexamined. The close reading offered here has tried to keep the accounts straight: every sense in the brawl gloss below is the editors' recovered property, and the argument about what the senses do in sequence is the article's own verdict, reached on the evidence of the scene's construction and offered for testing against the table that follows.

The verdict also changes how the lovers' scenes should be heard, and with that consequence the close reading can rest its case. Romeo and Juliet conduct their courtship in the most fastidiously beautiful verse in the play, a shared sonnet, a balcony exchange of light imagery, as if desire could be kept clean by being kept poetic. The servants' scene exists to show what the poetry is up against: a vernacular in which desire and violence were never separated in the first place, spoken by the world the lovers inhabit and must out-talk to survive. Every time the tragedy fuses the registers again, in Mercutio's bawdy dying, in Juliet's longing conflating bride-bed and grave, in Romeo's purchase of poison as a cordial, the fusion is a return to the servants' grammar rather than a new invention. The opening is the tragedy's dictionary. The lovers spend five acts trying to write a different language, and the play's sorrow is that the dictionary outlives them, in the mouths of men who will be punning over other graves.

A last consequence follows for the order in which the play asks to be read. Because the servants speak first, every subsequent scene is heard against their register: the fathers' rage is the duet with property behind it, Tybalt's punctilio is the duet with training behind it, Mercutio's wit is the duet with genius behind it and the same destination in view. Criticism that begins with the lovers and treats the opening as an overture to be hurried through reverses the play's own pedagogy. The text teaches its audience at the bottom of the house, in prose, with jokes, and then submits that audience to examination in verse for five acts. The verdict of this close reading is therefore also a reading instruction: start where the play starts, with two servants and a pun about coal, and the tragedy that follows will be legible as what it is, the story of a language of violence that a pair of lovers briefly, beautifully, and unsuccessfully tried to speak otherwise.

Neither man could reach the drawn sword alone at this speed. The feud, in the scene's mechanics, is a duet.

The table also serves as an audit instrument for performances and editions alike. A production that trims the servants' duet for pace can be measured against the rows: remove the wall sequence and the maidenheads threat loses its premise; remove the fish and flesh exchange and the drawing of the tool loses its verbal preparation; compress the deniability business and the ignition arrives unearned, as if the brawl were an accident of temper rather than a procedure completed. Directors cut the opening more often than any comparable stretch of the play's first act, and some cuttings are defensible as theatre, but the gloss fixes what each cutting costs in argument. The scene is the play's most expendable-looking fifteen minutes and its least expendable logic, and the table exists so that the difference can be checked rather than asserted.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does Sampson mean by "I strike quickly, being moved"?

Sampson means that once provoked he is fast to hit back, and the line is a boast about reflexes rather than judgement. Gregory instantly audits the claim by separating its halves: being moved is easy, since a dog of the rival house can do it, while striking quickly is the part Sampson has never demonstrated. The distinction matters because the whole exchange runs on it. Sampson's courage exists in the conditional tense, always ready in principle and always deferred in practice, until the final minutes when an audience of kinsmen and a two-word accusation make performance unavoidable. The line is the first entry in the scene's ledger of unspent bravery.

### Q: Why does Gregory keep deflating Sampson's boasts instead of joining them?

Gregory deflates the boasts because puncturing them is his function in the duet and his pleasure in the morning. Each correction, that Sampson is not quickly moved, that the moved man runs away, that Sampson is happily not fish, displays Gregory's quicker wit and keeps the game going, since a boast accepted would end the conversation. Yet the deflation is not dissent. Gregory never questions the feud, the errand, or the destination, only Sampson's credentials for the journey. When the moment arrives, it is Gregory who supplies the frown, the whisper, and the order implicit in naming the swashing blow. The auditor turns out to be the engineer, and his mockery was quality control on the provocation all along.

### Q: What does "take the wall" mean in the street code of the scene?

Taking the wall meant keeping the side of the street nearest the houses, which stayed cleaner, and forcing the other party toward the central channel where refuse ran. In a crowded street the gesture was a public ranking: the man at the wall had precedence, and the man in the gutter had been placed. Sampson's promise to take the wall of any man or maid of Montague's therefore claims precedence over the entire rival house in every chance encounter, a claim so total that it converts an ordinary walk into a sequence of duels of position. The proverb Gregory answers with, that the weakest goes to the wall, shows the same pavement serving as the place of humiliation for whoever is thrust against it.

### Q: Why does Sampson say the weakest go to the wall?

Sampson accepts Gregory's proverb because it hands him the premise his threat requires. If the wall is where the weak are thrust, and women are, in his borrowed phrase, the weaker vessels, then women are thrust to the wall by definition, and his programme of pushing Montague's men from the wall and thrusting the maids to it can pose as mere enforcement of the natural order. The reasoning is a parody syllogism, and the scene knows it: a street proverb about precedence is made to authorize first combat and then sexual threat, each step following with an air of logic from the last. Watching a premise about pavement become a warrant for violence against women is the scene's method in miniature.

### Q: Why does Sampson promise to cut off the maids' heads before Gregory questions him?

The beheading comes first because Sampson is still speaking the language of tyrant fantasy, in which the maids figure as the spoils of a victory over their men. His sequence is explicit: fight the men, then be civil with the maids, then cut off their heads. Gregory's interruption, "The heads of the maids?", is not shock but a prompt, an invitation to land the pun the speech has been circling. Sampson obliges by splitting heads into heads and maidenheads, and the exchange reveals how collaborative the obscenity is. The ugliest line in the scene is a joint production, set up by one man's question and paid off by the other's gloss, which is how the feud itself will operate at every scale.

### Q: What does Sampson mean by calling himself "a pretty piece of flesh"?

The phrase is Sampson's self-advertisement as a desirable man, offered as proof that the Montague maids will feel his power in the sense the pun requires. Flesh in the period's slang carried the body's appetites with it, and pretty piece is the language of the market, a cut of meat appraised for quality. The boast is revealing precisely because it is deluded in form: Sampson prices himself like goods while imagining himself a tyrant, and Gregory's fish joke answers in the same market language, downgrading the merchandise to poor John, the cheapest dried hake on the stall. Beneath the comedy sits the scene's bleakest transaction, in which women are spoils, men are cuts of flesh, and everyone in the street is priced.

### Q: What is the "poor John" joke in Gregory's answer about fish?

Poor John was dried hake, a fish so cheap and common that its name became proverbial for anything worthless, and Gregory's joke runs that if Sampson's celebrated flesh were fish flesh, it would be the lowest grade on the stall. The gag caps the maidenheads sequence by refusing Sampson even the dignity of his own bawdy: his sexual menace is reclassified as bargain stock. It also completes a small pattern in the scene's imagery, in which the servants keep appraising human beings as commodities, coal, flesh, fish, while imagining they are talking about honour. Gregory then pivots in the same breath to "Draw thy tool!", and the market closes. The next valuation in the scene will be conducted with swords.

### Q: What does "draw thy tool" mean when Gregory says it to Sampson?

On the surface it is an order to draw a sword, given because two Montague servants have come into view. Tool was the period's ordinary word for an implement and a ready slang word for the weapon a man carried, and the editions gloss its second, bawdy application as well, which the preceding thirty lines have thoroughly prepared. The line is the hinge of the scene because the registers fuse in it: the word that ends the punning contest is itself a pun, and the action it commands is the action the puns have been rehearsing. Gregory does not step out of the game to start the fight. The fight is the game's next move, announced in the game's own vocabulary.

### Q: Why do Sampson and Gregory want the Montague servants to begin the fight?

Beginning carried legal consequences, and the pair know it. Sampson states the strategy outright: take the law of their sides and let the others begin, so that the brawl, when the Prince's officers reconstruct it, will be a Montague aggression answered by Capulet servants. The wish exposes the pair's real position under the bragging. They are employees with something to lose, a place, a livery, a neck that Gregory's collar joke has already measured for the noose, and their honour must be exercised inside a budget of risk. The feud's street violence is therefore premeditated in form even when spontaneous in timing: provocation is engineered, in insult and gesture, precisely so that the first formal move belongs to the other side.

### Q: Why does Gregory plan only to frown as he passes the Montague servants?

The frown is a probe, the cheapest provocation in the pair's arsenal. Gregory will frown as he passes and let the Montagues take it as they list, which means the gesture costs nothing, commits him to nothing, and can be denied as a trick of the light if challenged. Its purpose is reconnaissance: if a frown draws a response, the quarrel can begin with the law arranged as Sampson requires, and if it does not, the thumb remains in reserve. The detail is easy to pass over and worth pausing on, because it shows the servants operating a graduated scale of insult with professional care. Verona's feud is not a brawl waiting to happen. It is a procedure, and its lowest employees know the procedure by heart.

### Q: What does Sampson's whispered question about the law reveal about the pair?

The aside, "Is the law of our side if I say ay?", reveals that Sampson's nerve fails at the exact moment his insult succeeds. Abram has taken the bait and asked the direct question, and Sampson, before answering, must consult Gregory on the legal position, in a whisper, in front of the enemy. The consultation strips the scene's pretence away: the man who would cut off heads and thrust maids to the wall cannot confirm a thumb-bite without advice. Gregory's one-word answer, "No", sends him into the famous retreat through grammar. The aside also completes the pair's portrait as the feud's true clerks, men whose deepest fluency is not in fighting but in the paperwork of deniability that fighting requires.

### Q: Why does Sampson deny biting his thumb at Abram while admitting that he bites his thumb?

The denial exploits the smallest available distinction, between performing a gesture and aiming it. Sampson concedes the act, "I do bite my thumb, sir", and denies the address, "I do not bite my thumb at you, sir", relocating the insult from intention to anatomy. The dodge is the punning habit applied to self-defence: one phrase, two senses, and the speaker sheltering in the harmless one. It nearly works, because Abram cannot prove aim, and the quarrel has to be restarted by other means. Within the exchange's design, the moment is the payoff of everything before it. A scene that began by teaching the audience that words carry double meanings ends its first movement by showing a man acquitting himself with one.

### Q: Why does Gregory whisper "Say 'better'" to Sampson?

Gregory's whisper is a calculation about witnesses. Sampson's claim that he serves as good a man as Abram has stalled at "No better" and a helpless "Well, sir", but a kinsman of Capulet is now approaching, and a claim made before family must be upgraded or the servants lose face in front of the house itself. "Say 'better'" therefore measures courage by audience with unusual honesty: the same comparison that was too dangerous to press against two Montagues becomes compulsory when a Capulet kinsman can hear it fail. Gregory, the scene's auditor, knows that the feud runs on spectatorship. Insults in this world are not exchanges between enemies. They are performances for one's own side, priced by who is watching.

### Q: What does Abram's "You lie" do to the quarrel?

It ends the era of deniability. Every provocation before it was built to be withdrawn, the frown, the gesture, the comparison, each with an innocent sense in reserve, but "You lie" is a direct charge of falsehood against a named claim, and the period's code of honour treated the lie as the one insult that could not be answered with words. Sampson's response accepts the new terms instantly: swords, and an appeal to manhood as the authority for drawing them. The two words also expose the machinery that preceded them, since the whole graduated procedure of insult existed to arrive exactly here, at a charge the other side must either swallow in disgrace or answer in steel. Abram, provoked past patience, supplies it, and the law's side is forgotten.

### Q: What is a "swashing blow," and why does Gregory name it as the swords come out?

A swashing blow was a showy, slashing stroke, the kind that advertises itself with noise against a buckler, more flourish than fence. Gregory's "remember thy swashing blow" is therefore part memory, part stage direction, part tease: Sampson has evidently described or rehearsed such a stroke before, and his friend calls it in like a debt at the moment of performance. The line tells the audience how to watch the fight that follows, as the execution of boasts they have spent seventy lines hearing. It also lands the scene's favourite joke one last time. Even at the threshold of real danger, Gregory's contribution is editorial, a footnote on Sampson's technique, and the auditor remains an auditor to the end.

### Q: Why do modern editions need so many explanatory notes for the first forty lines?

The opening exchange is the most densely punned passage in the play, and its puns run on materials that have expired: proverbs about coal-carriers, the street geography of wall and gutter, market slang for cheap fish, a gesture of contempt, and a sexual vocabulary that polite centuries stopped printing. A reader meeting the lines unglossed sees two servants chatting and then, apparently from nowhere, fighting. The notes restore the machinery, showing that the fight was assembled step by step inside the jokes. Editors from the Arden and Oxford traditions onward have treated the passage as a small anthology of the period's spoken aggression, and the density of annotation is itself evidence for the close reading: the text here is not thin banter but compressed social documentation.

### Q: How many lines of banter pass before the Montague servants enter the scene?

Roughly the first forty lines of the scene belong to Sampson and Gregory alone, a duet long enough to run the full chain from coals to maidenheads before Abram and Balthasar enter and the talk must become tactics. Editions differ by a line or two in where they mark the entrance, but the proportion is stable and significant: the playwright gives the feud's ignition a longer fuse than most productions allow it. The delay is the design. An audience that has watched forty lines of wordplay has been taught how to listen to it, has laughed at each link of the chain, and has therefore participated in the escalation it is about to watch turn earnest.

### Q: Why does Sampson call a Montague servant "a dog of that house"?

The phrase measures how far the feud's threshold of provocation has fallen. Sampson does not require Montague, a son of Montague, or even a man of Montague to move him; a dog of the house suffices, any creature marked with the wrong allegiance. The insult works two ways at once: it dehumanizes the enemy below the rank of servant, and it confesses the poverty of Sampson's own case, since a cause that a dog can trigger is not a cause but a reflex. Gregory's chain of move, stir, and stand builds directly on the word, turning Sampson's reflex into the scene's little treatise on how allegiance manufactures motion. The dog never appears. It does not need to. It has already done its work by entering the sentence.

### Q: Do Sampson and Gregory appear again after Act 1 Scene 1?

They do not. The pair who fire the play's first action vanish from it entirely once the brawl they ignited has drawn in their betters, and no later scene returns to them, rewards them, punishes them, or mentions their names. Other Capulet servants appear, a servingman with a guest list, cooks and attendants in the wedding bustle, but Sampson and Gregory are spent in the spending of their scene. The disappearance is part of the design the close reading has traced. The feud's instruments are disposable because the system that uses them is not: it will manufacture replacements at every level, from servingmen to heirs, and the tragedy closes over the pair without a ripple, which is the coldest fact their comedy carries.
