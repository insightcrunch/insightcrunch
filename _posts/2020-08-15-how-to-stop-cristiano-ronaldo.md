The plan for how to stop Cristiano Ronaldo begins with a decision that feels wrong to almost every defender who first hears it: you do not try to beat him at the two things he does best. You do not out-jump him, and you do not out-sprint his late arrival into the box. Both are contests he wins often enough that building a defensive scheme around winning them is a scheme built on a coin toss. The coherent answer sits one pass earlier, at the moment the ball is about to be delivered toward him, because a forward who scores from crosses, cutbacks and second balls is only as dangerous as the service that reaches those zones. Cut the quality of that service and both of his finishing systems lose their fuel at once.

![How to stop Cristiano Ronaldo, a defensive counter-plan - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-56.webp)

This is a counter blueprint, so it commits to a plan and states plainly what the plan concedes. It is built for the coach who has to prepare a back line, for the centre-back who has to mark a late runner without a clear sight of him, for the full-back and winger who have to treat pressing the crosser as a defensive duty rather than an attacking luxury, and for the goalkeeper who has to choose a starting position before a single ball is struck. It also speaks to the analyst who has to work out, from a couple of clips, which version of this forward a side is about to face, because the answer changes which half of the plan carries the load. His tactical profile, the mechanism behind every threat described here, is set out in full at [Cristiano Ronaldo: the complete profile](/2020/08/01/cristiano-ronaldo-tactical-profile/), and the limitations this plan leans on are anatomised at [Cristiano Ronaldo's weaknesses explained](/2020/09/01/cristiano-ronaldo-weaknesses-explained/). This article owns the counter-plan.

## What is the plan to stop Cristiano Ronaldo?

### How do you stop Cristiano Ronaldo in one sentence?

You attack the delivery, not the finisher. Ronaldo threatens in the air and by arriving late into the box, and the standard defensive settings for those two threats contradict each other, so the only plan that answers both at once is one that reduces the quality of the ball reaching him. Deny the service and both finishing systems starve.

That single sentence hides a structural claim that runs through everything below, and it deserves a name so the plan can refer back to it consistently. Call it the two-system problem. A forward who is genuinely elite in the air and genuinely elite arriving late into the penalty area asks a defence to run two different systems at the same time, and the natural settings of those two systems pull in opposite directions. The aerial system wants a defender goal-side and tight, feet set, ready to attack the ball at its highest point. The late-arrival system wants a defender who is not tight, who is instead scanning the space behind and to the side, ready to travel with a runner who begins outside his field of vision. Set your line for one and you loosen the other. Sit tight to win the header and the late run destroys you from a yard deeper. Drop off to track the run and you gift a free contact on the cross. No single defender, however good, resolves that contradiction on his own, which is why the answer cannot be a marking assignment. It has to be a plan that removes the shared input both systems depend on: a clean, well-weighted delivery arriving at the moment his movement has bought him a half-yard.

That is the spine. Everything else in this blueprint hangs off it. The shape exists to let a defender pressure the crosser. The line height exists to shrink the window in which a late run can be timed. The individual assignment exists to buy the extra tenth of a second that turns a scoring contact into a defensive clearance. The goalkeeper's starting position exists to shrink the target the delivery is aiming for. Read in isolation each of these looks like a familiar defensive idea. Assembled around the two-system problem, they become a plan that has a chance against a forward most sides simply hope has an off day.

It matters to say at the outset what the plan gives up, because a blueprint that pretends to concede nothing is selling a fantasy. This plan accepts that Ronaldo will get chances. He is too good and his movement is too well grooved for any scheme to reduce him to nothing across a full contest. What the plan does is change the character of the chances he gets. It trades the high-value chance, the free header six yards out or the tap-in at the far post after a clean cutback, for the low-value chance, the contested effort under pressure, the shot from distance, the header made while turning and off balance. Over ninety minutes that trade is the difference between a forward who scores and a forward who is contained. The plan does not stop him touching the ball. It changes what happens when he does.

## The shape and line height that frame the plan

Before any individual is asked to mark anyone, the team shape has to make delivery-denial possible, because a shape that leaves crossers and passers unpressured has already lost the sequence before the box is even loaded. The two most common defensive shapes against a strong box forward both fail here for the same underlying reason, and understanding why is the fastest way to see what the plan actually requires.

The first common answer is the deep, compact block. The instinct is understandable. A box occupier lives in the penalty area, so a defence packs the penalty area, everyone goal-side, numbers everywhere. The problem is that a deep block concentrates exactly the traffic his movement is designed to exploit. Ronaldo's late arrival works by finding the seam between two markers who are watching the ball, and a deep block manufactures those seams by the dozen: bodies packed tight means sightlines are blocked, means a runner starting from the edge of the crowd is invisible until he is already in front of goal. The deep block also hands the opposition a free crossing platform, because a defence sitting on its own eighteen-yard line cannot get pressure to a wide player thirty yards up the pitch. You end up defending the box perfectly and losing the game, because the box was never the place the sequence was won or lost.

The second common answer is the very high line, squeezing the pitch to keep play far from goal. Against many forwards that works. Against this one it invites the version of him that is hardest to defend, because a high line offers space in behind for a run to be timed into, and a forward who arrives late is precisely the forward who punishes a line that steps up a beat too early or a beat too late. A high line also asks defenders to make repeated recovery sprints while facing their own goal, which is the exact scenario in which turning and travelling with a late runner is hardest.

The plan sits between these two, and it sits there deliberately. It uses a mid-block with a line set around the halfway point of the defensive third when the ball is central, dropping only as the ball advances toward the box and only as far as the situation demands. The point of the mid-block is not compactness for its own sake. It is that a mid-block keeps the wide players close enough to the crossing zones to pressure a delivery, while keeping the back line high enough that the space behind is not a runway. The line breathes: it steps up when the ball is under pressure and drops when the ball is about to be delivered, and the timing of that breathing is one of the two or three things the plan has to rehearse until it is automatic.

Line height also carries a message the community debate often gets wrong, so it is worth stating directly. A common claim is that you should sit deep because he will punish any space in behind. That claim mistakes one version of the forward for all three. The wide-dribbler version wanted space to run into off the flank, and against that version a slightly deeper, more contained line made sense. The box-occupier version does not run in behind from wide; he arrives into the box from a starting position the defence can see if it is not packed too tight to see anything. Against the box occupier, sitting deep is the mistake, not the safeguard. Which version a side faces decides how the line is set, and reading the version correctly is the first job of preparation, covered below in the section on identifying the threat from two clips.

## The three versions and how the plan re-weights

The counter-plan is one spine with three settings, because this forward has been three distinct threats across his career and the same defence would prepare differently for each. The profile article traces the migration in full; here the concern is narrower and entirely practical, which is how the weighting of the plan shifts once a coach knows which of the three he is about to meet. The diagnostic itself, how to read the version from a couple of clips, is handled near the end for the analyst. This section is for the coach who already knows the version and has to decide where to put his resources.

Against the wide-dribbler version, the one whose value came from receiving on the flank and beating a full-back, the plan tilts away from the box and toward the carry. The pressing trigger fires earlier and further from goal, because the danger is the ball at his feet in space rather than the ball arriving to his head. The full-back defending him needs help sooner, and the cover behind is oriented to the cut inside onto the stronger foot. The aerial and late-run systems are still present, since even the dribbler version got into the box, but they are secondary, and a coach who over-invests in far-post cover against a version that lives on the touchline has spent his resources in the wrong half of the pitch. The one-on-one containment craft that this version demands, the jockeying and the delay against a wide carrier, is a general skill the plan borrows rather than owns.

Against the wide-forward version, the one whose runs began outside the defensive structure and finished inside it, the plan is at its most demanding, because this is the version that asks both systems to run at full intensity at once. He is wide enough to threaten a carry and a delivery, and central enough by the time the ball arrives to threaten the header and the late run. This is the version the whole blueprint is calibrated for, and there is no shortcut through it. The mid-block, the crosser pressure, the far-side reference and the cutback screen all have to work together, because dropping any one of them opens a lane this version is built to find. A coach facing this version is facing the hardest homework, and the plan's answer is that every link in the chain has to hold.

Against the box-occupier version, the one who lives inside the width of the six-yard box and does his work in short bursts, the plan tilts fully toward delivery-denial and away from anything happening outside the area. He barely touches the ball beyond the box, so pressing him is pointless and pressing his supply is everything. The whole weight of the plan moves to the wide areas and the cutback pocket, on the logic that a box occupier with no service is a box occupier with nothing to finish. The far-side reference becomes non-negotiable, because his movement is now almost entirely about finding the seam between markers, and the marker's scanning discipline is the difference between seeing the run and discovering it. Against this version a coach can, if anything, relax his concern about the space in behind, because the box occupier does not run in behind from wide; he arrives into a box the defence can see if it has not packed itself too tight to see anything.

The value of naming the three settings is that it stops a coach from preparing for the wrong forward. A defence drilled all week to contain a wide dribbler will be pulled apart by a box occupier, and a defence set up to deny service to a box occupier will be carried past by a dribbler in space. The spine does not change, but the dial does, and turning the dial to the right setting is the first decision of the match-week plan.

## Which defender profile fits, and why height is not the answer

### What kind of centre-back handles Cristiano Ronaldo best?

The best marker is not the tallest centre-back. It is the defender who can turn and travel with a late runner while keeping the ball in view, because the aerial duel is decided by who reaches the arrival point in balance, not by who is taller. A shorter defender with better position and momentum beats a taller one flat-footed.

This is the single most counter-intuitive part of the plan, and it is the part most sides get wrong, so it needs the mechanism spelled out. The instinct is to assign the biggest centre-back, sit him on Ronaldo, and consider the aerial problem solved. It is not solved. Height in isolation loses the duel more often than it wins it, because the aerial contest against this forward is not a standing jump between two set players. It is an approach-and-elevation contest, and the elevation is only the last part of it.

Watch how the header actually happens. Ronaldo does not stand next to his marker and wait for the ball. He starts a stride or two back, times a curved run so that he meets the delivery while moving forward and upward, and arrives at the contact point with momentum the defender does not have. That forward momentum is what generates the hang time and the power, and it is bought in the approach, not the leap. A defender who is taller but static is jumping from a standstill against a forward who is jumping from a run-up. The taller man loses because he is not moving, and the ball is met above him by someone travelling into it.

The defender who wins that duel more often is the one who can do two things. First, he can turn early, which means he reads the run developing behind or beside him and pivots his body to face the same direction as the runner before the ball is struck, so that he is travelling forward into the contact rather than backpedalling into it. Second, he can travel, which means he has the acceleration to match the run-up over the first three or four yards so that he arrives at the contact point in balance, feet under him, able to attack the ball or at least to occupy the space and force a contest. Turn early and travel: those are the two verbs, and neither of them is about height.

There is a body-position detail inside this that a coach can teach directly. The losing defensive posture is square-on and flat-footed, watching the ball come in over his shoulder, which forces a jump from a standstill and a jump that is late because he only reacts once the ball is above him. The winning posture is half-turned, one shoulder pointed toward the likely arrival zone, weight already shifting forward, so that the first movement is a stride into the run rather than a backpedal. The cue that triggers the turn is not the ball, it is the runner. A defender who watches the ball through the delivery is always a beat late, because the run tells you where the contest will be long before the ball does. This is the near-universal error against him, and it recurs in the mistakes section for every audience: watching the ball, not the runner.

None of this means height is worthless. All else equal, a taller defender who can also turn and travel is preferable, because he adds a genuine aerial contest at the top of the arc to the positional advantage. The point is that the ordering matters. Mobility and reading are the primary requirement and height is the secondary bonus, not the other way round. A coach choosing between a tall, heavy centre-back who cannot turn and a shorter, quicker one who can should pick the quicker one and accept the occasional lost header at the top of the jump, because that defender wins the far more numerous contests that are decided by who arrives in balance.

The full-back drawn into this duel deserves a word too, because a common question is whether a full-back should ever be left to defend Ronaldo alone. The short answer is that a full-back should never be the last line against him in the air, but a full-back with the right profile is often the better first responder to the run, precisely because full-backs are selected for the mobility that centre-backs are not always required to have. The plan uses that: it is comfortable letting a mobile full-back pick up the late run in wide-central areas, provided the cover behind is organised, which the collective triggers section handles next.

## The collective triggers and cover behaviour

An individual assignment, however well profiled, fails without the collective behaviour that supports it, and the collective behaviour against this forward has one non-negotiable at its centre: pressing the crosser is a defensive act, and it is everyone's job, not just the defenders'. This is where the wide players earn their place in the plan, and where a lot of teams simply do not do the work.

Start with the trigger. The moment the ball travels to a wide area from which a delivery is possible, the nearest pressing player, usually the winger or the full-back on that side, has to close the crosser's crossing foot, not the man. Closing the man means running at him and letting him roll away or cut inside. Closing the crossing foot means approaching at an angle that takes the natural delivery away, forcing the crosser either to check back, which buys the defence time to set, or to deliver early and off-balance, which is exactly the low-quality service the plan is designed to produce. The difference between those two approaches is a body angle of maybe twenty degrees, and it is the difference between a defence that pressures the delivery and a defence that merely arrives near the delivery after it has been struck.

The pressing player has to understand what he is buying. He is almost never going to block the cross outright, and chasing the block is how wingers get turned and full-backs get isolated. What he is buying is degradation. A crosser under genuine pressure delivers a ball with less pace, less curve, or a fraction late, and any of those three makes the finisher's job harder. A ball that hangs gives the goalkeeper time to claim and the defender time to set his feet. A ball delivered a fraction early arrives before the run has bought its half-yard. The pressing player's job is to make the delivery worse, and worse is often enough.

Behind the pressure comes the cover, and the cover behaviour is where the two-system problem gets reconciled in practice. While one player pressures the crosser, the near-side centre-back holds the front-post zone and the far-side centre-back holds the far-post reference, and crucially the far-side defender does not ball-watch. The far post is where a forward who threatens both posts does his quietest damage, gliding in behind a back line whose eyes have all followed the ball to the near side. The far-side defender's job is to keep a permanent reference on the space at the back post and on any runner drifting into it, even, especially, when the ball is nowhere near him. A far-side defender who watches the ball has already conceded the back-post header; a far-side defender who watches the space and the runner has a chance.

The midfield's job in the cover scheme is the screen. A defensive midfielder dropping into the cutback zone, the pocket at the top of the six-yard box's outer edge from which a pulled-back ball is finished, removes the single most dangerous delivery in the whole sequence. The cutback is more dangerous than the cross because it arrives to a forward facing goal with the defence facing the wrong way, and a screening midfielder who occupies that pocket forces the wide player to choose a lower-percentage delivery instead. This is unglamorous defending and it is often the most valuable job on the pitch in this plan, because it takes away the delivery Ronaldo finishes most cleanly.

Put the pieces together and the collective picture is a chain of denial: the crosser is pressured so the delivery degrades, the cutback pocket is screened so the best delivery is unavailable, the near post is held so the flick is contested, and the far post is watched so the quiet arrival is seen. Each link buys a fraction, and the fractions compound. The forward who was going to meet a clean ball in balance now meets a worse ball, later, in a more crowded space, with a defender who turned early already travelling into the contest. That is the plan working, and none of it required winning a jump.

## The delivery-denial ladder

The plan needs a single reference a coach can hold in his hand and a defence can rehearse against, and that reference is the delivery-denial ladder. It takes every type of service that reaches this forward, orders them, and states for each one the primary way to deny it, the fallback assignment inside the box if denial fails, the goalkeeper's starting position, and the concession the plan deliberately accepts. This is the findable artifact of the counter-plan, and it is the one table in the article.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wide cross from deep | Press the crosser's delivery foot; force early or checked ball | Near-side centre-back attacks front post, marker turns and travels with the run | On or just off the six-yard line, favouring near post | Contested header away from goal |
| Whipped cross from advanced wide area | Winger and full-back double the delivery lane; deny the touchline base | Marker travels goal-side of the run; far-side defender holds back post | Two yards off line, central, ready to claim the flat ball | Corner or throw conceded to prevent the clean strike |
| Cutback from the byline | Screening midfielder occupies the cutback pocket; full-back forces the byline, not the middle | Midfielder blocks the pull-back lane; centre-back steps to the edge of the six | Set for the low near-post shot, weight forward | Ball worked back to a wider, lower-percentage angle |
| Through ball for a late run | Line steps to compress the run's timing; marker keeps the runner in peripheral view | Marker travels shoulder-to-shoulder; cover defender pinches inside | Slightly higher, ready to sweep the space behind | A shot from a wide or narrow angle rather than central |
| Second ball after a knock-down | Screen the drop zone; nearest midfielder attacks the loose ball | First defender to the ball clears the line, second holds shape | Off line and vocal, commanding the six-yard box | A cleared ball retained in a wide area |

| Shot from distance | Deny the set: close the shooting foot, force it onto the weaker side | Nearest defender blocks the lane, does not dive in | Set and square, expecting power and dip | A blocked or held effort from range rather than a clean strike |

The ladder is read top to bottom as a hierarchy of danger for a box occupier, and it repays study because it makes the plan's logic legible at a glance. The primary denial column is almost always about pressure on the ball before it is delivered, which is the spine restated line by line. The box assignment column is the fallback, what the defence does in the seconds after denial has failed, and it is deliberately the second column, not the first, because a plan that starts by organising the box has already accepted the delivery it should have been trying to spoil. The goalkeeper column changes materially by service type, which is the point the goalkeeping section develops, because a keeper who sets the same starting position for a whipped cross and a shot from distance is wrong for at least one of them. And the concession column is the honesty of the plan: for every service type there is a specific, named thing the defence agrees to give up, because a plan that tries to deny everything denies nothing.

A coach building a match-week plan around this ladder can save it, annotate it against the specific crossers and set-piece takers in the opposition, and turn it into the session and the team meeting. ## The plan phase by phase

A blueprint is only useful if it sequences, so here is the plan as it unfolds across a single defensive sequence, from the moment the opposition has the ball in a building position to the moment the danger clears. Read it as a narration, because that is how a defence experiences it.

The sequence begins with the ball in the opposition's build-up, wide of the centre, not yet in a crossing position. In this phase the plan is patient. The line holds its mid-block height, the wide players stay connected to their full-backs, and nobody chases. The one active job in this phase belongs to the far-side defender and the screening midfielder, who are already establishing their references: the far-side defender clocks where the forward is and where the back-post space is, and the midfielder positions to protect the cutback pocket. The work that wins the sequence is often done here, quietly, before the ball has gone anywhere dangerous, because the defenders who will have to react in two seconds are already oriented.

The sequence advances when the ball travels to an advanced wide area from which a delivery is live. This is the trigger phase, and it is fast. The nearest pressing player closes the crossing foot at the angle that takes the natural delivery away. The full-back on that side either supports the press or tucks in to cover, depending on which of them is pressing. The line makes its small adjustment, stepping to compress the timing of any run being prepared, and the marker on the forward turns his body half-open, one shoulder to the arrival zone, eyes on the runner rather than the ball. The goalkeeper takes the starting position the ladder prescribes for the delivery type that is coming. Everything set in this half-second is what the next half-second is judged against.

The sequence peaks at the delivery. If the press has worked, the ball is worse than the crosser wanted, and the box wins its contest more easily. If the press has not worked and a clean ball comes in, the box assignments take over: near-side defender attacks the front post, marker travels goal-side and into the run, far-side defender holds the back post and does not follow the ball across, screening midfielder covers the pull-back. The defence's aim in this instant is not to win the ball cleanly, though it will try; it is to force the contact to be contested, late, or off balance, because a contested contact is a defensive victory even when it is not a clean clearance.

The sequence resolves in the aftermath, and the aftermath is where many otherwise sound defensive plans fall apart. A half-cleared ball, a knock-down, a rebound off the goalkeeper: these second-ball moments are where a box occupier scores his cheapest goals, because the defence relaxes the instant the first ball is dealt with. The plan does not relax. The screening midfielder and the nearest defender attack the drop zone, the first man to the loose ball clears it out rather than trying to play, and the line steps out together to reset the offside reference and push the danger back up the pitch. Only when the ball is retained in a wide or deep area, away from goal, does the sequence count as resolved, and only then does the defence exhale.

That is one sequence. A match is dozens of them, and the discipline the plan demands is that every sequence is defended with the same attention as the first, because tracker fatigue and concentration lapses are how a contained forward becomes a scorer in the final twenty minutes. The plan's demand on a defence is less about brilliance in any single moment than about refusing to switch off across ninety of them.

## Defending the late run: the vision problem

The run threat and the aerial threat need separate treatment, because they are separate problems with separate solutions, and collapsing them into one is how defences end up doing neither well. Take the run first.

The late arrival into the box is, at its heart, a vision problem for the defender, not a speed problem. Ronaldo does not typically beat a marker for pace over a long distance in this phase; he beats him by starting the run from a position the marker cannot see. He drifts to the edge of the defender's peripheral awareness, often a step behind and to the side, waits for the marker's eyes to be drawn to the ball, and then times a short, sharp run into the seam the ball-watching has opened. By the time the defender senses him, the half-yard is already bought and the contact is already coming. The defender was not slow. He was blind at the wrong moment.

The solution is a scanning discipline, and it can be coached like any other technical habit. The marker's default has to be a repeating check: ball, runner, ball, runner, a head on a swivel that never lets more than a second pass without relocating the man. Most defenders scan the ball constantly and the runner occasionally; the plan inverts that emphasis, because the ball's position is available in peripheral vision while the runner's position is not. The cue to prioritise the runner is proximity to a delivery: the closer the ball gets to a live crossing position, the more the marker's eyes belong on the man, because that is the window in which the late run will be launched.

Alongside scanning comes the far-side reference, which is the single most useful spatial habit against a forward who threatens both posts. The marker, and the whole back line, keep a fixed mental reference on the far-post zone, so that a runner drifting toward it is picked up early rather than discovered at the moment of contact. The far-side reference is what turns the back-post arrival from an unstoppable free header into a contested one, because the defender who has kept the reference is already goal-side and already moving when the ball is delivered, rather than turning to find the runner gone.

When the run is launched, the marker's job is to travel shoulder-to-shoulder and goal-side, not to try to intercept the run by cutting across it. Cutting across invites the forward to check and spin into the vacated space, which is exactly the movement he wants. Travelling goal-side and shoulder-to-shoulder keeps the defender between the runner and the goal, keeps him in balance, and forces the contact to happen with the defender's body already positioned to contest it. He may not win the header, but he denies the clean one, and denying the clean one is the plan's whole ambition in this phase.

There is a collective dimension to the run defence that the marker cannot supply alone, and it is the cover pinch. As the marker travels with the run, the nearest cover defender pinches inside to occupy the space the marker is vacating, so that a forward who does spin off the marker runs into a second body rather than into open goal. This is the arrival line the defence has to rehearse: the second defender's job is not to mark the forward but to be standing in the space the forward is trying to reach, a subtle difference that turns a one-on-one the forward often wins into a two-on-one he rarely does.

## The two-defender rule and the second defender's arrival line

The single most useful collective idea against a late-arriving forward is what this plan calls the two-defender rule, and it deserves precise treatment because the whole run defence turns on getting one defender's positioning exactly right. The rule states that the marker travels with the run while a second defender occupies the space the run is aiming for, and the value is entirely in the second defender's line, which is the detail most defences leave to chance.

Consider what happens with one defender. A marker tracking the late run shoulder-to-shoulder is doing everything right and still loses often, because the forward's craft is to check off the marker at the last instant and spin into the space the marker's momentum has committed him away from. A single defender, however good, is beaten by the check-and-spin because he cannot both travel with the run and occupy the space the run might vacate into. He has to choose, and whichever he chooses, the forward takes the other.

The two-defender rule removes the choice. As the marker travels with the run, the nearest cover defender pinches inside along a specific line, not toward the forward, but toward the space the forward is trying to reach. The distinction matters enormously. A cover defender who moves toward the forward is marking a man who is already marked, doubling up where there is no need and leaving a new gap behind him. A cover defender who moves toward the destination is standing, when the forward completes his spin, exactly where the forward wanted to arrive, so the spin ends not in space but in a second body. The forward is not beaten by being out-jumped or out-run; he is beaten by finding that the space he bought with his movement is already occupied.

Getting the arrival line right is a matter of timing and angle. The cover defender starts his pinch as the delivery is being prepared, not once it is struck, because a pinch that begins at the moment of contact arrives too late to occupy the destination. The angle of the pinch is inside and slightly goal-side, so the cover defender arrives between the forward's intended arrival point and the goal, which means even a forward who reaches the ball does so with a defender in front of him rather than behind. The rehearsal for this is the phase-of-play session, where the cover defender is coached to read the run developing and to move to the destination early, and the measure of success is that the forward's clever movement repeatedly ends in traffic rather than in space.

The two-defender rule also answers a question coaches ask about resources, which is whether committing a second defender to one forward is affordable. It is, because the second defender is not man-marking; he is holding a zone that the run threatens, and a zone can be held while still contributing to the wider shape. The cover defender who pinches to the destination is available to defend the next phase in a way a dedicated man-marker is not, which is why the two-defender rule is a first-choice behaviour while the man-mark is a last resort. One is a shared, sustainable job; the other stakes a defender's whole match on a single duel.

## Defending in the air: approach and elevation, not height

### How do you defend Cristiano Ronaldo in the air?

You defend the approach, not the leap. The aerial threat is generated in the run-up, where forward momentum creates the hang time and power, so a defender who turns early and travels into the contest arrives in balance and contests the ball, while a defender who backpedals and jumps from a standstill is beaten before he leaves the ground.

The aerial section builds on the profile mechanism, so it can move quickly to the defensive detail. The header against this forward is lost by defenders in a small number of repeatable ways, and naming them is the fastest route to fixing them.

The first failure is the standing jump. A defender who lets the forward approach from a run-up while he himself stays planted is conceding the contest before it starts, because the forward is converting horizontal momentum into vertical lift and the planted defender has no momentum to convert. The fix is the early turn already described: read the approach, half-open the body, and be moving into the contact so that the defender's own jump is loaded by a stride rather than launched from a stance.

The second failure is marking with height alone, the belief that a tall body next to the forward is sufficient. It is not, because the forward does not attack the ball from next to the defender; he attacks it from a curved run that arrives a yard in front of or behind the static marker, meeting the ball in space the marker is not occupying. The fix is spatial rather than physical: the defender has to occupy the arrival zone, the actual point in the air where the ball and the run will meet, rather than occupy a position next to the forward's starting point. Marking the man's feet loses; occupying the ball's destination contests.

The third failure is losing the far post. A back line that all attacks the near-side flight leaves the back post to a runner who has drifted there unseen, and the flighted ball to the back post is the delivery this forward finishes most emphatically because it arrives to a free header. The fix is the far-side reference already established, held permanently, so that the back post is never unattended even when every instinct is pulling eyes toward the ball at the near side.

The goalkeeper is a full participant in the aerial defence, not a spectator behind it, and his contribution changes the geometry of the contest. A goalkeeper who commands his six-yard box, who comes for the flighted ball he can reach and calls loudly for the one he cannot, shrinks the zone in which the forward can attack a clean header. The keeper's decision about whether to come has to be honest: he comes for the ball he can win and stays for the ball he cannot, and the worst outcome is the half-commitment that leaves both keeper and defender stranded. This is why the goalkeeper's starting position by delivery type, set out in the ladder, is not a detail but a load-bearing part of the plan.

## Near post or far post: reading his attacking movement

### How do you know whether Cristiano Ronaldo will attack the near or far post?

Read his first movement, not his position. A dart toward the near post is usually a genuine near-post attack or a decoy to drag a marker and open the far post; a delayed, drifting movement across the back line is almost always a far-post arrival. The tell is timing: early and sharp means near, late and gliding means far.

A forward who threatens both posts forces a defence to defend both, and defending both starts with reading which one a given movement is setting up, because a marker who guesses wrong is beaten before the ball arrives. The two attacking lines have different signatures, and a defender who learns the signatures buys himself the fraction of a second that decides the contest.

The near-post attack has an early, aggressive signature. The forward moves toward the near post ahead of the delivery, attacking the front of the six-yard box, and the danger is the flick or the powered header that beats the keeper at his near upper corner. The defensive read is that an early near-post movement demands an early response: the near-side defender attacks the same space, contesting the front-post contact so the flick is never clean, and the goalkeeper respects the near post in his positioning. The complication is the decoy, because the same early near-post dart is sometimes not an attack on the near post at all but a deliberate drag, pulling a marker toward the front post to vacate the far post for a teammate or for a delayed second movement. A defence that follows every near-post dart blindly is a defence that can be manipulated, which is why the far-side reference must be held even when the near post is being attacked.

The far-post attack has a late, gliding signature that is far harder to read and far more dangerous when missed. The forward does not sprint to the far post; he drifts, adjusting his position a yard at a time on the blind side of a marker, timing a delayed movement so that he arrives at the far post as the delivery does, unseen because the whole defence has followed the ball to the near side. The defensive read is the far-side reference already established as non-negotiable: the back-post zone is watched permanently, the far-side defender holds a reference on the space and on any drifting runner, and the delayed movement is picked up early rather than discovered at the contact. The observable cue for the coach and the analyst is the feet: a forward constantly making small positional adjustments on a marker's blind side is preparing a far-post arrival, and a defence warned that this is his habit defends the drift before it becomes a run.

The two lines are best understood as a single problem, which is that his movement is designed to make the defence commit to one post so he can attack the other. The counter is not to guess which post but to defend both structurally: the near post is contested by an assigned defender and respected by the keeper, the far post is held by a permanent reference, and no single delivery is allowed to pull the whole defence to one side. A defence that holds both references at once cannot be manipulated into vacating a post, which is the whole aim of his two-post movement, and denying that manipulation is worth as much as winning any individual duel.

## Defending the cutback: the most dangerous delivery

### Why is the cutback harder to defend than a cross?

Because it arrives to a forward facing goal while the defence faces the wrong way. A high cross forces a header, a low-percentage finish. A ball pulled back from the byline arrives flat to a runner who is set and facing an open goal, with defenders turned toward their own net. It is the delivery he finishes most cleanly.

The cutback earns its own section because it sits at the top of the danger hierarchy for a box occupier and because the community line that he only scores from crosses obscures exactly this. Many of his most routine finishes are not headers at all; they are first-time strikes from a ball pulled back into the pocket at the top of the six-yard box, taken while the goalkeeper is committed to the near post and the defenders are watching the byline. Understanding the geometry of the cutback is understanding where a large share of the danger actually lives.

The mechanism is worth slowing down. A wide attacker reaches the byline, and the defence's eyes and bodies rotate to track the ball toward the goal-line. That rotation is natural and it is fatal, because it leaves the pocket behind the front defenders unguarded and it points every defender the wrong way. The forward, meanwhile, has checked his run and settled into that pocket, facing the ball, facing the goal, waiting. The pull-back finds him in the one position from which finishing is easiest and defending is impossible, and the ball is in the net before the defenders have turned.

The counter is the screen, and the screen is a specific job for a specific player, usually the deepest midfielder. His task is to occupy the cutback pocket, to stand in the space the pulled-back ball wants to reach, so that the wide attacker looking up sees a body in the lane and has to choose a worse option. The screening midfielder does not chase the ball to the byline and he does not mark the forward; he holds the pocket, and holding the pocket is a discipline that runs against every instinct to go and help at the ball. The value of the job is invisible on the highlight reel, because a screened cutback is a cutback that never happens, and the goals it prevents are goals nobody sees.

Alongside the screen, the full-back's job is to force the byline rather than the middle. A wide attacker pushed toward the goal-line has a narrower angle and a worse platform for the cutback; a wide attacker allowed to cut back inside onto his stronger foot has the whole pocket to aim at. The full-back's body angle, showing the attacker down the line and away from the cutback angle, is a small technical choice that decides whether the most dangerous delivery is available at all. Combine the screen in the pocket with the full-back forcing the byline, and the cutback, the delivery that beats the plan's usual first line because it comes from too deep to press cleanly, is denied at its source.

There is a second-ball dimension to the cutback that completes the picture. Even a screened or blocked cutback can spill loose in the pocket, and the same forward who would have finished the clean pull-back is the quickest to the rebound. The screening midfielder's second job, once the cutback is denied, is to attack the loose ball in the pocket ahead of the forward, and the nearest defender's job is to clear it out of the area rather than trying to play from it. The pocket is dangerous on the first ball and on the second, and a plan that guards it once but relaxes for the rebound has guarded it for the less important of the two moments.

## Set pieces and dead balls

Set pieces deserve their own treatment because they strip away the delivery-denial spine: at a corner or a direct free-kick, the delivery is going to be clean by definition, the taker is unpressured, and the plan's usual first line of defence is unavailable. The counter has to shift from denying the service to denying the contact, and the mechanism for denying the contact is denying the run-up.

At an attacking corner, the forward's aerial threat is built, as ever, on the approach, so the defensive counter is to attack the approach before the ball is struck. A defender assigned to screen the run-up positions himself in the path the forward wants to take toward his arrival zone, so that the forward has to check, go around, or start from a standstill, any of which removes the momentum the header depends on. This is legal, physical, disciplined defending: it is not blocking with the arms or holding, it is occupying the space the run needs, the same principle as the cover pinch in open play applied to a dead-ball run-up. Combine the run-up screen with zonal markers holding the near and central arrival zones and a goalkeeper who attacks the flighted ball early, off his line, and the clean contact the corner is designed to produce becomes a contested flick instead.

At a direct free-kick in shooting range, the technical discussion stays technical, as the plan requires. The striking threat is real and it is generated by a specific mechanic: a firm strike with minimal spin that carries pace and dips late, aimed most often at the near-half top corner or dipping over the wall. The defensive answer is a wall set to protect the near half of the goal, denying the dipping near-post angle that the strike favours, with the goalkeeper positioned to cover the far and top-far quadrant that the wall leaves open. The wall's job is to make the taker choose the harder half of the goal, and the goalkeeper's job is to cover that harder half, and the concession the plan accepts is the perfectly struck ball that beats the wall to the far top corner, because that ball is unstoppable often enough that trying to cover it as well leaves a bigger door open elsewhere. The generalised craft of defending elite dead-ball takers, the wall geometry and the decision tree in full, belongs to [defending elite set-piece takers](/2030/06/01/defending-elite-set-piece-takers/), and a coach preparing specifically for a free-kick specialist should read the counter there alongside this section.

The penalty question comes up in every discussion of this forward, usually as a claim that his record is distorted by spot-kicks, and the plan treats it the only way it can be treated honestly: as a technical matter, not a statistical grievance. A penalty is a separate contest with its own preparation, the goalkeeper's homework on the taker's habits and the taker's method under pressure, and it sits outside the run-of-play counter-plan entirely. What the plan can say is that the best way to defend a penalty is to concede fewer of them, which loops back to the box-defence discipline that avoids the rushed challenge and the arm-in-the-face contact that gift spot-kicks in the first place. Beyond that, the penalty is the goalkeeper's duel, and the craft of that duel lives in the goalkeeping material rather than here.

## Transition: stopping the forward before the box is set

Transition moments, the seconds after a turnover when neither side is organised, are their own defensive problem because the plan's structure is not yet in place: the mid-block is stretched, the screening midfielder may be up the pitch, the far-side reference may not be established. A forward who is clever in transition exploits exactly this window, and the counter has to be a set of habits that hold even when the shape does not.

The first transition habit is the delay. The nearest defender to the ball at the moment of the turnover does not dive in to win it back; he delays, staying on his feet, showing the ball-carrier away from the central lane and buying the seconds the rest of the defence needs to recover its shape. Delay is unglamorous and it is the single most valuable transition behaviour, because it converts a fast break, which the plan cannot defend, into a slow attack, which the plan can.

The second transition habit is the recovery run's line. Defenders recovering toward their own goal have to run the line that keeps the dangerous forward in view and keeps themselves goal-side, not the shortest line back to position. A defender who sprints straight back to his starting spot while the forward drifts wide of his recovery line arrives in position having lost the man; a defender who curves his recovery to stay between the forward and the goal arrives with the man still marked. Against a forward who arrives late, the recovery run's line is often the whole difference, because the late arrival is being timed into precisely the space a poorly-lined recovery leaves open.

The third transition habit belongs to the goalkeeper, who in transition becomes the sweeper for the ball played in behind a stretched line. A keeper who reads the transition and starts a step higher, ready to come and claim or clear the through ball, buys his defenders the margin to recover, and takes away the one delivery, the ball slid in behind for a run, that a transition most dangerously offers.

## The goalkeeper's job in detail

The goalkeeper is treated as a full participant throughout this blueprint rather than as a last resort behind it, and he earns a section of his own because his starting position and his come-or-stay decision change the geometry of every contest the outfield players are fighting. A goalkeeper who sets the same position for every delivery is wrong for most of them, and getting his positioning right by service type is one of the cheapest large gains available to a defence.

Start with the come-or-stay decision, because it is the one that wins and loses matches. The honest rule is that the keeper comes for the ball he can win and stays for the ball he cannot, and the disaster is the half-commitment that strands both keeper and defender. Against a forward who attacks flighted balls early and powerfully, the temptation to come is strong and the punishment for coming late is severe, so the decision has to be made early and held. A keeper who commands his six-yard box, who claims the flighted ball he can reach and calls loudly and unambiguously for the one he cannot, shrinks the zone in which a clean header is possible, because the forward now has to attack a smaller pocket of air with a keeper contesting the top of it. A keeper who neither comes nor clearly stays leaves his defenders guessing, and a defender guessing about his keeper is a defender not set for the header.

The starting position changes by delivery type, and the ladder captured the settings; here is the reasoning. Against a wide cross from deep, the keeper favours the near post and stays close to his line, because the deep cross gives him time to react but also invites the near-post flick he must be positioned to cover. Against a whipped cross from an advanced area, he steps two yards off his line and centres himself, ready to claim the flat, fast ball that a whipped delivery produces, because that ball is claimable if he starts in a position to attack it and unclaimable if he starts pinned to his line. Against a cutback, he sets low and weight-forward for the near-post strike, because the cutback finish is a low, first-time shot and a keeper on his heels is beaten by it. Against a through ball for a late run, he starts a fraction higher, ready to sweep the space behind a stretched line. Against a shot from distance, he sets square and expects the late dip. These are not arbitrary; each position is the one that gives the keeper the best first move against the specific ball that service type produces.

The near-post power header deserves a specific word, because it is a contact this forward makes that many keepers are not set for. When he attacks a near-post delivery with momentum, the header arrives fast and flat toward the near upper corner, and a keeper positioned centrally has no time to reach it. The counter is a starting position that respects the near post on those deliveries, combined with defenders who contest the near-post contact so the header is never clean. The keeper cannot cover the near-post power header alone; it is a shared job, and the ladder's near-post box assignment exists precisely so the keeper is never left to face that contact unprotected.

Two more keeper habits complete the picture. The first is the sight-line demand: defenders blocking a shooting lane must not screen their own keeper, because a power shot the keeper picks up late off an obscured view is the shot that beats him, and a defender who blocks the lane while stepping across the keeper's eyes has helped the forward. The second is vocal command in the second-ball scramble, where the keeper who organises the six-yard box loudly, calling defenders onto loose balls and claiming what he can, prevents the scrappy rebound goal that a box occupier lives off. The goalkeeping craft in its fuller form, the positioning principles and the psychology of the one-on-one, is developed at [beating elite goalkeepers, a guide to the craft on both sides](/2024/08/01/beating-elite-goalkeepers-guide/), and a keeper preparing specifically for this forward should read it alongside this section.

## Rehearsing the plan: session design for the training pitch

A blueprint that lives only on paper does not survive Saturday, so the plan has to be rehearsed until its habits are automatic, and the good news for the coach is that the plan rehearses in a small number of focused sessions rather than a season of drills. The behaviours the plan needs, scanning, turning and travelling, pressing the delivery, screening the pocket, are all trainable, and each has a session that builds it.

The scanning habit is built with a constrained possession game in which defenders are rewarded not for winning the ball but for tracking a designated runner. Set up an attacking side that includes a nominated late runner and a defending side whose points come only from a defender being goal-side of that runner at the moment a delivery is played. The constraint forces the head-on-a-swivel habit, because a defender who watches the ball loses the runner and concedes the point, and a defender who scans keeps him. Repeat it until the scan is unconscious, because a scan that requires conscious effort in training will not happen under match pressure.

The turn-and-travel habit is built with a repeated one-on-one in the box, but designed so the forward always approaches from a run-up rather than a standstill. The defender starts square and flat-footed, the coach delivers a ball for the forward to attack on the move, and the defender is coached to turn early, half-open his body, and travel into the contact. The measure of success is not whether the defender wins the header but whether he arrives at the contact in balance, feet under him, contesting rather than backpedalling. Film it if you can, because a defender who sees his own flat-footed posture on video learns the early turn faster than one who is only told about it.

The delivery-denial behaviour is built with a wide-area pressing drill that rewards degradation rather than the block. A wide attacker tries to deliver, a pressing player closes the delivery foot, and the drill scores the press not on whether the cross is blocked but on whether the delivery is forced early, checked, or off balance. Coaching the pressers to chase degradation rather than the block is the whole point, because a presser hunting the block gets turned and a presser forcing a worse ball does his job. Pair it with a box-defence group behind, so the pressers see the connection between their work and the easier contest it creates for the defenders.

The cutback screen is built with a byline-and-pocket drill, in which a wide attacker reaches the goal-line and tries to pull the ball back to a forward waiting in the pocket, and a screening midfielder is rewarded for occupying the lane so the pull-back is denied. The discipline this drill teaches is the hardest to hold under match pressure, the discipline of not going to help at the ball, and it is worth rehearsing to the point of boredom, because the pocket is guarded by habit or it is not guarded at all.

Finally, the whole plan is rehearsed together in a phase-of-play session that runs the full sequence, build-up, delivery, box contest, second ball, from a wide starting position, with the defending side scoring for a clean resolution and the attacking side scoring for a clean contact. Running the sequence whole is what stitches the individual habits into a plan, because a defence that has drilled scanning and pressing and screening separately still has to learn to do them at once, in the same three seconds, which is what the match will demand. ## Game management: protecting a lead and the substitution triggers

The plan's demand on a defence is not brilliance in a single moment but concentration across ninety of them, and the phase that most tests that concentration is the closing stretch of a match, when a contained forward most often finds the lapse he has been waiting for. Game management is therefore part of the counter, not an afterthought to it, and the central idea is that the marker degrades before the runner does.

The mechanism is straightforward and it is a stamina problem rather than a quality problem. A defender tracking a repeat late-arriver is doing exhausting work, and his tracking degrades progressively across the contest: his early tracks are disciplined, his later ones are a step late, and the goal often arrives not because the forward improved but because the marker deteriorated. A coach who understands this defends the last twenty minutes differently from the first twenty, and the difference is planned in advance rather than improvised when the lead is already slipping.

The first game-management lever is the substitution for the tracker, not the attacker. When a marker's tracking is visibly fraying, the sign is a track that starts a beat late and a recovery that arrives after the contact, the plan substitutes fresh legs into the tracking job rather than waiting for the goal that fatigue is about to concede. This is a squad decision as much as a tactical one, and it means a coach picks his bench with the tracking job in mind, keeping a defender in reserve who can travel and turn when the starter can no longer.

The second lever is the shift to the fallbacks as the situation demands. Protecting a lead in the closing minutes is the situation the dedicated screen and, in the last resort, the man-mark were built for, and a coach holding a result drops the extra midfielder into the arrival zones and accepts the loss of attacking threat as the price of a guarded box. The escalation to the man-mark, the plan's most fragile fallback, belongs almost exclusively to this phase, where staking the defence on one duel to protect a result is a bet worth making that it would not be worth making at the start.

The third lever is the concentration reset at every restart. A box occupier scores a disproportionate share of his goals from the moments after a set-piece or a stoppage, when the defence relaxes, so the coach and the goalkeeper reset the shape loudly at every throw, corner and free-kick in the closing phase, refusing to let the defence drift out of its assignments in the moments it is most tempted to. The plan's whole ambition, containment rather than erasure, is won or lost in these late, tired, distracted minutes, and a defence that has held its discipline for seventy minutes only to lose it in the last twenty has not executed the plan; it has merely delayed conceding.

## The fallbacks: what to do when the first plan fails

No plan survives contact with a forward this good unamended, and a blueprint that does not state its fallbacks is not a plan, it is a hope. The plan has a sequence of fallbacks, escalating as the first answers prove insufficient.

The first fallback is the double-up on the crossing source. If a particular wide player is consistently getting clean deliveries away despite the press, the plan doubles that source: the winger and full-back combine to take away the crossing lane entirely, accepting that this frees a runner elsewhere and trusting the cover pinch and the far-side reference to handle the runner. This is a resource decision, spending two players to shut one supply line, and it is worth it when one supply line is producing most of the danger.

The second fallback is the dedicated screen. If the late run is repeatedly finding the seam despite the marking, the plan drops a midfielder permanently into the space in front of the back line, not to mark the forward but to clutter the arrival zones so that every run meets traffic. This concedes numbers higher up the pitch and invites pressure, and it is the fallback for the phase of a match when protecting the lead matters more than threatening the opposition.

The third fallback is the man-mark, and it is deliberately last because it is the most fragile. Assigning one defender to follow the forward everywhere solves the vision problem by force, but it creates two new problems: it lets the forward drag his marker out of shape and open space for others, and it stakes the whole defence on one duel that the forward wins often enough to make the stake reckless as a first choice. The plan man-marks only in specific, late-game, protect-a-result situations, only with a defender who has the profile to travel and turn, and only with the cover organised to handle the space the man-marker's movement opens. It is a tool for a closing phase, not a philosophy for a match.

The escalation matters as much as the individual fallbacks, because a coach who reaches for the man-mark first has skipped the two answers that cost less and fail less. Start with the shape and the press, escalate to the double-up, then the screen, and hold the man-mark in reserve for the situation that genuinely demands it.

## What to concede deliberately

A plan that tries to concede nothing concedes everything, so the counter states its deliberate concessions plainly, because a defence that knows what it is agreeing to give up defends the rest with more conviction.

The plan concedes the shot from distance, and it concedes it on purpose. Ronaldo strikes the ball from range with genuine power, and the community line that his distance shooting is a gift to defenders is only half right. It is a gift in that a shot from twenty-five yards is a far lower-percentage effort than a header from six, and steering him toward the former is a defensive win. It is not a gift in the sense of being harmless, because the strike carries enough power and dip to punish a poor set or a screened goalkeeper. The plan's stance is to accept the distance shot as the price of denying the close-range chance, while still defending it properly: close the shooting foot to force the strike onto the weaker side, block the lane without diving in, and set the goalkeeper square and ready for power and dip. Concede the shot, but concede it on the defence's terms.

The plan concedes the contested aerial ball away from goal. A header won by the forward that goes out for a throw, or is nodded into a wide channel under pressure, is a concession the plan is happy to make all day, because it is a header that does not threaten the goal. Trying to win every aerial contest cleanly is how defenders end up ball-watching and out of position for the one that matters; accepting the harmless contested header keeps the defence's shape intact for the dangerous one.

The plan concedes territory in exchange for the shape it needs. The mid-block gives up some ground compared to a high line, and it gives up some compactness compared to a deep block, and both of those are deliberate trades for the ability to pressure the delivery and to see the runner. A coach has to be at peace with the opposition having the ball in areas that feel uncomfortable, because the discomfort is the cost of the plan's core function, and a defence that panics and drops deep at the first sustained possession has abandoned the plan for the very shape the plan was designed to avoid.

## How the plan degrades with a weaker squad

### Can an amateur team use this plan to stop a strong header?

Yes, in reduced form. An amateur side cannot press every crosser or coach a scanning discipline to elite standard, so it keeps the one rule that matters most: defend the near and central zones and never leave the far post unattended. Force the delivery wide and low, screen the cutback, and accept the contested header away from goal.

The full plan assumes resources an amateur or youth side does not have: wingers fit enough to press crossers for ninety minutes, centre-backs coached in the early turn, a screening midfielder disciplined enough to hold the cutback pocket. Strip those away and the plan still has a spine, because the spine is a principle, not a personnel requirement.

The one rule that survives every reduction is this: when your side cannot press the crosser, defend the delivery's destination instead of the delivery. That means the whole defence concentrates on denying the near and central arrival zones and, above all, on never leaving the far post unattended, because the unattended far post is where a strong header punishes a weak team most cheaply. An amateur side that does nothing else but hold its shape, keep a body on the far post, and put a defender in front of the cutback pocket will concede fewer clean chances than a better-drilled side that presses badly and leaves the back post open.

The youth-coaching angle carries a caution alongside the plan, because some of these ideas are age-appropriate and some are not. The scanning discipline, the head on a swivel, the far-side reference: these are habits worth teaching young defenders early, because they are cheap to build and they transfer to defending any forward, not just this one. The physical duel, the turn-and-travel against a powerful late runner, is harder to teach a young defender whose athletic base is still developing, and a youth coach should focus the teaching on the positional habits that do not depend on physical maturity. Teaching a twelve-year-old to scan is a gift for a career; asking the same child to out-muscle a bigger forward in the air is a lesson in frustration. The plan for a developing player is the plan's brain, not its brawn.

The full-back-alone question returns here in its amateur form, because at grassroots level the mismatch is often a full-back against a forward who is simply bigger and stronger. The answer is the same as at the top level, only more emphatic: the full-back is never the last line in the air against him, and the amateur side has to organise so that a centre-back or the goalkeeper is always the second body in the box-defence, with the full-back's job reduced to forcing the play wide and low and screening the cutback rather than winning a duel he is not built to win.

## Why the whole team defends this forward

The hardest part of this plan to sell is not to the defenders, who accept that a great forward is their problem, but to the attacking players, who have to be convinced that pressing a crosser thirty yards from their own goal is their job too. The plan does not work without that buy-in, because the spine of the plan is delivery-denial and delivery-denial begins with the players furthest from the danger, so a coach who cannot persuade his front line to defend the supply has no plan at all.

The persuasion is a matter of showing the winger and the forward what their pressing actually buys. A winger who presses a crosser is not chasing a lost cause and he is not doing the defenders a favour; he is the first and cheapest line of the whole scheme, because a delivery spoiled thirty yards out never becomes a chance the defenders have to survive. The work he does up the pitch is worth more than the work the defenders do in the box, because it prevents the contest rather than merely contesting it, and a front line that understands this presses with conviction rather than gesture. The gesture press, the jog toward the crosser that arrives after the ball has gone, is worse than no press at all, because it commits a player without spoiling anything.

There is a cost, and the plan is honest about it. Asking the attacking players to press the supply spends energy they would otherwise keep for attacking, and it means the front line is sometimes deep and out of position when possession is won back, which slows the counter-attack. That is a real trade, and a coach makes it knowingly: against a forward whose whole threat is fed by clean service, spending attacking energy on defending the service is worth the slower transitions it produces. A team unwilling to make that trade is a team that has decided to defend the box alone, which the plan has shown is defending the last link of a chain whose earlier links were where the goal was made.

The contract extends to the whole side because the plan's jobs are distributed across it. The winger and full-back press the supply, the midfielder screens the pocket, the centre-backs turn and travel and hold the references, the goalkeeper commands his box and sets his line, and no one of these can be dropped without opening a lane. This is why containing this forward is described throughout as a team plan rather than a duel: there is no single defender whose excellence solves it, and there is no single job whose absence the others can cover. The plan is a chain, every link matters, and the team that defends him is the whole team or it is no one.

## The community debates, answered

Every discussion of how to stop this forward circles the same set of claims, and a counter-plan that ignores them leaves the reader's actual questions unanswered, so here is each position with a verdict.

The claim that you need a giant centre-back is the most common and the most wrong, for the reasons the defender-profile section established: the aerial contest is won in the approach, not at the top of the jump, so mobility and reading beat height, and a shorter defender who turns early and travels into the contest wins more of the duels that matter than a taller one who jumps from a standstill. Verdict: height helps only when it is added to mobility, and a coach forced to choose picks the mobile defender.

The claim that he only scores from crosses, so you simply defend the box, misreads the sequence. He scores from crosses, cutbacks, second balls and late runs, and a defence that camps in the box while allowing free delivery is defending the last link of a chain whose earlier links were where the goal was actually made. Verdict: defend the delivery, not just the box, or the box defence is defending a lost cause.

The claim that he is easy to defend when isolated is true and useless. Any forward is easier to defend with no service, and the whole point of an attacking side is to provide service, so a plan that relies on isolating him is a plan that relies on the opposition failing to do its job. Verdict: isolation is the effect the plan is trying to produce, not a precondition the plan can assume.

The claim that pressing his side is pointless because they will just play around it treats pressing as an all-or-nothing block rather than a degradation tool. The press is not trying to win the ball high every time; it is trying to make the eventual delivery worse, and a worse delivery is a partial win even when the press is played through. Verdict: press to degrade, not to dispossess, and the pressing is far from pointless.

The claim that his shooting from distance is a gift to defenders is half true, and the concessions section handled the other half: steering him toward the distance shot is a win, but the shot still carries enough power to punish a lazy set, so it is a gift only to a defence that keeps defending it properly. Verdict: welcome the distance shot, but do not stop defending it.

The claim that penalties distort the whole picture belongs to a different argument than this one. The counter-plan defends open play, and the penalty is a separate duel handled elsewhere; the best contribution the plan makes to the penalty question is to defend the box in a way that concedes fewer of them. Verdict: penalties are out of scope for a run-of-play plan, and conceding fewer is the plan's only relevant lever.

## Reading the version from two clips

The analyst's job in preparing this plan is to identify, quickly, which version of the forward a side is about to face, because the plan's weighting changes with the version and getting the weighting wrong wastes the whole preparation. Two clips are usually enough if you know what to look for.

Look first at where he receives and starts his actions. If he is picking the ball up wide, taking on a full-back, and carrying toward the box, the side is facing the wide-forward version, and the plan weights toward containing the carry and the cut inside, with the aerial and late-run systems as secondary concerns. If he is living inside the width of the six-yard box, barely touching the ball outside the area, and doing his work in short bursts of movement, the side is facing the box-occupier version, and the plan weights fully toward delivery-denial and the two-system reconciliation.

Look second at his movement without the ball in the final third. A version that drifts wide and comes short to combine is a version whose threat is partly in build-up, and the plan can afford to worry less about the back post. A version whose off-ball movement is all short, sharp runs across defenders toward the posts is the pure arrival threat, and the far-side reference becomes non-negotiable. The tell is in the feet: a forward constantly adjusting his position by a yard at a time on the blind side of a marker is preparing a late run, and a defence warned by the analyst that this is coming defends the run before it happens rather than after.

This diagnostic work is where the counter-plan connects to the broader category, because the box-occupier template is not unique to one forward, and a coach who has prepared for this one has most of the tools to prepare for the type. The general template for the box-occupying threat, the shared spine across every forward who lives in the penalty area and finishes what arrives there, is set out in [stopping elite strikers: the complete guide](/2020/01/01/stopping-elite-strikers-complete-guide/), and the goalkeeping craft the plan leans on so heavily, the starting positions and the decision to come or stay, is developed in full at [beating elite goalkeepers, a guide to the craft on both sides](/2024/08/01/beating-elite-goalkeepers-guide/).

## The closing verdict

The plan for how to stop Cristiano Ronaldo is, in the end, an argument about where a defensive sequence is actually won, and its answer is that the sequence is won one pass before the finish. A defence that measures itself by whether it out-jumps him or out-runs his late arrival is measuring itself by contests he wins often enough to make the measurement a losing bet. A defence that measures itself by the quality of the ball that reaches him, by whether the crosser was pressured and the cutback screened and the far post watched, is measuring itself by something it can actually control.

The two-system problem is the reason the plan has to work this way. Because his aerial threat and his late-arrival threat demand contradictory defensive settings, no arrangement of markers resolves both, and the only coherent response is to attack the input they share. Deny the delivery and both systems lose their fuel at the same moment, which is the one place a defence gets to solve two problems with a single action. Everything in the blueprint, the mid-block that keeps the pressers near the ball, the defender profile that turns and travels rather than merely towers, the far-side reference that never lets the back post go quiet, the goalkeeper's start position tuned to the service type, the ladder of concessions the plan agrees to make, exists to serve that single spine.

None of it reduces him to nothing, and the plan has said so throughout, because a blueprint that promised to erase a forward of this quality would be lying. What it does is change the chances he gets from the clean ones to the hard ones, and across a match that change is the whole game. The defender who takes one idea from this article should take the idea that he is not there to beat the finisher; he is there to spoil the pass. Do that link by link, sequence by sequence, refusing to switch off for the ninety minutes it takes, and a forward most sides simply endure becomes a forward a well-drilled defence can genuinely contain.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you stop Cristiano Ronaldo?

You stop him by attacking the delivery rather than the finisher. His two elite threats, the aerial contact and the late arrival into the box, demand contradictory defensive settings, so no marking scheme answers both. The coherent plan pressures the crosser and the passer to degrade the service, screens the cutback, holds the near post, and keeps a permanent reference on the far post. Deny the clean delivery and both his finishing systems lose their fuel at once. The plan does not stop him touching the ball; it changes the chances he gets from clean ones close to goal into contested efforts, late headers made off balance, and shots from range. Contained, not erased, is the honest target.

### Q: Should you double up on Cristiano Ronaldo when he starts wide?

Only against the wide-forward version, and only as a fallback. When he receives wide and carries at a full-back, doubling the carry can be worth it, because the full-back holds the touchline while a second player takes away the cut inside. Against the box-occupier version he barely touches the ball wide, so doubling wastes a body far from the danger. The better use of a double-up against the box occupier is on the crossing source: if one wide player keeps getting clean deliveries away, the winger and full-back combine to shut that lane entirely, trusting the cover pinch and the far-side reference to handle the runner the double-up frees. Spend the extra body where the danger is being manufactured, not where he happens to be standing.

### Q: How do you defend Cristiano Ronaldo in the air?

Defend the approach, not the leap. His header is powered by the run-up, where forward momentum becomes hang time and power, so the contest is decided before he jumps. The defender has to turn early, half-opening his body toward the arrival zone so his first move is a stride into the contact rather than a backpedal, and travel into the ball so he arrives in balance. Watching the ball through the flight is the classic error, because the run tells you where the contest will be long before the ball does. Marking with height alone fails too, since he attacks the ball in space a yard from a static marker; the defender must occupy the ball's destination, not stand next to the forward's starting point. Height helps only when added to mobility.

### Q: What line height works best against Cristiano Ronaldo?

A mid-block that breathes, not a fixed deep or high line. A deep block concentrates the traffic his late runs are designed to exploit and hands crossers a free platform, so you defend the box perfectly and lose the sequence earlier. A very high line offers space in behind for a run to be timed into and forces defenders into recovery sprints facing their own goal, the hardest scenario for turning with a late runner. The mid-block sits between them, keeping wide players close enough to pressure deliveries while holding the line high enough that the space behind is not a runway. It steps up when the ball is under pressure and drops as the ball nears a crossing position, and the timing of that breathing is a rehearsed habit.

### Q: How do you stop Cristiano Ronaldo attacking the far post?

Keep a permanent far-side reference. The back-post arrival is his quietest damage, made while a back line's eyes have all followed the ball to the near side. The far-side defender's job is to hold a fixed reference on the back-post space and on any runner drifting into it, even when the ball is nowhere near him, so the run is picked up early rather than discovered at the contact. A far-side defender who ball-watches has already conceded the header; one who watches the space is goal-side and moving when the ball arrives. The whole back line keeps this reference, and it is the single most useful spatial habit against a forward who threatens both posts, turning an unstoppable free header into a contested one.

### Q: Should a full-back defend Cristiano Ronaldo alone?

Never as the last line in the air, but often as the first responder to the run. Full-backs are selected for the mobility centre-backs are not always required to have, so a mobile full-back is well suited to travelling with a late run in wide-central areas, provided the cover behind is organised. What a full-back must not be is the final body contesting an aerial ball against him, because that is a mismatch of the wrong kind. The plan uses full-backs to force play wide and low, to screen the cutback, and to pick up runs early, while ensuring a centre-back or the goalkeeper is always the second body in the box-defence. At amateur level this is even more emphatic: force the play wide, screen the pull-back, and leave the duel to someone built for it.

### Q: How do you defend Cristiano Ronaldo at set pieces?

Deny the run-up, since the delivery will be clean by definition. At a corner his aerial threat is still built on the approach, so a defender screens his run-up by occupying the path he wants toward his arrival zone, forcing him to check or start from a standstill and stripping the momentum his header depends on. This is legal, disciplined defending, occupying space rather than holding. Combine the run-up screen with zonal markers on the near and central arrival zones and a goalkeeper who attacks the flighted ball early off his line. At a direct free-kick, set the wall to protect the near half against the dipping near-post strike, with the keeper covering the far-top quadrant, and accept the perfectly struck ball to the far top corner as the price of covering the more likely one.

### Q: How do you limit Cristiano Ronaldo's shooting opportunities?

Take away the set, not just the shot. His striking power comes from a clean, balanced contact, so the defensive aim is to deny the balanced base rather than to block after the strike. Close his shooting foot on the approach to force the ball onto his weaker side, block the shooting lane without diving in, since a committed lunge is beaten by a shift of the ball, and keep a screening midfielder in the cutback pocket so the pulled-back ball that arrives to a facing forward is unavailable. Reducing his opportunities is mostly about reducing the clean deliveries that create them, which loops back to pressuring the crosser and the passer. A forward starved of good service has fewer moments to set his feet, and fewer set feet means fewer dangerous strikes.

### Q: What does a goalkeeper do against Cristiano Ronaldo's power shooting?

Set square, expect power and dip, and stay big. His strike from range carries pace and a late dip, so a goalkeeper who is set, balanced and square gives himself the best chance to react to the dip rather than committing early and being beaten by the movement. The keeper must not be screened: defenders blocking the lane have to do so without obscuring the goalkeeper's sight of the ball, because a power shot the keeper sees late off a deflected view is the one that beats him. On flighted and crossed balls the keeper's job flips to commanding the six-yard box, coming for what he can win and calling loudly for what he cannot, which shrinks the zone where a clean header is possible. The starting position changes by service type, and getting that right is half the goalkeeper's contribution.

### Q: How do you defend the cross when Cristiano Ronaldo attacks the box?

Pressure the crosser first, then organise the box as a fallback. The winger or full-back closes the crosser's delivery foot at an angle that takes the natural ball away, forcing an early, checked or off-balance delivery, which is the low-quality service the plan wants. Chasing an outright block is how pressers get turned; the aim is degradation, not a clean block. Behind the pressure, the near-side centre-back attacks the front post, the marker turns and travels goal-side of the run, the far-side defender holds the back post without ball-watching, and a screening midfielder covers the cutback pocket. The box assignment is deliberately the fallback, because a defence that starts by organising the box has already accepted the delivery it should have been trying to spoil. Deny the ball, then defend the contact.

### Q: Should you show Cristiano Ronaldo onto his weaker side?

Yes where you can, but do not build the plan on it, because he finishes with either foot. Steering him onto his less favoured side reduces the quality of a strike or a first touch at the margin, and closing the stronger shooting foot is a sound habit, especially on shots from range where the extra fraction of difficulty matters. But he is genuinely two-footed in the box, so a plan that relies on forcing him weak is a plan that will be punished, because the weak side is not weak enough to be safe. Treat the weaker side as a small edge to claim when the situation allows, not as a solution. The larger and more reliable lever is always the delivery: a forward who never receives a clean ball has no strong-foot chance to take in the first place.

### Q: What do you concede deliberately to contain Cristiano Ronaldo?

You concede the low-percentage chance to deny the high-percentage one. Specifically, the plan accepts the shot from distance, the contested header cleared away from goal, and a measure of territory and possession in uncomfortable areas. Steering him toward a shot from range instead of a header from six yards is a defensive win, provided the range shot is still defended properly with a closed foot and a set keeper. Accepting the harmless contested aerial ball keeps the defence's shape intact for the dangerous one. And accepting territory is the cost of the mid-block that lets the defence pressure deliveries and see runners. A plan that tries to concede nothing concedes everything, so naming the trades lets the defence make them with conviction rather than panic into the deep block the plan is built to avoid.

### Q: How do you stop Cristiano Ronaldo in transition?

Delay first, then recover on the right line. The nearest defender at the turnover stays on his feet and shows the ball-carrier away from the central lane rather than diving in, converting a fast break the plan cannot defend into a slow attack it can. Recovering defenders run the line that keeps him in view and themselves goal-side, curving the recovery rather than sprinting the shortest route back, because a straight recovery loses a forward who drifts wide of it into a late-arriving position. The goalkeeper becomes the sweeper for the ball slid in behind a stretched line, starting a step higher and ready to claim or clear. Transition is dangerous because the plan's structure is not yet set, so the counter is a set of habits, delay, recovery line, sweeping keeper, that hold even when the shape does not.

### Q: What is the fallback if the plan against Cristiano Ronaldo fails?

Escalate through three fallbacks in order. First, double the crossing source: if one wide player keeps delivering cleanly, the winger and full-back shut that lane together and trust the cover to handle the freed runner. Second, drop a dedicated screen: a midfielder sits permanently in front of the back line to clutter the arrival zones so every run meets traffic, conceding ground higher up in exchange for a protected box. Third, and only as a last resort, man-mark with a mobile defender and organised cover, accepting that it lets him drag markers out of shape and stakes the defence on one duel. The order matters, because reaching for the man-mark first skips the two answers that cost less and fail less. Man-marking is a closing-phase tool, not a match-long philosophy.

### Q: How should an amateur side handle a Cristiano Ronaldo type forward?

Keep the spine, drop the parts you cannot resource. An amateur team cannot press every crosser or coach an elite scanning discipline, so it holds the one rule that survives every reduction: when you cannot press the crosser, defend the delivery's destination instead. Concentrate on the near and central arrival zones, never leave the far post unattended, force the play wide and low, and screen the cutback. A side that does nothing but hold its shape, keep a body on the back post, and block the pull-back lane concedes fewer clean chances than a better side that presses badly and leaves the far post open. For young defenders, teach the positional habits, scanning and the far-side reference, which do not depend on physical maturity, and do not ask a developing player to out-muscle a bigger forward in the air.

### Q: Which sides coped best with Cristiano Ronaldo and what did they do?

The sides that coped shared a method rather than a single hero defender. They pressured the wide areas to spoil the service, they screened the cutback zone with a disciplined midfielder, and they defended the box with mobility and organisation rather than trusting one tall marker to win every jump. Crucially, they refused to defend deep, because dropping off concentrates exactly the traffic his movement exploits and hands his teammates a free crossing platform. They accepted the shot from distance and the cleared contested header as the price of denying the close-range chance, and they kept their concentration through second balls and the final twenty minutes, when a contained forward most often finds the lapse he needs. The lesson is that containing him is a team plan executed for ninety minutes, not a duel won by one defender.
