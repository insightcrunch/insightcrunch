Translating Shakespeare's puns in Romeo and Juliet is the point where translation stops behaving like transfer and starts behaving like triage. A plot can cross a border intact. A metre can be rebuilt in a receiving language that keeps a comparable measure. An image can be exchanged for a cousin image and lose only its accent. A pun can do none of these things, because a pun is not a meaning carried by a sound but two meanings fused inside one sound, and the fusion is an accident of one particular language's history. When Mercutio, bleeding to death in the third act, promises that tomorrow his friends will find him a grave man, the joke and the death sentence occupy the same two syllables. English happens to store seriousness and burial in a single word. German does not. Russian does not. A translator standing at that line is not looking for an equivalent, since none exists in the language's inventory. The translator is deciding which half of the word to save and which half to bury, and the decision is visible forever in the finished text, an audited loss.

The verdict of this article can be stated before the evidence, because the evidence only prices it. The individual pun, taken one at a time, is frequently untranslatable in the strict sense: no target-language word holds the same two senses in the same shape, and any rendering that pretends otherwise is a forgery. The punning of the play, taken as a whole activity, is translatable, because wordplay is a function a text performs and functions can be redistributed. A translator who loses the grave joke can repay the text by inventing a joke at a nearby joint where the receiving language happens to offer an accident of its own. Dirk Delabastita, whose parallel-text study of Hamlet's wordplay in There's a Double Tongue (1993) remains the field's foundation, catalogued the manoeuvres translators actually perform and found every one of them in use: substitution, compensation, rhetorical replacement, omission, direct copying, and the editorial confession of the footnote. The play's punning survives translation the way an army survives a campaign, by losing named positions and holding the campaign's object. What cannot survive is any particular position the enemy has already surveyed. The word impossible, then, is the honest starting position of every translator who opens this play. It is a poor finishing position, and the history of the play in German, Russian, French, and Japanese is the record of craftsmen refusing to finish there.

## "Impossible" Is the Starting Position, Not the Conclusion

The claim that puns cannot be translated is usually made loosely, as a sigh, and it deserves to be made precisely, as a theorem, before it is graded. J. C. Catford gave the theorem its classic form in A Linguistic Theory of Translation (1965), distinguishing cultural untranslatability, where the receiving culture lacks the thing named, from linguistic untranslatability, where the receiving language lacks the structural feature the source text exploits. A pun built on an English homophone sits in the second class and near its hardest edge. Roman Jakobson had drawn the surrounding map six years earlier in "On Linguistic Aspects of Translation" (1959), arguing that languages differ essentially in what they must convey rather than in what they may convey, and that poetry, where the sound of the sign is part of the sense, is by definition untranslatable and open only to creative transposition. A pun is poetry compressed to a single word: the sound is not the clothing of the meaning but half of the meaning itself. On the theory of both men, the grave man does not cross. Only a transposition crosses, a new object built to do similar work in a different material.

Against the theorem stands the brute fact of the shelves. Romeo and Juliet has been translated into German since the prose Shakespeare of Christoph Martin Wieland in the seventeen-sixties, into French in the versions of Pierre Le Tourneur and later François-Victor Hugo, into Russian by a line of translators culminating in Boris Pasternak, into Japanese by Tsubouchi Shōyō as part of his complete Shakespeare, and into dozens of other languages besides. German theatres played the play for two centuries. Russian audiences wept at it. None of these publics received a punless skeleton and mistook it for the whole animal, because translators, whatever the theory said, kept finding ways to keep the text verbally alive at most joints even while surrendering at surveyed ones. The descriptive record assembled by Delabastita from parallel corpora shows the surrender and the salvage side by side, often on the same page of the same translation. Theory describes the wall correctly. Practice describes the ladders, the tunnels, and the honest white flags, and a verdict on translating the puns has to grade both descriptions at once.

The grading this article defends runs as follows. At the level of the single word, Catford and Jakobson win: equivalence fails, and fails provably, at a definable class of joints. At the level of the scene, the act, and the play, Delabastita's record wins: the density and placement of wordplay can be held approximately constant by redistribution, and the best translators in the play's history did exactly that, consciously or not. At the level of the reader's experience, one further distinction decides everything. A reader of the translation who never learns what was lost has received a play that puns less, in fewer places, with different jokes, and may still have received a punning play. A reader who is told, by a note or by an edition's honesty, exactly where the floor gave way has received something rarer: the map of the loss itself. The strategies differ in what they deliver, and the catalogue assembled below prices each one against the play's hardest joints. That pricing, not the sigh, is what the word impossible should be made to mean.

## Why a Pun Resists Carrying: Sound, Sense, and the Accident of Vocabulary

A pun's mechanism is worth stating exactly, because translators fail at it for exact reasons. Delabastita defines wordplay as the textual phenomenon in which structural features of a language, its sounds, its spellings, its word boundaries, are exploited so that two meanings collide inside formally similar material. The collisions sort into families. Homonymy gives one word two unrelated senses, as grave carries the tomb and seriousness. Homophony gives two words one sound, as ay, I, and eye share a syllable in the third act. Homography gives two senses one spelling with different sounds. Paronymy gives near-identical forms whose slight difference is the joke, the family to which the servants' opening chain of coals, colliers, choler, and collar belongs. Each family depends on a local accident: on which words a particular language happened to make identical, similar, or ambiguous across its history. The accidents are not shared between languages except by coincidence, because each language's history of sound change, borrowing, and spelling reform is its own. French did not undergo the same mergers as English. Russian built its ambiguities in a different workshop entirely.

### What makes a pun unlike every other difficulty a translator meets?

A pun differs in kind, not in degree. Other difficulties sit on a scale where labour finds better and worse solutions; a pun is a switch the receiving language either possesses or lacks. No skill manufactures a missing homonym, so the translator's work changes at a pun from finding equivalents to choosing among losses, or inventing fresh luck elsewhere.

A translator's ordinary difficulties are difficulties of degree: a word too broad, an idiom without a twin, a rhythm that resists the receiving measure. Each admits a better and a worse solution along a single scale, and labour moves the rendering up the scale. A pun presents a difficulty of kind. Its two senses are not a spectrum but a switch, and the target language either happens to possess a word that flips the same switch or does not. No amount of labour manufactures the missing homonym, because the homonym is a fact about the receiving language's whole history and not about the translator's skill. The work therefore changes character at a pun: from searching for the equivalent to choosing among losses, or to inventing an unrelated piece of luck at a different address.

The play distributes its wordplay across every register it commands, which is why the problem cannot be quarantined in the comic scenes. The servants open the tragedy fencing with puns on coals and colliers, choler and collar, on standing and on maidenheads, in prose whose second senses are bawdy (1.1). Romeo and Mercutio rally in the second act through the goose, the sole of a shoe, and the flower of courtesy (2.4). Mercutio, conjuring the absent Romeo in the Capulet orchard's street, descends to the medlar and the poperin pear, fruit names carrying obscenity (2.1). Juliet, waiting for news, hangs her fate on the single syllable ay and rings it against I and eye (3.2). Mercutio dies punning on grave (3.1). The Friar enters preaching in a rhyme that marries womb to tomb (2.3). Juliet's great speech of longing opens on a gallop that hides gall (3.2), and Romeo in Mantua pays the apothecary in gold he calls a worse poison than the drug (5.1). A translator who solved the comic joints and failed the grave ones would still have failed the play, because the tragedy's own thesis about language, that words in Verona are weapons before they are messages, is carried by the spread itself.

The bawdy layer doubles the exposure. A bawdy pun stores a respectable sense and a sexual sense in one word, and the receiving language must not only possess a comparable double word but must possess it at a comparable level of licence: sayable by servants on a stage, in print, in that decade, in that country. The medlar's English slang names have no German fruit equivalents with the same folk life, and the German solution examined below chose to let the obscenity lapse rather than counterfeiting a coarseness the words could not carry. Register is a second switch wired to the first, and both must flip together. This is why the bawdy joints, catalogued fully in the series' treatment of the play's double meanings, fail in translation at a higher rate than the witty ones, and why their failure is so often silent: a cleaned line reads smoothly, and smoothness is the perfect disguise for an amputation.

One more property of the play's punning raises the stakes beyond the local joke. The wordplay is load-bearing. The servants' chain in the first scene is the feud rehearsed as verbal sport minutes before the same men reach for steel, so that the audience learns the city's hatred as a habit of language before meeting it as a habit of arms. Mercutio's death stops the punning engine of the play at its loudest cylinder; after the third act's fight the verbal temperature drops, and the tragedy's second half runs on a leaner fuel of antithesis and oxymoron. M. M. Mahood's Shakespeare's Wordplay (1957) established the reading on which this article relies: the quibbles in Shakespeare are not decoration scattered over a serious structure but part of the structure, verbal ligatures binding scene to scene and theme to theme. A translator who treats each pun as a detachable ornament will misprice every decision in the triage, saving the laugh and spending the ligament. The catalogue below therefore records, for each hard joint, not only what the joke was but what work the joke was doing when the border stopped it.

## Mercutio's Dying Joke: The Grave Man at the Border

The hardest single line in the play to translate is nine words long, and its speaker is dying as he says it. Mercutio, stabbed under Romeo's arm in the third act's street fight, has already minimised the wound with a performer's instinct: "Ay, ay, a scratch, a scratch" (3.1). Then, led off toward a house, he makes his exit line a pun. "Ask for me tomorrow, and you shall find me a grave man" (3.1). The sentence promises that the jester will be serious by morning, and it delivers the information that he will be in his grave by morning, and the two messages are the same message arriving by two doors. The wit is the wound's measure: a man who can still fuse jest and corpse in one word is performing the exact aliveness the feud is in the act of cancelling. Editors from the Folger Shakespeare onward gloss the double sense because modern English has already half-lost it; grave as an adjective meaning solemn survives, but the instantaneous fusion, heard rather than parsed, belongs to the word's older weather.

German offers the translator no door at all. The German word for a grave, Grab, and the German for serious, ernst or feierlich, share no syllable, and no German adjective for solemnity leans toward burial. August Wilhelm Schlegel, whose German Romeo und Julia of 1796 and 1797 set the standard that German stages obeyed for two centuries, renders the promise as einen stillen Mann, a quiet man, a still man. The choice is intelligent and the loss is total, and both facts belong in the catalogue. The still man of tomorrow is indeed the dead man; the corpse's defining property, its silence, is carried across. What does not cross is the mechanism, the experience of one word meaning wit and burial in the same breath. Schlegel selected one sense at the cost of suppressing the other, the manoeuvre Delabastita's typology files as the rendering of a pun by a non-punning phrase. Later German translators faced the identical wall at the identical line, which acquits Schlegel of any personal failure: the loss belongs to the language pair, and only its handling, quiet, apt, and openly incomplete, is his.

### Why does the grave pun fall where the curse beside it survives?

The curse survives because it works by force: any language with imprecation can curse two houses, and the act lands even when the wording changes. The grave pun works by fusion: one word must mean solemn and tomb in the same breath, and where no such word exists the mechanism itself is gone, leaving only a report of it.

The same dying speech contains Mercutio's other famous sentence, the thrice-repeated "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1), and the comparison instructs. A curse is an act before it is a mechanism: its force travels through any language that possesses imprecation, and Schlegel's rendering, distributing the devil and the hangman across the repetitions where Shakespeare repeats the plague, keeps the speech's dramatic violence entire. The grave pun is a mechanism before it is an act: remove the fused word and nothing remains to perform. The border therefore sorts the speech's two set pieces by kind, sparing what works by force and stopping what works by fusion, and a translator's grief falls precisely where the audience's admiration was most mechanical.

The plague curse deserves its own line in the ledger, because its survival is only partial and the partiality is instructive. Shakespeare's repeated plague is not three curses but one curse with an echo built into the play's machinery: plague is the force that will seal Friar John in a quarantined house in the fifth act, stopping the letter that would have saved the lovers, so Mercutio's word reaches forward and touches the catastrophe's trigger. Schlegel's devil and hangman invoke punishment without disease, and the echo dies. German audiences meet a catastrophe of fate and solemn accident where English audiences meet one wired through a dying man's vocabulary. The substitution keeps the curse's heat and spends its wiring, and it stands in the catalogue as the clearest proof that a translation can succeed at the level of the speech while failing at the level of the play's design. Triage decisions compound: each local choice either preserves or severs threads that run the length of the text, and the threads are invisible to anyone auditing line by line.

## The Opening Scene: A Chain of Puns as the Feud in Miniature

Shakespeare begins the tragedy with a relay race of words. Sampson and Gregory, servants of Capulet, warm themselves for violence by trading on coals and colliers, on choler and collar, each man handing the next a sound and daring him to find the insult inside it, until the fence of talk becomes a fence of swords and Sampson bites his thumb at the Montague men (1.1). The chain is paronymy in Delabastita's sorting, near-identical forms colliding, and its dramatic function is exact: Verona's hatred is shown to be a verbal habit before it is a physical one, a city that has learned to hear offence in any syllable. The bawdy layer runs underneath, in the boasting about maidenheads and standing, so the scene's wordplay carries the feud and the body's comedy in the same breath. A full anatomy of the chain and its cousins across the play stands in the series' account of [the puns and quibbles that structure the play's talk, from the servants' coals to the death scene](/2025/01/17/puns-and-quibbles-romeo-juliet/); the question here is narrower and colder, namely what becomes of the relay when the batons are made of English.

The two great documented answers diverge so completely that together they map the strategy space. Schlegel, whose programme was formal correspondence, re-punned the scene in German: the servants' prose in his version fences with German jokes of its own, rebuilt at the same joints, keeping the stichomythic pace of insult answering insult at the English rhythm. The jokes are not Shakespeare's jokes; they are German accidents stationed where the English accidents stood, which is the definition of substitution and the boldest item in Delabastita's inventory. Pasternak, whose programme was the independent artistic work, dissolved the chain into general boasting: his servants swagger, trade insults, and fight, the plot loses nothing a synopsis would record, and the demonstration that hatred in this city is a habit of speech vanishes from the play's first minutes. The Russian audience meets the brawl as an event. The English audience meets it as a symptom. Both translations are by masters, both are defensible from their stated principles, and the distance between them at this one joint is the whole subject of the present article in miniature: what a translator believes translation is decides, in advance, which losses the text will be asked to bear.

Between those poles the scene's fate in other traditions can be graded quickly. Prose translations, from Wieland's German prose of the seventeen-sixties through the French prose line, tend toward Pasternak's end of the scale without his lyric compensation: the sense of the boasting crosses, the relay does not, and the scene reads as exposition with insults. Verse translations with a formal programme tend toward Schlegel's end: some verbal fencing is rebuilt because the form demands energy at the joint. Neither tendency is a law, and individual translators break their tradition's habit in both directions. What the record will not show is a version that carries the English chain itself, collier by choler by collar, into any language whose words for those things refuse to rhyme. The chain is the purest case in the play of Catford's theorem: the mechanism is the message, the mechanism is English, and the only choices are rebuilding, replacing, or reporting the loss.

## The Wordplay Duel: How Mercutio Scores a Friendship in Puns

Between the servants' chain and the death scene stands the play's most sustained piece of competitive punning, the street rally of the second act in which Mercutio tests whether the night has changed Romeo. The rally's stakes are easy to miss in any language, because they are carried by the wordplay's conduct rather than by anything the friends say outright. Romeo went into the Capulet feast silent, lovesick, and monosyllabic, a Petrarchan patient reciting symptoms. He returns at dawn trading blow for blow, and Mercutio's delight is diagnostic: "Now art thou sociable, now art thou Romeo" is the verdict the rally exists to reach, and it is reached by scoring the exchanges. The goose that runs through the rally, the pump and its flower, the jest sized as a single-soled shoe, are not decorations on the test. They are the test's instruments, and a translation that carries the conversation's content while flattening its instruments has preserved the examination's paperwork and lost the examination.

The rally exposes a class of translation difficulty the set pieces do not, because its wordplay is distributed rather than concentrated. A famous pun can be besieged by a translator for a week; a rally of forty quick exchanges, each turning on a small sound, must be rebuilt at speaking speed across a whole scene, and the rebuilding either sustains the impression of two minds fencing or it does not. Schlegel's prose in these exchanges keeps the tempo the audit of his text records, insult and riposte arriving at the English rhythm, and tempo is itself a translation achievement at exactly the joints where individual jokes are being replaced wholesale. The lesson generalises grimly for the catalogue: some losses are statistical rather than local. A rally can lose every single pun and keep its life, if the replacements keep arriving at the right speed with the right aggression, or keep every pun's sense in careful prose and die on its feet. Directors understand this instinctively and cut or commission accordingly; readers of parallel texts can watch it happen, since the rally is where literal versions most visibly slow down.

Mercutio's function in the machine deserves its sentence in a translation audit, because he is the character whose being is wordplay. The Queen Mab speech is a set piece of invention, the conjuration a set piece of obscenity, the rally a set piece of competition, and the death a set piece of fusion, and the four together mean that a translation's Mercutio is the index of its whole verbal policy. A version whose Mercutio merely says witty things has failed differently from a version whose Mercutio does witty things with words, and audiences feel the difference without naming it, since the character's electricity is the play's proof that language in Verona is a form of action. When the punning stops at his death, the tragedy's temperature drops in every language, because the drop is architectural. But the height from which it drops is set by the translation's verbal energy in his scenes, and a flattened Mercutio flattens the tragedy's hinge along with its jokes. The triage ledger therefore carries an entry no line-by-line audit will show: the aggregate voltage of one character's scenes, spent or saved as a block.

## Three Sounds, One Syllable: Ay, I, and Eye

Juliet's crisis in the third act is staged as a problem in phonetics. The Nurse has brought word of Tybalt dead and Romeo banished, and Juliet begs for the one syllable that will tell her whether Romeo lives: "Say thou but 'ay,' / And that bare vowel 'I' shall poison more / Than the death-darting eye of cockatrice; / I am not I, if there be such an 'ay'" (3.2). Three words, the affirmation ay, the pronoun I, and the organ eye, share a single sound, and Shakespeare builds the speech on the identity: the yes that saves her is homophonous with the self that speaks and the eye that weeps. Homophony is the most language-bound of all the pun families, since it depends on mergers in pronunciation that each language performed differently across its history. A receiving language in which yes, I, and eye fall apart into three distinct sounds cannot stage the speech's logic at all; it can only report that the logic existed.

The Schlegel edition's handling of this passage is the most honest document in the whole history of the problem, and it deserves quotation. The German text prints Juliet's speech and appends a bracketed note: "Ein Wortspiel mit den Wörtern aye (ja), I (ich) und eye (Auge), die alle gleich ausgesprochen werden," a wordplay on the words aye (yes), I, and eye, which are all pronounced alike. The note is scholarship standing where poetry failed, an accurate label affixed to an empty space. Nothing in the German lines performs what the note describes; a German reader learns that a fusion occurred in English the way a museum label reports that a destroyed fresco was beautiful. Delabastita's typology gives this manoeuvre its own category, the editorial technique, and the category carries no shame. A footnote is the strategy of a translator who refuses both forgery and silence: the meaning is saved, the laugh or the shiver is buried, and the burial is at least marked with a stone. Editions aimed at study lean on the device constantly, and the Schlegel circle's willingness to confess in its own margins remains the gold standard of the practice, since a text that marks its gaps can be trusted at the joints where it claims success.

The stage, of course, has no margins. An acting text cannot footnote, which is why the ay passage marks the point where reading editions and performing texts of the play in translation part company most sharply: the study edition confesses, the stage edition must either invent or amputate, and invention at this joint has defeated every documented attempt. The passage thus becomes the catalogue's limiting case for the editorial strategy itself. Footnotes serve readers. The play was written for hearers, and any account of translation that rests content with the note has quietly changed the work's medium in the act of saving its meaning. That quiet change is sometimes the best available bargain, and the catalogue records it as such, priced and labelled, rather than as a victory.

## The Bawdy Joints: Medlars, Soles, and Double Senses That Die Twice

Mercutio's conjuration of the absent Romeo, delivered to Benvolio in the street outside the Capulet orchard, reaches the play's most concentrated knot of bawdy wordplay. He imagines Romeo sitting under a medlar tree, wishes his mistress were that kind of fruit, and lands on the folk names of the medlar and the pear, the open-arse and the poperin pear, in a sentence whose obscenity is carried entirely by slang (2.1). The medlar was proverbially the fruit that is rotten before it is ripe, its folk name a piece of anatomy, and the pear's name carried a Flemish place and a sexual verb inside it. The joke is a small museum of English folk vocabulary, and every label in the museum is written in English. The series' gloss of [the play's bawdy language and the double meanings it trades in](/2025/01/24/bawdy-double-meanings-romeo-juliet/) sets out the senses; at the border, the senses are precisely what cannot travel, because a receiving language's fruit slang grew in different orchards.

Schlegel's handling of the passage is the catalogue's most instructive act of surrender. He gives three decorous lines in which Romeo sits leaning against a tree and wishes his sweetheart were the ripe fruit that fell into his lap. Tree, fruit, and lap survive; the medlar, its folk name, the pear, and the joke's entire anatomy are gone. Two features of the decision deserve emphasis. First, the pun was untranslatable in its English form, since its mechanism is a set of English slang equations, but substitution was not logically excluded: a German coarseness of some other kind could have been stationed at the joint, and Schlegel declined to station one. Where the knot could not be untied, he cut it, and chose the cut that preserved the scene's respectability. Second, the choice aligns with the version's larger economy, catalogued in the audit of his text as smoothing: where the English is roughest, in astrology, in plague, in obscenity, the German grows decorous. The medlar passage is therefore not merely a translation failure but a document of what a receiving culture, at a particular moment of its self-fashioning, was willing to hear from its adopted classic. Translation losses have authors as well as causes, and some of them are the receiving age's taste wearing the mask of necessity.

The rally in the second act shows the bawdy problem at a lighter pressure and lets the catalogue grade a subtler loss. Romeo, restored to wit by the night, trades courtesies with Mercutio until the talk turns to shoes: Mercutio scores Romeo's jest as a single-soled jest, solely singular for the singleness, and the exchange flowers into the pink of courtesy and a pump well flowered, where sole carries the shoe and singleness at once and flower leans toward flour (2.4). No single word here is obscene; the wit is social, a fencing match whose scoring system is the pun itself, since Mercutio is testing whether love has unmanned his friend's verbal reflexes. A translation can carry the courtesies, the shoe, and the flower as plain sense, and most do. What evaporates is the scoring: the audience of the translation watches two friends chat where the English audience watches a duel with declared points. The catalogue files this class of loss under the quietest heading in its ledger, the loss of function without loss of content, and it may be the commonest entry of all, because it leaves no scar for a reader of the translation to notice.

## The Name Problem: Wherefore, Roses, and the Play's Anti-Pun

The play's most famous speech about language argues, in effect, against the pun's whole philosophy. Juliet on the balcony reasons that names are conventions without substance: "What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other word would smell as sweet" (2.2). A pun is the counter-doctrine embodied, the claim that the sound of a word is part of its substance, that grave cannot be renamed without losing the tomb. The tragedy is written by a man who held both doctrines at once and set them fighting: Juliet's philosophy says the word is arbitrary, Mercutio's practice says the word is fate, and the plot, in which a name is precisely what kills the lovers, grades the argument for Mercutio. Translation replays the fight at the level of the text itself. Every translator must decide whether Juliet is right, in which case the sounds may be discarded with the senses preserved, or whether Mercutio is right, in which case discarding the sounds discards meaning. The strategies on the shelf are the possible answers, and a version's handling of the rose speech often predicts its handling of everything else.

The wherefore line concentrates the problem into one word. "O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?" (2.2) asks why, not where, and the mistranslation of the question into a search for Romeo's location is the most durable error in the play's popular afterlife in English itself. Translation into other languages has repeatedly had to recover the why against the gravitational pull of the balcony image, and the Chinese record supplies the catalogue's neatest documented recovery: Liang Shiqiu's rendering keeps the why, so that his Juliet asks the philosophical question the speech requires rather than calling down into a garden. The case matters beyond its charm. It proves that loss at a border is not a one-way tax on the receiving language; a careful translation can restore a sense that the source's own popular tradition has worn smooth, paying the source text a debt its home audience had stopped collecting. Fidelity, at such joints, is something the foreigner occasionally supplies to the natives.

Pasternak's rose couplet shows the same joint from the poet's side. His Juliet asks what a name means and answers with the rose that smells a rose whether it is called a rose or not, the argument carried entire into Russian in two lines of verse. The argument crosses because it is an argument, the most portable cargo a text can carry. Set it beside the same translator's Prologue, where the compounds fail, and the play's own hierarchy stands revealed inside one version: doctrine travels, mechanism stays. A translator reading the play for the first time can learn the whole campaign from those two exhibits, since everything else in the text sorts itself between the rose, which any good language can re-argue, and the grave, which no language can re-fuse.

## Sound-Play Beyond the Pun: Gallop, Gold, and the Womb-Tomb Rhyme

Not all of the play's sound machinery is punning in the strict sense, and the looser machinery crosses borders at a visibly higher rate, which is itself evidence about where the true wall stands. Juliet's epithalamium opens with an imperative, "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds" (3.2), and the verb carries gall inside it by sound: the bitterness that will fill the speech's close, where love's heralds turn to thoughts of death, is phonetically present in its first word. Translators into most languages keep the horses and the haste; the hidden bitterness, a paronymic shadow rather than a pun proper, falls away unless the receiving verb happens to cast the same shadow. Romeo's payment to the apothecary in Mantua works at the level of sense rather than sound: "There is thy gold, worse poison to men's souls" (5.1). The gold crosses every border as metal and as metaphor, since the inversion of wealth and poison is an argument, and arguments travel. What thins in transit is the sound-kinship that binds gold backward to the gallop and the gall, part of the metallic thread the English text runs from the orchard to the tomb. Arguments cross; acoustics stay home. The distinction explains the uneven survival rate across the whole catalogue, since the play's meaning is stored in both media and only one of them is portable by nature.

The Friar's first speech supplies the catalogue's counterweight, a documented case of a verbal mechanism rebuilt whole in another language. Shakespeare's Lorenzo preaches the earth's paradox in rhymed couplets: the earth that is nature's mother is her tomb, and what is her burying grave is her womb (2.3). The figure turns on the womb and tomb rhyme, two words whose English sound enforces the doctrine that birth and burial share a body. Schlegel answers figure with figure: "Die Mutter der Natur, die Erd, ist auch ihr Grab, und was ihr Schoß gebar, sinkt tot in ihn hinab," the mother of nature, the earth, is also her grave, and what her womb bore sinks dead back into it. Womb and grave do not rhyme in German as they do in English, so the German locks Schoß and Grab together as a conceptual rhyme at the couplet's close, rebuilding the enforcement by different carpentry. Delabastita's typology has a drawer for this too, the replacement of a pun by a related rhetorical device, and the entry matters disproportionately to its size. It proves the wall is not uniform. Where the source mechanism is a figure, a pattern of thought wearing sound, a poet-translator can sometimes re-tailor the sound to the thought in the receiving cloth. Where the mechanism is a lexical accident, a single word's double citizenship, no tailoring avails. The catalogue's verdicts are therefore issued joint by joint, and any theory that pronounces on the whole play at once, possible or impossible, has failed to notice that the play is built from both kinds of joint.

## Oxymorons and Antitheses: The Sound-Play That Travels Best

The play's other great verbal engine, the oxymoron, crosses borders at a rate that shames the pun, and the contrast is diagnostic. Romeo enters the play speaking in fused contradictions, "O brawling love, O loving hate," feather of lead, bright smoke, cold fire, sick health (1.1), and the device depends on no lexical accident at all: any language that can place two opposed terms in one phrase can rebuild it, since the fusion happens in the thought rather than in the dictionary. Juliet's parting oxymoron, sweet sorrow (2.2), is the device's most quoted instance, and its portability is the rule rather than the exception. Antithesis behaves the same way at the scale of the sentence: the Prince's closing audit, the Friar's paradoxes of poison and medicine, and Juliet's great chain of opposites over the banished Romeo all state their effects as structures of thought, and structures emigrate. A rough law of the catalogue can be framed from the comparison. The more an effect depends on what words mean together, the better it travels; the more it depends on what one word happens to contain, the worse. Meaning is shared human property in a way that vocabulary accidents are not.

Pasternak's handling of sweet sorrow marks the law's one instructive exception, and it is an exception about method rather than about language. His parting trades the oxymoron for the plain statement of a reluctance to leave: the feeling crosses, the figure does not, although Russian could have carried a fused contradiction as readily as English. The spend was not forced by the border. It was chosen by the levelling method, which converts figure into statement as a matter of policy across the whole text, so that the version's losses include a class the wall cannot be blamed for. Auditors of translated wordplay therefore keep two ledgers whether they say so or not: the losses the language pair imposed, and the losses the translator's poetics imposed on top of them. Schlegel's medlar belongs partly to the second ledger, since decorum, not German, declined the substitute grossness; Schlegel's grave man belongs wholly to the first. Confusing the ledgers produces the two standard errors of translation criticism, blaming craftsmen for geography and crediting geography to craftsmen, and the catalogue has kept them separate at every joint for exactly this reason.

## The Prologue's Compounds: A Whole Cosmology in Two Words

The play's first fourteen lines contain its most compressed translation problem, and it is not, strictly, a pun. "From forth the fatal loins of these two foes / A pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life," and the Chorus goes on to call them death-mark'd (Prologue). The two compounds compress an entire cosmology into adjectives: the lovers are crossed by stars, marked by death, their fates legible in a sky that governs by influence. A compound is a mechanism of the same family as the pun, a fusion performed inside a word's boundaries, and it fails at borders for the same reason, that the receiving language must either possess the fusing habit at that exact material or unpack the compound into a phrase and lose the compression. Unpacking is the standard fate. Pasternak's Prologue keeps the sonnet's frame and lets the astrology go, his fate setting snares where Shakespeare's stars cross; Schlegel likewise drops the star-crossing entire, rendering the lovers as death-dedicated, todgeweiht, and letting the marked half of the doctrine carry the line.

The German case repays a closer look, because it acquits the language and indicts the programme. German compounds as freely as English, more freely; a German star-compound at the joint was a formal possibility in a way that a German grave-fusion was not. The star-crossing fell not to linguistic impossibility but to the smoothing economy the audit of Schlegel's text documents at every elevated joint, in which the Prologue's astrology stands first on the list of knots quietly relieved. The distinction matters to the catalogue's justice. Catford's theorem covers the grave man; it does not cover the star-crossed lovers, whose loss belongs in the second ledger, the translator's poetics and the receiving culture's taste for solemnity over machinery. A criticism that files both under untranslatability has surrendered its sharpest instrument, the ability to say which losses were necessary. The necessary ones excuse the translator. The chosen ones describe him, and description, not excuse, is what an audit owes the record.

The compounds also explain a curiosity of the play's afterlife in translation: its most quoted phrases abroad are rarely its most Shakespearean mechanisms. The star-crossed lovers circulate in language after language as a borrowed English label, carried by the direct-copy drawer of the shelf, because the receiving tongues found the concept indispensable and the compound untransportable, and so imported the luggage unopened. A phrase that must be borrowed whole is a phrase whose translation has been declined, and the global career of star-crossed is the compliment the world's languages pay to a fusion they could admire and not rebuild. The Prologue's doctrine, that the lovers are read by the sky, crosses everywhere as argument. Its wording crosses almost nowhere as wording. The play's first lines thus administer, before any character speaks, the exact examination the rest of the text will keep setting.

## Declared Exchange Rates: What Translators Say They Are Doing

A translation's preface, letter, or workshop commentary is where its pricing policy is declared, and the play's major versions are unusually well documented in their declarations. Pasternak stated his rate in advance and in writing: the letter of March 1942 and the essay on translating Shakespeare set out the doctrine of the independent artistic work, the daily sectional discipline, and the refusal of the copy as the goal, so that a reader of his Romeo and Juliet holds the exchange rate before holding the goods. Schlegel declared his rate in his practice's most public feature, the formal programme itself, metre for metre and form for form, with the bracketed confession at the ay passage functioning as a footnote-rate published inside the text. Frank Günther's bilingual German Shakespeare, appearing since 1976, institutionalises the declaration as a workshop commentary in which departures from the letter are itemised by the translator himself, the auditors' work done pre-emptively by the audited. The pattern across three centuries is that the versions which endure are the ones whose pricing is inspectable. A reader may dispute Schlegel's rate or Pasternak's, but the dispute can proceed from documents, and criticism that can proceed from documents is the only kind that settles anything.

The counter-case is the anonymous commercial version, the school text and the acting edition churned out without a named translator's stated policy, in which the shelf is raided silently and the reader cannot tell a priced loss from an unnoticed one. Such versions dominate the world's classrooms by volume, and their Mercutios and servants are where most of the earth's students actually meet the play's wordplay, thinned by hands that left no ledger. The catalogue's severest judgement is reserved for this class, not because its losses are the largest, though they usually are, but because they are the only losses that cannot be audited, repaid, or even mourned at their correct addresses. A translation that declares nothing asks to be trusted as a window. The history assembled here suggests the window is always a painting, and the honest painters sign their work and publish their palette. The reader's defence is to demand the signature.

## The Strategy Shelf: Substitution, Compensation, Footnotes, Sacrifice

Delabastita's inventory, drawn from the observed behaviour of working translators rather than from theory's preferences, remains the most useful map of the choices, and the play's history exhibits every item on it. The inventory's value for the present catalogue is that it replaces the single question, can this pun be translated, with a shelf of named operations whose costs differ, so that a translator's page can be audited rather than merely praised or blamed. The four strategies the brief for this article names, substitution, compensation, the footnote, and sacrifice, are the shelf's main compartments; the map adds three smaller drawers that the play's record also uses.

Substitution, in the typology's notation the rendering of a pun by a pun, stations a target-language wordplay at the source wordplay's joint. It is the only strategy that preserves the local experience entire, the hearer's double take at the same moment of the scene, and it is correspondingly the rarest, because it requires the receiving language to offer a suitable accident at a suitable word within reach of the same line. Schlegel's re-punned opening scene is the play's great documented instance at the scale of a whole passage. Where the accident does not exist nearby, substitution is not a strategy but a wish, and translators who announce it as their general method are describing their hopes rather than their pages.

Compensation, the introduction of wordplay where the source has none, treats the play rather than the line as the unit of account. A pun surrendered at an impossible joint is repaid by an invented pun at a joint where the receiving language offers luck and the source text is comparatively plain, so that the translation's total density of wordplay approximates the original's even though the map of its positions has been redrawn. Delabastita's corpus work records translators doing this as a matter of practice, and the practice reframes the theoretical debate: Catford's theorem governs addresses, while compensation governs the neighbourhood, and a text can be locally untranslatable at every hard address while remaining, as a neighbourhood, verbally alive.

### What does compensation require a translator to keep count of?

Compensation requires a running balance of wordplay across the whole play: each surrendered pun entered as a debt, each invented pun as a payment, and the account kept in two currencies, quantity and register. A bawdy debt is not honestly repaid by a clerical quibble three acts later, nor a deathbed jest by enlivening a servant.

Compensation requires a ledger. A translator practising it must track the running balance of wordplay across the whole play, noting each surrendered pun as a debt and each invented pun as a payment, and must place payments where the receiving language offers accidents, which are rarely at the scene of the debt. The discipline also demands restraint about kind: a bawdy debt is not honestly repaid with a clerical quibble three acts later, and a dying man's pun cannot be offset by enlivening a servant. The ledger is therefore kept in two currencies at once, quantity and register, and its honest keeping is what separates compensation from mere decoration of the translation with jokes of the translator's own.

The footnote, filed in the typology among the editorial techniques, changes the work's contract with its audience. A footnoted translation addresses a reader rather than a hearer, offers the source pun in its original wording with an explanation, and delivers knowledge about wordplay in place of wordplay. The Schlegel edition's bracketed confession at the ay passage is the strategy's noblest early document in this play's history; the modern study edition, with its glossed margins, is the strategy industrialised. Its cost is timing. A joke explained is a joke whose moment has passed, and drama lives in moments. Its benefit is truth: of all the strategies, only the footnote refuses to counterfeit, and for a reader whose purpose is study the refusal is worth more than a substitute laugh.

### Why does the footnote rescue the sense and bury the joke?

A pun lives in the time of hearing, its second sense arriving with the first or not at all. A footnote lives in the time of study, sending the reader to an explanation after the moment has closed. Explanation can state the meanings, so the sense is rescued; the event cannot be restaged, so the joke is buried.

A pun works in the time of hearing: the second sense must arrive in the same instant as the first, before the sentence moves on, or the effect collapses into a puzzle. A footnote operates in the time of study, sending the reader down the page and back with an explanation assembled after the moment has closed. The sense is therefore rescued entire, since explanation can state both meanings with perfect accuracy, while the joke is buried under the explanation's own weight, since a fusion understood retrospectively is a mechanism admired rather than an event undergone. The footnote is a museum label on a demolished building: everything it says is true, and the building is still down.

Sacrifice, the rendering of a pun by a non-punning phrase, is the shelf's largest compartment and the honest workhorse of the play's translation history. It divides into two grades. The finer grade selects one sense and lets the other go, as Schlegel's still man keeps the corpse and drops the jest. The coarser grade paraphrases both senses in a non-punning conjunction, stating solemn and buried in successive words, which informs the reader and flattens the line into a report. Omission, the typology's pun-to-zero, stands at the compartment's far end: the passage containing the pun is dropped, the medlar's fate in decorous centuries and the fate of whole bawdy exchanges in school texts of every language. The three smaller drawers complete the map: the rhetorical replacement, which trades the pun for alliteration, rhyme, or paradox of comparable energy, as at the Friar's womb and tomb; the direct copy, which prints the English pun untranslated inside the receiving text, a device of bilingual editions; and the zero-to-pun insertion, new material carrying wordplay with no source warrant, compensation's unlicensed cousin. A translator of this play will use every drawer in the shelf before the fifth act. The audit question is never whether the shelf was used, but whether the choices were priced.

## Schlegel's Triage, Audited Joint by Joint

The fullest single audit of the strategies in action is the text examined at length in the series' account of [the Schlegel-Tieck German Romeo and Juliet and its two centuries of argument](/2030/04/26/schlegel-tieck-german-translation/), because Schlegel's programme, formal fidelity in metre and structure joined to the polished diction of Weimar classicism, forced a decision at every joint and left the decisions legible. Read as a triage record, his Romeo und Julia distributes itself across the whole shelf. At the first scene he substitutes, rebuilding German fencing where the English chain stood. At the medlar he omits toward decorum, declining substitution where the available German coarseness would have violated his receiving culture's contract with its new classic. At the plague curse he substitutes an imprecation and severs the disease echo. At the grave man he sacrifices to a single sense, chosen with a poet's ear for the sense that keeps the death inside the line. At the ay passage his edition footnotes, confessing in brackets what the verse could not perform. At the Friar's entrance he reinvents, locking womb and grave as a conceptual rhyme where the English locked them as a sound. No other translation of the play exhibits all six operations within five acts, which is why his text remains the teaching case: the triage is complete enough to grade as a system.

The grading has been contested for two centuries, and the contest itself clarifies the criteria. The celebrants of the naturalisation, Georg Gottfried Gervinus at their head from 1849, claimed a German classic, and as cultural history the claim holds: the version was adopted, memorised, and staged until its wording felt like the work itself. The auditors, whose classic statement in English is Margaret E. Atkinson's line-by-line study of 1958 and whose work continues in the reassessment edited by Christa Jansohn and Stefan Knödler in 2023, found the smoothing the case file records and priced it exactly. The practitioner Frank Günther, whose own German Shakespeare has been appearing since 1976, applies a third standard, playability for a present audience, and documents his departures in a workshop commentary that concedes the auditors' facts while rejecting their premise. For the pun question specifically, the auditors win on the evidence of the joints: the losses are real, located, and structural, and no account of the German play is honest that does not list them. The celebrants win on the evidence of the centuries: a text that had merely lost would not have been loved. The synthesis the catalogue adopts is that Schlegel's triage was the best available under his receiving culture's constraints, and that its failures cluster exactly where theory predicts failure must cluster, at the lexical accidents, while its successes cluster at the figures, where a poet's redistribution could operate.

### How can one translator invent boldly in the first scene and surrender in the third act?

The difference is supply, not temperament. The first scene's chain runs on near-rhymes in prose banter, and German stocked insults and market slang near those joints, so invention had material. The third act's fused word in a dying speech offered German nothing at the joint, and the speech's elevation forbade importing a lower-register joke as compensation on the spot.

The contrast is not inconsistency but diagnosis. In the first scene the material is a chain of near-rhymes in prose banter, and German, a language rich in its own insult-rhymes and market slang, offered accidents near the joints, so invention had material to work with. In the third act the material is a single fused word in a dying speech, the most compressed form of the problem, and German offered nothing at the joint, while the speech's elevation forbade importing a joke from a lower register to compensate on the spot. A translator's boldness is therefore not a character trait but a reading of local supplies: the same craftsman raids the shelf's top drawer where the language stocks it and its bottom drawer where the cupboard is bare, and the map of his choices is a map of the two languages' relative accidents.

## Pasternak's Ledger: Structure Saved, Wordplay Spent

The Russian case, set out in full in the series' study of [Pasternak's Russian Romeo and Juliet, the version that filled the Soviet stage](/2030/05/03/pasternak-russian-translation/), tests the shelf from the opposite programme. Pasternak declared in advance that a translation should be an independent artistic work producing an impression of life rather than of literature, and his Romeo and Juliet, made in Chistopol in the winter of 1941 to 1942 on a Detgiz commission, obeys the declaration with a consistency that lets the auditor predict the text from the principle. Whatever in Shakespeare is carried by arrangement crosses: the Prologue keeps its sonnet frame, the lovers' feast exchange keeps its shared sonnet, the antiphonal architecture of the orchard scene survives intact. Whatever is carried by a verbal mechanism is spent: the Prologue's star-crossed and death-marked compounds vanish into a fate that sets snares, the parting's sweet sorrow flattens into a statement of reluctance, and the servants' opening chain dissolves into general boasting. Portability is the exact measure of survival in this version, and the auditor who sorts the play's effects by their portability can reconstruct the Russian text's contents before opening it.

Georgy Shengeli's unpublished audit of 1945, "Pasternak's Shakespeare," charged falsification on a statistical, line-by-line basis, and on the wordplay entries the charge is simply accurate: the mechanisms are not there. The defence is not that the mechanisms are secretly present but that the audit applies the equivalence standard the translator had publicly renounced in his letter of March 1942, and that the stage's contrary verdict, decades of productions, twenty-nine standalone editions of this play, and a combined print run near 1,700,000 copies, grades the version by the standard it set itself. For the pun question, Pasternak's text is the limiting case of sacrifice practised as a system rather than a series of local defeats. He does not lose the puns one by one, the way Schlegel does, weighing each joint. He declines the category, trading the play's whole verbal mechanism for momentum, speakability, and a single lyric register, and the trade's honesty lies in its totality: a Russian reader is never in doubt about what kind of object is in hand, even though the object cannot show its reader where the English joints were. The catalogue prices the two masters' methods as mirror images. Schlegel preserves the map and loses named towns. Pasternak redraws the map entirely and loses the place-names while keeping the journey's speed.

### What crosses a border easily in this play, if wordplay cannot?

Proverbs cross nearly whole, because every culture keeps a stock of equivalent saws; the Friar's capped couplets keep their authority in Russian where the puns around them dissolve. Below proverbs on the gradient come arguments and images, which cross with losses of accent; then figures, which cross only when a poet rebuilds them; and last, puns.

The Friar's sententious couplets give the answer from inside Pasternak's own text. Proverbs are portable because every culture keeps a stock of equivalent saws, and the Friar's proverb-wisdom, the capped couplets with which he closes his scenes, retains its gnomic authority in Russian where the puns around it dissolve. The portability gradient runs from the proverb, which crosses nearly whole, through the argument and the image, which cross with losses of accent, down to the figure, which crosses only when a poet rebuilds it, and ends at the pun, which crosses only by accident or by replacement. A translator's first professional act on opening this play is to sort its effects along that gradient, since the sorting predicts the entire campaign: what will be easy, what will be labour, and what will be loss.

## The Second Half Problem: Translating the Quiet After Mercutio

A translator's ledger has a temporal dimension that the joint-by-joint audit misses, because the play's wordplay is not distributed evenly across its length. The punning is front-loaded by design: the first three acts run on chains, rallies, conjurations, and quibbles, and after Mercutio's death the engine stops. The tragedy's second half speaks a leaner language of antithesis, oxymoron, and plain statement under pressure, Juliet's chains of opposites and the Friar's paradoxes doing the work that Mercutio's fusions did before. The gradient is itself a meaning, the sound of a world losing its verbal playfulness as the deaths begin, and it imposes on the translator a discipline that compensation, carelessly practised, would violate. A version that repays first-half debts by enlivening the fourth and fifth acts has balanced its ledger and betrayed the design, stocking with jokes the very acts Shakespeare deliberately starved of them. Honest compensation observes the gradient: payments are made in the same act, ideally in the same scene, as the debts, or they are not made at all and the loss is confessed instead.

The gradient also reframes the two masters' ledgers one last time. Schlegel's smoothing falls heaviest on the first half's machinery, since that is where the machinery is, and his second half, built of figures and paradoxes, crosses with the higher survival rate his formalism predicts. Pasternak's levelling costs the first half its chains and rallies while his lyric register is, if anything, an enrichment of the second half's weather, which is one reason Russian audiences could experience his text as a great tragedy while missing a comedy of language they never knew had been scheduled. The two versions thus fail at opposite ends of the same gradient in opposite currencies, and a reader comparing them by acts rather than by totals gets the truest picture the catalogue can offer: not two translations of one play but two differently shaped survivals, each preserving the architecture and each spending a different stratum of the verbal life. The audit that reports only totals conceals the shapes. The shapes are the story.

## Other Tongues, Same Wall

The French tradition exhibits the sacrifice strategy at the scale of a national literature. The prose Shakespeares through which France first possessed the plays, Le Tourneur's in the eighteenth century and François-Victor Hugo's between 1859 and 1866, carried the sense with dignity and dropped the verbal machinery as a matter of course, prose being the medium in which a pun's absence leaves the least visible hole. The French stage tradition then inherited texts whose Mercutio could be brilliant in repartee of the translator's manufacture while the grave joke stood in the lines as a plain statement about tomorrow. Japanese exhibits the wall from the opposite side of the world. Tsubouchi Shōyō's Romeo and Juliet of 1910, inside his complete Shakespeare, carried the play in an archaic, kabuki-inflected register whose defence, in Daniel Gallimore's account, is that the archaism was always a deliberate strategy for giving the foreign classic a classical Japanese body; the strategy's cost fell on the lovers' voices first, and Fukuda Tsuneari's postwar retranslations, which governed the acting standard from 1955 into the early nineteen-seventies, rebuilt the dialogue toward speakable verse by other devices. The Japanese argument was never about whether wordplay could cross, since classical Japanese wordplay, the kakekotoba tradition of pivot words, is among the richest on earth. It was about which Japanese the imported classic should be allowed to speak, and the puns were casualties of a decision about register rather than about possibility. The Arabic tradition arrived at the same wall by a road that doubles the distance: the earliest Arabic versions of the play characteristically passed through French, so that Shakespeare's wordplay faced a second triage in a language not its own before facing its Arabic one, and the documented line of translators, from Najib al-Haddad's Martyrs of Love, staged at Cairo in 1891, through Ali Ahmed Bakathir's blank-verse version, to Mohamed Enani's two traversals of the play, worked inside a chain where the puns had already been priced once. The Chinese record adds the mirror-image footnote: Liang Shiqiu's rendering of the wherefore line keeps the why that English criticism had to recover, a reminder that a translation occasionally preserves, by the receiving language's plain grammar, a sense the source's own readers have begun to lose. The wall is real, and it has doors in unexpected places, and none of the doors is at the grave man's address.

## Translation Begins at Home: The English Reader's Own Border

The problem this article has chased across four languages starts, uncomfortably, inside English itself. A modern English reader meeting Mercutio's promise needs the joke glossed, because grave in its solemn sense has faded from the living word's front of house; the Folger Shakespeare, the edition cited throughout this article, annotates the double sense for native speakers whose English is separated from Shakespeare's by four centuries of sound change and semantic drift. Editors gloss the medlar, the poperin pear, the consort, and the sole for the same reason Schlegel's edition confessed at the ay passage: the fusion has stopped performing itself and must be reported. The intralingual border is lower than the interlingual one, since grammar, idiom, and most of the vocabulary hold, but at the punning joints the two borders coincide almost exactly. Where an effect depends on a lexical accident, the accident's expiry date matters more than the reader's passport, and the English of 1595 is, at those joints, a foreign language to the English of any later century.

This is why the study edition in English is the footnote strategy's industrial form, and why its existence reframes the whole inquiry. If English readers themselves receive the puns through annotation, then the difference between the home audience and the foreign one is a difference of degree in the apparatus, not a difference between possession and loss. The German reader of Schlegel's bracketed confession and the English reader of a Folger gloss stand in the same relation to the ay passage: both are told that a fusion occurred, both miss the event, and the German reader's edition was honest about the arrangement two centuries earlier. The reframing does not dissolve the problem, since a glossed pun in a first language still sits inside a living web of surrounding accidents that partially fire, while the translated text's web is rebuilt throughout. But it forbids the lazy form of the impossibility claim, the one that imagines an English audience in unmediated possession of effects that every audience, in every century including the first, has received through some mixture of performance, habit, and help. The border is real. It runs through London as well as through Leipzig, and it has been moving since the play was new.

## Are the Losses Worth Grieving? Johnson Against Mahood

Behind the strategy shelf stands a prior quarrel about whether anything valuable is being lost at all, and the article's verdict depends on settling it. Samuel Johnson, in the Preface to his edition of 1765, pronounced the prosecution's case in the most quoted sentence ever written against the quibble: a quibble was to Shakespeare the fatal Cleopatra for which he lost the world and was content to lose it, a fascination that led him out of his way, sacrificed reason, propriety, and truth, and cost him the dignity of his serious scenes. On Johnson's valuation, a translator who drops the puns is dropping a vice, and the German smoothing is an improvement wearing the mask of a loss. Mahood's Shakespeare's Wordplay answered in 1957 with the structural reading: tracked across a whole play, the quibbles form image patterns and thematic ligatures, the servants' coal-chain prefiguring the feud's mechanics, the grave joke concentrating the play's fusion of wit and mortality into its most famous death. On Mahood's valuation, the translator who drops the puns is cutting ligaments, and the smoothing is an amputation.

The adjudication this article delivers is for Mahood, with one clause reserved to Johnson. Mahood wins on the evidence of function: the 1.1 chain demonstrably teaches the audience the feud's verbal habit before its violence, the plague word demonstrably wires Mercutio's curse to the catastrophe, and the grave pun demonstrably performs the aliveness it announces the loss of. Effects that can be traced through the play's design are structure, not ornament, and structure is what translation exists to carry. Johnson's reserved clause concerns register: he saw, correctly, that Shakespeare will buy a pun at the price of a scene's decorum, and the medlar passage is his exhibit, a peak of wit purchased with an obscenity that many receiving cultures, Schlegel's Germany among them, would not pay for at any exchange rate. The translator's triage therefore inherits both insights. The losses are worth grieving because the ligaments are real, and some of the grief is properly directed not at the translator but at the source's own price list, which set certain effects in a currency only English holds. Delabastita's corpus closes the quarrel from the descriptive side: whatever the theorists of value decided, working translators in every tradition behaved as if Mahood were right, spending ingenuity, margins, and whole compensating inventions to keep wordplay alive in their texts. Practice voted with its labour.

## Facing Pages and Surtitles: The Modern Machinery of Confession

Two modern institutions have changed where the confession lives, and both deserve a paragraph in the catalogue's margins. The facing-page bilingual edition, in which the translation stands opposite the English and the reader's eye can commute between them, converts the footnote strategy from an interruption into a condition of reading: the original pun is permanently present, the translation's handling of it permanently inspectable, and the direct-copy drawer of the shelf is held open for the whole book. Günther's bilingual Shakespeare is the play's German monument to the arrangement. Its effect on the translator is disciplinary in a way no review can match, since every surrender is performed in public, beside the evidence, and its effect on the reader is to make the triage itself part of the literary experience, a second drama played in the gutter between the pages. The losses do not shrink. Their visibility becomes total, and visibility, this article has argued throughout, is the difference between a priced loss and a theft.

The surtitle is the confession's opposite, a machinery of concealment imposed by the stage's physics. A surtitled performance, in opera houses and in touring theatre, must compress whole speeches into a line or two of projected text timed to the second, and under that discipline wordplay is the first cargo thrown overboard: a pun cannot be projected, timed, and read in the window a surtitle allows, even where a rendering exists. Audiences of surtitled Shakespeare abroad therefore receive a text from which the punning has been structurally deleted before the translator's own choices begin, and the deletion is invisible because the titles present themselves as a transparent summary rather than as a version. The catalogue notes the institution because it mediates the play's foreign life at a scale print never reached, and because it completes the spectrum this article has traced: from Schlegel's bracketed confession, which told the reader exactly what was missing, to a technology that cannot afford to mention that anything is. Between those poles every translator of the play still works, choosing, joint by joint, how much of the loss the audience will be permitted to know about. The choice about the audience's knowledge is the one decision in the whole triage that is purely ethical, and it is the decision by which the catalogue finally grades them all.

## How an Auditor Counts What a Translation Lost

The verdicts quoted throughout this article rest on a method worth naming, because the method decides what counts as evidence. Delabastita built his inventory from parallel corpora: the source text and its translations laid side by side, joint by joint, in his foundational case the wordplay of Hamlet across its Dutch, French, and German versions. The method's strength is that it replaces impressions with alignments; a claim that a translator compensates can be checked by finding the invented puns at their unpunned addresses. Its limit is the obverse of the same virtue: alignment sees what corresponds and what vanishes at each address, and it cannot see the reading experience whole, the tempo of a rally, the voltage of a Mercutio, the accumulated trust an audience places in a text that keeps delighting it. The two great audit traditions of this play divide along exactly that line. The line-by-line auditors, Shengeli over Pasternak in 1945 and Atkinson over Schlegel in 1958, convict: their alignments are accurate and their verdicts, within the method, unanswerable. The stage and print records acquit: decades of occupancy, editions in the tens, print runs in the millions, a behaviour of cultures rather than of collators. A mature audit holds both instruments and states which one produced each finding. Losses of mechanism are alignment facts and are not negotiable. Losses of life are performance facts and are not visible to alignment at all. Most quarrels about translated wordplay are two auditors brandishing different instruments, each honestly reporting a different stratum of the same event, and the catalogue below was built to keep the strata labelled.

## The Verdict: What "Impossible" Gets Right and Where It Stops

### Has any verbal mechanism in the play ever crossed a border whole?

Figures have crossed whole when a poet rebuilt them: Schlegel locked womb and grave as a conceptual rhyme where English locked them by sound, and the paradox arrived entire. Proverbs cross whole as a class. The lexical accident itself, one word holding two citizenships in English alone, has never crossed whole anywhere; it is replaced, repaid, or reported, never carried.

One class has, and naming it disciplines the whole inquiry. Figures cross when a poet-translator rebuilds them, as Schlegel rebuilt the womb and tomb enforcement in German carpentry, and proverbs cross nearly whole because every culture stocks equivalents. What has never crossed whole is the lexical accident itself, the single word whose two citizenships exist only in English. The distinction is the article's answer to its title: the task is impossible at the level of the accident and possible at the level of the art that surrounds the accident, and a translation is graded by how knowingly it manages the boundary between the two.

The brief for this article asks for a verdict on translating the puns, and the evidence supports a three-part judgement. First, the sigh is justified at the joint: Catford's linguistic untranslatability is a real property of the grave man, the ay passage, and the medlar, and any translator or critic who promises equivalence at those addresses is selling a forgery. Second, the sigh is refuted at the text: Delabastita's record shows the function of wordplay surviving by substitution, compensation, rhetorical replacement, and honest annotation, and the play's two-century life in German, Russian, French, and Japanese is unintelligible unless the punning, redistributed and repriced, came across with the plot. Third, the quality of a translation of this play can be read directly off its triage: whether the translator knew the price of each joint, spent ingenuity where the receiving language offered luck, confessed where it offered none, and kept the ligament functions, the feud in miniature, the curse's wiring, the dying jest's performance of aliveness, at the top of the ledger. Loss in translation is inevitable. Unpriced loss is not. The translators who priced it, Schlegel confessing in his margins and re-inventing at his Friar's couplet, Pasternak declaring his exchange rate in advance and paying it uniformly, produced the versions their cultures could build on. For a reader choosing among translations, the catalogue converts into a practical instrument. The choice is rarely between a faithful version and a free one, since fidelity at the punning joints is not on any shelf; the choice is among declared and undeclared triages. A version with a named translator, a stated method, and an apparatus that confesses its gaps lets the reader hold the map of the losses while reading, which is the nearest thing to bilingual reading that a single language affords. A version that presents itself as a transparent window asks for a trust the record never justifies. Readers who know no word of the receiving language can still audit the choice from outside, by the paratext: prefaces, notes, commentaries, and the presence or absence of the English on facing pages. The pun problem, which begins as the translator's private grief at a desk, ends as the reader's public criterion at a bookshop, and the criterion is the one this article has applied throughout: not whether the version lost, since every version loses, but whether it priced its losses where the reader can inspect the figures.

The word impossible belongs at the head of the translator's worksheet, as the first fact the work must face. It has no business at the end of the worksheet, where the catalogue stands: twelve hard joints, every one of them decided, and the play still punning, in other words, on the far side of every border it has ever crossed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What counts as translating a pun successfully?

Success is graded by function, not by identity. A rendering succeeds when the hearer at the corresponding moment of the scene undergoes the corresponding event: a double take, a laugh with an edge, a word arriving by two doors at once. Identity of mechanism is the rarest path to that success, available only where the receiving language happens to stock the same accident. Substitution reaches success by a different accident at the same joint, compensation by restoring the play's overall density of wordplay after a local surrender, and the footnote reaches a lesser, readerly success by delivering exact knowledge of the effect instead of the effect. A translation fails when the moment passes as plain statement and no repayment is made anywhere: the audience has been given the information and denied the event the information was shaped to produce.

### Q: What is the difference between substitution and compensation?

The difference is the address at which the repair is made. Substitution rebuilds wordplay at the very joint where the source text puns: Schlegel's servants fence with German jokes where Shakespeare's fence with English ones, so the audience's double take lands at the same moment of the scene. Compensation accepts the surrender at the impossible joint and repays the debt elsewhere, planting wordplay at a place where the source text is comparatively plain but the receiving language offers an accident. Substitution preserves the scene's local design and is possible only where luck supplies material. Compensation preserves the play's overall verbal economy and is possible almost anywhere, at the price of redrawing the map of where the wit lives. Most real translations mix the two, substituting where they can and compensating where they cannot.

### Q: Why do translators keep a mental ledger of lost puns?

Because wordplay in this play is a quantity as well as a quality, and quantities can run a deficit. A translator who surrenders the grave joke, the medlar, and the servants' chain without repayment has not made three local decisions but has lowered the whole text's verbal temperature, changing what kind of object the audience receives: a solemn tragedy where the source is a tragedy whose wit is structural. The ledger, kept in two currencies, count and register, tells the translator when the deficit demands a compensating invention and where the receiving language offers the material for one. It also disciplines honesty. A translator who knows the balance can say, in a preface or a note, exactly what the version owes and where it paid, which is the difference between a priced loss and a silent one.

### Q: What did Schlegel put in the margin at Juliet's ay, I, and eye lines?

A confession, in brackets. Where Juliet's fate hangs on the single syllable ay and Shakespeare rings the changes on ay, I, and eye, three words with one sound, the German edition prints the speech and appends a note in the translators' own voice: "Ein Wortspiel mit den Wörtern aye (ja), I (ich) und eye (Auge), die alle gleich ausgesprochen werden," a wordplay on the words aye, I, and eye, which are all pronounced alike. The note performs no wordplay; it reports that wordplay exists in the English and declines to counterfeit a German equivalent. It is the editorial strategy at its most honourable, scholarship standing where poetry failed, and it marks the exact floor of the period's greatest verse translation: below this line, the English play's verbal life stopped crossing and the edition said so on the page.

### Q: Why does the same pun survive into one language and die in another?

Survival depends on the receiving language's private accidents, which differ from language to language for reasons of pure history. A pun lives where the target language happens to possess a word with the same double citizenship, or a sound merger that fuses the same trio, or a slang stock with a matching entry. German can rebuild the Friar's womb and tomb enforcement because German locks womb and grave into a conceptual rhyme; German cannot touch the grave man, because its words for solemn and burial share no syllable. Russian offers Pasternak different luck at different joints again. The pattern looks arbitrary because it is: each language pair draws its own map of possible puns from two unrelated histories of sound change and borrowing, and the translator inherits the map rather than drawing it. Skill decides what is done at the joints the map leaves open. The map itself is fate.

### Q: How does a translator handle Mercutio's consort, where one word means companions and concert at once?

Mercutio's taunt to Tybalt turns on consort meaning both a company of companions and a company of musicians: addressed as a consort, Tybalt is insulted as a hired minstrel, and Mercutio's fiddlestick, his rapier, will make him dance. A receiving language whose word for a companion group carries no music forces the standard triage. Most sense-carrying versions keep the companionship and let the minstrel machinery fall, preserving the insult's charge while losing its wit. A bolder hand substitutes whatever the receiving language offers for a musical insult at the joint, and an annotated edition explains the double sense in a note and lets the stage line carry one. The case is a compact specimen of the middle class of difficulty: the pun is neither fused into a death speech nor detachable as decoration, and the handling a translator chooses there is a fair sample of the method applied everywhere else.

### Q: Why are prose translations quieter about their pun losses than verse ones?

Because prose leaves no visible hole where a mechanism dies. A verse translator working under a formal programme, as Schlegel did, has promised the reader a corresponding structure, and a joint where nothing corresponds stands out against the promise: the missing pun is felt as a gap in a pattern, and an honest edition marks it. A prose rendering promises sense, and a pun's sense, or one of its two senses, can always be delivered in prose without any sign that a second sense stood beside it. The line reads smoothly, and smoothness conceals the amputation perfectly. The French and early German prose traditions therefore lost the play's wordplay at a high rate with a low visibility, and their Mercutios can be lively talkers whose liveliness is the translator's own manufacture, unmarked as such. Quietness is a property of the medium, not evidence that nothing was lost.

### Q: What is a punoid, and why do translators settle for one?

A punoid is a rhetorical device that stands near a pun in effect without being one: alliteration, rhyme, repetition, paradox, and their kin, the wordplay-adjacent machinery in Delabastita's inventory. Translators settle for a punoid where the exact mechanism is unavailable but the passage's energy is not negotiable. Schlegel's handling of the Friar's entrance is the play's model case: the womb and tomb pun, a rhyme enforcing a doctrine, is rebuilt as a conceptual rhyme locking womb and grave at the couplet's close, so the passage still enforces its paradox by sound-shaped means even though no German word puns. The settlement is honest because the device performs the same office, binding the speech's two poles together audibly, while making no false claim to be the original device. A punoid is the translator's way of paying in a neighbouring currency at a declared rate of exchange.

### Q: When is omitting a pun the honest choice rather than a failure?

Omission is honest when every alternative would counterfeit something the receiving culture cannot actually supply, and when the translator's edition or practice acknowledges the economy that produced the cut. Schlegel's medlar passage is the borderline case: the obscenity lived in English fruit slang with no German folk equivalent, a substitute grossness would have imported a coarseness with the wrong social address, and his decorous three lines at least refuse to pretend. The cut remains a loss, and the audit records it as one, but it is a priced loss taken under a stated constraint, his receiving culture's contract with its new classic. Omission becomes failure when it is habitual and unexamined, when bawdy or difficult joints fall throughout a version simply because falling is easier, and the text's temperature drops without any compensating life elsewhere. The difference lies not in the single cut but in whether the translator's ledger shows the cut was seen, weighed, and answered for.

### Q: Why do bawdy puns resist translation twice over?

A bawdy pun must clear two borders at once, and the second is patrolled by taste rather than vocabulary. The first resistance is mechanical: the word must carry a respectable sense and a sexual sense together, and the receiving language's candidate words rarely hold the same pair. The second resistance is social: the double sense must be sayable, by that speaker, on that stage, in print, in that decade of the receiving culture. The medlar's folk names were sayable in the London of the fifteen-nineties and unsayable in the Weimar classicism Schlegel served, so the joke's mechanism and its licence expired together. Many traditions repeat the pattern, cleaning the servants and Mercutio while preserving their sentiments. The double resistance explains why bawdy joints fail at a higher rate than witty ones in the catalogue, and why their failure is so often disguised: a cleaned line reads as decorum rather than as loss.

### Q: Why does the pun on sole in the shoe exchange defeat a word-for-word rendering?

Because the exchange is a scoring system before it is a conversation. When Mercutio calls Romeo's jest a single-soled jest, solely singular for the singleness, and the talk flowers into the pink of courtesy and a pump well flowered, sole is carrying the shoe and singleness in one word, and the friends are awarding points for the punning itself: Mercutio is testing whether love has dulled Romeo's reflexes, and the test only works if the audience can hear the joints click. A word-for-word rendering can deliver a shoe, a jest, and a flower as separate items of sense, and the items arrive as small talk. The duel's scoring, which is the scene's actual content, exists only inside the fused words. Defeat at this joint is therefore a loss of function without loss of content, the quietest class in the catalogue, and the one least visible to a reader auditing the translation against the original line by line.

### Q: Why do translators protect a play's structure when they cannot protect its jokes?

Because structure is portable and jokes are not, and professionals spend their strength where spending works. Pasternak's version is the documented extreme: the Prologue's sonnet frame, the lovers' shared sonnet at the feast, and the antiphonal build of the orchard scene all cross into Russian intact, because arrangement is a property of the work's architecture and architecture can be rebuilt in any material. The same version spends the Prologue's star-crossed compounds and the servants' pun chain without a fight, since those effects are properties of English vocabulary. The protection of structure is thus not a consolation prize but the soundest available strategy: an audience that receives the architecture receives the play's argument about itself, the pattern by which scenes answer scenes, and can be trusted to feel the design even where the local verbal life has thinned. Jokes die at borders. Designs emigrate.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch pun-translation catalogue to argue that translation loss is priced rather than random?

Begin from the catalogue's residue column, which shows the losses clustering by mechanism and not by chance: fused single words fall almost everywhere, figures fall only where no poet rebuilds them, proverbs and arguments cross nearly whole. That clustering is the essay's thesis in evidence form, since random loss would scatter evenly and priced loss sorts by portability. The essay should then take two joints of different classes, the grave man and the womb-tomb rhyme, and show the same translator failing at the first and succeeding at the second, which removes skill as the explanation and leaves the language pair's accidents as the cause. A final move grades the strategies by what they deliver, substitution giving an event, the footnote giving knowledge of an event, and so establishes the essay's measure: a translation is judged by the honesty and intelligence of its pricing, not by the impossible standard of zero loss.

### Q: What separates a translation that loses puns from one that loses punning?

The distinction is between losing positions and losing the campaign. Every translation of this play loses puns, since several of its mechanisms exist only in English and no skill manufactures them abroad. A translation loses punning only when the surrenders go unrepaired and the text's verbal temperature falls across whole scenes, so that the audience receives a play in which language is merely the message's vehicle rather than, as in Verona, its weapon and its sport. Schlegel's version loses the grave man and the medlar and does not lose punning, because the first scene fences in German and the Friar preaches in rebuilt rhymes. A version that carried every sense correctly and invented nothing would keep each pun's information and lose the punning entire. The first failure is local and priced. The second is constitutional, and it is the only one the catalogue treats as fatal.

### Q: How does a stage translation treat puns differently from a reading edition?

The stage has no margins, and timing is its law. A reading edition can pause at the ay passage, print the English words, and explain the fusion, delivering knowledge at the cost of the moment; a stage text must produce an effect at the speed of speech or accept the moment's death in silence. Stage translators therefore lean harder on substitution and compensation, inventing where the audience cannot be sent to a note, and accept bolder departures from the letter because a laugh delivered by a different joke serves the performance better than an accurate line delivered dead. Reading editions lean the other way, toward annotation and the honest confession, because their audience's purpose is study and study can afford the truth about the gap. The same translator will often make opposite choices for the two media, and the difference is not inconsistency but a correct reading of what each audience came for.

### Q: Why does a substitute pun have to be invented rather than found in a dictionary?

A dictionary records what words mean, and a substitute pun is not a meaning but an accident: a word in the receiving language that happens to hold two senses, or two words that happen to share a sound, at the exact joint where the scene needs a double take. Accidents of that kind are not listed under the source word's entry, because they belong to the receiving language's own history and surface only to a mind searching that language by ear rather than by lemma. The translator hunts through the target tongue's slang, proverbs, and sound-alikes for a usable collision near the required topic, a search conducted in the translator's own verbal memory and answerable to no reference work. Where the hunt fails, no authority can overrule the failure. This is why substitution is the strategy most dependent on the translator being a verbal artist in the receiving language, and why its successes read as strokes of the translator's own wit placed in the play's service.

### Q: Which losses in a translated pun are invisible to a reader who knows only the translation?

The losses that leave no scar: the functions performed inside fused words. A reader of Schlegel alone meets a Mercutio who promises to be still tomorrow and never learns that the English line performed the death it predicted. A reader of a prose version alone watches the servants boast and never sees the relay of sounds that taught an English audience the feud's verbal habit. The plague curse reads as a fine imprecation, and only a parallel text reveals that its key word wired the speech to the catastrophe five acts early. Such losses are invisible because the translation is coherent without them; nothing limps, nothing announces a gap, unless the edition confesses. This invisibility is the practical argument for annotated editions and for catalogues of the present kind: the reader of a translation is owed the map of what the border stopped, precisely because the reading experience itself will never supply it.

### Q: Why doesn't one successful substitution prove that substitution is always available?

Because substitution is a property of a language pair at a joint, not a power the translator carries from scene to scene. Schlegel re-punned the servants' chain because German offered insult-rhymes and market slang near those joints; the same man, at the grave man, found German offering nothing and surrendered to a single sense. The success and the failure sit three acts apart in one text by one master, which is the controlled experiment the question needs: skill held constant, outcomes opposite, therefore the variable is the material. A translator's record of substitutions measures the luck of the two languages' overlapping accidents across the play's particular set of topics, deaths, fruits, shoes, stars, and diseases. Announcing substitution as a universal method mistakes a favourable draw for a technique, and the catalogue's joint-by-joint grading exists to prevent exactly that mistake.

### Q: Is translating Shakespeare's puns impossible?

At the level of the individual pun, often yes, and the honest answer keeps the word. No German or Russian word holds the grave man's two senses, no language re-fuses ay and I and eye on demand, and the medlar's slang lives only in English folk speech. At the level of the play, no: the punning crosses by substitution, compensation, rhetorical rebuilding, and confessed annotation, and the play's two centuries of life in other languages are the demonstration. The word impossible is therefore the correct first entry in a translator's worksheet and an incorrect final verdict. It names the wall at surveyed joints. It does not name the campaign, which the record shows being won, at a price, by every master who priced the joints honestly and refused to pretend the wall was not there.
