The Companies Act 2013 is easiest to misunderstand as a bigger rulebook for registering and running companies. It does contain that rulebook, and company secretaries earn their living from it, but the statute Parliament enacted in 2013 was designed around a different diagnosis. Its premise is that Indian corporate failure is a governance failure before it is a disclosure failure: companies did not collapse because too little paper was filed with the Registrar, but because boards did not challenge promoters, auditors did not challenge boards, and shareholders who saw the damage coming had no remedy proportionate to the harm. A law written on that premise does not merely ask companies to report more. It reaches into the composition of the boardroom, fixes who may audit and for how long, gives classes of shareholders a collective remedy, builds regulators that did not exist, and attaches its most feared criminal provision to a definition of fraud wide enough to cover much of the conduct the earlier regime had treated as irregularity.

![Companies Act 2013 as a governance statute, showing board composition, auditor rotation and the new institutions it created - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-91.webp)

That design choice is the thread running through everything the Act does, and it is the thread this article follows. The reader who finishes it should be able to say which institutions the Act created that had no equivalent in the Companies Act 1956, which obligations bind which class of company, what the codified duties of a director actually require, how the fraud provision is framed and why it attracted the criticism it did, and which parts of the original criminal enforcement model survived the decriminalisation amendments of 2019 and 2020. The statute is long, and much of it is machinery, but its argument is short: change who sits in the room, change who checks the room, and give the people harmed by the room a way to answer back.

## What existed before: a statute built for a smaller corporate world

The Companies Act 1956 was written for an economy of a few tens of thousands of companies and a state that still licensed industrial capacity. By the time its replacement was being debated, the register held well over a million companies, the figure most often cited at the time of enactment, and the statute had been amended so often that its numbering, its schedules and its cross-references had become a subject of professional complaint in themselves. It ran to 658 sections and fifteen schedules. Its governance content, such as it was, sat lightly on boards: there was no statutory office of independent director, no codified statement of directors' duties, no rotation of auditors, no class action, no audit regulator independent of the profession, and no statutory fraud investigation office with the powers the 2013 Act would confer. The remedies for shareholders who believed a company was being run against their interests were the oppression and mismanagement petitions carried over from English practice, available in principle and slow in practice, heard for most of the period by a Company Law Board whose own position was anomalous within the statute.

The texture of the older regime is worth one more paragraph, because the 2013 Act is a reaction to it in detail and not only in scale. The 1956 Act was a permission statute in the manner of its era: it required the approval of the Central Government or its Company Law Board for a long list of corporate acts, from increasing the number of directors beyond the statutory dozen to appointing managing directors on terms the government prescribed, and it administered those approvals through a department whose habits were those of the licensing state. Its enforcement was complaint-driven and slow, its accounts provisions were outpaced by the accounting profession's own standards, and its tribunal provisions, inserted by amendment in 2002 to create the National Company Law Tribunal, could not be commenced because the constitutional litigation they attracted consumed the years in which they might have operated, leaving the Company Law Board, a body designed as a delegate of government administrative power, exercising judicial functions in oppression and mismanagement matters under a constitutional cloud of its own. When the 2013 Act abolished the approval habit in favour of shareholder approval, board process and disclosure, and re-founded the tribunal on a corrected design, it was settling accounts with this texture as much as with the Satyam scandal. The governance-by-composition method is the alternative it chose to the permission method: not the state's officer approving the act in advance, but the company's own room, composed by law, deciding in the open and answering afterwards.

The replacement project had a long gestation. The J. J. Irani Committee reported in 2005 and supplied much of the intellectual architecture for a modern company law, including the case for class actions and a lighter, more enabling statute. A Companies Bill was introduced in 2008 and lapsed with the dissolution of the Lok Sabha. A further Bill followed in 2011, was examined by the Standing Committee on Finance, and passed the Lok Sabha in December 2012 and the Rajya Sabha in August 2013. Between those two Bills fell the collapse of the Satyam Computer Services accounting fraud in January 2009, in which the company's founder confessed to inflating its accounts over years while auditors, independent directors and regulators failed to detect or disclose the fabrication. Satyam did not write the new statute, and the Irani architecture predates it, but the fraud hardened the political mood in which the Bill was finalised: provisions on auditor independence, fraud investigation and board accountability that might have been diluted in calmer conditions survived largely intact. The story of that replacement, including the tribunal provisions of the 1956 Act that could never be brought into force, belongs to the article on [how the 1956 Act came to be replaced](/2012/02/15/companies-act-1956-replacement/), and this pillar takes the enacted design as its subject rather than the contest that produced it.

## The shape of the Companies Act 2013

The Companies Act 2013, Act 18 of 2013, received the assent of the President on 29 August 2013 and was published in the Gazette the following day. As enacted it contains 470 sections arranged in twenty-nine chapters with seven schedules, a deliberate compression against the 658 sections and fifteen schedules of the statute it replaced, although the compression is partly an accounting effect: a large share of the operative detail that the 1956 Act carried in its sections was moved into rules prescribed by the Central Government, so the working law a practitioner consults is the Act and the rules read together, and the phrase "as may be prescribed" runs through the text hundreds of times. Section 2 alone carries the definitions on which the whole edifice rests, including the definitions of company, promoter, related party, key managerial personnel, independent director and One Person Company that later chapters put to work.

The Act did not commence on a single day. Section 1(3) allowed the Central Government to appoint different dates for different provisions, and the power was used extensively. A first tranche of 98 sections was notified with effect from 12 September 2013. The bulk of the statute, 283 sections, commenced on 1 April 2014, which is the date from which the 2013 Act operated as the general company law of India. Other provisions waited longer: the chapters constituting the National Company Law Tribunal and its appellate tribunal were notified with effect from 1 June 2016, and the National Financial Reporting Authority was not actually constituted until October 2018, five years after the section creating it had been enacted. Commencement in stages is not a footnote to this statute; it is part of its history, because several of its most advertised institutions existed on paper before they existed in fact, and the gap between enactment and operation is one of the recurring themes of the record.

### When did the Companies Act 2013 come into force?

The Act received presidential assent on 29 August 2013 and commenced in stages rather than on one date. A first tranche of 98 sections took effect on 12 September 2013, the main body of 283 sections followed on 1 April 2014, and the tribunal provisions commenced on 1 June 2016.

The internal organisation follows the life of a company in rough sequence, with the governance chapters in the middle carrying the statute's real weight. The early chapters deal with incorporation and prospectus and the raising of capital. Chapters on management and administration, accounts, and audit and auditors contain the obligations that occupy most companies most of the time. The chapter on directors codifies appointment, composition and duties. Later chapters govern compromise and winding up, companies incorporated outside India, and the miscellaneous provisions in which Section 447 sits. Alongside the compression of sections, the Act widened the cast of corporate forms: it recognised the One Person Company for a single promoter, defined the small company and gave it a lighter compliance regime, created the dormant company as a status for entities holding assets for a future project, and preserved the older categories of private, public, listed and government companies whose distinctions drive most of the threshold questions practitioners face. A provision-by-provision comparison of the two statutes, old text against new, is the work of the article setting out [the 1956 and 2013 Acts side by side](/2012/03/01/companies-act-1956-vs-2013-comparison/), and the pillar's task is the design rather than the diff.

The chapter map rewards a slower pass, because the distribution of weight inside the Act is itself an argument. The definitions and incorporation chapters are short. The chapters on prospectus and allotment, share capital and debentures, registration of charges, management and administration, meetings, accounts, audit, directors, and investigation together constitute the operating system of the going concern, and they are where the section count concentrates. The chapters on compromise and winding up and on the tribunal are the failure machinery. The miscellaneous chapter at the end, Chapter XXIX, is where Parliament placed the provisions it expected to be feared: the fraud offence, the punishment for false statements and false evidence, the liability for contraventions by officers, and the provisions on documents and fees that the machinery needs. A reader who wants the shortest honest summary of the Act's priorities can be given the map in one sentence: the middle of the Act governs how companies must be run, the end of the middle governs who verifies the running, and the end of the Act punishes the running when it is fraudulent. The schedules complete the design, with Schedule I supplying the model constitutions, Schedule II the useful lives for depreciation and the remuneration framework, Schedule III the financial statement formats, Schedule IV the independent directors' code, Schedule V the conditions for managerial appointment and remuneration where profits are inadequate, and Schedule VII the list of permitted corporate social responsibility activities.

One structural feature deserves emphasis before the machinery is examined, because it explains many of the arguments that followed. The Act applies its heaviest obligations by class and threshold rather than universally. Independent directors are required of listed public companies and of prescribed classes of unlisted public companies. The woman director requirement binds listed companies and large public companies. Audit committees, vigil mechanisms, secretarial audit and auditor rotation each attach to their own prescribed classes, and the thresholds sit in the rules, where they can be and have been revised without amending the Act. The consequence is that the question "what does the Companies Act require" almost never has a single answer. It has an answer per class of company, and the recurring error in writing about this statute is to state one class's obligation as every company's obligation. This article states the thresholds as the rules frame them and flags them as rule-based, which is where the brief for accuracy matters most.

## Capital, members and the decisions members take

A statute pillar must also account for the chapters on capital and membership, because they carry the Act's theory of the shareholder. Share capital is organised around the familiar kinds of shares, equity and preference, with the terms of issue, calls, forfeiture and transfer regulated in Chapter IV, and two provisions of the chapter mark the 2013 Act's temper. Private placement under Section 42 rebuilt the law on offers to a select group: an offer may be made only to persons identified by the board, within the numerical limits the section and rules prescribe, on the terms of a private placement offer letter, with the application money routed through a bank account and allotment completed within the period the section fixes, failing which the money must be refunded with interest and the company exposed to penalty. The provision replaced a permissive practice under which the line between a private placement and a disguised public offer had blurred, and its failure mode matters: an offer that breaches the conditions is treated as a public offer, dragging the company into the prospectus regime it sought to avoid. Buyback under Section 68 permits a company to purchase its own shares out of free reserves, the securities premium account or the proceeds of an earlier issue, subject to the ceiling of twenty five per cent of paid-up capital and free reserves, the post-buyback debt discipline the section prescribes, and a declaration of solvency, so that capital can be returned without the court-sanctioned reduction the older practice often required, while the route remains fenced against the extraction of capital to the prejudice of creditors.

The members' own decision machinery sits in Chapter VII. Ordinary and special resolutions under Sections 114 and following divide the business of the company between the simple majority and the three-fourths majority, with special notice, postal ballot under Section 110 for the items the rules reserve to it, and electronic voting under Section 108 for listed and large-membership companies, so that the general meeting is partly a room and partly a system. Class rights may be varied under Section 48 with the consent of the class, and the scheme provisions of Chapter XV, Sections 230 to 240, route compromises and arrangements, including mergers and demergers, through meetings of creditors and members and the sanction of the Tribunal, with cross-border mergers addressed in Section 234 subject to the Reserve Bank framework. The recurring design judgment is the one this article has tracked throughout: the Act trusts members with the decisions, but it prescribes the composition of the meeting, the majority, the disclosure and the forum of approval with a granularity the 1956 Act reserved for fewer questions, because its diagnosis was that shareholder democracy in the Indian register fails through procedure more often than through absence of rights.

## The corporate forms and the threshold map

Before the governance machinery can be understood, the reader needs the map of entities it applies to, because the Act is not one regime but a family of regimes graded by form and size. At the base sits incorporation itself under Chapter II. A company is formed by registration with the Registrar of Companies for the state in which its registered office will sit, on documents that include the memorandum and articles, the declarations of the subscribers and the first directors, and the particulars the rules prescribe, filed through the integrated incorporation forms of the Ministry of Corporate Affairs portal. Seven or more persons may form a public company, two or more a private company, and one person a One Person Company, and the certificate of incorporation is conclusive of the company's existence as a body corporate with perpetual succession and the capacity to hold property and sue in its own name.

### How is a company incorporated under the Companies Act 2013?

Incorporation is by registration with the Registrar of Companies on the prescribed forms, with the memorandum and articles, declarations by the subscribers and first directors, and the registered office particulars. The Registrar issues a certificate of incorporation, which brings the company into existence as a separate legal person distinct from its members.

The distinctions among forms drive everything downstream. A private company, defined in Section 2(68), restricts the transfer of its shares, limits its membership to two hundred, and prohibits any invitation to the public to subscribe for its securities; it is the default vehicle of Indian business, from the family enterprise to the wholly owned subsidiary. A public company, defined negatively in Section 2(71) as a company that is not a private company, carries the heavier regime because it may invite public money. A listed company, one whose securities are listed on a recognised stock exchange, carries the heaviest, because the securities regulator's listing regulations are layered over the Act. Within these forms the Act creates its special categories. The small company receives its lighter regime because Parliament accepted that the full calendar would crush it. The Section 8 company, licensed to promote commerce, art, science, sports, education, research, social welfare, religion, charity or environmental protection, applies its profits only to its objects and pays no dividend, and in exchange is relieved of parts of the ordinary regime. The government company carries the state as its majority shareholder and the exemptions Section 462 makes possible. The producer company serves the mutual-benefit objects of its farmer and artisan members. The dormant company, discussed in the questions below, is a status rather than a form. A reader who fixes these categories can predict, for almost any provision of the Act, roughly which companies it will bind; a reader who treats "companies" as a single class will misstate the law in the manner the errors column of professional commentary keeps recording.

The threshold map is the working form of the same point, and it answers the question practitioners are asked most often about this statute: which obligations apply to a company of a given size. Take a private company that is nobody's subsidiary and follows the common growth path. In its first years it owes the irreducible core: a registered office, at least two directors and a resident director, board meetings, maintenance of registers and minutes, financial statements prepared under Schedule III and audited by a statutory auditor, an annual general meeting, and the annual filings of financial statements and annual return with the Registrar. Almost none of the governance architecture applies to it. As it grows into a public company, or crosses the rule thresholds while remaining public, the composition rules arrive in sequence: paid-up capital of ten crore rupees or the turnover and borrowings thresholds bring independent directors, the audit committee, the nomination and remuneration committee and whole-time key managerial personnel; the larger thresholds for paid-up capital and turnover bring the woman director, secretarial audit and, if it borrows heavily from banks or accepts deposits, the vigil mechanism; auditor rotation binds listed companies and the prescribed classes that include substantial unlisted public companies and heavily borrowing private companies. Listing changes the register again, adding the one-third independent board as a statutory command rather than a threshold consequence and layering the securities regulator's governance code on top. Corporate social responsibility cuts across the sequence on its own three thresholds of net worth, turnover and net profit. The Act's defenders describe this grading as proportionality. Its critics reply that the thresholds, being rule-based and scattered across half a dozen rule books, make the applicable law genuinely difficult to state, and that a managing director of a mid-sized company can breach an obligation he had no practical way of knowing had attached to him. Both descriptions are accurate, and together they explain why this pillar states thresholds provision by provision instead of pretending the Act has a single applicability line.

The map becomes clearer walked than tabulated, so consider three companies drawn from the common run of the register. The first is a two-director private company supplying components to a single large customer, with share capital of a few lakh rupees and turnover below one crore. It owes the core calendar and almost nothing else: its accounts are audited because every company's accounts are audited, its directors owe the Section 166 duties because every director does, and the fraud offence hangs over its affairs as it does over every company's, but the governance architecture of committees, independent directors, whole-time key managerial personnel and rotation does not touch it, and its promoters encounter the Act mainly as an annual filing discipline and as the law of their own powers. The second company is the same business grown: converted to a public company to admit investors, with paid-up capital above ten crore rupees and turnover above one hundred crore. The composition rules attach in a body: independent directors join the board, the audit committee takes over approval of its related party dealings with the promoter group, a company secretary and chief financial officer join the managing director as whole-time key managerial personnel, its secretarial audit is outsourced to a practising secretary, and if it borrows from banks beyond the prescribed line its employees gain the vigil mechanism and its auditors face rotation when it crosses the separate borrowing and capital lines for that purpose. The third company is the second one listed, and listing adds the one-third independent board as a command, the securities regulator's second governance code, and the public disciplines of continuous disclosure that make its board papers the most regulated documents in its business. The three companies are one legal person at three sizes, and the statute they live under is materially three statutes. That is not an accident of drafting; it is the Act's theory of regulation by capacity, and most practical questions about the Companies Act are answered by first locating the company on this path.

One further feature of the map should be marked, because it recurs in the assessment. The grading is almost entirely by size and listing status, not by risk or by sector. A company whose business is handling other people's money at modest scale may carry a lighter statutory regime than a manufacturing company of greater size and simpler dangers, subject always to the sectoral regulator where one exists. The 2013 Act accepted that trade-off deliberately, preferring bright size lines that a Registrar can administer to a discretionary risk assessment that it could not, and the choice is part of the statute's character as a Registrar-administered code.

## Governance by composition: the board the Act imagines

The most distinctive method of the 2013 Act is that it legislates for the membership of the boardroom. Earlier Indian company law had regulated what boards must disclose and what shareholders must approve, and had left the question of who the directors were largely to the shareholders who appointed them and the promoters who in practice nominated them. The 2013 Act takes a different view. It prescribes that boards of the companies it cares most about must contain directors of a particular kind, chosen under statutory conditions, carrying statutory duties, protected and exposed by statutory rules about liability. If the composition is right, the argument runs, the decisions improve. If the composition is a formality, the whole apparatus is decoration. Much of the serious debate about this statute is a debate about which of those two things happened.

The centrepiece is the independent director. Section 149 requires every listed public company to have at least one-third of its board as independent directors, and the rules extend the requirement to prescribed classes of unlisted public companies: in the framing of the Companies (Appointment and Qualification of Directors) Rules, public companies with paid-up share capital of ten crore rupees or more, turnover of one hundred crore rupees or more, or outstanding loans, debentures and deposits exceeding fifty crore rupees. Independence is defined negatively and elaborately. An independent director must be a person of integrity with relevant expertise, must not be a promoter or related to promoters or directors of the company or its group, must have no pecuniary relationship with the company beyond permitted limits, and must satisfy a schedule of disqualifications aimed at the channels through which dependence usually flows: employment, professional engagement, shareholding above a small threshold, and office in entities connected to the promoters. The term is up to five consecutive years, renewable once by special resolution, followed by a cooling-off period of three years, which is a tenure design borrowed from the logic of auditor rotation: long enough to learn the business, short enough to limit capture.

Section 149 also does something no earlier Indian company statute had done: it attaches a code of conduct to the office. Schedule IV sets out the guidelines of professional conduct, the role and functions, and the duties of independent directors, including the duty to safeguard the interests of all stakeholders and to balance the conflicting interests of stakeholders where they arise. Independent directors meet separately from the rest of the board, their performance is formally evaluated, and their appointment and reappointment are disclosed to shareholders with a justification. Section 149(12) addresses the other side of the bargain: an independent director, and a non-executive director who is not a promoter or key managerial personnel, is liable only in respect of acts of omission or commission by the company that occurred with that director's knowledge, attributable through board processes, and with consent or connivance, or where the director lacked diligence. The limitation matters because without it the office would be uninsurable and unfillable; with it, the argument shifts to whether the protection is drawn so wide that accountability rarely survives it.

Alongside the independent director sits the woman director requirement. The second proviso to Section 149(1) obliges prescribed classes of company to appoint at least one woman director, and the rules prescribe listed companies and public companies with paid-up capital of one hundred crore rupees or more or turnover of three hundred crore rupees or more. The provision is a composition rule in the purest form: it does not regulate disclosure or approval but the presence of a category of person in the room. Its defenders present it as a floor that broke a self-reproducing pattern of appointment; its critics observe that a single mandated seat can be filled from within promoter families and that presence is not the same as influence. Both observations are true of the design, and the provision's record turns on how companies chose to comply with it, a matter taken up in the assessment below.

The third composition instrument is the category of key managerial personnel. Section 2(51) defines the term to cover the chief executive officer or managing director or manager, the company secretary, the whole-time director, the chief financial officer, and such other officers as are prescribed, and Section 203 requires listed companies and public companies with paid-up share capital of ten crore rupees or more to appoint whole-time key managerial personnel in the prescribed combination: a managing director or chief executive or manager, a company secretary, and a chief financial officer. The point of the category is accountability with a name attached. The Act repeatedly fastens obligations, and in case of default the status of officer in default, on these offices, so that the question of who inside a company answers for its compliance has a statutory answer rather than an organisational one. The company secretary's elevation is part of the same design: the office existed under the 1956 Act for larger companies, but the 2013 Act makes the secretary a key managerial person across the prescribed class and builds the compliance architecture around the office.

Around these composition rules sits the older machinery of appointment, which the 2013 Act retained and tightened. Every director must hold a Director Identification Number allotted under Section 153, and the disqualifications in Section 164, examined in the questions below, police who may sit at all. Directors are appointed by the shareholders in general meeting under Section 152, and in a public company two-thirds of the directors are liable to retire by rotation, one-third of those retiring at each annual general meeting, a survival from the earlier law that preserves a rhythm of shareholder reappointment inside the promoter-dominated register. Additional directors, alternate directors and nominee directors of financial institutions each have their statutory footing in Sections 161 and following, and the board may not exceed fifteen directors unless the shareholders resolve otherwise by special resolution. Managerial remuneration is capped in Section 197 by reference to the company's net profits, with an overall ceiling of eleven per cent for all directors together and tighter percentages for categories within it, computed under Section 198 and relaxable through the prescribed approvals, so that the pay of the persons who run the company is itself a matter of statutory arithmetic rather than private bargain at the prescribed scale. Board meetings under Section 173 must be held at least four times a year for most companies, with a maximum gap of one hundred and twenty days between them, on notice that the section and the secretarial standards regulate, and the participation of directors through video conferencing is recognised with prescribed exceptions. None of this is the celebrated part of the Act, but it is the part through which the celebrated parts operate: an independent director performs the Schedule IV functions through these meetings, these appointments and these papers, or not at all.

### What does Section 166 require of a company's directors?

Section 166 codifies directors' duties for the first time in Indian company law. A director must act in accordance with the articles, act in good faith to promote the company's objects for members as a whole and in the interests of the company, its employees, the community and the environment, and exercise due care, skill and diligence.

The codification also has a comparative pedigree that helps a reader place the Act internationally. The device of writing directors' general duties into the statute, rather than leaving them to fiduciary case law, follows the model of the United Kingdom's Companies Act 2006, whose own codified duties the Irani Committee had studied, and the Indian list tracks the same territory: loyalty, obedience to the constitution of the company, care and skill, and the management of conflicts. The Indian version departs from its model at the point that matters most to this article's argument, because the good-faith duty is expressed toward a constituency list that names employees, the community and the environment alongside the members, where the British formulation subordinates other interests to the success of the company for the benefit of its members. The departure was debated at the time and has been debated since, with one side reading it as a stakeholder charter in embryo and the other as a drafting generosity that no court can operationalise when the interests conflict. The pillar records the departure and the debate, because the examinations test it and the boardrooms feel it, and because it shows the 2013 Parliament legislating an aspiration into the centre of its governance code while leaving the ranking of the aspiration's objects to practice.

The codification in Section 166 is short enough to be quoted in substance and significant enough to carry a section of this article. A director must act in accordance with the company's articles. A director must act in good faith in order to promote the objects of the company for the benefit of its members as a whole, and in the best interests of the company, its employees, the shareholders, the community and for the protection of the environment. A director must exercise duties with due and reasonable care, skill and diligence, and exercise independent judgment. A director must not involve himself in a situation of direct or indirect conflict with the company's interest, must not achieve or attempt to achieve undue gain or advantage for himself or his relatives, partners or associates, and must not assign his office. The list matters less for its novelty, most of it restates the fiduciary position the courts had built in equity, than for its address. It is written into the statute that every director receives, it is enforceable through the company's organs and the tribunal, and it converts standards that previously had to be excavated from case law into text that a newly appointed director, a company secretary advising the board, or a civil services examination can point to directly. The duties are owed within a stakeholder frame rather than a purely shareholder frame, since employees, the community and the environment are named in the good-faith clause, which aligns the codification with the Act's broader insistence that the company is a regulated social institution and not only a contract among investors.

Board committees carry the composition logic one level down. Section 177 requires listed public companies and prescribed classes of public companies, framed in the rules by the same family of thresholds of paid-up capital, turnover and borrowings, to constitute an audit committee with a minimum of three directors and a majority of independent directors, charged with oversight of the financial reporting process, the auditors and the internal controls, and with approval of related party transactions. Section 178 requires the same classes to maintain a nomination and remuneration committee, again with independent director weight, to carry the board's work on appointments and pay. Section 135, taken up below, adds a corporate social responsibility committee for the companies it catches. The pattern is consistent: wherever the Act identifies a decision that promoters might bend, it creates a smaller room inside the boardroom, staffs that room disproportionately with the directors it has defined as independent, and routes the decision through it.

The deepest objection to this entire design is structural and honest, and the brief for this article requires it to be stated at strength. Independent directors are appointed by the shareholders in general meeting, which in most Indian listed companies means they are appointed on the effective nomination of the promoters they are meant to check. A director who owes the seat to the promoter, whose reappointment the promoter can block, and whose information comes from the management the promoter controls, is asked by the statute to perform a function that the manner of his appointment works against. Defenders of the design answer that the statute does what a statute can: it defines independence by excluding the measurable ties, fixes tenure so that a director need not court annual favour, protects the diligent director from liability for defaults he neither knew of nor connived at, and creates a separate meeting and an evaluation process so that independent directors at least possess a forum of their own. The empirical question of whether these devices change behaviour in the room cannot be answered by reading the Act, and this article returns to it in the assessment, because it is the question on which the statute's whole method stands or falls.

## Accounts, meetings and the annual calendar

Between the composition rules and the audit rules lies the calendar of obligations through which a company accounts to its members, and the pillar must give it its place, because it is the layer of the Act with which the largest number of companies actually live. The financial year is standardised by Section 2(41) to end on 31 March for nearly all companies, a uniformity the 1956 Act had not imposed. Financial statements, defined in Section 2(40) to include the balance sheet, the profit and loss account, the cash flow statement for companies outside the exempted classes, the statement of changes in equity and explanatory notes, must give a true and fair view and comply with the accounting standards notified under Section 133 and the format of Schedule III, with consolidated statements required of companies with subsidiaries, associates or joint ventures under Section 129. The board approves the statements, the directors sign them, and the board's report under Section 134, with the directors' responsibility statement, is laid with them before the members. The statement of responsibility is more than a form: the directors affirm that accounting standards were followed, policies applied consistently, records maintained, statements prepared on a going concern basis, internal financial controls laid down and followed, and systems devised to ensure compliance with all applicable laws, which converts the annual report into a signed governance assertion by the board as a whole.

The members receive the account at the annual general meeting, which Section 96 requires every company other than a One Person Company to hold within six months of the close of the financial year and within fifteen months of the previous meeting, at which the financial statements are adopted, dividends declared, retiring directors reappointed and auditors appointed. Alongside the meeting run the filings: the financial statements are filed with the Registrar within thirty days of the meeting under Section 137, and the annual return under Section 92, signed by a director and the company secretary and certified for listed and prescribed companies by a company secretary in practice, records the shareholding, indebtedness and management particulars from which the public register is built. Dividend under Section 123 may be declared only out of profits computed after depreciation or out of money provided by government in guarantee, and unpaid dividend moves to a dedicated account and, after seven years, to the Investor Education and Protection Fund, which also receives shares whose dividend has gone unclaimed for the matching period, a mechanism that has quietly transferred large volumes of forgotten shareholdings into a statutory fund from which owners may reclaim them.

The standards beneath the statements changed with the Act as well, and an account of the accounts chapters is incomplete without the convergence story. Section 133 empowers the Central Government to prescribe accounting standards on the recommendation of the Institute of Chartered Accountants and in consultation with the National Financial Reporting Authority once constituted, and under that power the Indian Accounting Standards, the Ind AS converged with the international financial reporting standards, were rolled out by a roadmap that began with voluntary adoption and made the standards mandatory in phases from the financial year 2016-17, first for listed companies and unlisted companies above a net worth threshold of five hundred crore rupees, and then for the wider listed class and companies above two hundred and fifty crore rupees, with holding, subsidiary, joint venture and associate companies of covered companies drawn in. The consequence for the reader's model is that the larger end of the register reports on a measurement framework substantially aligned with global practice, including fair value measurement and the expected credit loss approach to financial assets, while smaller companies continue under the older accounting standards, so that "company accounts" in India are two dialects of one language divided, once again, by a size line the rules draw. The audit committee's oversight of financial reporting, the auditor's report and the Authority's quality review all operate on this divided terrain, and the transition's frictions, above all in the financial sector where the regulator's timetable differed, belong to the implementation record of the cluster rather than to this pillar's design account.

Two chapters of the calendar deserve specific mention because they mark boundaries of the Act's trust. Deposits, in Chapter V, Sections 73 to 76, confine the acceptance of deposits from the public to eligible companies meeting prescribed conditions, with credit rating, deposit insurance and repayment reserve requirements, because the history of Indian company law includes repeated episodes of companies raising household savings as deposits and failing with them. The 1956 Act's deposit regime was one of its most amended and most litigated territories, and the 2013 Act's stricter version, under which a private company may ordinarily take money only from its members within limits and on conditions, is a considered retention of suspicion. Charges, in Chapter VI, require the registration of charges on a company's property with the Registrar within the period Section 77 prescribes, so that the security interests a lender takes are visible on the public register and an unregistered charge is void against the liquidator and creditors. Both chapters express the same philosophy as the governance provisions from the opposite side: where the Act does not trust the room, it makes the transaction public.

### What must a company file with the Registrar every year?

Every company must file its audited financial statements within thirty days of its annual general meeting and its annual return within sixty days of that meeting, together with event-based filings as changes occur in directors, share capital, charges and registered office. The filings build the public register on the Ministry of Corporate Affairs portal.

## Groups, subsidiaries and the movement of money inside them

Indian corporate failure is very often group failure: value is moved between companies under common control until the entity that owes the money is empty and the entity that holds the assets is beyond reach. The 2013 Act legislates for the group as a fact, through definitions and through a set of financial controls that sit between the accounts chapters and the fraud offence. A holding company and its subsidiaries are defined in Sections 2(46) and 2(87) by control of board composition or of more than half the total voting power, with the associate company of Section 2(6) defined by significant influence, taken as at least twenty per cent of total voting power or control of business decisions under an agreement. The layering of subsidiaries is itself restricted: the Act and the rules framed under the proviso to Section 2(87) confine companies to two layers of subsidiaries for most purposes, a provision moderated by amendment for specified classes, because the multi-layered pyramid was the characteristic instrument by which control was exercised with small capital and accountability was diffused. Consolidation under Section 129(3) makes the group present its accounts as one economic unit, which is the accounting form of the same recognition.

The financial controls police the movement of value within and beyond the group. Section 185 as enacted prohibited a company, with narrow exceptions, from advancing loans, including loans represented by book debt, to its directors or to persons in whom its directors are interested, and from giving guarantees or security for such loans; the prohibition was recast by the 2017 amendment, which preserved the bar on loans to directors themselves while permitting loans to persons in whom directors are interested where the shareholders authorise them by special resolution and the borrowing is used for the borrower's principal business activities, a recalibration that converted one of the Act's few absolute prohibitions into a gated one and illustrates, inside a single section, the same negotiation between governance suspicion and business practicality that the amendment arc conducts at the scale of the whole statute. Section 186 governs loans, guarantees, security and investments generally: beyond the ceiling of sixty per cent of paid-up share capital, free reserves and securities premium account, or one hundred per cent of free reserves and securities premium account, whichever is greater, the transaction requires authorisation by special resolution, must pass through a board resolution made with the consent of all directors present, and is recorded in a register open to members. Section 188's related party discipline, described in the questions below, is the third side of the triangle. Read together, the three provisions define the Act's posture toward the insider transaction: it is not forbidden, because groups legitimately finance and supply each other, but it is dragged into the open, priced at arm's length or justified, approved at a level above the interested parties, and recorded where a member or an investigator can find it. When the fraud cases are opened, the trail almost always runs through these sections' evasions, which is why a pillar account treats them as central rather than technical.

## The audit architecture: rotation, the NFRA and the investigation of fraud

If the board provisions govern who decides, the audit provisions govern who verifies, and the 2013 Act treats verification as a public function rather than a private engagement. Under the earlier regime the statutory auditor was appointed by the shareholders, in practice proposed by management, reappointed year after year with little friction, and regulated, if at all, by the Institute of Chartered Accountants of India disciplining its own members. The Satyam failure, in which the auditors of a flagship company certified accounts that its own founder later confessed were fabricated, was the public demonstration of what that arrangement could miss. The 2013 Act responds at four points: the tenure of the auditor, the independence of the auditor, the regulator of the auditor, and the investigation of the frauds audits fail to catch.

Tenure first. Section 139(2) imposes mandatory rotation on listed companies and on the classes of companies prescribed in the Companies (Audit and Auditors) Rules, which reach unlisted public companies above a paid-up capital threshold, private companies above a higher threshold as revised from time to time, and companies with public borrowings or deposits above fifty crore rupees. An individual auditor may hold office for one term of five consecutive years; an audit firm may hold office for two terms of five consecutive years; and after the term, a cooling-off period of five years applies before the same auditor or firm, or a firm with a common partner or network connection, may return. Rotation is a composition rule applied to the audit market: it accepts that long association between auditor and management breeds a familiarity that disclosure alone does not cure, and it breaks the association by calendar rather than by proof of fault.

The appointment mechanics complete the tenure picture. Under Section 139(1) the auditor is appointed at an annual general meeting for a term running to the sixth meeting from the meeting of appointment, which in the ordinary calendar is five years; the requirement that the appointment be ratified by the members at every annual general meeting, which the Act originally carried, was removed by the 2017 amendment as a formality that added process without scrutiny. First auditors, casual vacancies and the filling of a vacancy caused by resignation are dealt with in the same chapter, with the board and, for a resignation in a listed company, a sequence that brings the matter before the members. The appointment is thus long, deliberate and difficult to disturb, which is the necessary counterpart of rotation: an auditor who can be removed at will is not made independent by being rotated after five years, and the Act's pairing of a fixed term with protection against mid-term removal, described in the discussion of Sections 140 and 141 above, is a single design expressed at both ends of the tenure.

Independence second. Section 141 disqualifies persons whose connections compromise them, and Section 144 prohibits the statutory auditor from rendering a list of non-audit services to the company and its holding and subsidiary companies, including book-keeping, internal audit, design of financial information systems, actuarial services, investment advisory and management services. The audit committee's oversight of the auditor, the auditor's right and duty to report fraud under Section 143(12), and the requirement that the auditor attend general meetings complete a design in which the auditor is repositioned from the company's service provider to an officer of its accountability system, answerable to the audit committee and, through the reporting duty, to the Central Government when fraud crosses the prescribed threshold.

The regulator third. Section 132 empowers the constitution of the National Financial Reporting Authority, an independent regulator for accounting and auditing standards with power to monitor and enforce compliance, to oversee the quality of service of the professions associated with that compliance, and to investigate professional or other misconduct of auditors of the classes of companies brought within its remit, principally listed companies, large unlisted public companies, banks, insurance companies and bodies referred to it. Where the Authority investigates a matter, no other institution may initiate or continue proceedings on the same misconduct, which gives the NFRA an overriding disciplinary position over the chartered accountancy profession in its field, and it may impose penalties and debar an auditor or firm for a period extending to ten years. The power was controversial from the moment of enactment, because it displaced a self-regulatory settlement of long standing, and it was slow to arrive: the Authority was constituted only on 1 October 2018, when the relevant sub-sections of Section 132 were brought into force, and its rules confine its remit to the public-interest end of the market rather than the generality of companies. In the February 2018 judgment in S. Sukumar v Secretary, Institute of Chartered Accountants of India, the Supreme Court, examining the disciplinary framework for chartered accountants after the Satyam and allied failures, held the existing self-regulatory arrangements inadequate and pointed toward an independent oversight mechanism of the kind the statute had already designed, a sequence that captures the oddity of the NFRA's history: the institution was legislated before the courts finished demanding it, and constituted after.

The Authority's record since constitution illustrates both halves of the statute's wager on regulators. It built its jurisdiction deliberately, beginning with the audits of the failed financial groups whose papers were already in the public domain of insolvency and investigation, and its orders debarred engagement partners and firms in matters arising from those audits, which established that the disciplinary displacement of the profession's institute was operational rather than theoretical. It also published audit quality review reports on the audit practices of the largest firms, moving the oversight conversation from individual misconduct toward firm-wide systems, in the manner of the overseas audit regulators on which its design was modelled. The contest over its remit has continued in litigation, including the question whether it may examine audits of periods before its own constitution, on which the appellate tribunal upheld its jurisdiction and the matter travelled further; the pillar records the proposition that the Authority's retrospective reach has been sustained at the appellate level, and leaves the doctrine to the interpretation articles. The measured verdict on the NFRA mirrors the verdict on the Act: an institution whose creation changed what auditors of large companies expect, whose caseload is small against the size of the market it oversees, and whose deterrent effect is asserted by its defenders and questioned by its critics on evidence that is necessarily indirect.

Investigation fourth. The Serious Fraud Investigation Office had existed since 2003 as an office created by government resolution, multidisciplinary in staffing and chronically uncertain in legal authority. Section 211 gives it statutory establishment, and Section 212 governs the investigation of a company's affairs by the office on assignment by the Central Government, on the triggers the section lists, including a special audit report, the opinion of the Registrar or an inspector, a resolution of the company itself, or the public interest. An investigation by the SFIO displaces parallel proceedings, its report carries the status the section confers, and later amendments strengthened its hand in relation to the fraud offence, including a power of arrest connected with Section 447. The design judgment is the same as in the audit provisions: fraud of the serious kind is treated as a matter for a specialist state investigator rather than for the ordinary registrar machinery.

The fraud reporting duty inside Section 143 is the hinge between the audit and the enforcement ladder, and it deserves its own statement. Where an auditor, in the course of duty, has reason to believe that an offence involving fraud is being or has been committed against the company by its officers or employees, the matter must be reported upward: frauds of or above the threshold prescribed in the rules, one crore rupees, are reported to the Central Government through the audit committee or the board within the prescribed sequence and period, and frauds below the threshold are reported to the audit committee or the board and disclosed in the board's report. The duty is significant in two directions. It gives the auditor a statutory channel that bypasses the management that may be implicated, answering the oldest structural complaint about audits commissioned and paid for by the audited. It also places the auditor inside the enforcement system as a reporting officer, a position the profession has not always welcomed, since it converts professional suspicion into a state-facing obligation with consequences for the auditor who fails to report. Cost audit under Section 148, imposed on prescribed classes of companies in specified sectors, and the auditor's removal and resignation discipline under Sections 140 and 141, including the special resolution and Central Government approval required to remove an auditor before term, complete a regime in which the auditor's tenure, services, duties and exit are all matters of statute rather than engagement letter.

Two further instruments complete the verification architecture and both are composition devices in their own way. Secretarial audit under Section 204 requires every listed company and the prescribed classes, framed by paid-up capital and turnover thresholds in the rules and extended to companies with large outstanding bank borrowings, to annex to the board's report a secretarial audit report given by a company secretary in practice, extending the audit idea from finance to compliance with the whole body of applicable law. Registered valuers under Section 247 create a regulated profession for the valuations the Act repeatedly requires, in schemes, in issues of shares and in winding up, replacing a market in which any person's valuation could be put before a tribunal with a regime of registration, a code of conduct and discipline administered under the rules, with the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India designated as the authority for the purpose. Neither instrument attracted the attention given to rotation or the NFRA, but both express the same instinct: the Act trusts processes performed by identified, regulated, answerable professionals, and it manufactures such professionals where the market had not.

## The six new institutions

An inventory clarifies what the Act actually built. Several of its most discussed features, independent directors, the woman director, codified duties, are rules rather than institutions. The institutions are the durable creations: a new corporate form, new adjudicatory and regulatory bodies, a new collective remedy, a new investigative office on a statutory footing, and a new spending mandate with its own committee. The table below states the six that had no equivalent in the 1956 Act, with the class of company each binds, the failure each is designed to prevent, and the record each has accumulated.

### Which institutions did the Companies Act 2013 create that the 1956 Act lacked?

The Act created the One Person Company as a corporate form, the National Company Law Tribunal as the single company forum, the National Financial Reporting Authority as an independent audit regulator, a class action for members and depositors, and a corporate social responsibility mandate, and it placed the Serious Fraud Investigation Office on a statutory footing.

| Institution | Provision | Class of company bound | Designed to prevent | Record so far |
|---|---|---|---|
| One Person Company | Sections 2(62) and 3(1)(c) | A single promoter forming a private company | The fiction of the dummy second shareholder and the exclusion of the sole entrepreneur from limited liability | Widely adopted as an entry form; eligibility later widened beyond resident citizens and the conversion and capital restrictions progressively relaxed |
| National Company Law Tribunal | Sections 407 to 434, notified from 1 June 2016 | All companies, as the forum for oppression, winding up, schemes and class actions | The fragmented forum of courts, Company Law Board and BIFR that made company disputes interminable | Constituted and carrying the company law docket, with the insolvency jurisdiction added by the 2016 Code; pendency and bench strength remain the standing complaints |
| National Financial Reporting Authority | Section 132, constituted 1 October 2018 | Auditors of listed companies, large unlisted public companies, banks, insurers and referred bodies | Self-regulation of auditors by their own profession after audit failures of the Satyam kind | Constituted five years after enactment; disciplinary orders against auditors in failed-company audits issued; remit confined to the public-interest end of the market |
| Serious Fraud Investigation Office on a statutory footing | Sections 211 and 212 | Companies assigned by the Central Government on the triggers in Section 212 | Fraud investigation by machinery without legal authority or specialist staff | Investigated the major corporate failures referred to it; prosecutions proceed through special courts and remain slow relative to the scale of reference |

The One Person Company rewards a closer look because it is the form through which the largest number of new entrants meet the Act. Before 2013, the sole entrepreneur who wanted limited liability had to find a second person to hold a share, and the registers were full of spouses and relatives holding single shares in companies they took no part in, a fiction the law required and everyone understood. The OPC abolished the fiction and replaced it with the nominee mechanism described in the questions below: the single member names a person who becomes the member on the member's death or incapacity, so that the company's continuity does not depend on a probate of its only shareholder's estate. The form as enacted carried restrictions that limited its use, a ceiling configuration that forced conversion into a private or public company when paid-up capital or turnover crossed the prescribed lines, and eligibility confined to a resident Indian citizen; the amendments of the rules in 2021 removed the forced conversion triggers, reduced the residence qualifying period to one hundred and twenty days and opened eligibility to non-resident Indians, converting the OPC from a tightly fenced experiment into the register's standard small entry form. The record of the form is the record of a good institutional idea initially drafted too cautiously and then corrected by the same executive layer that adjusted the rest of the statute, which is why it belongs in a table of the Act's institutions and in the assessment of how the Act learns.

The table is confined, as the brief directs, to the six institutions with the largest governance footprint, but the institutional inventory of the Act is longer, and one further creation should be recorded so the map is not mistaken for the territory. The Investor Education and Protection Fund, carried in Section 125, gathers the unpaid dividends, matured deposits, application money and other amounts that remain unclaimed for seven years, together with the shares on which dividend has gone unclaimed for the matching period, and is administered by a statutory authority constituted under the section, from which the rightful owners may claim refunds through the prescribed process. The Fund is a small institution beside the tribunal and the audit regulator, but it is characteristically of this Act: a constituency that could not protect itself, the absent small investor, is given a custodian by statute, and a leakage of the older system, in which unclaimed money simply rested with the company, is closed by an act of institutional design.

Two features of the table deserve comment. The first is the staggered arrival of the institutions: the tribunal chapters waited until 2016, the Authority until 2018, the class action thresholds until 2019, so that the statute's advertised machinery became real across most of a decade rather than on its commencement date. The second is that four of the six are instruments of accountability pointed at the company from outside or below, the tribunal, the Authority, the investigator and the class action, which is the institutional form of the governance diagnosis with which this article began.

## Shareholders with a remedy: class action and the tribunal

The 1956 Act gave the aggrieved shareholder two routes: the oppression and mismanagement petition, and the derivative grievance that Indian law never developed into a usable form. The first route survived into the 2013 Act as Sections 241 to 244, with the forum changed; the second gap the 2013 Act filled with the class action, the remedy its drafting history, from the Irani Committee onward, had repeatedly recommended and the Satyam episode made politically unavoidable, since thousands of small shareholders in a failed company are the paradigm case of a class holding a common grievance and no individual incentive to sue.

Section 245 permits the prescribed number of members or depositors, or any class of them, who are of the opinion that the management or control of the company has been conducted in a manner prejudicial to the interests of the company or its members or depositors, to apply to the Tribunal for orders across a wide remedial list: restraining the company from acts contrary to its memorandum or articles or to resolutions of the members, declaring void resolutions passed by suppression of material facts, restraining directors from acting on such resolutions, removing directors, recovering damages or compensation from the company, its directors, its auditor including the audit firm, and any expert, advisor or consultant, for fraudulent, unlawful or wrongful acts, and claiming damages from such persons. The order of the Tribunal binds the company and all its members or depositors, which is what makes the action a class action rather than a bundle of individual claims. The section was brought into force on 1 June 2016, but the numbers that make it usable were left to prescription, and they arrived only with the amendment of the National Company Law Tribunal Rules in May 2019: for a company with share capital, at least five per cent of the total number of members or one hundred members, whichever is less, or members holding at least five per cent of the issued share capital in an unlisted company and two per cent in a listed company; for depositors, five per cent of their number or one hundred, whichever is less, or depositors owed five per cent of the total deposits; and for a company without share capital, one-fifth of its total membership. The record since has been thin, and the honest summary is that the remedy exists, is procedurally complete, and has been used sparingly, with its best-known application contested at the threshold rather than decided on the merits. The reasons for the thinness are structural and worth naming, because they qualify what the remedy's existence proves. A class action must be organised before it can be filed, and Indian shareholding in the affected companies is dispersed among holders with small individual stakes, no litigation funding market of the kind that sustains such actions in the United States, and a costs environment in which the applicant class bears the expense of establishing the very facts that the company's records contain. The oppression petition competes for the same grievances with a lower organisational burden where a substantial minority holder exists, and the securities regulator's enforcement and the fraud investigation machinery offer routes that cost the shareholder nothing. The class action's significance is therefore best measured as a change in the bargaining structure rather than in the case count: a remedy that can be invoked by two per cent of a listed company's capital alters the settlement conversation even when it is rarely carried to judgment, and the admissibility litigation in the Jindal Poly Films matter, in which the maintainability of the country's most prominent class action was fought through the tribunal system, is the process by which the remedy's real thresholds of good faith, common interest and class definition will be fixed.

### Where do oppression and mismanagement petitions go under the Act?

Petitions alleging oppression and mismanagement under Sections 241 to 244 are filed before the National Company Law Tribunal, which replaced the Company Law Board and the courts as the forum for company disputes when its provisions commenced on 1 June 2016. Appeals from the Tribunal lie to the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal.

The oppression jurisdiction itself repays attention because it is where the Act's governance theory meets litigation practice. Section 241 lets a member complain that the affairs of the company are being conducted in a manner prejudicial to the public interest, to the company's interest, or oppressive to any member, and Section 242 arms the Tribunal with remedial powers of unusual breadth, including regulating the conduct of the company's affairs in future, ordering the purchase of shares by other members or by the company, restricting the transfer of shares, terminating or modifying agreements with directors and others, and setting aside transactions. The threshold in Section 244 requires, in a company with share capital, the lesser of one hundred members or one-tenth of the total membership, or members holding one-tenth of the issued share capital, with a discretion in the Tribunal to waive the requirement. The jurisdiction's modern history is dominated by the dispute between Tata Sons and the companies associated with Cyrus Mistry after his removal as chairman in 2016: petitions alleging oppression were dismissed by the Tribunal in 2018, allowed in substantial part by the Appellate Tribunal in December 2019, and finally resolved by the Supreme Court in March 2021, which set aside the appellate order and held that the removal was not oppressive within the meaning of the provision and that the conversion and governance arrangements challenged before it did not make out the statutory case. Stated at the level of proposition, which is the level this pillar maintains for contested doctrine, the decision confirmed both the breadth of the Tribunal's inquiry and the discipline of its threshold: conduct that is unfair in the eyes of the removed director is not, without more, oppression of the shareholder. The detailed judicial treatment of these provisions belongs to the interpretation articles of the cluster; the institution article on [the NCLT and NCLAT](/2012/05/01/nclt-nclat-explained/) carries the forum's own record, and the adjudication sentence of this pillar points the reader there for the tribunal's composition, benches and appellate structure.

## Corporate social responsibility at pillar level

Section 135 is the provision by which the Act is known to many readers who never open it, and it is deliberately treated briefly here, because the cluster assigns it a specialist article of its own. In outline: every company with net worth of five hundred crore rupees or more, or turnover of one thousand crore rupees or more, or net profit of five crore rupees or more in the immediately preceding financial year must constitute a corporate social responsibility committee of the board, formulate a CSR policy, and spend at least two per cent of the average net profits of the three immediately preceding financial years on the activities listed in Schedule VII, or explain the failure to spend. The design converted social expenditure from philanthropy at managerial discretion into a board-level statutory function with a committee, a policy and a disclosure, which is the composition method applied to an external obligation. The 2019 and 2020 amendments hardened the regime, moving unspent amounts into specified funds and an Unspent CSR Account and attaching consequences that the original comply-or-explain design had avoided, and the questions of which companies are caught, what spending qualifies, and what happens to unspent money are owned by the article on [CSR and Section 135](/2012/03/15/csr-law-india-section-135/), to which the reader needing the operative detail should go directly. The design attracts two opposed readings, and the pillar should state them since they recur in every argument about the section. The first reading treats the two per cent as a quasi-tax, a levy on profit routed through the board rather than the exchequer, and objects that a legislature which wishes to fund schools and sanitation should tax and spend through budgets answerable to Parliament, not conscript corporate treasuries into welfare delivery under a list of permitted objects drawn up in a schedule. The second reading treats the mandate as the price of the corporate form's privileges in a poor country: limited liability and perpetual succession are grants of the legal system, and a statute that attaches a measured social return to those grants, leaving the choice of object and geography with the board, is a bargain rather than a confiscation. The comply-or-explain origin of the design supports the second reading, since the section as enacted trusted disclosure to discipline the board, while the later hardening, with its transfer of unspent amounts and its consequences, supports the first reading's suspicion that the mandate was always intended to become compulsory in effect. The honest summary for the pillar is that Section 135 began as the composition method applied to generosity and has drifted, by amendment, toward a spending obligation with the board retained as its administrator, and that both the drift and the resistance to it are live questions the specialist article carries.

What belongs to this pillar is only the classification: Section 135 is the clearest single proof that the 2013 Act understands the company as an institution with obligations to constituencies beyond its shareholders, an understanding that Section 166's list of interests, employees, the community and the environment, states as a duty of the board itself.

## The professional machinery: secretarial standards and the compliance professions

A feature of the 2013 Act that foreign readers find unusual, and that belongs in any complete account, is the statutory position it gives to the professions that operate it. Section 118(10) obliges every company to observe the secretarial standards with respect to general and board meetings issued by the Institute of Company Secretaries of India and approved by the Central Government, which gives the standards on meetings, SS-1 and SS-2, a statutory force that professional guidance rarely carries anywhere: the notice periods, the agenda papers, the conduct of polls and the custody of minutes are matters of law because a professional institute's standard says so and the section adopts it. The company secretary, as key managerial personnel, is the officer through whom this discipline runs, and the Act's repeated resort to certification by professionals in practice, of annual returns, of secretarial audit, of the declarations that accompany schemes and reductions, builds a regulated intermediary layer between the company and the Registrar. The design has a cost that the critics record: it makes the professions gatekeepers of their own market, and the compliance calendar's complexity is a standing professional interest. It also has the benefit the defenders record: a statute administered by a thin Registrar service across more than a million companies is only administrable because an organised profession performs the first level of verification, and the Act's wager is that disciplined intermediaries are cheaper than inspectors. The wager connects to the larger one this article has tracked throughout, since the secretarial standard, the registered valuer, the rotated auditor and the independent director are all instances of a single technique: the manufacture of answerable roles, filled by identifiable persons, through which the statute governs institutions far larger than its own administrative reach.

## Fraud, Section 447 and the enforcement ladder

Every statute in this series has a provision that concentrates its philosophy, and in the Companies Act 2013 it is Section 447. The explanation to the section defines fraud widely: any act, omission, concealment of any fact or abuse of position committed by any person, or by any other person with that person's connivance, in any manner, with intent to deceive, to gain undue advantage from, or to injure the interests of, the company or its shareholders or its creditors or any other person, whether or not there is any wrongful gain or wrongful loss. The breadth is in the design and it is deliberate: the wrong is complete on the intent to deceive or injure, without proof that anyone gained or lost. The punishment in the section as enacted is imprisonment for a term not less than six months and extending to ten years, with a fine not less than the amount involved in the fraud and extending to three times that amount, and where the fraud involves public interest the minimum imprisonment rises to three years. Fraud is non-compoundable, and a conviction carries consequences for the offender's fitness to hold office.

The criticism the section attracted was immediate and came from within the profession that had to advise under it. The definition, the argument ran, sweeps in conduct far beyond the popular meaning of fraud: a concealment of fact with intent to injure the interests of the company may describe acts of omission, aggressive accounting positions and boardroom failures that earlier law would have treated as negligence, breach of duty or misstatement, each with its own calibrated consequence, rather than as a single offence carrying a ten-year maximum and a floor of imprisonment. The investigating machinery amplified the concern, since a Section 447 allegation could be set in motion on the reports of registrars, inspectors and the SFIO, and the bail discipline attached to the section made arrest itself a punishment of substance. The counter-argument, made by the statute's defenders in the aftermath of the accounting scandals that framed its passage, is that the breadth was the point: the promoter who strips a company rarely commits a tidy, labelled offence, and a fraud provision confined to the classic elements would leave the characteristic Indian corporate fraud, the siphoning of funds through layered transactions and related parties, arguing about labels while the money moved. Both arguments describe real features of the section, and the later treatment of Section 447, which preserved the offence while building a lesser track beside it for small frauds not involving public interest, is best read as Parliament declining to choose between them and instead sorting by scale.

The enforcement ladder around Section 447 runs from the Registrar of Companies through inspection and inquiry under Sections 206 to 209, investigation under Sections 210 to 213, and the SFIO for the gravest references, with prosecution before special courts designated under the Act. The rungs are distinct legal instruments and the distinctions matter in practice. Inspection under Section 206 begins with the Registrar's power to call for information and explanations and to inspect books where the material filed or received gives reason; inquiry under Section 207 follows where the Registrar is satisfied a fuller examination is warranted, conducted by the Registrar or inspectors with powers to examine officers on oath. Investigation under Sections 210 to 213 is the escalated form, ordered by the Central Government on the opinion formed from a Registrar's or inspector's report, on intimation of a special resolution of the company, in the public interest, or on the order of a court or the Tribunal, and it carries the inspector's powers to examine, to require production from other bodies corporate in the group, and to seek freezing of assets and restrictions on securities where the investigation would otherwise be frustrated. Section 213 adds the Tribunal's power to order investigation on the application of the prescribed number of members or of a person authorised by the Central Government, which gives the shareholder body a route into the investigative machinery that parallels the class action and is older in spirit. The SFIO investigation described earlier in this article is the form the ladder takes at its top for fraud of complexity, and its report is treated for prosecution purposes with the standing the section confers. A reader who understands the ladder understands why the Act's enforcement feels different at different heights: the Registrar's correspondence is administrative, the inspector's examination is inquisitorial, and the SFIO's investigation is the prelude to prosecution for the fraud offence itself. The Registrar's powers to call for information, inspect books and conduct inquiries are the everyday machinery; the investigation provisions are the heavy machinery, and the distinction matters for the reader's model of the Act, because most companies experience the statute through the Registrar and the filing system, while the investigative chapters exist for the minority of cases in which compliance failure shades into suspected fraud. Winding up, since the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code of 2016, runs on a separate track that its own article in this series owns: the tribunal retains winding up on grounds other than inability to pay debts, most significantly where the company's affairs have been conducted fraudulently or the company was formed for a fraudulent purpose, which ties the end of the enforcement ladder back to the fraud concept with which it began.

The prosecution machinery at the ladder's end has its own discipline, which completes the enforcement picture. Offences under the Act are non-cognizable within the meaning Section 439 gives that term in this code, and a court may take cognizance of an offence only on the written complaint of the Registrar, of a shareholder or member of the company, or of a person authorised by the Central Government, with a distinct channel for the securities regulator where the offence concerns issue and transfer of securities; the exception carved out for complaints by liquidators and by persons in the course of investigation or inspection proceedings keeps the machinery usable by those who uncover the defaults. Trials run before the special courts designated under Section 435, which are courts of session designated for the offences under this Act, with jurisdiction organised so that the fraud and public interest offences are tried by the forum equipped for them, and the special court's procedure is summary where the sentence in contemplation is short. The discipline matters because it is the point at which the decriminalisation question was actually decided in practice: an offence that only the Registrar or a member can set in motion, triable before a designated court, was a heavier instrument than its penalty suggested, and the migration of the procedural defaults out of this machinery is what the two amendment tranches, measured institutionally, principally achieved.

One enforcement instrument connects this cluster to the previous one, and it deserves its sentence. Section 177(9) requires every listed company and the prescribed classes, which include companies that accept deposits from the public and companies with borrowings from banks and public financial institutions above fifty crore rupees, to establish a vigil mechanism for directors and employees to report genuine concerns about unethical behaviour, actual or suspected fraud, or violation of the company's code of conduct, with safeguards against victimisation and direct access to the chairperson of the audit committee in exceptional cases. It is the corporate counterpart of the public whistleblower statute examined in the article on [the Whistle Blowers Protection Act 2014](/2012/01/01/whistle-blowers-protection-act-2014/), and the comparison is instructive: the public statute was enacted and never brought into force, while the corporate mechanism was built into the audit committee's charter and operates wherever the prescribed classes run it, so that the channel through which an Indian employee is most reliably able to report fraud in practice is the one this section created inside the company rather than the one Parliament created for the state. The practical value of such channels depends on the safety of the person using them, and the same problem of keeping documents and identities secure that confronts any insider confronts the journalists who report on corporate fraud; the account of [how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) is a useful study of the discipline involved, for readers building a research file on a company's record as much as for the reporters it describes.

## The Registrar, adjudication and the machinery of defaults

Underneath the governance architecture sits the administrative system that runs the Act daily, and no pillar account is complete without it. The Registrar of Companies for each state maintains the register of companies, receives the filings, issues the certificates and exercises the inspection and information powers of Sections 206 and following. Above the Registrars sit the Regional Directors and the Ministry of Corporate Affairs, which administers the Act, frames the rules and issues the circulars through which much of the Act's practical meaning is settled. The whole system runs on the MCA21 electronic registry, which replaced paper filing and made the Indian company register a searchable public database; the transparency consequences of that single administrative fact are easy to underrate, since journalists, lenders, litigants and researchers build on the register as a primary source in a way the paper era did not permit, and a company that files late or falsely does so on a record its counterparties can read.

Defaults meet this machinery through three doors, whose sorting is the practical core of the enforcement philosophy. The first door is adjudication of penalties under Section 454. Where a provision carries a penalty rather than a fine or imprisonment, the adjudicating officer, ordinarily the Registrar, issues notice, hears the company and its officers, and imposes the penalty by order, with an appeal to the Regional Director; the penalty is monetary, is computed in several provisions by a per-day measure subject to ceilings that differ for the company and its officers, and may be accompanied by a direction to rectify the default. The second door is compounding under Section 441, by which offences punishable with fine, or with imprisonment or fine, may be compounded by the Tribunal or the Regional Director according to the quantum involved, on payment of a sum up to the maximum fine, so that the offence is settled without conviction; offences punishable with imprisonment only, or with imprisonment and fine, and offences under investigation or repeated within three years, are excluded. The third door is prosecution before the special courts designated under Section 435 for the offences that remain criminal, which after the decriminalisation amendments are concentrated where the legislature intended them to be: fraud, offences involving public interest, and the defaults that resist the civil doors.

### How are penalties decided under the in-house adjudication mechanism?

Under Section 454 the adjudicating officer, ordinarily the Registrar of Companies, considers the default after hearing the company and its officers, and imposes the penalty fixed by the relevant provision, having regard to the company's size, the nature and repetition of the default and the loss caused. The order is appealable to the Regional Director.

Behind the three doors stands the residuary provision that reveals the original Act's drafting habit. Section 450 provides that where a company or any officer contravenes a provision of the Act or the rules for which no penalty or punishment is separately provided, the company and every officer in default are liable to a penalty of ten thousand rupees, and a further penalty of one thousand rupees for each day of continuing default, subject to ceilings. A residuary penalty of this kind is a confession that the draftsman cannot foresee every default a code of this size will generate, and it converts the code's silences into defaults rather than gaps. Its existence in the original design, alongside the hundreds of specified penal provisions, is the textual measure of the criminalisation the amendment arc later unwound: the Act began from the premise that every obligation should carry a sanction and every sanction should be findable, and the work of 2019 and 2020 was to sort that undifferentiated sanction mass into the civil and criminal piles the Srinivas principle describes.

The sorting matters to the honest assessment of the Act because the adjudication door is where the post-2020 statute lives for most companies. Its defenders point to volume and speed: penalties are imposed in large numbers through an electronic process, defaults are rectified alongside, and the criminal courts are relieved of a docket of procedural prosecutions that historically produced delay rather than deterrence. Its critics answer that penalty ceilings fixed in nominal terms erode with inflation, that adjudication by the filing authority concentrates investigator and judge in one office, and that the rectification-accompanying-penalty model can price non-compliance for companies large enough to treat penalties as a fee. The statute's own design answer is the escalation ladder: a default that is in truth a fraud is not meant to remain at the penalty door, because Section 447 and the investigation chapters stand behind the adjudication machinery for the cases that warrant them. The soundness of that answer depends, again, on whether the sorting is done well in practice, which is an administrative question the text cannot settle.

## Exemptions and the Act the executive can adjust

A complete pillar account must register how much of this statute the executive can adjust without returning to Parliament, because it conditions everything said above about thresholds and softening. Three instruments do the work. The first is the rules themselves: the thresholds for independent directors, committees, rotation and audit sit in rules, and the history of the Act's first decade is in part a history of those rules being revised, deferred and reissued. The second is Section 462, under which the Central Government may direct that any provision of the Act shall not apply to a class of companies, or shall apply with modifications, by notification laid before Parliament; the exemption notifications for private companies, government companies, Section 8 companies and companies in the international financial services centre have used this power extensively, so that the applicable text for a given company is the Act as filtered through the exemption notification addressed to its class, and the professional's first question after "what does the Act require" is "what has been exempted for this class". The third is the removal of difficulties power in Section 470, time-limited in form, and the schedule-amending power in Section 467. The defenders of this architecture point to the 1956 Act's rigidity and to the speed with which a threshold can be corrected when it catches the wrong companies. The critics point to a constitutional discomfort that the commentary has never quite put down: a statute whose obligations can be substantially rewritten by notification is, in part, a delegation of the legislative function to the ministry that administers it, and the decriminalisation-era softening was achieved as much through this executive layer as through the amending Acts. Both readings agree on the fact, which is the one the reader should retain: the Companies Act as it binds a particular company on a particular day is the enacted text as adjusted by rules and notifications, and any statement of its obligations that does not check the adjustments is incomplete.

## The amendment arc: from criminal statute to civil penalties

The Act that commenced in April 2014 was, among its other characteristics, a heavily criminalised statute. A large number of its obligations carried imprisonment, fines were expressed as criminal consequences, and the professional complaint that began almost immediately was that procedural and technical defaults, a late filing, a defective notice, a missed register entry, were punishable in the same register as substantive wrongdoing, exposing directors and officers to prosecution for the failures of a compliance calendar. The amendment sequence that followed is the record of that complaint winning, in stages, against the statute's original enforcement theory. The Companies (Amendment) Act 2015 came first and worked at the margins of incorporation and procedure, removing the minimum paid-up capital requirement and making the common seal optional among a set of ease-of-business corrections. The Companies (Amendment) Act 2017, enacted on the recommendations of the Company Law Committee of 2016, rationalised a wider set of provisions and adjusted definitions and procedures across the Act, among them the harmonisation of the private placement and issue provisions with the securities regulator's framework, the recalibration of the restrictions on layers of subsidiaries, the alignment of the related party definition and approvals, and a set of corrections to provisions that had proved unworkable in the first three years of operation. Neither altered the enforcement philosophy. The 2019 amendment, beside its decriminalisation tranche, also addressed the governance machinery itself: it required independent directors to be enrolled in a databank maintained for the purpose and introduced an online proficiency self-assessment for those without the prescribed experience, a measure that treated the quality of the independent director pool as a regulatory problem in its own right, and it tightened the corporate social responsibility regime's treatment of unspent amounts, a hardening completed by the 2020 amendment and the amended rules that followed. That the same Parliament which was decriminalising the procedural calendar was simultaneously raising the qualification bar for the statute's central governance office is the clearest evidence that the softening was directed at process rather than at governance as such. The change of philosophy came in two deliberate decriminalisation tranches.

The first tranche followed the report in 2018 of the committee chaired by Injeti Srinivas, constituted to review the offences under the Act, which adopted a principle that shaped everything after: violations that are technical or procedural, objectively determinable and without an element of fraud, deceit or injury to public interest should be dealt with as civil defaults through an in-house adjudication mechanism, and criminal process should be reserved for the rest. The Companies (Amendment) Act 2019 implemented the first part of that programme by shifting sixteen compoundable offences into the in-house adjudication framework under Section 454, in which adjudicating officers, in practice the Registrars, impose penalties through a technology-driven process without a criminal trial. The second tranche followed the Company Law Committee's 2019 report. The Companies (Amendment) Act 2020 decriminalised thirty-five more compoundable offences and, in eleven further offences, removed imprisonment while retaining fines, beside a set of penalty reductions and the lesser track for small frauds within Section 447 described above. The figures in this paragraph are the Ministry of Corporate Affairs' own account of the two phases, given in answer to Parliament, and they describe a genuine migration: after the two tranches, the Act's penal provisions divide between a majority of defaults handled as civil penalties by adjudicating officers and a residue of offences, concentrated around fraud and public interest, that remain criminal.

The same period softened the statute at other points, and a complete account of the arc should record the pattern rather than only the decriminalisation headline. Exemptions for private companies and for government companies multiplied by notification. Thresholds in the rules were revised, generally upward, withdrawing smaller companies from obligations designed for larger ones. The databank requirement for independent directors, introduced in 2019 with a proficiency test, was itself moderated after professional protest. Read whole, the arc from 2015 to 2020 shows a statute whose governance ambitions were left substantially intact in text while its coercive machinery was recalibrated downward, which is the precise sense in which the Act was softened in operation without being softened in ambition. Whether that recalibration was a correction or a dilution is the contested question the assessment takes up.

## Directors' liability and the officer in default

The composition design has a liability shadow, and the debate about it is one of the live arguments the profession conducts about this statute. Section 2(60) defines the officer who is in default through a list that runs from the whole-time director and key managerial personnel to directors aware of a contravention by participation, consent or connivance, and directors generally where no officer is identified. In the Act's original register, that definition, combined with penal provisions across the chapters, exposed directors, including non-executive and independent directors, to prosecution for defaults in filings and procedures that occurred in parts of the organisation they did not run. The professional complaint, voiced persistently after 2014, was that board seats, above all independent seats, were becoming difficult to fill with persons of quality because the office carried criminal exposure disproportionate to its information and power, and that resignations of independent directors, sometimes accompanied by public letters recording disagreement with management, were the visible symptom. The statute itself contained the limiting principle in Section 149(12) for independent and non-promoter non-executive directors, confining liability to acts within knowledge, consent, connivance or want of diligence, but the principle had to be invoked in defence rather than operating at the point of prosecution, and the Ministry of Corporate Affairs eventually supplemented it with administrative guidance, in a circular issued in March 2020, directing that proceedings against independent and non-executive directors follow a due process that tests the statutory limitation before process is launched, and that the records of board process be examined to locate responsibility. The decriminalisation tranches relieved the same pressure from the other side, by removing the criminal character of the defaults for which directors were most often prosecuted. The position the three instruments together produce is coherent in design: the diligent independent director is protected in liability, the procedural default is a civil penalty, and the fraud offence remains for the director who participated. Whether the protection operates early enough in practice is the question practitioners continue to press, and it is the everyday form of the structural objection stated earlier, since a check that must be litigated to be enjoyed is a weaker check on management than the statute's text promises.

## The courts and the tribunal experiment

The judicial treatment of the 2013 Act, at the level of proposition this pillar maintains, has run on two tracks. The first is the constitutional litigation over the tribunal itself. The National Company Law Tribunal was first designed into the 1956 Act by a 2002 amendment that was never brought into force, a history the replacement article owns; when the 2013 Act re-enacted the design, the Madras Bar Association's challenges tested the tribunal's composition and the qualifications of its members, and the Supreme Court in 2015 upheld the creation of the tribunal while reading the selection and qualification provisions against constitutional requirements for judicial independence in adjudicatory bodies, requiring corrections in the composition of the selection machinery. The tribunal that commenced work in June 2016 was therefore a judicially supervised institution from its first day, and the continuing litigation over tribunal appointments across statutes, of which the company tribunal is one instance, forms the background against which its benches are staffed. The second track is the tribunal's own jurisprudence on the provisions this article has described: the oppression jurisdiction exercised through the Tata and Mistry litigation to the Supreme Court's 2021 restatement, the scheme jurisdiction through which the large mergers of the Indian market are sanctioned, the class action's admissibility jurisprudence still in formation, and the steady body of orders on restoration of struck-off companies, compounding and approval applications that constitutes the tribunal's daily work. The specialist articles carry these lines in sequence; the pillar records the structural fact that company law in India is administered by a tribunal system the courts built alongside Parliament, and that the system's capacity, measured in bench strength and pendency, is the standing constraint on every remedy the Act creates. The appellate structure completes the picture a practitioner needs. An appeal from the Tribunal lies to the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal under Section 421 within forty five days of the order, on fact and law, and a further appeal lies to the Supreme Court under Section 423 on a question of law alone, within sixty days. The two-tier route, with the constitutional court confined to law, is the standard Indian tribunal design, and its practical consequence is that the Appellate Tribunal is the effective final court for most company disputes, which is why its oppression jurisprudence, and the Supreme Court's occasional correction of it, carry the weight described above. Orders of the Tribunal are enforceable as decrees, and its contempt and execution machinery borrows the civil court's, so that the forum the Act created is not a recommendatory body but a court for company purposes in everything except name.

## The honest assessment: composition, burden and the two critiques

Two serious critiques of this statute face each other, and the stance of this series requires each to be stated as its makers state it. The compliance-burden critique, pressed by industry bodies and felt most sharply by smaller companies and their professional advisers, holds that a statute designed in the shadow of listed-company scandals was applied through thresholds that still catch businesses without the administrative capacity the obligations assume, so that the cost of the governance architecture falls regressively on the companies least able to carry it and least likely to have produced the failures that justified it. On this account the decriminalisation amendments were a necessary correction of an enforcement theory that had confused default with dishonesty, and the proof offered is the character of the defaults migrated: late filings, register defects and procedural lapses that no one defends as fraud.

The governance critique answers from the opposite direction. Its makers, who include investor advocates and a strand of the commentary that followed each successive corporate failure after 2014, argue that the criminal consequence was the only deterrent promoters actually priced, that civil penalties computed in lakhs are a cost of doing business to groups whose diversions run in crores, and that the migration of offences into an adjudication mechanism staffed by the same Registrar machinery whose limitations the Act was written against cannot carry the weight the criminal process, for all its faults, was built to carry. On this account the correct reading of the two tranches is dilution, and the proof offered is that the governance failures the statute exists to prevent did not stop occurring during the years of its softening.

The evidence the two critiques fight over includes failures that occurred with the architecture in place, and the strongest of them should be faced directly. The collapse of the Infrastructure Leasing and Financial Services group in 2018, a systemically important non-banking financial group whose defaults cascaded through the debt markets, displayed the full sequence the 2013 Act was built to interrupt: a board with the prescribed composition, auditors who had certified through the period of strain and were later proceeded against, including by the National Financial Reporting Authority in respect of the audit of its financial services arm, credit rating failures examined in their own proceedings, and a government supersession of the board under Section 241 and the investigation machinery that followed. The governance critique reads IL&FS as the design's refutation: every mandated seat was filled and the failure happened anyway. The statute's defenders read the same episode as a demonstration of its instruments working late rather than never: the supersession power, the tribunal route, the audit regulator and the fraud investigation office were the tools the response actually used, and several of them did not exist in usable form under the 1956 Act. Both readings are available on the same facts, and the difference between them is a judgment about whether an accountability architecture should be measured by the failures it prevents, which cannot be counted, or by the response it makes possible when failure comes, which can. The professional examinations that test this statute, in company secretary and chartered accountancy papers and in the law papers of the public examinations, have settled on the same set of concepts as the hardest to examine and the most worth examining: the independent director's real content, the reach of Section 447, the mechanics of the class action, and the meaning of the decriminalisation migration, and a student who can argue the two critiques against each other on IL&FS holds the statute the way its examiners intend.

Scale disciplines both critiques, and the assessment should end with it. The register this statute governs holds companies counted in lakhs, of which the listed companies that dominate the commentary number only a few thousand. For the overwhelming majority of the register, the Companies Act is not the governance architecture at all; it is the filing calendar, the register of members, the annual return and the occasional notice from the Registrar, administered by directors who are also the shareholders and the managers. The compliance-burden critique is, at bottom, the complaint of this majority that a code written against the failures of the few thousand is priced for the lakhs, and the threshold map is the statute's continuing negotiation with that complaint. The governance critique is, at bottom, the observation that within the few thousand the architecture's presence has not prevented the failures it was built against, and that deterrence priced in penalties will not substitute for the independence the design assumes. A pillar does its work if the reader can see that both complaints are about the same statute viewed from different floors of it, and that the amendment arc, which lightened the calendar for the lakhs while keeping the fraud offence for the few, is Parliament's own attempt to govern both floors at once.

The record supports parts of both, and the honest verdict distinguishes the two halves of the statute. On composition, the Act changed the furniture of Indian boardrooms permanently: independent directors with a statutory code sit on the boards of listed and large public companies, audit committees approve related party transactions, auditors rotate, and no serious account of Indian corporate governance can be written in the categories of the 1956 Act. That is a structural achievement regardless of how any individual board behaves. On behaviour, the evidence is necessarily thinner and more contested, because the statute cannot itself supply the independence of mind its central office requires, and the critics' structural objection, that appointment by the checked party is a weak foundation for checking, has not been answered by any amendment. On enforcement, the decriminalisation of genuinely procedural defaults was justified on the principle the Srinivas Committee stated, and the retention of Section 447 and the SFIO for fraud of substance means the statute did not disarm; what it surrendered was the threat of prosecution for the calendar of compliance, and whether that threat was protecting governance or merely taxing it is the value judgment on which the two critiques ultimately divide. This article's verdict is that the design assumption identified at the outset, that Indian corporate failure is a governance failure located in the boardroom, remains the Act's strength and its exposure in a single proposition: it built the most complete governance architecture in Indian company law, and its success still depends on persons the statute can seat, define and protect, but cannot make courageous. The general account given here is legal information and analysis rather than advice on any individual company's position, and a company secretary or advocate should be consulted on the application of these provisions to a specific set of facts.

## Schemes, mergers and the restructuring chapters

The compromise and arrangement chapters, Sections 230 to 240, are where the Act governs the largest transactions in corporate life, and they complete the pillar's map of the operative provisions. A scheme of compromise or arrangement, which includes mergers, demergers, capital reductions structured as schemes and corporate debt restructuring, is proposed by the company, put to meetings of its creditors and members convened under the Tribunal's directions, and requires approval by a majority representing three-fourths in value of each class present and voting, followed by the Tribunal's sanction, which binds all creditors and members of the class and the company itself. The scheme machinery is the Act's principal instrument of consensual restructuring, and its design repeats the statute's grammar: the decision is taken by the affected constituencies in prescribed majorities, on prescribed disclosure that includes the scheme's effect on promoters, creditors, directors and key managerial personnel, and is then confirmed by the tribunal forum the Act created. Section 233 carves out a fast track for mergers between small companies and between a holding company and its wholly owned subsidiary, and for the other prescribed classes, in which the scheme is approved by the members and creditors at the prescribed high majorities and confirmed by the Central Government through the Regional Director rather than by the Tribunal, unless the Registrar or the official liquidator objects and the matter is referred upward. Section 234 admits mergers of Indian companies with companies in notified foreign jurisdictions, subject to the Reserve Bank's framework, which reversed the one-way position of the earlier law under which only the foreign company's merger into India had a statutory path of this kind. The purchase of a minority shareholding after a scheme, the takeover offers addressed in Section 235 and 236, and the registration of charges and the dissenting shareholder's exit complete a code within the code. For the pillar's purpose the point is jurisdictional: the largest reorganisations of Indian business pass through these sections and the tribunal they name, and a reader who knows the scheme route knows how Indian corporate combinations are legally made.

## The Act among its neighbours: winding up, insolvency and the sectoral regulators

No statute governs alone, and the 2013 Act's boundaries are part of its meaning. The sharpest boundary is with insolvency. As enacted, the Act carried a full winding up code in Chapter XX, including winding up by the Tribunal on grounds that included inability to pay debts. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016 removed the inability-to-pay ground from the Act and made the Code the route for corporate insolvency resolution and liquidation on default, with the same Tribunal as the adjudicating authority, while the Act retained winding up on the other grounds in Section 271, including fraud, default in holding meetings or filing statements of the specified kinds, and the just and equitable ground. Voluntary winding up migrated to the Code as well. Beside the two sits the strike-off power in Section 248, by which the Registrar removes from the register companies that have not commenced or carried on business, after notice and subject to the restoration jurisdiction of the Tribunal in Section 252; strike off, not liquidation, is how the register sheds its dead in practice, and the mass strike-off drives conducted under the section are the register's periodic correction of its own inflation. The reader should hold the division plainly: the Companies Act governs the company while it lives and while it is solvent, and the insolvency code governs its failure to pay.

The second boundary is with the securities regulator. For listed companies the Act shares the field with the Securities and Exchange Board of India, whose listing obligations and disclosure requirements impose a governance code that in several particulars, the proportion of independent directors, the woman independent director for the largest companies, the related party approval of material transactions by shareholders, runs ahead of the Act's floor. The two regimes are cumulative rather than competing: the Act is the floor of company law for every company, and the listing regulations are the additional storey for companies that have taken public money through the exchanges. Disputes about which regime governs a failure are usually misposed; the same conduct commonly breaches both, and the IL&FS and Satyam responses alike proceeded under company law, securities law and criminal law in parallel. The third boundary is with the sectoral regulators of banking, insurance and non-banking finance, whose statutes overlay the Act for the entities they license, and with the Competition Act for combinations, whose thresholds operate independently of the Tribunal's sanction of a scheme. The pillar's point is jurisdictional honesty: a reader who knows where the Companies Act stops knows a large part of what it is.

## Verdict

Two silences in the design are worth recording before the verdict, because a pillar should mark what its statute does not attempt. The Act does not adopt the benefit corporation or any form that subordinates profit to a stated social purpose; its social instrument is the Section 135 spending mandate laid on the ordinary company, not a new organisational form for the willing. It does not regulate takeovers, which remain with the securities regulator's code, nor does it codify the takeover-era doctrines of board neutrality that other jurisdictions place in company law. And it does not settle the constitutional question of the shareholder's place that its own Section 166 quietly complicates by directing the director's good faith toward members as a whole and, in the same sentence, toward employees, the community and the environment: the Act distributes the director's loyalty across constituencies without ranking them for the case of conflict, and the courts have been left to the fiduciary tradition to manage the ranking when it matters. A statute is partly the list of questions it declines to answer, and these three declinatures are the ones the specialist literature returns to.

The Companies Act 2013 is the baseline statute of Indian corporate life: the text against which boards are composed, audits are rotated, fraud is defined and shareholders organise. Its pillar position in this series means the cluster's remaining articles test the baseline from different sides, the replacement story and the head-to-head comparison of the two Acts, the CSR mandate in depth, the tribunal that hears its disputes, and the insolvency code that took over the largest part of its winding-up territory. What this article has established is the design: a governance statute that legislates for the composition of rooms, manufactures regulators and professions to police them, and then, within a decade, renegotiated with itself about how much of its own criminal machinery it believed in. The reader who holds that design clearly can read any of the specialist arguments that follow, which is the work a pillar exists to do. The working habits the pillar recommends follow from the design. Never ask what the Act requires of companies in general; ask what it requires of this company, in this class, at this size, after the exemption notification addressed to its class has been read. Never cite a threshold from the Act alone when the threshold lives in the rules; the rules are where this statute keeps its numbers, and the numbers move. Treat the board's composition, the auditor's tenure and the committee structure as the first facts to establish about any company whose conduct is in question, because the Act itself treats them as the causes of which conduct is the effect. And read the fraud provision last rather than first: Section 447 is the sanction that gives the architecture its shadow, but the architecture, the calendar, the committees, the registers and the filings, is the statute that a million companies actually inhabit, and the pillar's claim is that Indian corporate life is governed less by the provision everyone quotes than by the machinery almost nobody describes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the Companies Act 2013 govern?

The Companies Act 2013 is the principal statute governing companies incorporated in India. It governs the whole corporate life cycle: incorporation and the forms a company may take, the raising of capital through prospectus and private placement, management and administration, board composition and directors' duties, accounts and audit, investigations, compromises and winding up, and the adjudication of company disputes by the National Company Law Tribunal. Beyond procedure, it operates as a governance statute, prescribing who must sit on boards, how auditors are appointed and rotated, how related party transactions are approved, and which remedies shareholders and depositors may pursue when a company's affairs are conducted against their interests.

### Q: What is a One Person Company and who can form one?

A One Person Company is a company with a single member, introduced by Section 2(62) and Section 3(1)(c) of the Companies Act 2013 so that a sole entrepreneur can obtain the limited liability and separate legal personality of a company without recruiting a nominal second shareholder. Only a natural person may incorporate one, and the incorporator must nominate a second person who steps into membership if the original member dies or becomes incapable of contracting. As originally framed, eligibility was confined to an Indian citizen resident in India; later rule changes widened it to non-resident Indians and shortened the qualifying period of residence, and restrictions on capital and conversion were progressively relaxed.

### Q: Which companies must appoint independent directors?

Section 149 of the Companies Act 2013 requires every listed public company to have at least one-third of its board consisting of independent directors. The requirement is extended by the Companies (Appointment and Qualification of Directors) Rules to prescribed classes of unlisted public companies, namely those with paid-up share capital of ten crore rupees or more, turnover of one hundred crore rupees or more, or outstanding loans, debentures and deposits exceeding fifty crore rupees in aggregate. Private companies are outside the mandate unless they are subsidiaries of public companies caught by the rules. An independent director must satisfy the independence conditions of Section 149(6) and the code in Schedule IV.

### Q: Who counts as key managerial personnel in a company?

Key managerial personnel are defined by Section 2(51) of the Companies Act 2013. The category covers the chief executive officer or the managing director or the manager, the company secretary, the whole-time director, the chief financial officer, and any other officer prescribed under the rules, who include the chief risk officer where appointed for specified classes. Section 203 requires every listed company, and every other public company with paid-up share capital of ten crore rupees or more, to appoint whole-time key managerial personnel in the combination of a managing director or chief executive or manager, a company secretary and a chief financial officer.

### Q: What counts as fraud under the Companies Act 2013?

The explanation to Section 447 defines fraud as any act, omission, concealment of fact or abuse of position committed by any person, or by another person with that person's connivance, with intent to deceive, to gain undue advantage, or to injure the interests of the company, its shareholders, its creditors or any other person, whether or not there is wrongful gain or wrongful loss. The definition is complete on the intent and does not require proof that anyone profited or suffered loss. Fraud under the section carries imprisonment of six months to ten years with a mandatory minimum, and a fine linked to the amount involved in the fraud.

### Q: Can shareholders file a class action in India?

Yes. Section 245 of the Companies Act 2013, brought into force on 1 June 2016, permits the prescribed number of members or depositors to apply to the National Company Law Tribunal where they consider that a company's affairs are being conducted in a manner prejudicial to the interests of the company, its members or its depositors. The thresholds prescribed in the tribunal rules in 2019 are, for a company with share capital, five per cent of the total members or one hundred members, whichever is less, or members holding five per cent of the issued share capital in an unlisted company and two per cent in a listed company. The Tribunal's order binds the whole class.

### Q: Why does the Companies Act require auditor rotation?

Section 139(2) of the Companies Act 2013 imposes rotation because long, uninterrupted association between an auditor and a company's management was judged to erode the independence the audit exists to supply, a judgment hardened by the accounting scandals in which auditors certified accounts over many years while those accounts were being fabricated. Rotation breaks the association by calendar: an individual auditor may serve one term of five consecutive years and an audit firm two terms of five consecutive years for listed companies and the prescribed classes, after which a five-year cooling-off period bars the same auditor, firm or network affiliate from returning to that company.

### Q: Which companies must appoint a woman director?

The second proviso to Section 149(1) of the Companies Act 2013 requires prescribed classes of company to appoint at least one woman director, and the rules prescribe every listed company and every other public company with paid-up share capital of one hundred crore rupees or more or turnover of three hundred crore rupees or more. The obligation is a composition rule: it governs the membership of the board rather than any disclosure about it. An intermittent vacancy must be filled within the period the Act prescribes, and listed companies are additionally subject to the securities regulator's board composition requirements, which operate alongside the statutory floor.

### Q: What is a small company under the Companies Act 2013?

A small company is defined by Section 2(85) as a company other than a public company whose paid-up share capital and turnover do not exceed the limits prescribed in the rules, and which is not a holding or subsidiary company, a company registered for charitable purposes under Section 8, or a company governed by a special Act. The limits began at fifty lakh rupees of paid-up capital and two crore rupees of turnover and were raised in stages, reaching four crore rupees of paid-up capital and forty crore rupees of turnover. Small companies carry a lighter compliance regime, including fewer board meetings, no cash flow statement and reduced penalties for defaults.

### Q: What is the Serious Fraud Investigation Office and what does it investigate?

The Serious Fraud Investigation Office is the specialist fraud investigation office established under Section 211 of the Companies Act 2013, having existed since 2003 under a government resolution before receiving its statutory footing. Under Section 212 the Central Government may assign the investigation of a company's affairs to the office where a report of the Registrar or an inspector, a special resolution of the company, the public interest, or a request from another government authority makes investigation necessary. The office is staffed on a multidisciplinary model drawn from banking, corporate affairs, taxation, forensic audit and information technology, and an investigation assigned to it displaces parallel proceedings on the same matter.

### Q: What is secretarial audit and which companies must obtain one?

Secretarial audit is an audit of a company's compliance with the Companies Act, securities laws and the other laws applicable to it, conducted by a company secretary in practice and reported under Section 204 of the Companies Act 2013. Every listed company must obtain it, and the rules extend the requirement to public companies with paid-up share capital of fifty crore rupees or more or turnover of two hundred and fifty crore rupees or more, and to companies with outstanding loans or borrowings from banks or public financial institutions of one hundred crore rupees or more. The report is annexed to the board's report, and the board must explain any qualification the auditor records.

### Q: What is an audit committee and which companies must form one?

The audit committee is a committee of the board constituted under Section 177 of the Companies Act 2013 to oversee the company's financial reporting process, its auditors and its internal controls. Every listed public company must form one, and the rules extend the requirement to prescribed classes of public company framed by paid-up capital, turnover and outstanding borrowings thresholds. The committee must have a minimum of three directors with independent directors forming a majority, and its statutory role includes recommending the appointment and remuneration of auditors, reviewing financial statements, approving related party transactions and operating the company's vigil mechanism for reporting suspected fraud.

### Q: What are related party transactions and how does the Act regulate them?

A related party is defined by Section 2(76) of the Companies Act 2013 and includes directors, key managerial personnel and their relatives, firms and companies in which they hold prescribed positions or stakes, and holding, subsidiary and associate companies. Section 188 regulates the transactions a company enters into with such parties, covering the sale, purchase or supply of goods, the sale or purchase of property, agency arrangements, leasing, the rendering of services and appointments to offices of profit. Such transactions require approval of the board, and transactions above thresholds prescribed in the rules, framed mainly as percentages of turnover or net worth, require prior approval of the shareholders, from which the related party must generally abstain.

### Q: How many directors must a company have under the Companies Act 2013?

Section 149(1) fixes the minimum board size by class of company: a public company must have at least three directors, a private company at least two, and a One Person Company at least one. The maximum is fifteen directors, which the company may exceed by passing a special resolution. Every company must also have at least one director who has stayed in India for a total period of not less than one hundred and eighty two days during the financial year, the resident director requirement. The ceilings and floors operate alongside the composition rules for listed and prescribed companies, which additionally require independent directors and a woman director.

### Q: What is a dormant company under the Companies Act 2013?

A dormant company is a company that obtains the status provided by Section 455 of the Companies Act 2013. A company formed and registered for a future project, or to hold an asset or intellectual property, which has no significant accounting transaction may apply to the Registrar for the status of a dormant company, and the Registrar may also confer it on a company that has not filed financial statements or annual returns for two financial years consecutively after notice. A dormant company is relieved of much of the ordinary compliance calendar, including the frequency of board meetings and the constitution of its filings, until it applies to become active again.

### Q: Who is a promoter under the Companies Act 2013?

A promoter is defined by Section 2(69) of the Companies Act 2013 in three alternative ways. A promoter is a person who has been named as such in a prospectus or identified by the company in its annual return; a person who has control over the affairs of the company, directly or indirectly, whether as a shareholder, director or otherwise; or a person in accordance with whose advice, directions or instructions the board of directors is accustomed to act. The third limb does not catch a person who gives advice only in a professional capacity. The definition matters because promoters carry specific liabilities under the Act, including liability for misleading statements in a prospectus.

### Q: What is a registered valuer and when does a company need one?

A registered valuer is a person registered under Section 247 of the Companies Act 2013 to carry out valuations the Act requires to be independently performed. Where any property, stock, share, debenture, goodwill or net worth of a company is required to be valued under the Act, as in a further issue of shares, a scheme of arrangement, or the purchase of a minority shareholding, the valuation must be made by a registered valuer appointed by the audit committee or, in its absence, the board. Valuers are governed by the Companies (Registered Valuers and Valuation) Rules, which designate the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India as the authority for their registration and discipline.

### Q: Which offences did the Companies (Amendment) Act 2020 decriminalise?

The Companies (Amendment) Act 2020 was the second phase of the decriminalisation programme begun in 2019, when sixteen compoundable offences were shifted out of the criminal process. The 2020 Act decriminalised thirty-five more compoundable offences and removed imprisonment from eleven further offences while retaining fines, on the principle adopted from the Company Law Committee that defaults which are technical or procedural, objectively determinable and without fraud, deceit or injury to public interest should be civil defaults rather than crimes. The shifted defaults, which concern matters such as filing, register maintenance and procedural lapses, are dealt with through the in-house adjudication mechanism under Section 454, in which adjudicating officers impose penalties without a criminal trial.

### Q: When can a director be disqualified under the Companies Act 2013?

Section 164 lists the disqualifications. A person is disqualified on grounds personal to the individual, including being of unsound mind as declared by a court, being an undischarged insolvent, conviction of an offence involving moral turpitude with a sentence of six months or more, failure to pay calls on shares held, and conviction for offences relating to related party transactions or insider trading within the specified periods. A second set of disqualifications operates through the company: a person who is a director of a company that has failed to file financial statements or annual returns for three continuous financial years, or has failed for a year to repay deposits or debenture obligations, is disqualified from appointment as a director for five years.

### Q: Does the Companies Act 2013 apply to government companies and foreign companies?

It applies to both, with modifications. A government company, defined by Section 2(45) as one in which the Central Government, a state government, or both together hold at least fifty one per cent of the paid-up share capital, is a company under the Act, but Section 462 empowers the Central Government to direct by notification that provisions of the Act shall not apply, or shall apply with modifications, to government companies, and exemption notifications have been issued under that power. Foreign companies are governed by the chapter beginning with Section 379, which applies prescribed provisions of the Act to companies incorporated outside India that have a place of business in India, including requirements on accounts, registration of documents and charges.
