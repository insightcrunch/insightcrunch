## The Verdict First: A Frame Retired, Then Manhandled

The Chorus of Romeo and Juliet speaks two sonnets and then falls silent for three acts and a catastrophe, and the question this article answers is where that silence was made: in the study, by the dramatist, or in the playhouse, by the cut. Was the Chorus cut, in performance or in transmission, from a frame that once ran the length of the play, or did the frame stop where its author stopped writing it? The two halves of that question have different answers, and keeping them apart is the whole of the case. The speeches that survive were indeed cut, thinned, anonymized, and dropped by performers and by playhouse paperwork from the earliest years of the play's life, and the record of that treatment is documentary. The speeches that do not survive, a chorus before the third, fourth, and fifth acts and a closing chorus to round the action off, were never written at all. No early text contains one, no early text leaves a gap where one stood, and the play's own machinery explains the retirement. After the second act the tragedy runs on secrets, and a voice licensed to tell the truth has no honest work left to do. The Chorus was not cut from a complete frame. It was dismissed by the dramatist who had finished with it, and the theatre's later handling of the two speeches it left behind has disguised a compositional decision as a theatrical loss.

![The Chorus and the vanishing frame in Romeo and Juliet - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The distinction matters beyond pedantry, because each answer sends the play's handlers in opposite directions. If a fuller frame was written and lost, then editors are custodians of a mutilated design, directors who restore only the two surviving sonnets are staging a fragment, and the critic's task is reconstruction, an imagining of sonnets no document preserves. If the frame was designed to stop, then the two sonnets are complete in themselves, a matched pair of thresholds rather than the stump of a colonnade, and the silence after them is part of the play's meaning, the point at which narration yields to machinery and the audience is left alone with what the characters cannot see. The first reading makes the gap an accident of the archive. The second makes it an event in the play. This article argues the second, on evidence, and argues it against the strongest form of the first: the cut theory has real documents behind it, and they deserve to be weighed at full strength before they are set aside. What the weighing shows is that every document the cut theory owns describes damage to speeches that exist. About speeches that never existed, the documents are unanimous and silent, and their silence, properly read, is the loudest testimony in the case.

Three classes of evidence decide the question, and each will be heard in turn. The early texts come first: six editions between 1597 and 1637, whose treatment of the two surviving speeches is precise, patterned, and strange, and whose agreement about the missing ones is total. The period's choral practice comes second: what frames the dramatist's contemporaries built, what he built in other plays before and after, and what a two-speech frame looks like when set in that company. The stage history comes third: four centuries of productions, acting editions, and adaptations, which constitute a long experiment in what performers do with this material when nobody compels them to keep it. A standard of proof runs through all three. A written speech that was performed, copied, and then lost leaves traces of the kinds that lost speeches elsewhere in the period demonstrably leave: a gap in a text, an allusion in a document, a part in a plot, a complaint from a handler, an entry in an account. The case for a cut or lost chorus must produce traces of that order, or confess that its evidence is the shape of an expectation and nothing more.

## Two Sonnets and a Silence: The Shape of the Puzzle

The first speech is the more famous fourteen lines in English drama. The Chorus enters before any character, in a play whose printed persons it never joins, and delivers a sonnet that does the work of a whole exposition: "Two households, both alike in dignity, / In fair Verona, where we lay our scene" (Prologue.1-2). The feud is stated, its antiquity and its renewal; the lovers are introduced as "A pair of star-cross'd lovers" (Prologue.6) who take their life; the ending is given away entire, the deaths named as the instrument that buries the parents' strife; and the speech closes by contracting with the house for the performance, "The which if you with patient ears attend, / What here shall miss, our toil shall strive to mend" (Prologue.13-14). Every element of the tragedy's premise is in the room before a sword is drawn. The speech is a prologue in kind and a chorus in name, since the second quarto heads it "The Prologue" and prefixes it to the Chorus, and the doubleness is the first fact any theory must carry: the figure is a functionary of the frame, standing outside the fiction, possessed of the ending, addressing the audience in the company's person, "our stage," "our toil."

The second speech stands at the play's first interior joint, after the feast and before the orchard, and it is again a sonnet, again prefixed to the Chorus in the second quarto. Its opening turns the first act into a completed past: "Now old desire doth in his deathbed lie, / And young affection gapes to be his heir" (2.0.1-2). Rosaline is dismissed in a quatrain, "That fair for which love groaned for and would die, / With tender Juliet matched, is now not fair" (2.0.3-4); the new love's danger is stated in the second quatrain, a lover bound to his "foe supposed" (2.0.7), a beloved stealing "love's sweet bait from fearful hooks" (2.0.8); and the couplet promises the engine of the middle acts, "But passion lends them power, time means, to meet, / Tempering extremities with extreme sweet" (2.0.13-14). The speech is load-bearing in a quieter way than its predecessor. The Prologue prophesies; the second Chorus summarizes. Between them the frame's two great offices, foreknowledge and retrospect, announcement and audit, are both discharged once. Then the voice stops, and the remaining three acts, containing a secret marriage, a double killing, a banishment, a forced wedding, a potion, a plague-stopped letter, and a double suicide, unfold without one syllable of choral comment.

The puzzle is the sequence, not either speech. A single prologue is among the commonest furnishings of the period's stages, and a play that used the Chorus once and never again would raise no eyebrow. Two speeches at consecutive thresholds establish a series, and a series creates its own expectation with the force of a promise: having spoken before the first act and before the second, the Chorus is looked for before the third, and the third act is the hinge of the whole construction, the street fight in which the comedy of the opening curdles into the tragedy of the close. Silence there is conspicuous. Silence before the fourth act, over the potion plot, is conspicuous again. Silence before the fifth, and silence at the end, where the Senecan and academic traditions placed a choral envoi and where this play instead gives its last word to the Prince inside the fiction, completes a pattern that has struck readers for centuries as either an amputation or an abandonment. The series' study of the Chorus's identity settles whose voice the frame carries: the play's one authorized narrator, holder of the author's own powers of foresight and immunity, in the account given by [the Chorus in Romeo and Juliet and the question of who is speaking](/2023/03/17/chorus-in-romeo-and-juliet-analysis/). What that voice does after its second speech is the separate question owned here. A voice of that authority does not simply fade. Either somebody silenced it, or its owner withdrew it, and the difference between those events is the difference between a damaged play and a designed one.

### Did any early edition print a third Chorus speech?

No. Six early editions carry the play between 1597 and 1637, and the fullest of them, the second quarto of 1599, prints exactly the two speeches that modern editions print. No quarto and no folio preserves a chorus before the third, fourth, or fifth act, and none preserves a closing chorus of any kind.

The negative deserves its weight stated plainly, because the whole cut theory must at some point confront it. The second quarto is a full text, derived from the author's own papers on the standard account of its provenance, and it is generous to the frame: it prints both sonnets complete, each under the Chorus name, at the two joints where the action changes state. A manuscript that preserved the frame's first two members with that care is an unlikely place for three further members to have evaporated without trace before copying. The reprint line that descends from it, the quartos of 1609, 1622, and 1637, transmits the same two speeches and no more. The performance-shaped witnesses, examined in the next section, each sacrifice one of the two; neither sacrifices, mentions, or makes room for any other. Whatever happened to the Chorus, it happened identically in the study line and the stage line: the frame that reached every seventeenth-century handler of the play had two members and a silence, and any theory that populates the silence with lost sonnets must explain why the loss is invisible in every surviving channel at once.

## What the Early Texts Show: A Frame Damaged, Never Longer

The textual evidence is the foundation of the case, and it cuts both ways with unusual cleanliness, which is why both theories claim it. The full documentary account belongs to the series' study of [the Prologue's strange textual status](/2026/07/17/prologue-textual-status/); the findings that bear on the performance question can be stated compactly. Six early editions carry the play. The second quarto of 1599 prints both Chorus speeches complete, the first headed "The Prologue" and both prefixed to the Chorus, and it descends, on the standard account, from the author's draft rather than from the playhouse. The third, fourth, and fifth quartos reprint that text and keep the frame entire. Against that line stand two witnesses shaped by performance. The first quarto of 1597, a shortened and corrupted version carried into print through the theatre's orbit, prints the Prologue in twelve lines instead of fourteen, its rhymes broken and its sense coarsened, and omits the second Chorus altogether. The First Folio of 1623, set from a copy of the third quarto annotated against playhouse documents, makes the mirror-image sacrifice: it omits the Prologue entire and prints the second Chorus stripped of heading, entrance, and speaker, an anonymous sonnet lodged between the feast and the orchard. Between them, the two performance-shaped witnesses damaged the frame at every point where damage could fall, and the two damages were different damages.

The profile of the first quarto's Prologue shows the medium at work. Its opening line turns the matched institutions of the second quarto, "Two households, both alike in dignity" (Prologue.1), into "Two household friends alike in dignity," the civic chill warmed into gossip while the sense survives. Its third line trades the temporal architecture of "From ancient grudge break to new mutiny" (Prologue.3) for a past-tense tangle, and its ninth line, the line that should carry the death-mark'd love, vanishes as a line, the strong word washing up inside a parenthetical patch reassigned to the parents' rage. Lines are shed from inside quatrains, leaving rhyme partners unrhymed, while damaged matter is retained and patched. That is not the profile of a reviser shortening a speech for the stage, since a reviser cuts whole lines and keeps the scheme standing for the speaker's memory and the audience's ear. It is the profile of recall, of a sonnet carried in the ear by someone who heard it across a stage rather than read it on a page. The second Chorus, in the same witness, is not damaged but absent, and its absence belongs to the same economy: a version of the play shortened for performance and reported through memory sheds commentary before it sheds story, because commentary is what a playing text can spare and what a reporter's memory holds worst.

### What does the First Folio put where the Prologue should stand?

Nothing of the Chorus at all. The Folio opens the tragedy with a structural label, "Actus Primus. Scoena Prima," and the serving-men's brawl, so the play begins inside its architecture with drawn swords. The Prologue stood outside that architecture, before the first act began, and the copy's preparation had no shelf for it.

The Folio's treatment of the second Chorus completes the pattern, and its detail rewards attention. The fourteen lines stand in the volume, occupying through lines 733 to 746 of the tragedy in the Folio's own numeration, and they agree with the second quarto in substance at nearly every point, with only the drift of accidentals between them: an open "death bed," a "yong" for "young," a levelled participle. What the Folio stripped was not wording but standing. Stripped of its prefix, the speech ceases to be a person's utterance and becomes an insertion, legible in place, attributable to nobody. The preparation that produced the Folio's copy was conservative toward matter embedded in the sequence of scenes and ruthless toward apparatus that stood outside it, and the two Chorus speeches fell on opposite sides of that habit by their positions alone: the second stood inside the act structure the Folio was busy regularizing, the first stood before it. Survival, in the collected edition, was a matter of filing. Compositorial accident explains none of this, since the Folio's compositors, whose stints are documented across the volume, set what their copy gave them, including a speech without a prefix, an irregularity no workman invents to save labour. The frame's fortunes were decided upstream of the presses, in documents prepared by men who used the play rather than read it.

Set the witnesses side by side and a single pattern governs them. The speeches are safest in the line of transmission that runs through print shops, from the author's papers to the second quarto and down its reprint descent, and they are damaged or missing precisely where the line runs through the playhouse: in a text shaped by performance and report, and in a text whose copy was annotated against theatrical documents. A purely bibliographical accident would not sort the witnesses so neatly by their distance from the stage. The frame survived where the play was treated as a book and suffered where the play was treated as a show. That distribution is the cut theory's genuine evidence, and this article concedes it at full strength: performance and its paperwork were hard on the Chorus from the beginning. But the distribution has a second edge, and the edge is decisive. The witnesses disagree about everything except the frame's extent. The playhouse witnesses each found two speeches in the play and left one; the study witness found two and kept both; the reprint line found two and passed both on. No channel, however rough its handling, testifies to a third. Handlers who demonstrably cut the Chorus when they met it met it as a pair, and a theory of lost later choruses must posit speeches that vanished before the earliest of these channels opened, leaving the pair's neat survival as a coincidence. Evidence does not get to be that convenient. The honest summary of the textual record is that the frame was always two speeches long and frequently one speech shorter, depending on who was handling it.

One staging fact disciplines the vocabulary of the whole debate, and the texts state it plainly. Neither quarto prints act or scene divisions anywhere in the play. The numbered architecture through which the action is habitually discussed, the five acts with their joints at which a chorus might stand, is an inheritance from the Folio's presentation, refined by later editors, and it describes the play as a book is filed rather than as an afternoon was played. On the continuous stage of the 1590s, without act intervals of the later indoor kind, the action ran as a stream, and the second Chorus stands in that stream as Erne's numbering of the second quarto catches it, a speech at the feast's tail, cited as 1.5.144-57 in the undivided text, less the herald of a new act than a hinge inside a single movement. The stations at which this article, following the universal shorthand, locates the frame's gaps are therefore editorial coordinates, useful for reference and treacherous as psychology: nobody in the first audiences sat through an interval waiting for a third sonnet, because nobody sat through an interval at all. What an audience experienced was simpler and more decisive. Early in the stream, twice, a voice outside the fiction stepped forward and explained the water. Then the explaining stopped, the stream quickened, and the fiction carried its own meaning at its own speed for the remaining two thirds of the afternoon. A retirement spread across a continuous action is harder to mistake for an amputation than a missing act furniture would be, and the continuous stage, properly remembered, removes the last prop from under the image of a colonnade with columns knocked out. There was no colonnade. There was a voice that spoke while the play still needed one.

## The Cut Theory at Full Strength

The cut theory deserves its strongest form, because its strongest form is formidable. It begins from a fact about the period's paperwork that Tiffany Stern's research on early modern performance documents established and that the series' textual study adopted in its adjudication: a prologue was not necessarily part of a play's book at all. Prologues were occasional pieces, composed for first performances, court nights, and particular audiences, carried on separate sheets, sometimes posted, sometimes printed apart from the plays they introduced, and dispensable the moment the occasion passed. A speech of that kind lives at the mercy of the playhouse's filing habits. It can be spoken at a premiere, omitted at a revival, detached from the promptbook, and lost without any scene noticing its absence, because no scene depends on it. The Chorus of this play wears the prologue's livery in its first speech and the chorus's name in its prefix, and a theory that treats both speeches as playhouse furnishings of the detachable kind explains their documented fates with economy: the first quarto's makers dropped one, the Folio's annotator dropped the other, and nobody in either shop thought the loss worth recording. The theory then extends itself one step further, and the extension is its bid for the whole question. If the playhouse treated the frame as furnishings, the frame the author delivered may have been fuller than the frame the documents preserve: choruses before the later acts, a closing chorus, each written on the same perishable footing as the Prologue, each discarded as the play settled into repertory, their loss complete because their medium was.

The length of the play gives the extension its muscle. Lukas Erne's edition of the first quarto, the standard modern presentation of the short text, describes a version shorter than the second quarto by some seven hundred lines, almost a third again as brief in the other direction, tightened scene by scene, with fuller and more theatrical stage directions than the longer text carries. Erne's introduction weighs the first quarto as a stage abridgement, a version cut down for playing, and his edition closes with a casting and doubling appendix that treats the short text as a company document, a thing a troupe could actually perform. The play's two early shapes, on this account, are a reading text and a playing text, and the playing text is the shorter by a margin no theory of mere corruption explains. Set that against the Prologue's own advertisement, that the story "Is now the two hours' traffic of our stage" (Prologue.12). Daniel's parallel-text count, still quoted in the short quarto's editions, gives the first quarto 2,232 lines against the second quarto's 3,307. A company promising two hours of traffic had hard arithmetic to do, and the arithmetic fell where arithmetic always falls in a playhouse, on the passages whose removal the plot would never report. The full anatomy of the short quarto, its provenance and its witnesses, is laid out in the series' account of [the first quarto of 1597 and the 'bad' quarto question](/2026/03/27/first-quarto-1597-bad-quarto/); the bearing here is narrower. The first quarto's shape proves that the play in performance was a cut play, and that the cutting reached the frame in the company's own lifetime or close behind it.

The Chorus, considered as a candidate for the knife, is the rational first cut in any playhouse, and the cut theory is right to insist on the point. It belongs to nobody in the story, so its removal opens no hole in the plot and demands no repair in any scene. It stands outside the act structure, at the thresholds, where a performance in a period without act intervals feels no join. Its information is duplicated by the action itself, which shows the feud, the meeting, and the transfer of desire in scenes that lose little by arriving unannounced. And its first speech does work that expires: a prologue advertises a novelty, begs patience for a new play, and promises a span of hours, and a company reviving a proven success needs none of the three errands run again. Samuel Johnson, editing the play in 1765, supplied the cutting centuries with their critical charter when he reached the second Chorus and wrote its dismissal as doctrine: the use of the speech, he held, is not easily discovered, since it conduces nothing to the progress of the play, relates only what is already known or what the next scenes will show, and relates it without adding the improvement of any moral sentiment. A passage that fails the test of progress, of information, and of instruction is, by the Augustan audit, dead weight, and Johnson's audit is the playhouse's instinct dressed as criticism. The two institutions, stage and study, arrived at the same valuation by different roads, and the Chorus's survival in print owes nothing to either road's traffic.

### How much playing time would cutting both Chorus speeches actually save?

Almost none: twenty-eight lines of verse, delivered at the period's pace, occupy roughly two to three minutes of an afternoon. The saving was never the motive. What the cut buys a company is not time but emphasis, the removal of a pause in which the action stops to be explained.

That concession, small as it looks, turns the cut theory's best evidence against its largest claim. The arithmetic that forced a company to shorten the play fell on seven hundred lines of story, dialogue, and scene; the frame's twenty-eight lines are a rounding error inside that reduction, and they survive or fall by a different logic than length. The first quarto cut the second Chorus and kept a damaged Prologue, the Folio's copy cut the Prologue and kept a demoted second Chorus, and neither pattern is the pattern of a shop economizing minutes. Each is the pattern of handlers discarding the piece of frame apparatus their particular document had least use for: the occasional door piece in one shop, the detachable summary in the other. The cut theory explains those choices well. What it cannot explain is the thing it was built to explain, the silence after the second act. Cutting is an operation performed on a text that exists. The documents show the operation performed, repeatedly, on two speeches. They show no third speech, no scar where one was removed, no list, part, plot, or allusion registering one, in an archive that preserves the frame's damaged members in embarrassing detail. A theory that accounts for the damage and invents the corpus has the evidence exactly backwards, and the next section states the positive case the documents actually support.

## The Design Case: A Frame Retired by Its Author

The comparative evidence reframes the puzzle, because it shows what complete choral frames look like in the drama the playwright inherited, and how variously he himself handled the device across his career. The academic and Senecan tradition ran a full system: Gorboduc, the earliest English tragedy, stations a chorus of four old men of Britain at the end of every act to moralize the action in turn, and the university and Inns of Court stages kept that architecture standing for a generation. The commercial stage's nearest great precedent is closer to hand. The Chorus of Doctor Faustus opens the play, returns in its middle to conduct the hero through the courts of Europe, and closes it with an epilogue over the scholar's remains, "Cut is the branch that might have grown full straight." Beginning, middle, and end: a frame that intends to run the whole length of a tragedy announces itself by returning, and its returns are the proof of its design. Nobody has ever needed to argue that the Faustus choruses were cut down from a larger set, because the set that survives is complete in kind, each station discharged. Set against that completeness, the frame of Romeo and Juliet is visibly a different object: two speeches at consecutive thresholds, discharging exposition and transition, and then nothing at the three stations where the Faustus pattern, and Gorboduc's, place their most solemn matter, the hinge, the crisis, and the close.

The playwright's own practice is the control group, and it refuses to convict him of a broken habit. He wrote frames of every size and treated the size as a decision, not a default. Troilus and Cressida gets a single armed Prologue, entering in armour to announce a story begun in the middle, "In Troy, there lies the scene," and no second speech of any kind, and the one speech's singularity has never been read as the stump of a system, because the speech behaves like a complete instrument, an overture that knows its play begins in medias res. The second part of Henry IV gets a single induction, Rumour painted full of tongues, and no more framing. The Winter's Tale gets Time, a self-declared chorus figure who appears exactly once, in the middle of the play, to carry the action across sixteen years, and whose single appearance is the design, since the gap he bridges exists exactly once. Pericles gets Gower at the head of act after act, a resurrected medieval poet as master of ceremonies. Henry V, a few years after the Verona tragedy, gets the most elaborate frame in the canon, a Chorus before every act and an epilogue besides, apologizing for the stage, commanding the audience's imagination, confessing the distance between the event and its representation. The series' study of the Friar's supposed authorship draws the inference this census supports: a dramatist who would build that apparatus when he wanted it, and who built a single-speech frame when he wanted that, was exercising choice in the tragedy of the lovers as well. The two-speech frame sits inside his demonstrated range, between the Troilus prologue and the Henry V system, and nothing in the range marks two as a mutilated number.

The second sonnet carries the retirement inside its own text, for a reader attending to register. The Prologue speaks the language of decree: households, fate compounds, a pair "star-cross'd" (Prologue.6), a love "death-mark'd" (Prologue.9), the whole action pronounced before it occurs. The second Chorus, measured in the series' collation of [star-crossed or death-marked: the Prologue variants](/2030/10/11/star-crossed-or-death-marked/), lets both compounds go. The astrology evaporates after one speech; what remains is a domestic image, old desire on its deathbed, young affection gaping to be its heir, and a prose of obstacles: access denied, vows unbreathable, bait and hooks. The frame's second utterance is already less a voice from above the action than a summary from just behind it, and its tense has changed with its register. The first speech is prophecy, cast in futures. The second is retrospect with a short forecast attached, a report on a completed transfer of desire and a note of the dangers pending. Johnson's complaint, that the speech relates only what is already known or what the next scenes will show, is the sound of a critic registering that demotion without recognizing it: the frame in its second appearance has descended from oracle to chronicler, and a chronicler of this play is a function the action itself performs better, in scene after scene of report, letter, and narration. A device being wound down by its author looks exactly like this. Its powers contract speech by speech, its register falls toward the play's own, and its remaining work shrinks to what one more speech could do before silence becomes indistinguishable from design. The cut theory must read the contraction as damage. The design reading takes it as the author's hand on the instrument, easing it out of the play.

### Why does the second sonnet look backward when the first looked forward?

Because its work has changed with the play's state. The first speech opens a story nobody in the fiction has lived, so it prophesies. The second opens an act whose premise, the transfer of Romeo's desire, the first act has already played, so it audits what has happened and prices the dangers ahead.

The deepest reason for the retirement is structural, and it is the reason the frame stops where it stops rather than a speech earlier or later. The first two acts of the play run on knowledge that can be public: a feud everyone knows, a feast anyone may enter, a meeting conducted in a crowd. A truthful chorus can narrate such a world without cost, and the Prologue's total spoiler is safe for the same reason, since it names outcomes and withholds mechanisms, arriving before the audience's loyalties have formed around any character's hopes. From the marriage onward the play runs on secrets, and its machinery is epistemic: a wedding known to four people, a killing misread by a city, a banishment reported by instalments, a potion whose truth is confined to a friar's cell, a letter whose failure nobody alive understands until the tomb. A chorus bound to its office, possessed of the ending and licensed to address the house, cannot narrate that world honestly. Announce the marriage, and the fiction's premise of concealment collapses in the mouth of its own frame. Announce the potion, and the fifth act's agony is spent in advance, mechanism and all. Fall silent on selected facts, and the voice that promised to mend what the summary missed becomes one more partial knower among the play's rationed knowers, its authority spent. The office and the plot have become incompatible, and the incompatibility dates itself precisely: the Chorus vanishes at the moment the Friar's plotting begins in earnest, when the story passes from the public square into the cell, the orchard wall, and the sealed letter. Even the play's sonnet form observes the same withdrawal. The lovers' first exchange at the feast is a shared sonnet built line about line between two speakers, the frame's form migrating into the dialogue at the exact centre of the first act; after the second Chorus speaks, no sonnet is completed anywhere in the play. The instrument is not smashed. It is handed over, spent, and left behind, in form as in office.

The frame's origin confirms the reading, because the Chorus begins its life as a piece of a book's furniture rather than a piece of a play's. Arthur Brooke's Romeus and Juliet of 1562, the poem from which the dramatist took the story, reaches its readers through front matter: a prose preface addressed to the reader, moralizing the tale in advance, and a verse argument that squeezes the whole subject into fourteen lines, the action summed step by step with its ending included. The Prologue of the play is that argument given a body and a voice. The measure is the same, fourteen lines; the matter is the same, the feud, the lovers, the deaths, the reconciliation purchased by them; the total foreknowledge is the same, a story told entire before it is enacted. What print performs at a book's threshold, by a page the reader passes once and does not expect to meet again, the stage performs at the play's threshold by a speaker. The conversion is a young dramatist's elegant solution to a real problem, how to give an audience of playgoers the orientation a reader gets from front matter. It also predicts the abandonment with embarrassing fidelity. Paratext is threshold furniture. It ushers the audience into the work, and its work ends at the door; nobody expects a poem's argument to be reissued at intervals through the poem, and nobody misses it. The second Chorus is the usher lingering at one further doorway, summarizing the room just quitted and pointing to the next, a courtesy the occasion barely requires. By the third doorway the guests are seated, the rooms are known, and the usher's continued attendance would be an intrusion. A frame born as front matter behaves as front matter still, and its two speeches mark the exact distance a book's preliminaries can walk into a play before the play's own life takes over the introductions.

## Where the Chorus Went: The Offices Inherited

A vanished functionary's work does not vanish with it, and the surest way to see that the Chorus's silence is a designed retirement rather than an amputation is to watch its offices being discharged, at reduced power, by the fiction that remains. The frame had three offices: to know the ending in advance, to summarize the action at its joints, and to deliver the summing judgment at the close. All three are performed in the later acts, and all three are performed worse, by men inside the story, under pressure, with partial knowledge and something to lose. The degradation is the signature of design. A cut leaves a hole. A retirement leaves heirs, and the heirs' inadequacy is the measure of what the retired office was worth.

Foreknowledge passes to the audience itself. The Prologue's gift, the ending announced before the action, is never renewed by any speaker after the second Chorus, and it does not need to be: the house carries the first sonnet in memory through every subsequent scene, and the tragedy's celebrated irony is that memory at work. Summary passes to the Friar, whose long narration in the tomb scene tells the story backward, after it happens, in some forty lines, under arrest, with the watch at the door and judgment reserved, and whose telling is accepted only when a dead boy's letter corroborates it. The contrast with the frame's narration is total and, on the design reading, intended. The Chorus told the story forward, before it happened, with complete accuracy and complete calm, from outside the events, immune to them. The Friar tells it in custody, as a suspect, and his account has the standing of testimony rather than knowledge. Johnson heard the demotion and disliked its product from the other side: at the Friar's "I will be brief," he wrote that it is much to be lamented the poet did not conclude the dialogue with the action and avoid a narrative of events the audience had already known. The note is the second Chorus's dismissal repeated at the other end of the play, and its recurrence is instructive. Wherever narration survives in this tragedy, the critical instinct reaches for the knife, and the playwright who withdrew the frame after two speeches had anticipated the instinct by three acts. Judgment, the third office, passes to the Prince, whose closing speech allocates the catastrophe across both houses and his own misgovernment, and whose final couplet, "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo," performs in the fiction's own person the envoi a closing chorus would have sung from outside it.

### Who speaks the play's last lines, and what office do they discharge?

The Prince of Verona speaks them, from inside the fiction and under the weight of his own failure. His closing speech is the frame's third office performed by an heir: summary of the woes, allocation of blame across both houses and his own rule, and a rhymed envoi for the whole action.

None of this makes the Friar a stand-in for the playwright, and the fence matters. The series' study of the Friar's supposed authorship is exact on the division of labour: the Chorus holds the author's powers, foresight, immunity, direct access to the house, a text events cannot falsify, and the Friar holds a character's equipment, partial knowledge and a body the plot can arrest. The offices pass inward; the authority does not. That is why the inheritance reads as elegy rather than substitution. The play keeps demonstrating, at each joint where the frame would have spoken, what it costs a story to be told by the people inside it: delay, error, self-exculpation, the truth arriving as evidence in a trial rather than as a gift before a feast. The silence of the Chorus is the price tag on the fiction's realism, and a playwright does not arrange so consistent a demonstration by accident or lose it by a playhouse's filing error. He builds it.

## The Stage's Own Verdict: Four Centuries of Cutting

The performance record, heard last, is the cut theory's home ground, and it delivers everything the theory could wish, about the speeches that exist. The tragedy's stage life after the theatres reopened begins with William Davenant's production at Lincoln's Inn Fields in 1662, and within a generation the play itself had been displaced by Thomas Otway's History and Fall of Caius Marius of 1679, which transposed the lovers to ancient Rome as Marius and Lavinia and held the stage as the only version of the story for some seventy years. Otway's play opens, in the Restoration manner, with a prologue of the era's own kind, in which the adapter frankly owned that about half his piece was taken from the earlier tragedy. The contrast is the period's whole frame culture in miniature. The Restoration prologue was an occasional address, a wit's apologia or puff for a particular night, written to be admired and discarded, and it had nothing in common with a structural chorus except the threshold it stood on. Shakespeare's two sonnets, in that culture, were not so much cut as unintelligible as an institution: the stage had a use for a voice before the curtain and no use for a narrator inside the play's machinery, and the story survived the century in a form that had replaced its heroine's death mechanics and never missed its frame.

The eighteenth century restored the play and completed the frame's erasure in the act of celebrating it. Theophilus Cibber brought the tragedy back in 1744 in a version closer to the original, keeping Otway's device of the heroine waking before the hero's death. David Garrick's production at Drury Lane in 1748, with Spranger Barry and Susannah Cibber as the lovers, made the play a standard of the repertory, and Garrick's printed acting text, issued as the play was performed at Drury Lane, shows what the standard contained. Its list of persons names the lovers, the friar, the nurse, the citizens and attendants, and no Chorus. Its text opens with the street scene and the serving-men, as the Folio had opened it a century before. Neither sonnet stands anywhere in the book. The single survival of the word is the period's neatest irony: Garrick's added funeral procession for Juliet, introduced in the rival productions of 1750 and retained in his text, sets a sung dirge over the heroine's bier, its words by Garrick himself and its music by William Boyce, and the dirge's refrains are headed "Chorus." The word survived in the acting version of the play for a hundred years, attached to hired singers processing behind a coffin in a scene the author never wrote, while the speaker of the sonnets, the author's own narrator, was nowhere in the volume. A more exact emblem of the frame's stage fortune could not be commissioned: the theatre kept the label, reassigned it to spectacle, and discarded the office.

### Did the Restoration stage keep Shakespeare's Chorus speeches?

No. After Davenant's revival of 1662 the story passed to Otway's Roman adaptation, which held the stage for some seventy years, and the Restoration's own prologues were occasional addresses by the adapter, not narrative choruses. Shakespeare's two sonnets had no place in the version of the story that audiences of the period actually saw.

Garrick's text governed the English stage until Charlotte Cushman restored the author's play in America in 1845 and Samuel Phelps followed at Sadler's Wells in 1847, and the Chorus returned to performance as editorial property, carried back into the theatre on the authority of the printed scholarship that had preserved it. The pattern since that restoration completes the evidence. Harley Granville-Barker, whose Savoy productions were credited with playing Shakespeare as written rather than cut and rearranged, brought the producer's conscience to the play in his Preface, treating its text as an integral score. The reading tradition, from Rowe onward, has been unanimous in the same direction: every scholarly edition prints both speeches from the second quarto and records the omissions of the other witnesses as facts about those witnesses. And yet the living stage, freed by that scholarship to keep everything, keeps the frame asymmetrically. The Prologue survives nearly everywhere, as the play's trademark, though it is redistributed with a freedom no character's lines would be granted: spoken by a full company in unison, assigned to the Prince as the city's voice, reassigned to a narrator. The second Chorus remains the most expendable famous speech in the play. The current Cambridge edition's own commentary notes what every playgoer learns, that the speech is commonly omitted in performance. The two great screen versions enact the same split. Franco Zeffirelli's film of 1968 gives the Prologue to Laurence Olivier's uncredited voice and brings that voice back for an epilogue, while the second Chorus has no existence in the film at all. Baz Luhrmann's film of 1996 delivers the Prologue through a television newsreader, twice over, and returns to the same newsreader for the play's last lines; the cast list credits the newsreader with assuming the role of the Chorus, and the role, as cast, consists of the first sonnet and the closing lines, with the second sonnet nowhere in it. Four centuries of handlers, given the whole text and perfect freedom, reproduce the earliest witnesses' behaviour with documentary fidelity: the Prologue kept and reassigned, the second Chorus dropped for pace. Performance, given its head, treats the frame exactly as the first quarto and the Folio's copy treated it. That is the cut theory's evidence, it is real, and its limit is inscribed in its own consistency. Every hand in the record is cutting from the same two-speech text. The stage's scissors testify about the speeches they met. About speeches no hand ever met, they are silent.

## The Cost of a Chorus: Casting, Doubling, and the Empty Chair

The economics of the role sharpen the question, because they remove the crudest motive for inventing a cut. The Chorus of this play is twenty-eight lines of verse, spoken at two thresholds, by a figure with no costume specified in any early text, no entrance in the fiction, no exit that matters, and no interaction with any character. No document records which actor first spoke the part, and no actor's part or company plot for the play survives in which the role could be looked up. That evidentiary blank is itself characteristic of frame material, since the paperwork of the period's companies, the plots hung backstage and the parts copied out for individual players, survives for only a handful of productions, and a prologue speaker's fourteen lines were the least likely matter in a play to be preserved by either form. But the blank cuts against the cut theory in one specific way. A role of this shape costs a company nothing it can save. The speaker must be free before the first scene and again at the feast's end, and the play offers a crowd of tracks that satisfy the condition: the Prince, absent from the stage for long stretches and present at the close to inherit the frame's last office; the Apothecary, who exists for one scene in the fifth act; Montague, whose appearances bookend the action; any hired man whose other duties lie in the crowd scenes. Doubling the Chorus with any of these is a rehearsal-room commonplace of the period, and modern productions perform the same arithmetic when they give the speeches to the Prince, to the Friar's colleagues, or to the company entire. A company does not save a salary by silencing the Chorus. It does not save a costume, a property, or a rehearsal hour worth counting.

What the cut buys, when a company makes it, is pace and emphasis, and the purchase explains the pattern of the damage better than any economics of scarcity. The second Chorus stands at the one point in the evening where the action has just achieved its highest velocity, the feast's discovery and flight, and where the next scene, the orchard, must begin in a hush of held breath. A summarizing sonnet between those temperatures is a deliberate deceleration, a held chord, and a company playing for momentum experiences it as drag. The first quarto's omission and the modern stage's omission fall at the identical joint for the identical reason, and the reason is dramaturgical, not financial. This is also why the crude form of the cut theory, in which a hard-pressed troupe trimmed the frame to fit an afternoon, misreads its own best document. Erne's casting and doubling appendix for the first quarto treats the short text as a performable company version, and the version it describes is not a frame with speeches knocked out at random: it is the whole play, story and all, compressed by a quarter, with the commentary going first because commentary is what pace spends. The frame in performance was not persecuted. It was outbid, at its weakest station, by the action it was summarizing.

The empty chair at the later stations is a different matter, and no casting logic fills it. Had choruses stood before the third, fourth, and fifth acts, they would have cost the company the same nothing as the first two: the same doubled speaker, the same fourteen lines, the same threshold. No economy explains declining a free asset at three stations while paying for it, in attention if not in wages, at two. The design explanation pays no such toll. The stations differ in what a chorus would have to say, and the later stations are exactly where the play's secrets make the saying impossible, as the design case established. Casting arithmetic thus joins the textual record and the comparative census at the same conclusion from a third direction. The two speeches were kept while the office had work, at a cost of nothing; they were dropped, in the documents and on the stages, when handlers valued pace over commentary; and the speeches that never appear were never there to be kept, because the office itself had been retired at the point where the story it served changed its nature. The chair is empty after the second act because the dramatist stopped writing anyone into it.

## What a Lost Chorus Would Have Left Behind

The case turns, in the end, on a question of evidence discipline: what does loss look like in this archive, and does the silence after the second act look like it? The early modern theatre's paperwork is fragmentary, but it is not mute, and frame material in particular leaves a recognizable debris field when it is lost. Actors' parts survive for a scatter of roles, copied sheets of cues and speeches that outlived their productions in actors' keeping. Backstage plots survive for a scatter of plays, scene-by-scene skeletons listing who enters and what the property master must supply, documents in which a chorus standing at an act joint would register as a line of personnel. Theatrical accounts survive in which occasional prologues and epilogues appear as separate commissions, paid for as separate pieces of work for separate occasions, the paperwork reality behind the separate-documents account of prologues. Printed prologues survive detached from their plays in collections and preliminaries, admired as performances in their own right. Contemporaries allude to frame speeches that struck them. Loss, in this world, is rarely clean. It is partial, noisy, and contested by the survivors.

The two surviving speeches demonstrate the debris field from inside this very play. When the Prologue passed through a performance-shaped channel, it did not vanish; it arrived damaged, twelve lines for fourteen, its rhymes unsprung, its households renamed, its strongest line dissolved into a parenthesis, a wreck that announces a lost original in every line. When the second Chorus passed through the Folio's playhouse-annotated copy, it did not vanish either; it arrived demoted, its wording intact and its speaker abolished, a demotion the apparatus of every modern edition still records. Even the Prologue's total absence from the Folio is a noisy event, explained by the copy's preparation and reversed by Rowe at the first editorial opportunity, on quarto authority, without controversy. Frame loss in this archive shouts. Against that demonstrated noisiness, set the record at the frame's later stations: six editions across forty years, three independent lines of transmission, one of them descending from the author's draft, one shaped by performance, one annotated in a playhouse, and at the stations before the third, fourth, and fifth acts and at the close, nothing. No wreck, no demotion, no gap in a numeration, no speech heading without a speech, no allusion in the period's voluminous commentary on this famous play, no part, no plot line, no payment. The cut theory asks the archive to have been surgically silent about three or four speeches after being incapable of silence about the damage to two. That is not a gap in the evidence. It is the evidence.

The strongest rejoinder available to the cut theory deserves its hearing, because it is genuinely strong. A closing chorus, of all frame pieces, is the most detachable object in a play: spoken last, copied last, filed last, and useless to any scene that follows it, since none does. If any choral speech could evaporate without trace, an epilogue chorus is the candidate, and the separate-documents account gives the evaporation a mechanism. The rejoinder fails on the play's own construction, at two points. First, the Act 2 Chorus stands as the control: it is the same kind of object, a frame sonnet at an act joint, and it survived in the author's draft embedded in the play's sequence, between the feast and the orchard, which is precisely why the Folio's copy, ruthless toward apparatus standing outside the sequence, preserved it while dropping the Prologue that stood outside. A closing chorus composed with the play would have stood in that same draft, in that same sequence, after the tomb scene, and the line of transmission that carried its two siblings intact to the second quarto would have had to perform a third, invisible excision at the draft's end, before any playhouse touched it. Second, the play already ends with a composed envoi in the fiction's mouth, the Prince's summing couplet, and the lost-chorus hypothesis must therefore posit the dramatist writing two endings, a choral one to be discarded and a princely one to survive, or the company discarding the one and the author's papers preserving only the other. Hypotheses multiply; documents do not. What survives at the close is a complete formal gesture, couplet answering the Prologue's couplet across the whole play, and completeness of that order is not the residue of an amputation. It is the shape of a design whose last office was reassigned, deliberately, to the ruler whose failure the summing-up indicts.

## The Expectation Itself: Where the Missing Chorus Comes From

Before the verdict, the expectation that generates the whole debate deserves an audit of its own, because the missing chorus is partly an artifact manufactured by the play's later furniture. The early texts print no list of persons. The dramatis personae that opens modern editions is an editorial habit of the eighteenth century, and in those lists the Chorus stands enrolled among the persons of the play, a name in a cast, sometimes placed with the citizens and attendants at the list's foot. A name in a cast list makes a promise no frame speech makes for itself: persons in a list are expected to persist, to reappear, to be accounted for, and a reader formed by the list experiences the Chorus's silence as a disappearance, a character who walks out of the play. The texts promise no such thing. They give a function a label at two thresholds and never enroll it in the fiction at all. Half the pathos of the vanishing Chorus is a typographical accident of the editions that restored it.

The rest of the expectation is imported from grander frames. The Chorus that the culture knows best in this canon is the Chorus of Henry V, six interventions and an epilogue, a figure of genuine sublimity who apologizes for the wooden O and turns the audience into co-authors, and readers formed on that magnificence carry its completeness backward into the earlier play and find the earlier frame wanting by a standard set four years later in a different genre. Behind Henry V stands the classroom's classical template, the Greek and Senecan chorus commenting between acts as a condition of tragedy itself, taught before the early modern evidence is met. Between the template and the retrojection, a two-speech frame can only look like a failure of supply. Set in its own decade's company instead, among the single prologues, single inductions, single mid-play apparitions of Time, and the one contemporary tragedy frame that runs complete, the same two speeches look like what they are: one size among the period's sizes, chosen and then, for reasons the play itself supplies, not enlarged. The expectation also explains a datum of the stage history that neither main theory owns. Productions have been known to invent chorus material for the later acts, writing the missing members of the series themselves, and the invention is the expectation made visible: directors who feel the gap as a wound treat it surgically. On the design reading the wound is imaginary, and the invented speeches, however skilful, paper over the play's most instructive silence, the point where narration hands the story to machinery and trusts the house to carry the foreknowledge alone.

What the gap, restored to its actual size, reveals about the play's composition is the brief's deepest question, and it is the point on which the two theories' practical difference dissolves into a single picture. The tragedy belongs to the dramatist's early maturity, written in the middle of the last decade of the century by a playwright still stocked with the instruments of his schooling: the sonnet, the set debate, the academic frame. The Chorus is his reach for the classical instrument at the moment of his most ambitious early tragedy, an attempt to give a novella of star-crossed teenagers the architecture of Seneca. Two acts of writing taught him what the story was: an engine of secrets, letters, and minutes, whose effects depend on the audience knowing outcomes and being denied mechanisms, and on no voice existing that could relieve the pressure by commentary. The frame stops because the play outgrew it in the writing, and the fossil of the experiment remains at the front of the text, two sonnets marking the stations where the instrument still fitted the machine. A later dramatist, writing Henry V, found a story, chronicle history, whose public matter a chorus could narrate without spoiling, and built the full system there. The incomplete frame of the Verona tragedy is not the ruin of that system. It is the record of the search that found, four years on, the material the system needed.

## A Cover for the Orchard: The Second Chorus at Work

One further piece of staging evidence explains the second speech's peculiar double fate, kept by the Folio's copy and dropped by nearly everyone else: it has a job inside the performance that the Prologue does not. The Prologue stands before the action, in clear space, and its removal costs a production nothing but information. The second Chorus stands at the busiest joint in the play's staging. Behind it, the feast must clear: dancers, maskers, musicians with their instruments, the debris of a banquet in a great hall. Ahead of it, the orchard must exist: a wall to be climbed, trees to be named, the geography of the play's most famous scene. On the unlocalized stage of the 1590s, with its continuous flow and its properties carried on and off in view, that transition is work, and work takes time. A fourteen-line sonnet spoken downstage while the hall empties and the orchard assembles is not decoration at that joint. It is cover, the period's standard technology for changing the stage's world without stopping the evening, and the current Cambridge edition's commentary raises exactly this possibility for the speech, noting the musicians to be cleared and the trees to be set that the following scene's language requires.

The cover function illuminates the documentary pattern at two points. It explains why the second Chorus lives inside the play's sequence rather than before it, embedded between scenes in a way the Prologue is not: cover is part of the playing text's plumbing, and plumbing is copied with the book. The Folio's copy, which discarded the detachable door piece, kept the plumbing, because the plumbing was in the walls. And it explains why the speech became, in later centuries, the most expendable famous passage in the play. A theatre with a curtain, a scene dock, and a lighting board does not need cover at that joint; it needs pace, and the sonnet's remaining functions, summary and transition, are the ones a production can perform with a look and a lighting change. The speech did not fall from favour because its poetry failed. Its engineering was superseded, and the summary it carried was the kind of cargo Johnson taught the centuries to refuse. Technologies of the stage expire in a way that scenes do not, and a speech whose deepest reason for existing was a property problem leaves the repertory when the property problem does. The Prologue, which never had an engineering function, survived on its poetry and its fame. The second Chorus, the working member of the pair, was retired by progress in stagecraft, the final sense in which the frame's history is a history of performance rather than of loss.

## The Verdict: Two Questions, Two Answers

The debate has lasted because it fuses two questions that the evidence answers separately, and the verdict of this article is the separation itself, defended on all three classes of evidence. Question one: were the Chorus speeches the play possesses cut in performance and its paperwork? Answer: yes, demonstrably and repeatedly, from the play's first years. The first quarto drops the second Chorus entire and hands down the Prologue as a wreck of memory. The Folio's playhouse-annotated copy drops the Prologue entire and strips the second Chorus of its speaker. The Restoration replaced the whole frame culture with the occasional prologue and lost the story's author to a Roman adaptation for seventy years. The eighteenth century's standard acting text enrolled no Chorus among its persons, opened with the brawl, and kept the word only for hired singers behind a coffin. The modern stage, holding a complete scholarly text, cuts the second Chorus as a matter of routine and redistributes the first with a liberty it grants no character. On this question the cut theory is not a theory; it is the biography of the speeches, written in documents.

Question two: was a fuller frame, choruses before the later acts and a chorus at the close, written and then cut or lost? Answer: no evidence supports it, and the evidence that exists excludes it at the standard this article set at the outset. Three independent lines of transmission agree on the frame's extent while disagreeing on everything else. The witness descending from the author's draft prints the pair complete and nothing beyond the pair. The performance-shaped witnesses, which demonstrably cut frame material when they met it, met it as a pair and cut it down to one. The period's archive, which preserves the debris of frame loss noisily wherever frame loss occurs, including twice inside this play, preserves no debris at the later stations: no wrecked lines, no demoted speech, no gap in a numeration, no part, no plot entry, no payment, no allusion. The comparative census removes the premise that two is a broken number, since the dramatist built frames of one speech, of two, and of six across the same decade, sizing the instrument to the play. The play's internal evidence supplies the motive for the retirement at the exact station where it occurs: the second sonnet's register contracts from decree to summary, its astrology evaporates, and the plot it would have to narrate passes into secrecy, where a truthful frame cannot follow. And the offices the frame vacated are found discharged inside the fiction by heirs whose inadequacy is systematic, the audience's memory for foreknowledge, an arrested friar for summary, a self-indicted prince for judgment, which is the signature of a designed succession rather than an accidental hole. A lost chorus is an unnecessary hypothesis multiplied against a silent archive. The Chorus was not cut. The Chorus was stood down.

The critics divide, on inspection, along the seam between the two questions, and the adjudication the brief requires follows from assigning each authority to the question that authority actually answers. Lukas Erne, editing the first quarto, establishes the playing text's reality: a version shorter by some seven hundred lines, shaped for performance, in which the frame's second speech is among the few whole movements gone. Harry Hoppe, whose account of the first quarto as memorial reconstruction the series' textual study upheld on the wording evidence, establishes the complementary half: the Prologue that survives in that quarto is not a reviser's abridgement but a sonnet remembered, its rhymes unsprung by recall rather than by scissors. Held together, the two accounts divide the first quarto's damage between them with nothing left over: the excision of a whole speech is the abridger's gesture, the collapse of a speech's form is memory's, and neither gesture touches the frame's extent, since both operate on a text that already contained two speeches and no more. Tiffany Stern's account of prologues as separate, occasional documents explains the Prologue's detachment and its Folio fate better than any rival, and her framework licenses imagining occasional pieces lost around any play of the period; what it cannot do is manufacture members for a series whose embedded second member survived inside the play's own sequence, in the copy tradition least likely to preserve detachable matter. The editors, from Rowe restoring the Prologue on quarto authority in 1709 to Brian Gibbons, G. Blakemore Evans, Jill Levenson, and René Weis printing both speeches from the second quarto with the omissions recorded as facts about the omitters, deliver the study's unanimous judgment: the frame as transmitted is the frame as designed, and its gaps belong to its handlers. Johnson stands apart, the one great authority for the cutting instinct itself, and his dismissal of the second Chorus, repeated at the Friar's narration thirty years of stage practice later in his own notes, survives in this verdict as evidence about the temperament the frame always faced, not about the frame's history. Each authority is right about the question it answers. The error is only in the fusion.

Modern scholarship on the early modern chorus has been converging on the same picture from the direction of theatre history. Silvia Bigliazzi's survey of chorus and chorality in the drama of the period traces a half-century transformation in which the ancient institution, a plural and lyrical body commenting from the margins, is progressively reduced, conflated with prologues and other framing texts, and remade as a solo metatheatrical voice. Within that survey the Verona tragedy holds a marked place, as one of the first plays to experiment with the new choral experience: a lyric and narrative solo performance appended to the action, a voice set in competition with the representational power of the drama itself. The description matters for the verdict because it names the Chorus's condition exactly. A voice in competition with the play it frames is a voice the play can defeat, and the drama of the 1590s was learning, play by play, that its scenes could carry exposition, summary, and judgment without a spokesman. The experiment Bigliazzi describes has a result, and the result is legible in the text: the solo voice wins its two early contests, against a feud the audience has not yet seen and a transfer of desire it has only just watched, and then loses the competition for the play's remaining needs to the machinery of the scenes themselves. The frame's incompleteness, on this account, is not an accident that befell an experiment. It is the experiment's data, preserved because the dramatist published the working rather than tidying it.

What the incomplete frame finally reveals about the play's composition and staging can be stated in one paragraph each. About composition: the tragedy is the work of a dramatist discovering, inside a single act of writing, that his inherited instrument and his invented story were incompatible, and leaving the discovery legible rather than revising it away. He did not go back and strike the two sonnets, though they advertise a system he abandoned; he did not go back and write the three he had led his audience to expect. He let the frame stand at the front of the play as the record of an intention overtaken by a better one, and the tolerance is characteristic of a writer whose drafts became books with their working visible. About staging: the frame's history warns every producer that the two speeches are the play's most revealing test of intention. A production that keeps both, in one voice, at full weight, stages the design, exposition surrendering to machinery at the joint where the design surrenders it. A production that cuts the second speech repeats the first quarto and should know it is voting with the playhouse against the study. A production that invents the missing choruses stages the expectation instead of the play. Each of the three honest options is a reading of the same evidence, and the evidence, unusually for a question of this age, is complete enough to grade the readings against one another: the closer a staging stays to the transmitted pair of speeches and the transmitted silence after them, the closer it stays to the only frame any document in four centuries has ever contained, and the further it travels from that pair and that silence, the more plainly it is staging the history of the question rather than the play the question is about. The documents leave the choice open. They do not leave it uninformed.

## For the Director: Staging a Frame That Stops

The evidence settles into three honest stagings, and a fourth that the record discourages. The first keeps both sonnets, spoken by one voice, at full ceremonial weight, and lets the silence after them accumulate meaning as the acts proceed. This is the staging of the design: the audience is given an authorized narrator, taught to trust the narration, and then deprived of it at the exact point where the characters are deprived of every reliable guide, so the house experiences the play's epistemic condition rather than merely observing it. The deprivation only registers if the voice was established as a presence, which argues for a single speaker with a consistent station and manner, the same figure at both thresholds, recognized on return and missed thereafter. Distributing the two speeches among the company, a fashion with real virtues for the Prologue's communal "our stage," dilutes exactly the asset the design banks: a person whose absence can be felt. The second honest staging reassigns the speeches to a figure inside the fiction, most plausibly the Prince, whose arc they complete: the ruler who opens the play's public world in the frame's voice ends it in his own, delivering the verdict the frame would have delivered, and the doubling makes the inheritance of offices visible in a single body. The cost is the loss of the voice's externality, since a prince who prophesies is a different metaphysical object from a chorus who knows, and the production should take the reassignment as an interpretation to be played, not a convenience to be hidden. The third honest staging is the Folio's: open with the brawl, keep the second Chorus as an unattributed threshold or cut it with the first quarto's precedent, and play the tragedy as its first collected edition presents it, a story without an authorized narrator from the first minute. The precedent is impeccable and the effect is a colder, faster play, one in which the audience's foreknowledge must be supplied by the story's fame rather than by its frame.

The discouraged option is the completion: writing the missing choruses, whether in imitation verse or in modern commentary, and stationing them before the later acts and at the close. The impulse is understandable, since the expectation the pair creates is real, and skilled productions have succumbed to it. But completion stages the audience's schooling rather than the author's play. It fills the one silence in the text that carries an argument, the withdrawal of narration before a machinery of secrets, and it does so with material whose authority no document supports. A production that wants a commenting voice in the later acts has honest instruments to hand: the musicians, the watch, the citizens who open and close the public scenes, figures the text itself stations at the thresholds of the fiction. What it should not do is counterfeit the frame. The gap is the evidence. Leave it standing.

## For the Reader: How the Gap Should Be Read and Taught

Editions already embody the verdict, and the reader's task is to notice that they do. Every scholarly text prints both speeches from the second quarto, unbracketed, as the play's own matter, and confines the omissions to the apparatus, where they are recorded as events in the witnesses' histories. That presentation is an argument: the frame belongs to the work, its damage belongs to the work's transmission, and the silence after the second speech belongs to the work most of all, since no apparatus entry anywhere registers a loss at the later stations. The one piece of editorial furniture that argues the other way is the dramatis personae, and readers should handle it as the eighteenth-century addition it is. Listing the Chorus among the persons invites the question a cast member's disappearance always invites, and the question, pursued honestly, is this article's whole subject; but the listing itself is evidence about editors, not about the play, and the Chorus is more accurately pictured as the list's guest than as its member, a voice the book admits at its thresholds and never seats at its table.

Taught that way, the vanishing Chorus becomes one of the play's most useful lessons in evidence, a case where every instinct must be audited against documents. The instinct of pattern says a series begun must have been completed. The instinct of theatrical realism says the playhouse cuts what does not earn its keep. The instinct of reverence says the author's design is seamless. The documents correct all three: the series was begun by an author who sized frames variably across his whole career; the playhouse did cut, but cut the speeches that exist, in patterns that sort by document type rather than by any hostility to choruses; and the design is not seamless but legibly experimental, a classical instrument tried against a story that refused it and set down, mid-play, where the refusal became clear. A student who has watched three plausible stories fail against six editions has learned the discipline the play's own characters never learn in time: to distinguish what the evidence shows from what the pattern promises. The Chorus, which exists to tell the truth about the action in advance, ends by teaching the truth about texts in retrospect, and the lesson is the same lesson in both tenses. What a voice says it will do and what the record shows it doing are different objects, and judgement belongs to the record.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How many lines would a complete choral frame have added to the play?

At fourteen lines a speech, a frame on the full Senecan and Gorboduc pattern, a chorus before each of the five acts and an epilogue at the close, runs to six speeches and eighty-four lines. The play as transmitted contains two speeches and twenty-eight lines, so completion would have added four speeches and fifty-six lines to the text, roughly five minutes of playing time at the period's pace of delivery. The arithmetic matters because it prices the supposed loss. What the cut theory imagines the playhouse discarding across three acts and a close is less verse than a single middling soliloquy, a quantity no company needed to shed for time and no reporter needed to forget entire in every channel at once. The missing frame is small enough to have been carried without effort by any of the witnesses that carried the rest of the play, which is one more reason its total absence from all of them reads as design rather than as the residue of damage.

### Q: Which roles in the evening's casting could the Chorus speaker most plausibly have doubled?

Any track that stands clear of the stage at both thresholds qualifies, and the play offers several. The Prince enters late in the first scene and is absent for long stretches thereafter, free before the feast's end and again before the orchard, and his closing speech inherits the frame's final office, which makes the doubling interpretively neat as well as practical. The Apothecary exists for a single scene in the fifth act and is unoccupied at both choral stations. Montague appears at the first brawl and at the close and nowhere between. A hired man carrying crowd duties could take the speeches with no doubling strain at all. The point of the list is economic. The Chorus costs a company no salary, no costume of record, and no rehearsal problem worth naming, since the figure must simply be present, speak fourteen lines twice, and be gone. Whatever made the later stations empty, it was not the price of filling them.

### Q: What happened to Shakespeare's Chorus when Otway rebuilt the story as Caius Marius?

It disappeared along with the play it belonged to. Thomas Otway's History and Fall of Caius Marius, first staged in 1679, transposed the lovers to ancient Rome as Marius and Lavinia, set the feud between patricians and plebeians, and held the stage as the only version of the story for some seventy years. The adaptation opens with a prologue of the Restoration's own kind, in which Otway frankly owned that about half his play was taken from the earlier tragedy. That prologue is an adapter's apologia, an occasional address for an opening, and it has nothing in common with a narrative chorus stationed inside a play's machinery. Otway's piece carries no chorus at any point, no voice stationed between its acts to summarize or judge, and the audiences of two generations learned the story of the star-crossed lovers in a form from which the frame was absent not by cutting but by constitution. The Chorus was not removed from Caius Marius. The world it was built for had been replaced.

### Q: Does Garrick's printed acting version contain either of the Chorus sonnets?

Neither. The acting text David Garrick published as the play was performed at Drury Lane opens its list of persons with the lovers, the friar, the nurse, the citizens and attendants, and enrolls no Chorus among them. Its text begins with the street scene and the serving-men, as the First Folio's text had begun a century and a quarter earlier, and neither the Prologue nor the second Chorus stands anywhere in the volume. The word survives once, in an unexpected place. Garrick's added funeral procession for Juliet carries a sung dirge, its words by Garrick and its music by William Boyce, and the dirge's refrains are headed "Chorus," sung by hired voices behind the heroine's bier in a scene the author never wrote. The acting version that governed the English stage for a hundred years thus transmitted the tragedy with the speaking frame removed entire and the label reassigned to spectacle. Performance, handed the complete text, cut the Chorus. The document also marks the limit of the lesson, since the text Garrick cut from contained the same two speeches and no more.

### Q: Why is the Act 2 Chorus the speech modern productions omit most readily?

Four pressures converge on it and on no other famous passage in the play. It stands at the evening's point of highest velocity, between the feast's flight and the orchard's hush, where a summarizing sonnet registers as drag to any company playing for momentum. Its information duplicates the scenes around it, restating a transfer of desire the audience has just watched, which exposes it to the audit Samuel Johnson wrote for it in 1765. Its original engineering purpose, covering the clearing of the feast and the setting of the orchard on a continuous stage, has been obsolete since stages acquired curtains and lighting boards. And it lacks the protection its sibling enjoys: the Prologue is the play's trademark, anthologized and memorized as the tragedy's motto, while the second speech is famous mainly to readers. The current Cambridge edition's commentary records the result as a commonplace of production, a speech commonly omitted in performance. Fame protects the first sonnet. Nothing protects the second except the text itself.

### Q: How does the Chorus of Doctor Faustus handle the closing speech that this play never gives its Chorus?

By delivering it. The Chorus of Marlowe's tragedy opens the play with the hero's biography, returns in the middle to conduct him through the courts and wonders of Europe, and closes the action with an epilogue over his remains, "Cut is the branch that might have grown full straight." Beginning, middle, and end are all discharged, and the completeness is the point: a frame designed to run the length of a tragedy identifies itself by returning at the stations, above all at the close, where its moral is the evening's last word. The Verona play inverts the arrangement. Its frame falls silent after the second station, and the closing word is given instead to a character inside the fiction, the Prince, whose summing couplet performs the envoi under the weight of his own failure to prevent the deaths. Marlowe's design trusts its narrator to the end. Shakespeare's retires its narrator and makes the failed ruler pronounce the moral, and the difference between the two endings is a difference of intention, not of survival.

### Q: If a closing Chorus had been written and then lost, what traces would the loss be expected to leave?

The traces that frame loss demonstrably leaves elsewhere in this play's own archive. A speech passing through a performance-shaped channel arrives damaged or demoted, as the Prologue arrives in the first quarto with its rhymes unsprung and its households renamed, and as the second Chorus arrives in the Folio with its wording intact and its speaker abolished. A speech dropped in a copy's preparation leaves an editorial event behind it, as the Prologue's Folio omission left one, reversed by Rowe in 1709 and recorded in every apparatus since. A speech performed by a company tends to register in the period's paperwork, a part, a plot entry, a payment, an allusion from a contemporary who heard it. The archive surrounding this frame is demonstrably noisy about damage: it preserved two injuries in detail across four centuries. At the closing station it preserves nothing, in six editions, three lines of transmission, and the whole commentary tradition of a famous play. Silence of that completeness, in an archive of that noisiness, is not the signature of loss. It is the signature of absence.

### Q: Why might a plot that runs on secrets refuse a truthful Chorus after its second act?

Because the office and the machinery become incompatible at the marriage. The Chorus speaks with the author's knowledge and the company's licence, addressing the house directly, and everything it says carries the frame's guarantee of truth. From the wedding onward, every decisive fact of the play is confined: a marriage known to four people, a potion whose nature is locked in a friar's cell, a letter whose failure nobody alive understands until the tomb. A truthful chorus at the later stations must either announce the marriage, collapsing the concealment the fiction lives on, announce the potion, spending the fifth act's agony in advance, or select its truths, becoming one more partial knower and spending the authority that made it worth hearing. The Prologue's total spoiler is safe because it names outcomes and withholds mechanisms, arriving before loyalties form. Act-by-act narration would have to traffic in mechanisms. The frame retires at the exact point where honesty becomes unaffordable, and the coincidence of that timing with the Friar's plotting is the design's clearest fingerprint.

### Q: What does the single appearance of Time in The Winter's Tale show about a chorus used once by design?

It shows that a chorus figure in this dramatist's practice is sized to the need that summons it, and that a single appearance can be a complete design. Time enters at the play's middle, announces himself as a chorus, turns his glass, and carries the action across a gap of sixteen years, a gap that exists exactly once in the story and so requires its bridge exactly once. No scholar posits further Time speeches written and lost, because the single appearance is legibly sufficient: the office is discharged and the figure's work is done. The example removes the presumption on which the lost-chorus theory quietly rests, that a frame begun must by nature be a frame continued at every station regardless of need. In the same career, frames run to one speech, to two, and to six, and the number follows the play. The Winter's Tale needed its chorus once and got it once. The Verona tragedy needed its chorus while its world was public and its facts speakable, and the need, like Time's, had a term.

### Q: Why is the gap before Act 3 the most conspicuous of the frame's missing stations?

Because the third act is where the play changes kind, and a frame speech is the instrument the period used to mark exactly such a change. The street fight kills Mercutio and Tybalt, earns the hero his banishment, and converts the comedy of the opening into the tragedy of the close, and it all arrives unannounced, without the summary, warning, or reclassification that a third sonnet, on the pattern of the first two, would have supplied. The expectation is at its maximum there for a mechanical reason as well: two consecutive speeches establish a series, and the third station is where a series is either confirmed or broken, so the silence at the hinge is the first silence an audience can be sure it is hearing. The later gaps extend a breach already registered. The missing chorus before the third act is the moment the retirement announces itself, placed at the action's most violent modulation, where the want of a guide is felt in the same instant as the turn it would have glossed.

### Q: How did Laurence Olivier come to be the Chorus of the 1968 film without a screen credit?

By happenstance, on a visit to another man's set. Franco Zeffirelli was shooting his film of the play in Rome in 1967 while Olivier was in the city filming The Shoes of the Fisherman, and Olivier called at the studio, asked whether there was anything he could do, and was handed the Prologue to read. The courtesy grew: he spoke the film's epilogue as well, and dubbed the voice of the Italian actor playing Lord Montague along with other minor parts, and the whole contribution went uncredited on screen, though cast lists name him in the role of Chorus. The film that resulted uses the most celebrated stage voice of the century for exactly the frame's first office and its last, the opening sonnet and the closing lines, and gives the second Chorus no existence at all. Cinema's definitive Chorus is thus an afternoon's favour by a visiting actor, engaged for the threshold and the envoi, in a film that repeats the documentary asymmetry of the quartos without knowing it.

### Q: How does Baz Luhrmann's 1996 film cast the Chorus, and which speeches survive the casting?

The Chorus is cast as a television newsreader, played by Edwina Moore, who delivers the Prologue from a studio set inside a television screen as the opening bulletin of a news programme, foretelling the feud and the deaths in the play's own words while the screen fills with the faces of the two houses. The Prologue is then delivered a second time in voice-over across a montage of headlines and footage, and the newsreader returns at the film's end to speak the play's last lines as the closing bulletin, framing the whole story as an item of broadcast news. Cast lists credit the newsreader with assuming the role of the Chorus, and the role as cast consists of the first sonnet and the closing lines. The second Chorus has no place in the film. The casting is an exact translation of the frame's function into the announcement medium of its decade, a public voice reporting a civic catastrophe to a city of watchers, and its selectivity reproduces the record of the early witnesses: the opening speech kept, doubled in weight, the hinge speech discarded.

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch chorus note, and what does its station-by-station table record?

The InsightCrunch chorus note is this article's reference table of the play's incomplete choral frame. It sets out the frame's six stations in sequence, the threshold before each of the five acts and the close of the play, and records for each station the speech the established pattern leads an audience to expect, the state of the early texts at that station, and the consequence performance drew from it. Its pattern is the article's argument in miniature. The first two rows record damage of different kinds in different witnesses, a Prologue wrecked in one quarto and omitted in the Folio, a second Chorus omitted in that quarto and anonymized in the Folio. The remaining four rows record uniform absence in every witness, no speech, no gap, no trace. Read down the rows, the table separates the two phenomena that the debate habitually fuses: a frame that was handled roughly by the theatre that transmitted it, and a frame that was never longer than the two speeches every handler received.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch chorus note to argue that the frame's damage and its incompleteness have different causes?

By arguing the table's two halves separately and refusing to let evidence migrate between them. For the first two stations, the essay can cite the performance-shaped witnesses, the abridged first quarto and the Folio's playhouse-annotated copy, and show the damage sorting by each document's distance from the stage, which establishes a theatrical cause for the frame's injuries. For the remaining stations, the essay should cite the table's unanimity: three independent lines of transmission, one descending from the author's draft, agreeing on the frame's extent while agreeing on nothing else, which establishes that no theatrical process is needed to explain the silence, since the silence stood in the copy before any theatre touched it. The performance column then supplies the coda, showing later handlers reproducing the early witnesses' behaviour toward the two speeches they all received. The conclusion follows the table's own shape: the damage has a theatrical history, the incompleteness has a compositional one, and a single cause stretched across both halves explains neither.

### Q: Why does no character inside the fiction ever mention or answer the Chorus?

Because the figure stands outside the fiction's rules of knowledge, and any answer would break them. The Chorus addresses the audience in the company's person, speaking of "our stage" and "our toil," and it possesses from the first minute the knowledge the whole tragedy is spent rationing among the characters: the ending. A Veronese who heard that voice, or replied to it, would import the audience's knowledge into the action, and the play's machinery of secrets, misreadings, and late arrivals would collapse around the importation. The characters' silence about the frame is therefore not an oversight but a definition: the Chorus is audible to the house and inaudible to Verona, in the way of prologues everywhere in the period. The nearest approach the fiction makes to acknowledging its narrator is the Prince's closing speech, where a character performs the frame's summing office from inside the story, under judgment, without ever naming the office he has inherited. The inheritance is silent because the boundary it crosses must stay invisible for the play to work.

### Q: What separates the Chorus's two speeches from the spoken prologues of the Restoration stage?

Nearly everything except the threshold they stand on. The Restoration prologue was an occasional commodity: commissioned for a first night, a benefit, or a revival, often written by a hired wit or by the adapter of the evening, topical in matter, self-advertising in manner, printed separately when it was printed at all, and discarded without record when its occasion passed. Otway's prologue to Caius Marius, apologizing for his borrowings from the earlier tragedy, is the type specimen. The Verona speeches are structural members of a particular work. They are sonnets in a play that thinks in sonnets, the second of them woven into the action's sequence as cover and hinge, discharging exposition and transition inside the fiction's economy, attributed to a standing office rather than to an evening's entertainment, and preserved in the author's draft as part of the book. One kind of speech is an advertisement nailed to the playhouse door for a night. The other is a threshold built into the house, and the confusion of the two kinds is what lets the detachable prologue's known fragility be argued, wrongly, onto the structural frame.

### Q: Did the first quarto's makers cut the Act 2 Chorus, or lose it?

The two verbs name the two scholarly accounts of the short quarto, and the evidence divides between them. On Lukas Erne's account of the first quarto as a stage abridgement, the clean excision of a whole speech belongs to the cutter's work: the short text drops entire movements, the Capulet serving-men's sequence among them, and the second Chorus, a block of pure commentary, is the sort of unit an abridger removes entire. On Harry Hoppe's account of the quarto as a memorial reconstruction, the speech belongs to the matter reporters lose worst, narration addressed to the house rather than dialogue exchanged in cue. The weighing this article adopts assigns the Prologue's internal collapse to memory, since its rhymes are unsprung in a way no reviser produces, and the second Chorus's wholesale absence to the same performance economy the abridgement describes, whether the instrument was a knife or a memory instructed by pace. Either verb delivers the datum the verdict needs: the speech was present in the play the company performed, and performance disposed of it.

### Q: What explains the split between the stage's habit of dropping the Chorus and the study's habit of restoring it?

The two institutions are buying different goods with different currencies. A stage buys an evening: pace, emphasis, the held attention of a house that has paid for momentum, and it pays with whatever in the text does not earn its minutes, which is why commentary falls first in every century and why the second Chorus, a summary at the play's fastest joint, falls before anything. A study buys the work entire: completeness of the document, the author's design recoverable across its whole extent, and it pays with paper, which is cheap, and with apparatus, which is patient. Rowe's edition of 1709 set the study's pattern by printing the Prologue on quarto authority and treating the Folio's silence as the Folio's defect, and three centuries of editors have renewed the choice. The split is not hypocrisy on either side. It is two economies working as designed, and its consequence for judgement is asymmetrical: the stage's cuts, however constant across four hundred years, are evidence about performance, and carry no authority against the study's text, which is evidence about the play.

### Q: Could the two Chorus speeches have been delivered by several voices together in early performance?

No surviving document describes the delivery, and the texts point the other way. Both speeches are single continuous sonnets, addressed to the house in a company's collective pronouns, "our stage," "our toil," but voiced through one prefixed speaker in the second quarto, and the frame speakers of the period's stages were solo functionaries, as its prologues were solo turns. Classical choruses had been plural bodies, yet the early modern reduction of the chorus toward a single framing voice is the documented direction of the period's practice, and scholarship on chorality in the drama of the time treats this figure as a solo lyric and narrative instrument. Modern productions sometimes distribute the speeches across a company in unison, and the device has real virtues as a solution to a casting problem. It should be recognized as an invention rather than a restoration. It dissolves the single knower, the one voice possessed of the ending, whose presence at two thresholds and absence at the rest is the design's central fact, and replaces a person with a texture.

### Q: How does the Prologue's promise of two hours of stage traffic bear on the length of the text a company could actually play?

The promise prices the performance, and the two early texts stand on opposite sides of the price. The Prologue advertises the story as "Is now the two hours' traffic of our stage" (Prologue.12), and the second quarto's text, which Daniel's parallel-text count gives as 3,307 lines against the first quarto's 2,232, strains that span at any plausible pace of period delivery, while the short quarto's tightened text sits inside it. The performed play of the company's own era was therefore, on the arithmetic, almost certainly a cut play, and the first quarto's shape is what such a cutting looks like. The bearing on the Chorus is narrower and cuts against the use usually made of it. The reduction the arithmetic forced fell across some seven hundred lines of the whole text, story, dialogue, and scene, and the frame's twenty-eight lines are a rounding error inside it, too small to have been worth any company's deliberation as a saving of time. Length explains why a playing text exists. It cannot explain why the playing text disposed of the second Chorus entire while keeping a damaged Prologue, which was a choice about commentary, not about minutes.
