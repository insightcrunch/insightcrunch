The agency that funds most American basic research outside medicine exists because a fight about who would appoint its director lasted five years and ended with a presidential veto. The National Science Foundation Act of 1950 created an institution famous for scientific autonomy, yet that autonomy was granted only after the scientists lost the argument about self-governance. The statute says nothing about peer review, the practice the world associates most closely with the agency, and it excludes medical research entirely, because another federal agency already owned that territory. A reader who can explain those four facts, the veto, the missing peer review mandate, the medical exclusion, and the five-year delay, understands the measure better than any summary that treats it as the inevitable product of a wartime report.

![](/assets/images/blog/blog-74.webp)

The story most often told about the founding of the National Science Foundation is simple and wrong in the way that simple stories usually are. In that telling, a wartime science administrator wrote a celebrated report in 1945, Congress read it, and the agency followed. The legislative record tells a different story, one in which the report was the opening bid rather than the blueprint, in which the central question was never whether the government should support science but who would control the money, and in which a president vetoed a bill passed by Congress because the bill let scientists choose their own chief. The enacted statute of 1950 bears the marks of that constitutional dispute on every one of its structural provisions. The director is appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate. The governing board is appointed the same way. The social sciences, the geographic distribution formulas, and the patent rules that the competing bills fought over were each resolved in the text, sometimes by compromise and sometimes by deferral. This article is a profile of the statute as an enacted design, which means it treats the five-year fight, the veto, the board and director structure, the review practice, and the later shifts as one continuous account rather than as separate topics.

## The statute and its legal identity

Precision about the legal identity of the measure anchors everything that follows, because the details fix the statute in the legislative record and prevent the casual errors that surround it. The enrolled short title is the National Science Foundation Act of 1950. The public law number is Public Law 81-507. The Statutes at Large citation is 64 Stat. 149. President Harry S. Truman signed it on May 10, 1950, during the second session of the 81st Congress. The signing came nearly five years after the wartime report that started the debate, and it followed a veto of an earlier version in 1947, which makes this one of the rare American organizing statutes whose enactment date is less important than the fight that preceded it. The measure created an independent agency in the executive branch, consisting of a National Science Board and a Director, and directed it to promote the progress of science through grants, contracts, fellowships, and the other instruments the statute enumerates.

The codification history matters for readers who go looking for the text. The Act was originally codified at 42 U.S.C. sections 1861 and following, as chapter 16 of title 42, and the enrolled sections of the 1950 Act map onto those code sections in a way that later amendments have preserved. The establishment provision, section 2 of the Act, stands at 42 U.S.C. 1861. The functions provision, section 3, stands at 42 U.S.C. 1862. Later amendments, including the 1968 reauthorization that expressly added the social sciences and applied research, were layered onto this structure without disturbing the original numbering scheme, which is why a reader who pulls up the current code sees a statute that looks broader than the one Truman signed. The distinction between the 1950 text and the amended text is one this article maintains throughout, because the argument of the piece depends on what the enacted design actually said in 1950 rather than on what the code says after decades of amendment.

The date on the signing pen fixes the statute in a particular political season, and the season helps explain the urgency of the final push. The second session of the 81st Congress was the session in which the Korean War would begin in June 1950, barely a month after the signing, and in which the Cold War reorganization of American science was already underway. The five-year debate had begun in the afterglow of the wartime mobilization of science, when the question was how to preserve the research capacity the war had built. By 1950 the question had acquired a harder edge, because the Soviet atomic test of 1949 had made scientific capacity look like a condition of national survival. The statute that emerged was still the product of the 1945 to 1950 design argument rather than a war measure, but the atmosphere in which Congress finally acted helps explain why the compromises that had eluded legislators for five years suddenly became acceptable.

## The founding report: Science The Endless Frontier

The document that started the five-year fight was a report delivered to the President in July 1945 by Vannevar Bush, the wartime head of the Office of Scientific Research and Development. The report bore the title Science The Endless Frontier, and its core argument can be stated in three propositions that the brief for this article quotes in compressed form. First, basic research is the pacemaker of technological progress, which means that the stream of new products, new industries, and new military capabilities depends on a prior stream of fundamental discovery. Second, basic research is chronically underfunded by private industry, because its returns cannot be captured by the firm that pays for it and therefore cannot be justified on a commercial balance sheet. Third, the federal government should create a permanent agency to support basic research through grants to universities and other nonprofit institutions, while leaving the choice of specific projects to the scientists themselves. The report proposed a National Research Foundation as the institutional vehicle, and it sketched the agency's divisions, its fellowship program, and its relationship to the rest of the government.

The circumstances of the report's commissioning belong in the account, because they show how personal the founding document was. In November 1944, President Franklin D. Roosevelt wrote to Bush asking for recommendations on how the wartime apparatus of science could be turned to peacetime purposes, posing four questions about the release of wartime scientific information, the war against disease, the government's role in supporting research, and the development of scientific talent. Roosevelt died in April 1945 before the report was finished, and Bush delivered it to President Truman instead. The report was therefore written for one president and delivered to another, a detail that matters because the successor administration did not feel the same sense of obligation to its recommendations that the commissioning president might have. The Truman administration's science policy took a different institutional shape, through the Steelman report of 1947, and the Bush proposal had to survive in Congress on its own merits rather than as the late president's dying instruction.

### What did Vannevar Bush's 1945 report actually propose?

The report proposed a permanent National Research Foundation that would fund basic research through grants to universities and research institutes, run a national fellowship program for training scientists, and leave the choice of specific projects to the researchers themselves rather than to administrators or politicians.

The direct answer above states the institutional core, but the report's full design was more elaborate and its elaborations are where the later fights began. Bush proposed an agency governed by a board of scientists who would appoint the agency's director themselves, insulating the institution from presidential direction. He proposed divisions covering the medical, natural, and physical sciences, and his draft included medicine within the foundation's remit, a point the enacted statute would reject. He favored excellence over geographic distribution, arguing that the best science should be supported wherever it was found, without formulas directing money to underrepresented regions. He left patent policy flexible, trusting the agency to work out ownership of inventions case by case. Each of these design choices reflected a single philosophy, that the people who understood science should govern science, and each of them would be contested in Congress for the next five years.

The report's rhetoric deserves attention because it shaped the terms in which the whole debate was conducted. The frontier metaphor cast scientific research as the continuation of the American westward expansion, a boundless territory open to all citizens, and the report argued that public support for science would benefit everyone through its contributions to the peacetime economy and national security. The metaphor did political work. It made federal funding of research sound like a national tradition rather than a new departure, and it gave legislators a language in which supporting science was patriotic rather than merely technocratic. The metaphor also concealed the hard questions, because a frontier open to all says nothing about who decides which expeditions get funded, and that silence is where the legislative fight lived.

The report's treatment of the universities was as consequential as its treatment of the agency. Bush argued that the grants should flow primarily to colleges, universities, and research institutes rather than to government laboratories, on the ground that the universities were the natural home of basic inquiry and that federal support would strengthen them as institutions. This was a choice with permanent consequences. It meant the new agency would be a patron rather than a performer of research, a funder rather than a laboratory system, and that choice is the reason the American basic research enterprise grew up as a partnership between a federal paymaster and a university system rather than as a network of government institutes. The medical agency that already existed had its own intramural laboratories, which is one of the structural differences between the two agencies that the later sections of this article examine.

## The report's institutional blueprint and the philosophy behind it

Bush's proposed agency embodied a theory of how science should be governed, and the theory can be stated plainly. Scientists, by virtue of their expertise, were the only people competent to judge scientific merit, and therefore they should control the institution that distributed scientific funds. The board would be composed of scientists, the board would choose the director, and the director would answer to the board. The President's role was limited to appointing the board members themselves, and even that was conceived as a ratification of scientific eminence rather than as an instrument of political direction. The agency would be independent of the existing departments, free from the military's claim on research, and free from the kind of political supervision that Bush believed had hampered science in other countries. The philosophy had a name in the later literature, scientific autonomy, and the report is the founding document of that ideal in American science policy.

The autonomy philosophy had a blind spot that the congressional debate exposed within months. Bush's design assumed that the judgment of scientists about scientific merit was the only judgment that mattered in distributing public money, but public money carries democratic obligations that scientific merit alone cannot satisfy. A legislator from a state with no major research university could reasonably ask why federal taxes collected in that state should fund laboratories in other states. A president could reasonably ask why an agency spending public funds should be headed by someone the president did not choose. A patent lawyer could reasonably ask who would own the inventions produced with public money. None of these questions has a scientific answer, and Bush's design had no place for them, which is why the design could not survive contact with the legislature unchanged.

The report also embodied a particular sociology of science that favored established centers of excellence. Bush argued that the best science should be supported wherever it was found, without geographic formulas, and the practical effect of that position was to direct money toward the institutions that already had the strongest research capacity, principally the established universities of the Northeast and California. The position was defended as meritocracy, and its defenders argued that diluting support across regions would weaken the whole enterprise. Its opponents called it concentration and argued that a national program funded by national taxes should build capacity across the nation rather than enrich the institutions that were already rich. The geographic question would become one of the five contested themes of the legislative fight, and its resolution in the 1950 statute, a soft objective rather than a hard formula, bears the marks of the compromise.

The exclusion question that would later define the agency's identity was present in the report in a form that surprised later readers. Bush's proposed foundation included medicine within its divisions, which reflected the wartime experience in which medical research had been part of the same mobilization as physics and chemistry. The enacted statute dropped medicine entirely, because the health research establishment already existed and was expanding, and the division of labor between the two agencies became one of the permanent features of American science policy. The fact that the founding report and the enacted statute differ on this point is the first evidence for the complication this article must address, that the report was not simply implemented, and the later sections return to it in detail.

## The five-year fight: why the design took so long

The interval between the July 1945 report and the May 1950 signing is the longest gestation of any major American science organizing statute, and its length needs explanation, because nothing about the underlying policy was mysterious. Everyone involved agreed that the federal government should support basic research. The disagreement was about control, and control questions are the hardest kind to compromise, because they allocate power rather than money and because each side reads the other's proposal as an attempt at capture. The five contested themes that the Congressional Research Service later identified, political and administrative control, inclusion of the social sciences, geographic distribution of funding, ownership of patents, and the balance between basic and applied research, were all control questions in disguise. Each asked who would decide, by what criteria, and with what accountability, and each divided the legislators along lines that no single bill could satisfy for five years.

The legislative sequence ran through three Congresses and produced a series of bills that mapped the disagreement with unusual clarity. In July 1945, on the same day the White House released the Bush report, Senator Warren Magnuson of Washington introduced S. 1285, a bill drafted in consultation with Bush that hewed closely to the report's design. Senator Harley Kilgore of West Virginia introduced a rival bill, S. 1297, that embodied a different philosophy of democratic control. The Senate held hearings on science legislation in the fall of 1945, with testimony that included social scientists arguing for the inclusion of their disciplines, and the House held its own hearings in the spring of 1946. A compromise Kilgore-Magnuson bill cleared the Senate in July 1946 but stalled. The 80th Congress produced S. 526, the National Science Foundation Act of 1947, which Congress passed and President Truman vetoed. Only in the 81st Congress, after the 1948 election had changed the political arithmetic, did a bill emerge that could pass both houses and survive the President's signature.

### Which five disputes delayed the foundation's creation for five years?

Competing bills disagreed about who would control the agency, whether the social sciences counted as science, how money should be distributed geographically, who would own patents on funded inventions, and whether applied research qualified, and the 1947 presidential veto forced Congress to redesign the governance compromise before the 1950 enactment.

The direct answer names the causes, but the texture of the delay matters as much as its causes, because the five years were not idle. Each Congress refined the issues, narrowed the differences, and tested the political strength of the rival positions. The 1945 hearings aired the full range of disagreement. The 1946 compromise showed that the Senate could agree on a bill but not on one the House would take up. The 1947 veto clarified the one point on which the President would not yield, the appointment of the director. The 1948 election broke the stalemate by returning a Congress more sympathetic to the administration's position. The delay was therefore productive in the way that legislative delay often is, each failure taught the drafters something about what could pass, and the 1950 statute is the accumulated wisdom of the failures.

The role of the 1948 election deserves emphasis because it is the least appreciated part of the story. The 80th Congress, which passed the vetoed bill, was a Republican Congress elected in the 1946 midterms, operating in tension with a Democratic president. The 81st Congress, elected alongside Truman's surprise victory in 1948, was Democratic, and the administration's science policy preferences carried more weight in its deliberations. The change in party control did not resolve the substantive disagreements, but it changed the bargaining positions, making the presidential-appointment model the default rather than the concession. Institutional design arguments are never purely intellectual, and this one was mediated at every stage by the partisan composition of the Congress doing the designing.

A parallel track of executive-branch science policy complicated the legislative debate throughout the five years. In 1947, John Steelman, the chairman of the President's Scientific Research Board, produced a five-volume report titled Science and Public Policy that offered an alternative vision to the Bush report, one more sympathetic to presidential direction, to applied research, and to the coordination of science with national economic policy. The Steelman report gave the administration an intellectual foundation independent of Bush, which meant the Bush proposal could not claim to be the only serious thinking on the subject. The existence of a rival report, commissioned by the sitting president, weakened the moral authority of the 1945 document and gave legislators who opposed the Bush design a respectable alternative to cite. The five-year fight was therefore not just a fight between bills but a fight between philosophies of science policy, each with its own founding text.

## The Kilgore vision: democratic control and broad distribution

Senator Harley Kilgore's bill, S. 1297 in the 79th Congress, embodied the proposition that public money for science must answer to democratic institutions, and its provisions read as a point-by-point rebuttal of the Bush design. Where Bush proposed a board of scientists choosing their own director, Kilgore proposed a publicly appointed director, chosen by the President, advised by a board rather than governed by one. Where Bush favored excellence without geographic formulas, Kilgore required broad distribution of funding and research findings, on the ground that a national program should build scientific capacity across the nation rather than concentrate it in established centers. Where Bush defined research narrowly as basic inquiry, Kilgore defined research and development broadly to include theoretical exploration, practical application, plans and specifications, economic and industrial studies, experimental production, and pilot plants. The Kilgore bill was, in short, the democratic-control alternative, and its supporters argued that science had grown too important to national life to be left to the unsupervised judgment of scientists.

The geographic distribution provisions of the Kilgore approach deserve close attention, because they reveal the political economy that made the issue so durable. The argument for distribution ran as follows. Federal funds for research came from taxpayers in every state. The existing concentration of research capacity in a handful of states was partly an artifact of past federal investment, including the wartime contracts that had enriched the same institutions. A new agency that simply followed existing excellence would therefore lock in the advantages of the past and deny other regions the chance to build their own capacity. Mandatory distribution formulas, whether by population or by region, would counteract the natural tendency toward centralization and stimulate the growth of science throughout the nation. The opponents of formulas, following Bush, called this pork-barrel science and argued that it would hamper the foundation by forcing it to fund mediocrity in the name of equity. Both arguments had force, and the 1950 statute's resolution, a soft objective rather than a hard formula, reflected the fact that neither side could defeat the other outright.

The Kilgore bill's treatment of patents reflected the same democratic-control philosophy. The bill's broad definition of research and development, extending into practical application and pilot plants, implied a regime in which the government would retain substantial rights in inventions produced with public money, on the theory that the public had paid for the research and should own its fruits. The specific patent provisions were among the most technically complex parts of the debate, involving questions about exclusive licenses, march-in rights, and the incentives for commercialization that would occupy Congress for decades afterward. For the purposes of the 1945 to 1950 fight, the patent question functioned as a proxy for the larger control question, with the democratic-control camp favoring government retention of rights and the scientific-autonomy camp favoring flexibility for the agency and its grantees.

The political base of the Kilgore position was as important as its intellectual content. Kilgore, a New Deal Democrat from West Virginia, spoke for the regions and the political tradition that distrusted concentrations of unaccountable power, whether in corporations or in expert elites. His bill found support among legislators who saw the Bush design as an attempt to create a self-perpetuating scientific establishment immune from democratic oversight. The charge was not that scientists were corrupt but that any group given public money without public accountability would inevitably serve its own institutional interests, and that the history of expert bodies justified the suspicion. This was the democratic theory of the Kilgore bill, and it is worth stating at its strongest, because the 1950 statute ultimately accepted its central premise on the question of control.

## The Magnuson vision: scientists governing science

Senator Warren Magnuson's bill, S. 1285, introduced the day the Bush report was released and drafted in consultation with Bush himself, embodied the opposite proposition, that the people who understood science should govern the institution that funded it. The bill created a foundation led by a publicly appointed board that would select, direct, and supervise a director, putting most of the authority in the hands of the board rather than in a presidential appointee. The board would develop national science policy and support basic research in the mathematical, physical, and biological sciences. The bill contained no provisions for broad geographic distribution of funding, reflecting Bush's view that excellence should be supported wherever it was found. It authorized the publication and dissemination of research findings but left the harder questions of control to the scientists themselves.

The Magnuson bill's board-centered governance was the purest legislative expression of the scientific-autonomy philosophy, and its defeat is the central fact of the five-year fight. Under S. 1285, the director would have been the board's employee, chosen by the board, removable by the board, and answerable to the board. The President's connection to the agency would have run entirely through the appointment of board members, a attenuated link that gave the elected chief executive no direct control over the agency's chief operating officer. The supporters of this design argued that it was the only way to protect science from political interference, pointing to the history of politically directed research in other countries as a warning. The opponents argued that it created an agency accountable to no one, spending public money under the direction of a self-selected elite, and that the Constitution's vesting of executive power in the President forbade such an arrangement.

The social science question divided the two bills in a way that foreshadowed decades of controversy. The Magnuson bill's enumeration of the mathematical, physical, and biological sciences reflected Bush's own ambivalence about whether the social sciences counted as science for the purposes of federal support. Bush had argued that the social sciences did not contribute to national progress in the same way as the natural sciences, a position that the social science community contested vigorously in the 1945 hearings, with witnesses from the Social Science Research Council testifying for inclusion. The Kilgore bill's broader definitions were more hospitable to the social sciences, though the issue was never fully resolved in any of the pre-1950 bills. The 1950 statute would settle it by omission, authorizing support for the physical, biological, and engineering sciences and mathematics without mentioning the social sciences, a silence that lasted until the 1968 amendments expressly authorized social science research.

The basic-versus-applied distinction was the fifth contested theme, and it cut across the other four in complicated ways. The Magnuson bill focused on basic research, following Bush's argument that the government's proper role was to support fundamental inquiry whose returns could not be captured commercially, leaving applied development to industry. The Kilgore bill's expansive definition of research and development blurred the line, authorizing support for practical application, pilot plants, and industrial studies. The distinction mattered for the agency's identity, because a basic-research agency would be a patron of universities while an applied-research agency would compete with industry and with the mission agencies. The 1950 statute came down on the basic-research side, directing the foundation to initiate and support basic scientific research, and the applied-research authority would wait until 1968. The choice shaped the agency's culture permanently, orienting it toward the universities and toward investigator-initiated inquiry rather than toward directed development programs.

## The 1947 veto: the constitutional objection

The 80th Congress passed S. 526, the National Science Foundation Act of 1947, and presented it to President Truman on July 25, 1947. Truman vetoed it. The veto message stated two concerns, but the first was the decisive one and the one this article's namable claim turns on. The bill provided that the foundation's director would be appointed by the agency's own board rather than by the President. Truman objected that this arrangement implied, in the veto message's phrase, a distinct lack of faith in democratic processes, and that it would impede rather than promote the government's efforts to encourage scientific research. The constitutional logic was straightforward. The director of an agency spending public money and exercising executive authority had to be accountable to the elected President, and a director chosen by an unelected board of scientists broke that chain of accountability. No override was attempted, and the bill died.

The veto deserves to be read carefully, because its reasoning is the purest statement of the democratic-control position in the whole five-year fight. Truman did not object to the foundation's mission, its budget, or its scientific program. He did not dispute that the government should support basic research, that grants should flow to universities, or that fellowships should train new scientists. He objected to a single structural provision, the method of choosing the chief executive, and he objected on separation of powers grounds rather than on policy grounds. The distinction is the series thesis thread of this article in miniature. The enacted design of the agency reflects a constitutional dispute about appointment power rather than a policy dispute about science, and the veto is the moment that dispute became dispositive.

### What defect did Truman cite in vetoing the 1947 foundation bill?

Yes. Congress passed S. 526 in 1947, and Truman vetoed it because the bill let the agency's board appoint the director instead of the President, which he called a lack of faith in democratic processes that would impede the government's encouragement of research. No override was attempted.

The political context of the veto sharpened its meaning. The 80th Congress was a Republican Congress, and the bill it passed reflected a compromise in which the board-appointment provision had survived as the price of getting the measure through. Truman, a Democratic president heading into the 1948 election, had no political incentive to sign a bill whose governance structure he believed to be unconstitutional, and every incentive to draw a clear line on presidential prerogative. The veto message's language about democratic processes was not mere rhetoric. It stated a theory of executive accountability that would become the non-negotiable condition of the 1950 statute, that the director of a federal agency must be the President's appointee, confirmed by the Senate, removable within the constitutional understandings of the office.

The veto also clarified the stakes for the scientists who had championed the board-appointment model. The scientific community, which had invested five years in the Bush design, learned that the autonomy it prized most, the right to choose its own chief, was the one provision the constitutional system would not tolerate. The lesson was not that science would go unfunded but that it would be funded on the President's terms, through an agency whose leadership answered to elected authority. The adjustment this required was psychological as much as institutional. The vision of a republic of science governing itself with public money died with the veto, and what replaced it was a partnership in which scientists advised and the President decided. The 1950 statute is the charter of that partnership.

Readers who want the mechanics of presidential vetoes in the American system, the difference between a regular veto and a pocket veto, the two-thirds override threshold, and why overrides of science bills are rare, will find the general framework explained in the series companion on the veto power itself, which uses the 1947 foundation veto as one of its illustrations of a policy veto that succeeded because Congress accepted the constitutional objection rather than fighting it ([veto and override explained](/2022/04/01/veto-and-override-explained/)).

The two years between the veto and the 1950 signing were not a simple rerun of the earlier debate. The 1948 election returned Democratic majorities and gave Truman a second term, which changed the negotiating environment in two ways. First, the administration's preference for presidential appointment became the starting point of drafting rather than a demand to be extracted in conference. Second, the legislators who had championed the board-appointment model understood that the veto had settled the question, and they redirected their energies to the issues where compromise was still possible, the geographic language, the patent provisions, and the scope of the authorized sciences. The 1950 bill was therefore not a repudiation of the vetoed bill but a revision of it, keeping the institutional architecture while replacing the governance provisions that had drawn the veto.

## The 1950 compromise: Public Law 81-507

The bill that became Public Law 81-507 kept the foundation's mission and rebuilt its governance around the veto's lesson. The director would be appointed by the President, by and with the advice and consent of the Senate, for a term of six years. The National Science Board would consist of twenty-four members, appointed by the President with Senate confirmation, serving staggered six-year terms, with policy and advisory functions but not the power to choose the director. The social sciences were left out of the enumerated fields, the geographic distribution question was resolved with hortatory language rather than a formula, and the patent questions were left to the foundation to work out through its contracts and grants. Truman signed the measure on May 10, 1950, and the five-year fight ended not with the triumph of either philosophy but with a constitutional settlement that both sides could live with.

The structure of the compromise rewards close reading, because each provision encodes a specific resolution of a specific dispute. The presidential appointment of the director resolved the control question in favor of democratic accountability, accepting the Kilgore position and the veto's logic. The twenty-four member board, appointed the same way but with staggered terms that outlasted any single presidency, preserved a measure of the scientific community's influence, accepting the Magnuson position that scientists should have a formal voice in governance without giving them the choosing power. The six-year terms for both the director and the board members were longer than the presidential term, which meant the agency's leadership would always include appointees of more than one president, a design choice that insulated the institution from abrupt political swings while keeping it within the constitutional chain of command. The staggered board terms, with a fraction of the membership turning over every two years, ensured continuity of institutional memory across administrations.

The geographic compromise is visible in the precise wording of section 3(b), which made it one of the objectives of the foundation to strengthen basic research and education in the sciences throughout the United States, including its territories and possessions, and to avoid undue concentration of such research and education. The language is hortatory rather than mandatory. It states an objective, not a formula. It directs the foundation to avoid undue concentration without defining what concentration counts as undue or what distribution would correct it. The Kilgore camp got the principle written into law, that geographic equity was a legitimate objective of the agency. The Bush camp got the absence of any enforceable mechanism, which meant the foundation could pursue excellence as it judged best while gesturing toward distribution. The provision satisfied both sides precisely because it committed neither to a number, and its vagueness is the reason the geographic debate continued for decades after 1950.

The patent compromise was a deferral rather than a resolution. The 1950 Act left patent questions to the foundation to decide through the contract process, giving the agency discretion to set patent policy in its grants and contracts rather than writing a statutory rule. This was a victory for flexibility and a defeat for everyone who wanted a clear statutory answer, whether the Kilgore camp's preference for government retention of rights or the autonomy camp's preference for grantee ownership. The deferral meant the patent question would be fought again in every era, through agency regulation, through executive policy, and eventually through the 1980 legislation that reversed the default for university research. But in 1950, deferral was the only solution available, because the competing positions were irreconcilable and the bill needed to pass.

The scope compromise, the enumeration of the authorized sciences, is the quietest of the resolutions and in some ways the most consequential. The 1950 Act directed the foundation to support the mathematical, physical, biological, and engineering sciences, with fellowships and grants flowing to those fields. The social sciences were not named. Medicine was not named. The omissions were deliberate, reflecting the legislative judgment that the social sciences remained contested as science and that medicine belonged to the health research establishment. The statute thus created an agency whose jurisdiction was defined as much by its exclusions as by its inclusions, and the exclusions shaped the American research system for the rest of the century.

## The National Science Board: twenty-four members and a defined role

The National Science Board as enacted in 1950 was a deliberate hybrid, a body of scientists and engineers with real policy functions but without the choosing power that had drawn the veto. The statute set the membership at twenty-four, each appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate, each serving a six-year term, with the terms staggered so that a portion of the board turned over every two years. The staggering was the mechanism that gave the board its continuity and its partial insulation from any single administration. A president serving a full two terms would appoint the entire board over time, but no president could remake it at once, and the board always contained members chosen by predecessors. The design balanced democratic accountability, every member was a presidential appointee confirmed by the Senate, with institutional stability, no president could capture the body in a single stroke.

The board's functions under the 1950 Act were advisory and policy-setting rather than executive. The board established the policies of the foundation within the framework of the statute, advised the director, and exercised the kind of oversight that a governing board exercises over a chief executive it does not choose. The distinction between choosing and advising is the constitutional heart of the 1950 design. A board that chooses the director is the director's principal, and the President is shut out. A board that advises a presidentially appointed director is a source of expertise within the executive branch, and the chain of accountability runs unbroken from the director to the President to the electorate. The 1950 statute drew this line with precision, and the line is the reason the veto's objection does not apply to the enacted design.

The composition requirements reflected the autonomy philosophy in its surviving form. The members were to be eminent in the fields of the basic sciences, engineering, or other areas of the foundation's work, which meant the board would in practice be drawn from the scientific and engineering communities rather than from political life. The eminence standard served two functions. It assured the scientific community that the board would understand the work it oversaw, preserving the principle that scientific judgment should inform scientific governance. It also assured Congress that the board would be a body of recognized achievement rather than a patronage vehicle, which mattered to legislators who had watched other boards become dumping grounds for political favorites. The standard was enforced through the confirmation process, which gave the Senate a check on appointments that departed from it.

The board's relationship to the director was the most carefully negotiated part of the governance design, and the statute's treatment of it repays attention. The director was the chief executive officer of the foundation, responsible for its administration, its programs, and its staff. The board set policy and advised, but it did not administer. This division of labor, policy to the board and execution to the director, mirrored the corporate governance model familiar from private institutions, and it gave each side of the five-year fight something to claim. The autonomy camp could point to a board of eminent scientists setting the policies under which the agency operated. The democratic-control camp could point to a director appointed by the President and answerable to the President for the agency's administration. The division worked because it separated the question both sides cared about most, who sets the direction of American science, into two questions, who sets policy and who executes it, and answered them differently.

## The Director: presidential appointment and the six-year term

The director's office as created in 1950 embodied the veto's lesson in a single provision. The director was to be appointed by the President, by and with the advice and consent of the Senate, for a term of six years. The appointment method placed the agency's chief executive squarely within the constitutional chain of command, answering the separation of powers objection that had killed the 1947 bill. The six-year term, longer than the four-year presidential term, gave the director a measure of independence within that chain, ensuring that a director would normally serve across presidential transitions and could not be treated as a purely political appointee serving at the pleasure of the current administration. The term length was the compromise between accountability and continuity, and it has defined the office ever since.

The six-year term deserves emphasis because it is the provision that made presidential appointment acceptable to the scientific community. A director serving at the pleasure of the President would have been a political officer in fact as well as in form, subject to removal whenever the agency's judgments displeased the White House. A director with a fixed six-year term had the security to make scientific judgments without calculating their political reception, while remaining ultimately accountable through the appointment process and through congressional oversight. The term did not make the director independent of the President in the constitutional sense, but it made the office independent in the practical sense that mattered to scientists, the freedom to fund the best research without political direction of individual awards. The distinction between constitutional accountability and practical independence is the key to understanding why the 1950 design held.

The statute also created a Deputy Director, appointed in the manner the Act specified, to perform the director's functions during absence or vacancy and to assist in administration. The deputy provision was administrative rather than philosophical, but it reflected the same design sensibility as the rest of the statute, that the agency should have continuity of leadership and a clear line of succession. The Act further authorized the director to appoint the agency's staff, including scientific and technical personnel, and to organize the foundation into divisions corresponding to the fields of science it supported. The divisions provision gave statutory recognition to the internal structure through which the agency would actually operate, with separate programmatic homes for the mathematical, physical, biological, and engineering sciences.

The appointment provisions taken together resolved the control question that had consumed five years. The President appointed the director. The President appointed the board. The Senate confirmed both. The board set policy and advised. The director administered. No provision allowed the scientists to choose their own chief, and no provision allowed the President to direct individual funding decisions. The design was neither the Bush republic of science nor a political spoils system, but a constitutional hybrid, democratic in its accountability and scientific in its judgment, and its durability suggests the hybrid captured something true about how a democracy should fund inquiry it cannot fully understand.

## What the statute directs: functions, grants, and fellowships

Section 3 of the Act, the functions provision, is the operational heart of the statute, and it reads as a charter for a patron of research rather than a performer of it. The foundation was authorized and directed to initiate and support basic scientific research and programs to strengthen scientific research potential and science education programs at all levels in the mathematical, physical, biological, and engineering sciences. The instruments were grants, contracts, and other arrangements with universities, colleges, nonprofit institutions, and individuals. The foundation could award scholarships and graduate fellowships for study and research in the sciences and engineering. It could foster the interchange of scientific information among scientists in the United States and foreign countries. It could serve as a central clearinghouse for information about scientific and technical personnel. It could establish research divisions corresponding to the fields it supported. The enumeration is worth reading in full, because it shows an agency designed to work through others, funding, training, connecting, and informing, rather than building its own laboratories.

The grant mechanism was the statute's central innovation in the organization of American science, and its implications unfolded over decades. Before 1950, federal support for university research had flowed primarily through wartime contracts and through the mission agencies, the military departments and the health establishment, each funding research that served its own institutional purposes. The foundation's grants were different in two respects. They were awarded for basic research without a mission requirement, which meant a physicist could propose an investigation for its scientific merit rather than for its relevance to weapons or health. And they were awarded to institutions and individuals across the country through a national program, which meant the foundation became the first federal agency whose sole purpose was the support of inquiry for its own sake. The grant, as an instrument, carried the Bush philosophy forward even as the governance provisions rejected it, because the grant left the choice of projects to the proposing scientists.

The fellowship and scholarship provisions addressed the pipeline question that the Bush report had posed in Roosevelt's fourth question, how to develop scientific talent for the postwar era. The foundation was authorized to award graduate fellowships for study and research in the sciences and engineering, creating a national program of support for the training of new researchers. The fellowships were portable in conception, supporting individuals rather than institutions, which meant a talented student could pursue training at the institution best suited to the work rather than being bound to a single university's resources. The scholarship provisions extended the pipeline downward, supporting undergraduate study in the sciences. Together, the fellowship and scholarship authorities made the foundation a patron of scientific careers and of scientific projects, and the generations of researchers trained under its fellowships became one of the agency's most durable legacies.

The information functions of the statute are easy to overlook and important to the design. The foundation was to foster the interchange of scientific information domestically and internationally, to maintain a register of scientific and technical personnel, and to serve as a clearinghouse for knowledge about the state of American science. These provisions reflected the wartime experience, in which the coordination of scientific information had been as important as the funding of research, and they gave the agency a role in the infrastructure of science beyond the awarding of money. The clearinghouse function, in particular, anticipated the agency's later role as a collector and publisher of statistics on the scientific enterprise, the surveys of research funding, personnel, and output that became essential tools of science policy analysis.

The division structure authorized by the statute gave the agency its internal shape. The foundation could establish divisions for the mathematical, physical, biological, and engineering sciences, each with its own programmatic identity and its own community of grantees. The divisions became the agency's face to the scientific world, the units to which proposals were submitted and from which awards were made, and their boundaries shaped the organization of American science by defining which fields had institutional homes and which did not. The absence of a medical division and the long absence of a social science division were as consequential as the presence of the others, because the division structure told researchers where the money was and, by omission, where it was not.

## Peer review: the famous practice the statute never mentions

The most surprising fact about the National Science Foundation Act, for readers who know the agency by reputation, is that the statute never mentions peer review. The practice for which the foundation is known worldwide, the evaluation of grant proposals by panels of expert scientists, the system adopted by research funders in dozens of countries, appears nowhere in Public Law 81-507. The Act directs the foundation to award grants, fellowships, and contracts. It establishes the board and the director. It enumerates the fields of support. It says nothing about how proposals shall be judged, who shall judge them, or what criteria shall apply. Peer review is an agency practice, developed administratively in the years after 1950, not a statutory command, and the distinction matters for understanding both the statute and the agency.

### Where did the foundation's peer review practice come from, if not the statute?

No. The 1950 statute directs the foundation to award grants and fellowships but prescribes no review method whatsoever. Peer review is an administrative practice the agency developed on its own in the years after enactment, not a legal requirement imposed by Congress.

The direct answer corrects the most common misconception about the statute, but the history of how the practice developed is where the deeper lesson lies. In the agency's early years, the staff faced a practical problem the statute had not solved. Thousands of proposals would arrive from universities across the country, each claiming scientific merit, and the small headquarters staff could not judge them all on their own expertise. The solution was to send proposals to outside experts for evaluation, to convene panels of scientists who would read, rank, and recommend, and to make funding decisions on the basis of those recommendations. The practice grew organically from administrative necessity, was formalized over the years into the elaborate system of panels, mail reviews, and program-officer discretion that became the agency's worldwide signature, and was eventually written into the agency's regulations and policy manuals. But it was never written into the statute, which means Congress could not have mandated it in 1950 even if it had wanted to, because the practice did not yet exist in its mature form.

The non-statutory status of peer review has consequences that surprise even sophisticated observers. Because the practice is administrative rather than legislative, the agency can modify it without an act of Congress, and it has done so repeatedly, adjusting the balance between panel review and mail review, between program-officer discretion and panel rankings, between the criteria of intellectual merit and broader impacts. Because the practice is not in the statute, a future Congress could direct the agency to use a different method, or to add criteria the scientific community would not choose, without amending any provision that mentions review, since there is none to amend. The flexibility is a strength and a vulnerability at once. It lets the agency adapt its procedures to experience, but it leaves the procedures exposed to political direction in a way that a statutory mandate would not.

The irony of the peer review story is that the practice embodies the Bush philosophy of scientific self-governance more purely than any provision of the statute does. The scientists who lost the fight over appointing the director won, in practice, the fight over judging proposals, because the agency they could not govern they could at least staff with their judgments. Peer review is scientific autonomy operationalized, the community deciding which of its members' work deserves support, and it functions as a kind of compensation for the governance defeat of 1947 to 1950. The statute created an agency accountable to the President. The practice created a funding system accountable to the scientific community. The two accountabilities coexist in tension, and the tension is the living legacy of the five-year fight.

The global influence of the practice deserves mention because it shows how an administrative improvisation became an international standard. Research funders in dozens of countries adopted versions of the American peer review system, sending proposals to expert panels and funding on the basis of ranked recommendations, often citing the foundation's practice as the model. The diffusion happened without any statutory mandate, through professional networks, through the movement of scientists between countries, and through the sheer prestige of the American system's results. An agency practice, developed to solve a staffing problem in Washington, became the world's default method for allocating research funds, which is a reminder that the most consequential parts of an institution are sometimes the parts its charter never mentions.

## Why medicine was left out: the division of labor with the health agency

The National Science Foundation does not fund medical research, and the reason is structural rather than philosophical. The health research agency already existed and was expanding rapidly when the foundation was created, and Congress saw no reason to build a second medical research establishment. The Public Health Service, with the National Institutes of Health as its research arm, had been growing through the 1940s, funding and performing medical research through its own grants and its own intramural laboratories. The division of labor was therefore settled before the foundation opened its doors. The new agency would support the physical, biological, and engineering sciences and mathematics. Medical research would remain with the health establishment. The American research funding system has been split between these two very differently structured agencies ever since.

The contrast between the two agencies' structures illuminates the choice. The health establishment combined extramural grants with a large intramural laboratory system, performing research in its own facilities as well as funding it elsewhere, and it was organized around disease categories and medical missions. The foundation, by statutory design, was a purely extramural patron, funding research performed by others, principally at universities, and organized around disciplines rather than missions. The two models reflected different theories of how the government should support inquiry. The health model kept a hand in the work itself, building government laboratories that could pursue long-term programs. The foundation model kept the government at arm's length from performance, writing checks and judging proposals but not running laboratories. The coexistence of the two models gave American science a pluralism it might otherwise have lacked, and the pluralism was an accident of timing rather than a plan.

The Bush report's inclusion of medicine makes the exclusion more striking. The 1945 report had proposed a medical division within the new foundation, reflecting the wartime integration of medical and physical research under the Office of Scientific Research and Development. The enacted statute dropped it, and the reasons were practical. The health establishment's constituencies, the medical schools, the voluntary health organizations, the Public Health Service itself, had no interest in seeing medical research funding moved to a new agency where it would compete with physics and engineering. The appropriators had no interest in reorganizing an expanding program that was working. And the legislators who wrote the 1950 Act understood that adding medicine would have complicated the bill's passage by dragging the health establishment's politics into the science foundation fight. The exclusion was therefore overdetermined, supported by bureaucratic interest, congressional convenience, and the substantive judgment that medical research had a workable home.

The boundary between the two agencies' jurisdictions was never perfectly clean, and the imperfections are instructive. Biological research that bore on medical questions could be funded by either agency, and the foundation supported fields like biophysics and aspects of neuroscience that touched the edges of medicine. The statute's silence on medicine did not forbid the foundation from funding research with medical implications, and the agency interpreted its biological sciences mandate broadly enough to cover fundamental work that the health agency might also have claimed. The overlap was managed through interagency understandings rather than statutory precision, which is typical of jurisdictional boundaries drawn by omission. The important point for the statute's profile is the principle, not the border disputes. The principle was that medical research belonged elsewhere, and the principle held.

The long-term consequence of the division was a research funding system with two centers of gravity, each with its own culture, its own constituencies, and its own relationship to Congress. The health agency grew into the largest federal funder of research, dwarfing the foundation in budget, because disease constituencies could mobilize political support that basic physics could not. The foundation remained the smaller agency but the more prestigious in the eyes of basic scientists, because its grants carried no mission requirement and its peer review embodied the community's own standards. The asymmetry was built into the 1950 design, and it has structured every debate about federal science funding since, from the Mansfield Amendment's redirection of defense research dollars to the modern arguments about the balance between basic and applied support.

## The Mansfield Amendment: the later shift that reshaped basic research funding

Nearly two decades after the foundation's creation, a restriction enacted at the end of the 1960s redirected a substantial share of American basic research support toward the agency and reshaped who funds American science. The provision known as the Mansfield Amendment, section 203 of the military authorization act for fiscal year 1970, Public Law 91-121, enacted in 1969, limited Department of Defense funding of research to projects with a direct and apparent relationship to a specific military function or operation. The amendment was named for Senator Mike Mansfield of Montana, its sponsor, and it responded to the political pressures of the Vietnam era, when defense funding of university research had become a target of campus protest and congressional scrutiny. Its effect on the research funding system was structural and permanent.

### Why did basic research money migrate from the Pentagon to the foundation after 1969?

The 1969 Mansfield Amendment barred defense dollars from basic research lacking a direct military relationship, which pushed a large share of university basic research support away from the Pentagon and toward the National Science Foundation, the only federal agency chartered for basic inquiry without a mission qualifier.

The direct answer states the mechanism, but the context explains why a single section of a military authorization act could move so much money. Through the 1950s and 1960s, the Department of Defense had been a major funder of basic research at American universities, supporting work in physics, mathematics, materials science, and electronics through the Office of Naval Research, the Army Research Office, and the Air Force Office of Scientific Research. The defense agencies funded basic inquiry generously, partly from genuine interest in the scientific foundations of military technology and partly because the post-Sputnik consensus treated scientific strength as national strength. By the late 1960s, this support had become politically vulnerable. University critics argued that defense money corrupted academic independence, that classified and mission-directed funding distorted the research agenda, and that the Pentagon had no business supporting work with no military purpose. The Mansfield Amendment gave those criticisms statutory force.

The amendment's language was deceptively simple and its implementation was complex. The requirement of a direct and apparent relationship to a specific military function forced the defense research offices to review their entire university grant portfolios and terminate or transfer the projects that could not meet the test. Fundamental work in mathematics, theoretical physics, and other fields far from any weapons application lost its defense support. Some of it found new homes in the mission agencies with plausible claims, the Atomic Energy Commission for nuclear-related physics, the health establishment for biologically related work. Much of it migrated to the foundation, which was the only federal agency whose statutory mission was basic research without a mission qualifier. The migration increased the foundation's share of federal basic research support at universities substantially and made the agency more central to the American research system than its 1950 designers had anticipated.

The amendment's effects on the defense research offices were as significant as its effects on the foundation. The Office of Naval Research, which had been the most generous defense patron of university basic science, contracted its university programs and refocused on naval missions. The reorientation was painful for the affected fields and institutions, particularly in the short term, as grants were terminated and researchers scrambled for alternative support. But it also clarified the division of labor that the 1950 statute had implied without fully establishing. Basic research without a mission requirement would be the foundation's job. Mission research would belong to the mission agencies. The Mansfield Amendment wrote that division into law for the defense establishment, completing the architecture that the National Science Foundation Act had begun.

The political irony of the amendment deserves notice. A provision intended by its sponsors to reduce the militarization of university research ended up strengthening the civilian agency that the 1950 statute had created, which was arguably the best possible outcome for the amendment's supporters. But it also concentrated basic research funding in a single agency to a degree the 1950 designers had not foreseen, making the foundation's budget decisions more consequential for American science and making the agency a larger target in the political fights over research funding that followed. The concentration had intellectual consequences as well. Fields that had thrived under the plural patronage of defense, health, and foundation support found themselves more dependent on a single agency's priorities and procedures, including the peer review system that the statute never mentioned. The Mansfield Amendment is therefore the second great redesign of the American basic research system, after the 1950 Act itself, and this article treats it as the closing chapter of the founding era.

## The education companion: science funding meets science schooling

The foundation's statutory mandate for science education programs at all levels connected the 1950 Act to the education legislation that followed, and the connection runs most directly to the measure Congress passed eight years later in response to Sputnik. The National Defense Education Act of 1958 addressed the schooling side of the scientific enterprise, funding science and mathematics education, student loans, and graduate fellowships, while the foundation addressed the research side, funding the investigators and the investigations. The two statutes divided the labor of building American scientific capacity between education and research, between training minds and funding their work, and they are best understood as companion measures in a single national project even though they were enacted separately and administered separately. The series companion on the education measure traces how the fellowship and curriculum programs of 1958 complemented the research grants of 1950, and how the two together shaped the postwar university ([national defense education act of 1958](/2017/02/15/national-defense-education-act-1958/)).

The education provisions of the 1950 Act itself were modest compared with what followed, but they established the principle that the research agency had a legitimate interest in the pipeline of trained scientists. The foundation's fellowship programs supported graduate students directly, and its science education programs, developed over the years within the statutory authorization, supported curriculum development, teacher training, and educational experimentation. The principle was contested at times, with critics arguing that an agency chartered to support research should not stray into schooling, and defenders arguing that research without researchers was an empty mandate. The 1958 education act relieved some of the pressure by creating a larger and more explicit federal role in science education, but the foundation's education mission persisted, a reminder that the 1950 statute's framers understood scientific capacity as a system rather than a collection of grants.

## The modern authorization: the agency's later statutory home

The 1950 Act created the foundation, but the agency's statutory authority was revisited and extended many times afterward, including in the comprehensive legislation enacted in 2022 that reauthorized the agency alongside a major investment in semiconductor research and development. That measure, enacted in 2022, included a full reauthorization of the foundation with new directorates, new programs, and new reporting requirements, and it represents a later chapter in the statutory history that began with Public Law 81-507. The relationship between the original act and the modern authorization illustrates how organizing statutes evolve. The 1950 design, the board, the director, the grant mechanism, the disciplinary divisions, survived intact, while Congress layered new missions, new structures, and new oversight mechanisms onto the original frame. The series companion on the 2022 measure explains the reauthorization in detail, including the provisions that trace directly back to the 1950 design choices examined in this article ([chips and science act guide](/2017/03/15/chips-and-science-act-2022-guide/)).

The continuity between 1950 and the modern authorization is the strongest evidence for the durability of the original design. A statute written to resolve a five-year fight about appointment power created an institution flexible enough to absorb the Mansfield Amendment's redirection of defense research dollars, the 1968 addition of the social sciences and applied research, the growth of science education programs, and the periodic reorganizations of its internal divisions, without any fundamental change to its governance. The board still sets policy. The director still administers. The grants still flow to universities on the basis of expert judgment. The features that made the design controversial in 1950, the presidential appointment, the fixed terms, the deliberate vagueness of the geographic and patent provisions, turned out to be the features that made it adaptable. A more rigid statute, one that had written peer review or patent rules or distribution formulas into law, would have required amendment with every change in practice. The 1950 Act's silences were its shock absorbers.

The distinction between the statute and the funding mechanics reinforces the article's central theme. The 1950 Act created the authority to make grants and left the method to the agency, which means the entire operational apparatus of American basic research funding, the solicitations, the panels, the awards, the oversight, grew up in the space the statute left empty. The emptiness was deliberate, a product of the 1950 compromise's preference for flexibility over prescription, and it is the reason the agency could develop peer review, could adjust its procedures, and could respond to the Mansfield Amendment's influx of new responsibilities without returning to Congress for new authority. The statute is the skeleton. The funding mechanics are the flesh. The companion article describes the flesh.

## The sequence: where the 1950 Act sits in American science policy legislation

The National Science Foundation Act was neither the first nor the last major statute in the American science policy sequence, and its meaning is clearer when placed among its neighbors. Before it came the wartime mobilization and the 1945 report that proposed the agency. Alongside it came the expansion of the health research establishment and the creation of the Atomic Energy Commission, the other two pillars of the postwar federal research system. After it came the 1958 education act, the 1968 amendments that added the social sciences and applied research, the 1969 Mansfield Amendment that redirected defense research dollars, the 1980 patent legislation that reversed the default on university inventions, and the modern reauthorizations that extended the agency into new fields and new missions. Each of these measures responded to the design the 1950 Act had established, whether by extending it, correcting it, or building alongside it. The series companion on the legislation of American science policy lays out the full sequence, showing how the 1950 Act functions as the hinge between the wartime origins and the mature system ([us science policy legislation](/2017/06/01/us-science-policy-legislation/)).

The hinge metaphor captures the statute's historical position precisely. Looking backward, the Act resolved the questions the 1945 report had raised but could not answer, who governs, who decides, who owns. Looking forward, it created the institutional platform on which the next seventy years of science policy were built, the agency whose grants, fellowships, and procedures became the default infrastructure of American basic research. Hinge statutes are rare in any policy domain, and they are recognizable by the test this article's framework applies. A reader who understands the hinge understands the whole sequence, because the later measures are intelligible as responses to the design the hinge established. The 1968 social science authorization responded to the 1950 omission. The Mansfield Amendment responded to the 1950 division of labor. The modern reauthorization extended the 1950 frame. The sequence is the statute's afterlife, and the afterlife confirms the design.

## The namable claim: the scientists lost the argument about self-governance

The five-year delay and the veto turned on presidential appointment power, which means the agency that embodies scientific autonomy was created only after the scientists lost the argument about self-governance. The claim can be stated as a paradox and defended as a fact. The National Science Foundation is the institutional expression of the idea that scientists should judge science, through peer review, through the board's policy role, through the grant mechanism that leaves project choice to investigators. Yet the statute that created it rejected the institutional expression of that idea that its champions cared about most, the right of the scientific community to choose the agency's chief executive. The autonomy the agency enjoys is therefore a granted autonomy, conferred by a statute that reserves the ultimate accountability to the President, and it exists at the pleasure of the constitutional order rather than outside it.

The paradox resolves when the two kinds of autonomy are distinguished. What the scientists lost was governance autonomy, the power to constitute the agency's leadership and thereby to make the institution answerable only to themselves. What they kept, and what the statute protected through its silences, was judgment autonomy, the power to decide which proposals merited support, which fields deserved emphasis, and which researchers showed promise. The 1950 design separated the two with surgical precision, giving the governance question to the democratic-control camp and the judgment question to the scientists, and the separation is the reason the design worked. An agency governed by scientists but funded by the public would have faced a permanent crisis of legitimacy. An agency governed accountably but judging scientifically faced only the ordinary tensions of democratic administration, and those it managed.

The loss had a disciplining effect on the scientific community that its champions did not anticipate but that served the institution well. Denied the power to choose the director, the community invested its energies in the practices it could control, peer review, professional standards, the cultivation of excellence, and it built those practices into the most admired research funding system in the world. The energy that might have gone into defending a self-governing board went instead into making the grant system work, and the result was an agency whose scientific credibility exceeded what any governance provision could have conferred. Defeat in the constitutional argument produced victory in the practical one, which is not the usual outcome of political losses but is the distinctive outcome of this one.

The claim also corrects the sentimental version of the founding story, in which the scientists' wisdom prevailed and the agency was their reward. The sentimental version cannot explain the veto, the five-year delay, or the specific provisions of the statute, and it flatters the scientific community at the expense of the democratic institutions that actually created the agency. The accurate version gives the credit where the record puts it, to the legislators who insisted on accountability, to the president who vetoed the unconstitutional design, and to the drafters who built a hybrid that honored both science and democracy. The scientists got their agency, but they got it on the republic's terms, and the terms are written into every governance provision of Public Law 81-507.

## The complication: the founding report was not simply implemented

The account in which the Bush report was simply implemented collapses under the weight of the differences between the proposal and the statute, and the differences matter because they show how much of the design was legislative rather than authorial. Bush preferred an agency insulated from presidential control, with a board-appointed director, and he lost that point to the veto. He preferred the exclusion of the social sciences from the definition of science worthy of support, and the enacted statute's omission, followed by the 1968 reversal, resolved the question against his preferences in two stages. He preferred excellence without geographic distribution, and the statute's hortatory language, followed by decades of political pressure for distribution, resolved that question against him as well. He proposed a medical division, and the statute excluded medicine entirely. On the four questions he cared about most, the author of the founding report lost all four.

The Steelman report of 1947 sharpens the complication, because it shows that the Bush report was not even the administration's own thinking by the time the legislative fight peaked. The President's Scientific Research Board, under John Steelman, produced a rival vision more sympathetic to presidential direction, to applied research, and to the coordination of science with economic policy, and the existence of that rival deprived the Bush report of the authority it might otherwise have claimed as the government's plan. The legislative debate of 1947 to 1950 was therefore not a referendum on implementing Bush but a contest among philosophies, Bush's autonomy, Steelman's coordination, Kilgore's democratic control, and the statute that emerged was a settlement among them rather than the victory of any one. To say the report was implemented is to erase the Steelman alternative, the Kilgore alternative, and the veto, which is to erase most of what actually happened.

The persistence of the simple story is itself a datum worth explaining. The Bush report is a magnificent document, clearly written, nobly argued, and quotable in a way that legislative compromises never are. The statute is a technical text, its compromises visible only to readers who know what to look for. Memory favors the document that reads well, and the scientific community, which revered Bush, had every incentive to remember the founding as the triumph of his vision. The legislative record tells the harder story, of a visionary proposal tested against democratic institutions and modified at every point where it conflicted with constitutional accountability. This article has told the harder story, because the series exists to explain statutes rather than to celebrate reports, and the statute is the thing that actually governs.

## The series thesis: a constitutional design, not a policy design

The enacted design of the National Science Foundation Act reflects a constitutional dispute rather than a policy one, and the distinction organizes everything this article has established. A policy dispute asks what the government should do, how much it should spend, which fields it should favor, what outcomes it should seek. A constitutional dispute asks who decides, through what institutions, under what accountability, and with what limits on power. The five-year fight over the foundation was of the second kind. No participant disputed that the government should support basic research. The fight was about whether the director would be chosen by scientists or by the President, whether the board would govern or advise, whether public money could be spent by an agency whose chief executive answered to no elected official. These are questions about the structure of republican government, and the answers are written into the statute's governance provisions.

The constitutional character of the dispute explains features of the statute that a policy reading cannot. It explains why the veto message discussed democratic processes rather than scientific programs, because the objection was to the structure of accountability, not to the substance of research. It explains why the 1950 compromise gave both sides something on governance but deferred the policy questions, patents, distribution formulas, applied research, because the constitutional question had to be settled before the policy questions could be addressed. It explains why the statute's silences, on peer review, on patents, on the precise meaning of undue concentration, were functional rather than careless, because a statute that had settled the constitutional question could afford to leave the policy questions to administration. The design is constitutional first and programmatic second, and reading it in the reverse order produces the misunderstandings this article has corrected.

The thesis also explains the statute's durability, which a policy reading would find puzzling. Policy designs decay as policies change, as new fields emerge, as budgets shift, as political coalitions turn over. Constitutional designs endure because they settle the framework within which policy debates happen rather than settling the debates themselves. The 1950 Act settled who decides, and the decades since have been a long series of policy debates conducted within that settlement, about the social sciences, about patents, about defense research, about education, about new directorates. The framework held through all of them, not because the policy answers were right but because the constitutional answers were. A statute profile that misses this distinction mistakes the building for its tenants.

## What the reader can explain

A reader who has followed the argument this far can answer the One Test posed at the outset, and the answers are worth restating in the order the brief requires. The agency that funds most American basic research outside medicine took five years and a presidential veto to create, because the fight was about who would appoint the director rather than about whether science deserved support. The peer review system the agency is famous for appears nowhere in the statute, because it is an administrative practice developed after 1950 rather than a legislative command. The agency does not fund medical research because the health research establishment already existed and was expanding, and Congress built the new foundation around the physical, biological, and engineering sciences and mathematics instead. The enacted agency differs from the founding report's proposal at every point the author cared about, which means the report was the opening bid rather than the blueprint. And the design that emerged reflects a constitutional settlement about appointment power and accountability, not a policy consensus about science.

The study of this statute repays the effort this article has demanded because the design questions it raises recur wherever democracies fund inquiry they cannot fully direct. Who should judge the merit of proposed research, and by what criteria. How should public money be distributed across regions and institutions without corrupting the judgment of merit. Who should own the inventions that public money produces. How should the governance of a scientific agency balance expertise against accountability. The 1950 Act answered these questions for one agency at one moment, and its answers, modified by amendment and by practice, still structure the American system. ## The appropriations question: an agency authorized without a guaranteed budget

The 1950 Act created the foundation and authorized its programs, but it did not appropriate a dollar, and the distinction between authorization and appropriation shaped the agency's early life in ways the statute's text does not advertise. An authorization act creates the legal authority to operate and sets the ceiling of what Congress may fund. An appropriation act provides the money. The foundation, like most federal agencies, has lived its entire life in the gap between the two, dependent on annual decisions by appropriators who were not bound by the authorizers' intentions. The 1950 statute's silence on funding levels was not an oversight but the normal practice of organizing legislation, which establishes institutions and leaves the funding to the separate appropriations process. The consequence was that the agency's ambitions, as stated in section 3, were always hostage to the appropriations committees' willingness to pay for them.

The authorization structure itself changed over time in ways that reflected Congress's evolving attitude toward oversight of the agency. As originally enacted, and as modified by a 1953 enactment, the foundation operated under an indefinite authorization, which meant Congress did not have to revisit the agency's statutory authority on any fixed schedule. The 1968 amendments, Public Law 90-407, repealed the indefinite authorization and replaced it with an annual authorization cycle, so that for two decades the authorizing committees reconsidered the foundation's programs and funding levels every year. The shift from indefinite to annual authorization was a move toward tighter congressional control, giving the authorizers a regular opportunity to examine the agency's activities, to add or restrict programs, and to assert the legislature's ongoing supervision. The annual cycle lasted in general form from fiscal year 1969 through fiscal year 1989, after which Congress moved to multi-year authorizations. The authorization history is a reminder that the 1950 design, for all its constitutional carefulness about governance, left the agency's lifeblood, its money, to political processes the statute did not attempt to regulate.

The practical effect of the authorization-appropriation split was to make the foundation a supplicant twice over, before the authorizers for permission and before the appropriators for money. In the agency's early years, when its budget was small and its constituency was still forming, the double supplication kept the foundation modest and cautious, reluctant to promise more than the appropriators were likely to deliver. As the agency grew, particularly after the Mansfield Amendment redirected defense research dollars toward it, the stakes of the appropriations process grew with it, and the foundation learned the arts of congressional justification that every federal agency must master. The statute's grant of authority was permanent. The money was annual. The tension between the two is the permanent condition of the agency's existence, and it was built into the design from the day Truman signed.

The indefinite-to-annual-to-multiyear progression also tells something about how Congress learns to oversee a new institution. In 1950, the legislature created the agency and trusted it, granting indefinite authority in the expectation that the design was sound. By 1968, after the Daddario committee's three-year examination of the foundation's activities, Congress wanted closer supervision and imposed the annual cycle. By the late 1980s, the annual cycle had become burdensome to both the agency and the committees, and multi-year authorizations restored a measure of stability. Each change was a recalibration of the balance between trust and oversight, and each left the 1950 governance design untouched. Congress fiddled with the authorization clock repeatedly while never reconsidering the board, the director, or the grant mechanism, which suggests the original design answered the questions it was meant to answer.

## The fellowship generation: training scientists as a statutory mission

The 1950 Act's fellowship and scholarship provisions are sometimes treated as secondary to the research grants, but they embodied a distinct theory of what a science agency should do, and that theory deserves its own examination. The grants funded projects. The fellowships funded people. The distinction mattered because the Bush report had identified the development of scientific talent as one of the four great postwar tasks, alongside the support of research itself, and the statute gave that task institutional form. The foundation was authorized to award graduate fellowships for study and research in the sciences and engineering, and to award scholarships for undergraduate study, creating a national system of portable support that followed the individual rather than the institution.

The portability of the fellowships was the design choice that gave them their power. A fellowship tied to an institution keeps the recipient where the institution is. A fellowship tied to the individual lets the recipient go where the best training is, which meant the foundation's fellows concentrated at the strongest graduate programs and thereby reinforced the very excellence the agency was chartered to support. The geographic-distribution advocates had reason to view the fellowships with suspicion, since portable merit-based awards would flow to the established centers just as surely as the research grants would. But the fellowship defenders argued that training was different from project funding, that the nation needed its most talented students trained at the best facilities regardless of region, and that diluting fellowship support across geography would weaken the training itself. The tension reproduced the larger distribution debate in miniature, and it was resolved the same way, by leaving the criteria to the agency.

The fellowships also served a demographic function that the statute's framers understood imperfectly but that became clear over time. By providing independent support to graduate students, the fellowships freed recipients from dependence on teaching assistantships and on faculty research grants, giving them the autonomy to pursue their own intellectual directions. The freedom mattered most for students whose backgrounds gave them the fewest alternative resources, because a portable fellowship was a credential that opened doors regardless of the student's origins. The statute did not frame the fellowships in these terms, and the 1950 Congress did not debate them as instruments of access, but the design had egalitarian consequences that the meritocratic rhetoric concealed. A national competition, judged on promise rather than pedigree, was more open than the informal patronage networks it partially replaced.

The scholarship provisions extended the pipeline downward to undergraduate education, though in practice the graduate fellowships became the more prominent program. The distinction between the two levels reflected a judgment about where federal intervention could do the most good. Undergraduate science education was primarily the business of the colleges themselves, supported by tuition, endowment, and state funding. Graduate training in the sciences was expensive, specialized, and closely tied to the research enterprise, which made it the natural point of federal leverage. The foundation's fellowships therefore concentrated at the graduate level, where a relatively small federal investment could shape the formation of the next generation of researchers. The generations trained under the program became the agency's most loyal constituency, a community of scientists whose careers had been launched by federal fellowships and who carried the foundation's model of merit-based support into every institution they joined.

## The board in operation: policy without administration

The 1950 Act gave the National Science Board the power to set policy and denied it the power to administer, and the distinction defined the board's working life for the decades that followed. In practice, the board concerned itself with the large questions, the balance among fields, the criteria for award decisions, the agency's long-range planning, the state of American science as a whole, while leaving the thousands of individual funding decisions to the director and the staff. The division of labor was the one the statute's drafters had intended, but its operation revealed subtleties the text did not spell out. Policy and administration bleed into each other at the margins, and the board repeatedly faced the question of where its policy role ended and the director's administrative discretion began.

The board's most visible products were its policy statements and its assessments of the scientific enterprise, the documents through which a part-time body of eminent scientists could exert influence disproportionate to its meeting schedule. The board issued statements on the directions of American science, on the needs of particular fields, on the relationship between the foundation and the universities, and on the principles that should govern federal support of research. These statements carried weight because they spoke with the collective authority of the scientific community's most recognized figures, and agency directors ignored them at their peril. But the statements were advisory in the precise sense the statute intended. They bound no one, they allocated no money, and their influence depended on persuasion rather than command. The board governed by prestige rather than by power, which was exactly the role the 1950 compromise had designed for it.

The confirmation process gave the Senate a continuing role in the board's composition that the statute's framers had anticipated but whose political significance grew over time. Each of the twenty-four members required Senate confirmation, which meant the Senate could, in principle, reject nominees it found unqualified or objectionable. In practice, confirmations were rarely contested, because the eminence standard produced nominees whose credentials were difficult to challenge and because the board's advisory role made its membership less politically salient than the directorship. But the confirmation requirement served its constitutional function even when it was not exercised adversarially. It kept the board within the appointments framework the veto had demanded, ensuring that every member of the body that set science policy had passed through presidential nomination and senatorial consent. The legitimacy of the board's pronouncements rested in part on this democratic pedigree, thin though the confirmation scrutiny usually was.

The staggered terms produced the continuity the drafters had sought, and the continuity had intellectual as well as administrative value. A board whose membership turned over entirely with each administration would have lurched between priorities, abandoning long-range initiatives whenever the White House changed hands. The staggered six-year terms meant the board always contained members appointed by more than one president, which moderated abrupt shifts and preserved institutional memory across political transitions. The moderation was not always welcome to incoming administrations with strong views about science policy, but it was the price of the design, and the 1950 Congress had deliberately chosen stability over responsiveness. The board's slowness to change direction, sometimes criticized as inertia, was the intended consequence of a structure built to outlast the political cycle.

## The divisions as disciplinary architecture

The 1950 Act authorized the foundation to establish divisions corresponding to the fields of science it supported, and the divisions became the agency's internal architecture, the units through which proposals flowed and awards were made. The original fields, the mathematical, physical, biological, and engineering sciences, each received a programmatic home, and the divisional structure did more than organize the staff. It organized American science itself, by defining which fields had institutional standing within the federal funding system and which did not. A field with a division had program officers, advisory panels, a budget line, and a community of grantees who identified with it. A field without one had to seek support through neighboring divisions or go without. The architecture was therefore a map of scientific legitimacy as the 1950 Congress understood it.

The disciplinary boundaries drawn by the divisions shaped research in ways that went beyond administration. Scientists learned to frame their proposals in the language of the division most likely to fund them, which meant the divisional structure influenced the questions researchers asked and the collaborations they formed. Interdisciplinary work, which by definition fell between divisions, faced the structural disadvantage of belonging fully to none, and the agency spent decades developing mechanisms, cross-divisional programs, special initiatives, to compensate for the boundaries its own architecture had created. The problem was not unique to the foundation, since every organized funding system draws lines that some worthy work will cross, but the 1950 Act's divisional authorization made the lines unusually durable, because they were written into the organic statute rather than left entirely to administrative discretion.

The absence of certain divisions was as consequential as the presence of others. The long absence of a social science division told social scientists that their fields stood outside the agency's core mission, a message reinforced by the statute's silence and only partially softened by the limited programs the agency developed on its own authority. The absence of a medical division told biomedical researchers to look to the health establishment, reinforcing the jurisdictional split the 1950 Congress had accepted. The engineering division, by contrast, gave engineering a standing within a basic-research agency that the Bush report's pure-science vision might not have provided, reflecting the Kilgore camp's broader conception of research and development. Each divisional presence or absence encoded a judgment about what counted as science worthy of federal support, and the judgments accumulated into a disciplinary hierarchy that persisted long after the original reasons were forgotten.

The later reorganizations of the divisional structure, into directorates and offices with shifting names and boundaries, did not erase the original architecture's influence. New fields, from computer science to environmental science to the social and behavioral sciences after 1968, were accommodated within a framework whose bones were set in 1950, and the accommodation often took the form of adding new units alongside the old rather than redrawing the map from scratch. The accretion produced an agency whose internal organization told the history of American science in sedimentary layers, each new directorate a fossil of the era that created it. The 1950 divisions were the bedrock layer, and their outlines remain visible in the agency's structure no matter how many new units have been deposited on top.

## Basic research as a statutory term

The 1950 Act directed the foundation to support basic scientific research, and the phrase carried a theoretical load that the statute did not unpack. In the Bush report's usage, basic research meant fundamental inquiry pursued without thought of practical application, the pacemaker whose beats would eventually drive the technological heart. The definition implied a linear model of innovation, in which basic discovery led to applied development, which led to products and processes, in an orderly sequence from the laboratory to the marketplace. The linear model justified the government's role with elegant simplicity. Industry would fund the later stages, where returns could be captured, but only the government could fund the first stage, where returns were diffuse and distant. The statute adopted the term without adopting the theory explicitly, but the theory was the air the term breathed.

The linear model had critics even in 1950, and their criticisms grew louder in the decades that followed. Historians and sociologists of science argued that the traffic between basic and applied work ran in both directions, that technological problems often posed the questions basic research answered, and that the clean separation of the two was an administrative convenience rather than a description of how discovery worked. The Kilgore bill's expansive definition of research and development had embodied this skepticism, refusing to draw a sharp line between theoretical exploration and practical application. The 1950 statute drew the line anyway, because the line was what distinguished the new agency from the mission agencies and from industry, and because without it the foundation would have had no principled boundary for its jurisdiction. The statutory term was therefore a strategic simplification, useful for institutional design even if inadequate as philosophy of science.

The practical meaning of basic research was worked out not in the statute but in the agency's daily decisions, in the thousands of judgments by program officers and panels about which proposals counted as basic and which did not. The working definition that emerged was procedural rather than philosophical. Basic research was what the foundation funded through its disciplinary programs on the basis of intellectual merit, as distinct from the directed programs of the mission agencies. The circularity was real but functional. It gave the agency a way to defend its jurisdiction without settling the philosophical questions, and it allowed the boundary to shift as fields evolved. Work that looked applied in one decade, such as early computer science, could look basic in the next, as the field's fundamental questions came into focus. The statutory term was a vessel, and the agency filled it with the content each era required.

The 1968 authorization of applied research complicated the vessel without breaking it. Public Law 90-407 gave the foundation express authority to support applied as well as basic research, which might have seemed to dissolve the distinction on which the agency's identity rested. In practice, the agency maintained the distinction culturally even after the statute abandoned it legally, continuing to orient its core programs around investigator-initiated fundamental inquiry while treating applied work as a separate and secondary mission. The persistence of the basic-research identity after the legal distinction faded is evidence of how deeply the 1950 design had shaped the institution. The statute's term had become the agency's self-conception, and self-conceptions outlast the legal texts that form them.

## The international dimension: information without borders

The 1950 Act directed the foundation to foster the interchange of scientific information among scientists in the United States and foreign countries, and the provision gave the agency an international role that grew far beyond what the spare statutory language suggested. In the early Cold War years, the international provisions served the strategic purpose of maintaining scientific contacts across political boundaries, supporting the exchange programs, conferences, and collaborations through which American science engaged the world. The foundation's international activities were modest in budget but significant in symbolism, representing the proposition that science was a universal enterprise even when politics divided its practitioners. The statutory mandate for information interchange was the legal basis for programs that brought foreign scientists to American laboratories and sent American scientists abroad.

The diffusion of peer review was the most consequential international effect of the American system, though it traveled through professional networks rather than through any statutory program. Research funders in dozens of countries adopted versions of the foundation's panel-based evaluation, often explicitly citing the American model, and the practice became the global default for allocating research funds. The diffusion is remarkable because the practice itself was never statutory, which means the world's most widely copied research funding procedure originated as an administrative improvisation in Washington and spread by example rather than by law. The international adoption ratified the Bush philosophy of scientific self-governance more completely than any American statute could have, creating a worldwide community of practice in which scientists judged scientists across national boundaries.

The international dimension also exposed the tension between the universalism of science and the nationalism of its funding. The foundation's money came from American taxpayers and was justified to Congress as serving American interests, in economic competitiveness, in national security, in the prestige of American science. Yet the knowledge the money produced was published openly and belonged to the world, and the foreign scientists trained under foundation-supported programs carried their expertise home. The statute's information provisions embraced this openness, directing the agency to share knowledge rather than hoard it, but the embrace sat uneasily beside the national-interest justifications offered in appropriations hearings. The tension was never resolved, because it reflected a genuine duality in the enterprise itself. American science was both a national asset and a universal good, and the 1950 Act's international provisions kept both truths in play.

## The 1968 amendments: the deferred questions return

The 1968 amendments to the National Science Foundation Act, Public Law 90-407, enacted after a three-year examination of the agency by the House Subcommittee on Science, Research, and Development under Representative Emilio Daddario, resolved two of the questions the 1950 statute had deferred and tightened congressional oversight in the process. The amendments expressly authorized foundation activities in the social sciences, ending the eighteen-year silence that had begun with the 1950 omission, and specifically authorized support for applied research, ending the basic-research exclusivity of the original charter. The two authorizations harked back directly to the establishment debates of the 1940s, settling in legislation the issues that the 1950 compromise had left to practice and implication. The Daddario committee's work was the most thorough congressional reexamination of the agency since its founding, and its product showed how the deferred questions had ripened.

The social science authorization was the more symbolically charged of the two, because it reversed the Bush position that the social sciences did not contribute to national progress in the way the natural sciences did. By 1968, the social sciences had demonstrated their relevance to the policy concerns of the decade, in urban affairs, in education, in the behavioral dimensions of health and defense, and the argument for their exclusion had weakened. The express authorization did not end the controversy, since the social sciences remained politically vulnerable in ways the natural sciences were not, but it gave the agency's social science programs a statutory foundation they had previously lacked. The eighteen-year gap between the 1950 omission and the 1968 authorization measures the time it took for the legislative judgment about the scientific status of the social disciplines to change.

The applied research authorization was the more institutionally significant, because it gave the foundation legal authority for work the agency had been edging toward in practice. The original charter's limitation to basic research had always been somewhat notional, since the boundary between basic and applied was porous in the daily work of funding decisions, but the express authorization legitimized programs of directed and problem-oriented research that the agency had previously justified under strained readings of the basic-research mandate. The authorization also reflected the influence of the Kilgore philosophy, eighteen years after its defeat, since the expansive conception of research and development that Kilgore had championed in 1945 finally found its way into the statute through the back door of amendment.

The 1968 act also restructured the authorization cycle, repealing the indefinite authorization and imposing the annual cycle that lasted for two decades, and the oversight tightening was as important as the substantive expansions. The Daddario committee had concluded that the agency needed closer legislative supervision, and the annual authorization gave the authorizing committees a regular vehicle for examining programs, questioning priorities, and asserting direction. The combination of expanded authority and tightened oversight captured the mature congressional attitude toward the agency: trust its scientific judgment on the merits of individual awards, but supervise its programs, its priorities, and its purse with increasing care. The 1950 governance design survived the reexamination intact. The board, the director, the grant mechanism, and the disciplinary structure all emerged unchanged, which confirmed that the constitutional settlement of 1950 had settled the questions it needed to settle.

## The veto's constitutional logic: why the appointment method mattered

The 1947 veto turned on a question of constitutional structure that deserves a fuller exposition than the veto section gave it, because the question explains why the governance fight could not be compromised in the way the policy fights were. The Constitution vests the executive power in the President and provides that the President shall appoint the officers of the United States, with the Senate's advice and consent. The director of a federal agency that disburses public funds, enters contracts, hires staff, and sets national policy for research support is unmistakably an officer of the United States exercising executive authority. A statute providing that such an officer be chosen by a board of private citizens, however eminent, removes the appointment from the President and thereby breaks the chain of democratic accountability that the appointments provisions were designed to secure. Truman's objection was not a technicality. It was the core of the constitutional design.

The defenders of the board-appointment model had an answer, and it deserves to be stated fairly before being set aside. They argued that the foundation was not an ordinary executive agency but a trustee of the scientific enterprise, that its work required insulation from political direction, and that presidential appointment would expose funding decisions to partisan pressure. They pointed to the independence of the judiciary as an analogy, suggesting that just as judges were insulated to protect their impartiality, so science administrators should be insulated to protect their objectivity. The analogy had surface appeal but collapsed under scrutiny. Judges were insulated by the Constitution itself, through life tenure and salary protections written into Article III. No such constitutional insulation existed for a research funding agency, and Congress could not create the equivalent of judicial independence by statute without confronting the appointments problem. The board-appointment model tried to borrow the judiciary's independence without its constitutional foundation.

The 1950 solution, presidential appointment with a fixed six-year term, can be understood as the closest the constitutional order comes to the independence the scientists sought. The fixed term gave the director security against removal for political reasons, which provided the practical independence that mattered for funding decisions. The appointment method preserved the constitutional accountability that the veto demanded. The combination did not give the scientists everything they wanted, since the President still chose the director, but it gave them the thing they needed most, a chief executive who could fund research on the merits without fear of dismissal. The distinction between wanting to choose and needing to be free from political direction is the key to the compromise, and the 1950 statute honored the need while denying the want.

The broader significance of the veto's logic extends beyond the foundation to the whole class of independent agencies that populate the federal government. The mid-twentieth century saw Congress create numerous agencies with varying degrees of insulation from presidential direction, through fixed terms, for-cause removal protections, and bipartisan membership requirements, and the Supreme Court's decisions on these structures, beginning with Humphrey's Executor in 1935, mapped the constitutional boundaries. The foundation's design fits within those boundaries comfortably, since the director's fixed term provides a mild form of insulation without challenging the President's appointment prerogative. The veto ensured that the agency would be built inside the constitutional lines rather than outside them, and the lines have held. No serious constitutional challenge to the foundation's governance has ever been mounted, which is the quiet vindication of Truman's objection.

## Reading the statute in order: the architecture of Public Law 81-507

A walk through the Act's provisions in their enacted order reveals an architecture that the thematic treatment of this article has necessarily fragmented, and the sequential reading repays the effort. The short title section gives the measure its name. The establishment section creates the independent agency, names it the National Science Foundation, and constitutes it as a board and a director, the two organs whose relationship is the statute's central subject. The functions section, the longest in the Act, enumerates what the agency is authorized and directed to do, from supporting basic research to awarding fellowships to maintaining the register of scientific personnel, and its subsection on geographic distribution states the hortatory objective that resolved the distribution fight. The board section sets the membership at twenty-four, prescribes the appointment method and the staggered terms, and defines the policy and advisory functions. The director section provides for presidential appointment, the six-year term, and the deputy, and vests administration in the chief executive.

The remaining provisions fill out the institutional machinery with the unglamorous precision that organizing statutes require. The personnel provisions authorize the director to appoint staff, including scientific and technical personnel outside the ordinary civil service constraints where the effective administration of the Act requires it, a flexibility that recognized the agency would need to hire working scientists rather than career bureaucrats. The patent provisions delegate the ownership questions to the agency's contracts, the deferral examined earlier. The appropriations provisions authorize the funding without providing it, maintaining the constitutional separation between authorization and appropriation. The general provisions handle the transition, the definitions, and the separability of the Act's parts. Each provision is modest on its own. Together they constitute a complete institutional charter, an agency that could open its doors and begin operating the day the President signed.

The sequential reading also reveals the statute's rhetorical strategy, which was to state large purposes in general language and to leave the contested details either vague or delegated. The functions section speaks of promoting the progress of science, strengthening research potential, and advancing education, phrases broad enough to accommodate decades of programmatic evolution. The geographic subsection speaks of avoiding undue concentration without defining the term. The patent provisions speak of contracts without specifying terms. The generality was not imprecision but design, the product of a five-year education in what could be written into law and what had to be left to administration. A statute that had tried to specify everything would have specified the controversies into permanence. A statute that specified the framework and delegated the details could evolve, and the 1950 Act's evolution, through amendment and through practice, vindicated the strategy.

The architecture's most striking feature, visible only in the sequential reading, is the absence of any provision for how the agency should judge the proposals it received. The functions section directs the foundation to make grants. No section directs it how to choose among applicants. The omission is the negative space in which peer review grew, and its placement in the architecture is instructive. The drafters specified who would govern, who would administer, what fields would be supported, and what instruments would be used. They did not specify the method of judgment, because no method commanded consensus and because the drafters understood, whether consciously or not, that the method would have to be discovered in practice. The sequential reading thus confirms the article's central claim about the statute's silences. They were load-bearing.

## What the statute did not do: the productive omissions

The omissions of Public Law 81-507 form a catalog as instructive as its provisions, and collecting them in one place shows the pattern that the thematic sections have traced separately. The statute did not prescribe peer review or any other method of judging proposals. It did not set a patent rule for funded inventions, delegating the question to the agency's contracts. It did not establish a geographic distribution formula, stating only the hortatory objective of avoiding undue concentration. It did not include the social sciences among the authorized fields. It did not include medicine. It did not authorize applied research. It did not appropriate funds. It did not create government laboratories. It did not give the board executive power. Each omission was a decision, whether conscious or merely the residue of failed agreement, and each shaped the agency as surely as any provision.

The pattern of the omissions reveals the 1950 compromise's governing logic, which was to settle the constitutional questions in the text and defer the policy questions to administration, amendment, or practice. The constitutional questions, who appoints the director, what the board may do, how the agency fits within the executive branch, were answered with precision, because they had to be answered for the bill to survive the veto. The policy questions, how to judge proposals, who owns inventions, how to distribute funds geographically, whether to support applied work, were left open, because answering them would have reignited the fights the compromise had quieted. The logic was sound statecraft. Constitutional settlements are durable because they concern the framework of decision. Policy settlements are fragile because they concern the substance, which changes with knowledge, politics, and circumstance. The 1950 Congress settled what it could settle permanently and left the rest to the future.

The later history validated the pattern omission by omission. Peer review emerged from practice and became the global standard, an outcome no statutory prescription could have achieved as well. The patent questions were eventually addressed by later legislation built on decades of agency experience, producing better answers than the 1950 Congress could have written. The geographic distribution debate continued politically, as it would have under any formula, but without the rigidity a statutory formula would have imposed. The social sciences and applied research were added by amendment when the legislative judgment changed, through the democratic process rather than by administrative improvisation. Medicine stayed with the health establishment, where the division of labor proved stable. Each deferred question found its resolution in the forum best suited to it, which suggests the omissions were not failures of drafting but instances of institutional wisdom.

The catalog of omissions also serves as a corrective to the common reading of statutes as comprehensive codes. Readers accustomed to regulations, which attempt to specify every contingency, sometimes approach an organizing statute expecting the same completeness and find the 1950 Act puzzlingly thin. The thinness is the point. An organizing statute is a constitution for an agency, not an operating manual, and constitutions work by establishing the framework of legitimate decision rather than by deciding everything in advance. The 1950 Act established who could decide, through what offices, under what accountability, and it left the decisions themselves to the officers it created. The productive omissions are the evidence that the drafters understood the genre they were working in, and the agency's durability is the evidence that they worked in it well.

## The first years: from statute to institution

The interval between the May 1950 signing and the agency's emergence as a working funder of research illustrates the distinction this article has drawn throughout between a statute and an institution. The Act created the legal authority, defined the offices, and stated the purposes. Building the institution required appointing the board and the director, hiring the staff, organizing the divisions, designing the application forms, working out the procedures for judging proposals, and making the first awards. Each of these steps involved choices the statute had left open, and the accumulation of those choices produced the agency the world knows. The first years were therefore the period in which the productive omissions of the statute were filled, not by amendment but by administration.

The staffing choices were the most consequential of the early administrative decisions, because the kind of people the agency hired determined the kind of institution it became. The personnel provisions of the Act gave the director flexibility to hire scientific and technical staff outside the ordinary civil service constraints, and the agency used that flexibility to recruit working scientists into program-officer roles. The program officers became the indispensable intermediaries of the system, the officials who received proposals, selected reviewers, convened panels, and made funding recommendations. Their dual identity, as scientists who understood the research and as administrators who understood the agency, gave the foundation a culture distinct from both the universities and the ordinary bureaucracy. The statute had authorized the hiring flexibility. The agency's use of it created the profession of the science program officer.

The development of review procedures in the early years was the process by which peer review grew from improvisation into institution. The first proposals were judged by ad hoc consultations with outside experts, by correspondence and conversation rather than by formal panels. As the volume of proposals grew, the consultations were systematized into the panel system, with standing groups of experts meeting periodically to rank the proposals in their fields. The systematization was driven by necessity rather than by theory, since no other method could handle the volume fairly, but it had the effect of creating the community's own governance of funding decisions. The scientists who had lost the fight to choose the director found themselves choosing, in effect, which research would be supported, through a procedure the statute had never mentioned. The irony noted earlier in this article was thus produced in the first years, as administrative practice filled the statute's largest silence.

The early grant decisions set precedents that shaped the agency's culture for decades. The choice to fund investigator-initiated proposals, rather than to direct research toward agency-defined priorities, established the bottom-up character of the foundation's support. The choice to make awards to institutions on behalf of individual investigators, rather than to individuals directly, created the university-grant relationship that became the standard form. The choice to fund the best proposals regardless of geography, while maintaining the hortatory commitment to avoiding undue concentration, set the terms of the distribution debate that never ended. None of these choices was dictated by the statute. Each was an administrative interpretation of broad language, and each could have gone differently. The agency that emerged was one possible realization of the 1950 design among several, and its particular character reflected the judgments of the people who built it.

The fellowship programs of the early years had a parallel formative effect on the scientific community. The first national competitions for graduate fellowships created a visible meritocracy of young scientists, identified early and supported generously, and the fellows formed a cohort whose careers demonstrated the program's value. The visibility mattered politically as well as scientifically, because the fellowships gave the agency a constituency of grateful recipients in every congressional district, a diffuse but real base of support for the appropriations the agency needed. The statute's framers had understood fellowships as an investment in talent. The early years showed they were also an investment in legitimacy, creating the human evidence that federal support of science produced results.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the National Science Foundation Act of 1950 establish?

The Act established the National Science Foundation as an independent agency in the executive branch, consisting of a National Science Board and a Director. Public Law 81-507, signed May 10, 1950, directed the foundation to promote the progress of science by initiating and supporting basic scientific research, strengthening scientific research potential and science education at all levels, awarding scholarships and graduate fellowships, fostering the exchange of scientific information domestically and abroad, maintaining a register of scientific personnel, and establishing research divisions. The statute organized the agency around grants and contracts to universities, colleges, nonprofit institutions, and individuals, making it a patron of research rather than a performer of it. The governance design, a presidentially appointed director serving a six-year term alongside a twenty-four member presidentially appointed board with staggered terms, resolved the constitutional dispute that had produced a presidential veto of the 1947 version.

### Q: Why did it take five years to create the National Science Foundation?

The five years between the 1945 Bush report and the 1950 signing were consumed by a fight about control rather than about science. Competing bills disagreed on five themes: whether the director would be appointed by the agency's own board of scientists or by the President, whether the social sciences counted as science for funding purposes, whether awards had to be distributed geographically by formula, who would own patents on inventions produced with public money, and whether the agency should support applied research as well as basic inquiry. Senator Magnuson's bill embodied the Bush vision of scientists governing science, while Senator Kilgore's bill embodied democratic control with broad distribution. A 1946 compromise cleared the Senate but stalled, the 1947 bill was vetoed over the appointment question, and only after the 1948 election changed the congressional arithmetic did a bill emerge that could pass and be signed.

### Q: Did Truman veto an earlier National Science Foundation bill?

Yes. The 80th Congress passed S. 526, the National Science Foundation Act of 1947, and presented it to President Harry S. Truman on July 25, 1947. Truman vetoed it, objecting chiefly that the bill provided for the foundation's director to be appointed by the agency's own board rather than by the President. His veto message described the arrangement as implying a distinct lack of faith in democratic processes and warned that it would impede rather than promote the government's efforts to encourage scientific research. The objection was constitutional rather than programmatic: Truman did not dispute the mission of supporting basic research but insisted that the chief executive of an agency spending public money had to be accountable to the elected President. No override was attempted, and the bill died, forcing Congress to redesign the governance provisions before the successful 1950 enactment.

### Q: What was Science The Endless Frontier and the National Science Foundation?

Science The Endless Frontier was a report delivered to President Truman in July 1945 by Vannevar Bush, the wartime head of the Office of Scientific Research and Development, at the request originally made by President Roosevelt in November 1944. It argued that basic research is the pacemaker of technological progress, that private industry chronically underfunds it because its returns cannot be captured commercially, and that the federal government should create a permanent agency to support basic research through grants to universities while leaving project choice to scientists. The report proposed a National Research Foundation governed by a board of scientists who would appoint their own director. Its relationship to the enacted agency is often misunderstood: the 1950 statute rejected Bush's governance model after Truman's veto, omitted the social sciences and medicine, and softened his opposition to geographic distribution, so the report was the opening bid rather than the blueprint.

### Q: Is peer review required by the National Science Foundation Act?

No. Peer review appears nowhere in Public Law 81-507. The statute directs the foundation to award grants, fellowships, and contracts, establishes the board and the director, and enumerates the fields of support, but it prescribes no method for judging proposals and mentions no panels, referees, or review criteria. Peer review is an administrative practice the agency developed on its own in the years after 1950, when a small headquarters staff facing thousands of proposals began sending them to outside experts for evaluation and making awards on the basis of those recommendations. Because the practice is administrative rather than statutory, the agency has been able to modify its procedures over the decades without an act of Congress. The irony is that peer review embodies the Bush philosophy of scientific self-governance more purely than any provision of the statute, functioning as the community's compensation for losing the 1947 fight over appointing the director.

### Q: Why does the National Science Foundation not fund medical research?

The foundation does not fund medical research because the health research establishment already existed and was expanding when the foundation was created, and Congress saw no reason to build a second medical research system. The Public Health Service, with the National Institutes of Health as its research arm, was growing through the 1940s with its own grants and its own intramural laboratories. The division of labor was therefore settled before the new agency opened: the foundation would support the physical, biological, and engineering sciences and mathematics, while medical research remained with the health establishment. Notably, the 1945 Bush report had proposed a medical division within the new foundation, so the exclusion represents one of the points on which the enacted statute departed from the founding proposal. The American research funding system has been split between these two very differently structured agencies ever since, one a purely extramural patron organized by discipline, the other combining grants with government laboratories organized around health missions.

### Q: What is the National Science Board in the National Science Foundation Act?

The National Science Board is the twenty-four member governing body created by the 1950 Act to set the foundation's policies and advise its director. Each member is appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate for a six-year term, with the terms staggered so that a portion of the board turns over every two years. The members are required to be eminent in the basic sciences, engineering, or related fields, which in practice draws the board from the scientific and engineering communities. The board's role is advisory and policy-setting rather than executive: it establishes policies within the statutory framework and counsels the director, but it does not administer programs and, decisively, it does not choose the director. That limitation is the direct legacy of Truman's 1947 veto, which rejected a design giving the board the appointment power. The staggered terms give the board continuity across presidential administrations while keeping every member within the constitutional chain of appointment.

### Q: How did the Mansfield Amendment change National Science Foundation funding?

The Mansfield Amendment, section 203 of the fiscal year 1970 military authorization act (Public Law 91-121), enacted in 1969, limited Department of Defense funding of research to projects with a direct and apparent relationship to a specific military function or operation. Through the 1950s and 1960s the Pentagon had been a major patron of basic research at universities through the Office of Naval Research and its sister offices, supporting work in physics, mathematics, and electronics far removed from weapons. The amendment forced those offices to shed projects that could not meet the military-relationship test, and much of the displaced support migrated to the foundation, the only federal agency whose statutory mission was basic research without a mission qualifier. The shift substantially increased the foundation's share of federal basic research funding at universities and completed the division of labor the 1950 Act had implied: basic research without a mission requirement belonged to the foundation, while mission research belonged to the mission agencies.

### Q: Who appoints the director of the National Science Foundation under the 1950 Act?

Under the 1950 Act the director is appointed by the President, by and with the advice and consent of the Senate, for a term of six years. This provision was the direct answer to Truman's 1947 veto, which had rejected a bill letting the agency's own board choose the director. The appointment method places the foundation's chief executive squarely within the constitutional chain of command, so that the official spending public money on research is accountable to the elected President and, through confirmation, to the Senate. The six-year term, longer than the four-year presidential term, gives the director practical independence within that accountability: a director normally serves across presidential transitions and cannot be treated as a purely political appointee. The statute also creates a Deputy Director to assist in administration and to act during the director's absence. The design separates governance accountability, which runs to the President, from scientific judgment, which the statute leaves to the agency's expert processes.

### Q: How many members serve on the National Science Board and how long are their terms?

The 1950 Act sets the board's membership at twenty-four, with each member serving a six-year term. The terms are staggered so that a fraction of the membership turns over every two years, which gives the board continuity of institutional memory across presidential administrations. Every member is appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate, and the statute requires members to be eminent in the basic sciences, engineering, or other fields within the foundation's purview. The staggering mechanism means no president can remake the entire board at once, while the presidential appointment of every member keeps the body within the democratic chain of accountability that Truman's veto demanded. The board's functions are to establish the foundation's policies within the statutory framework and to advise the director. It does not administer programs, hire staff, or make individual grant decisions, and it does not choose the director, the power whose inclusion in the 1947 bill drew the veto.

### Q: What did Truman's 1947 veto message object to in the foundation bill?

The veto message objected chiefly to the provision letting the foundation's own board appoint the agency's director rather than the President. Truman described the arrangement as implying a distinct lack of faith in democratic processes and warned that it would impede rather than promote the government's efforts to encourage scientific research. The objection was structural and constitutional: the director of an agency exercising executive authority and spending public funds had to be accountable to the elected chief executive, and a director chosen by an unelected board of scientists broke that chain. The message also reflected a second concern about the concentration of unchecked power in expert hands, though the appointment question was the dispositive one. Truman did not oppose the foundation's mission, its grant programs, or its fellowships. Congress accepted the constitutional objection rather than attempting an override, and the 1950 statute was drafted to satisfy it by giving the President the appointment power with Senate confirmation alongside a board limited to policy and advisory functions.

### Q: Did the 1950 Act cover the social sciences?

The 1950 Act did not name the social sciences among the fields the foundation was directed to support. The statute enumerated the mathematical, physical, biological, and engineering sciences, and authorized fellowships, grants, and divisions in those fields. The omission was deliberate and reflected the legislative judgment, following the Bush report's own ambivalence, that the social sciences remained contested as science worthy of federal support. Social scientists had testified for inclusion during the 1945 hearings, and the Kilgore bill's broader definitions were more hospitable to them, but the enacted text left them out. The foundation in practice developed limited social science programs well before any statutory change, yet express congressional authorization waited until the 1968 amendments, Public Law 90-407, which explicitly authorized foundation activities in the social sciences along with support for applied research. The two-stage resolution, omission in 1950 and express authorization in 1968, shows how the statute's silences functioned as deferrals rather than permanent exclusions.

### Q: How did the 1950 Act handle patents on inventions from funded research?

The 1950 Act left patent policy to the foundation to work out through its contracts and grants rather than writing a statutory rule. Congress gave the agency discretion to set the terms on which inventions produced with public money would be owned, licensed, or dedicated to the public, instead of choosing between the competing positions in the five-year debate. The Kilgore camp had favored government retention of patent rights on the ground that the public paid for the research, while the scientific-autonomy camp favored flexibility for the agency and its grantees. Because the positions were irreconcilable and the bill needed to pass, deferral was the only available solution. The delegation meant patent questions were fought again in every era through agency regulation and executive policy, until later legislation reversed the default for university research. The 1950 approach illustrates the statute's characteristic technique: settle the constitutional questions in the text and leave the policy questions to administration.

### Q: What did the 1950 Act say about geographic distribution of research awards?

Section 3(b) of the Act made it one of the foundation's objectives to strengthen basic research and education in the sciences throughout the United States, including its territories and possessions, and to avoid undue concentration of such research and education. The language was hortatory rather than mandatory: it stated an objective without defining undue concentration, without prescribing any formula, and without creating an enforceable requirement. The provision was a compromise between the Kilgore camp, which wanted mandatory distribution to build scientific capacity across the nation and counteract centralization in established institutions, and the Bush camp, which opposed formulas as pork-barrel science that would force the funding of mediocrity. Each side got something: the principle of geographic equity was written into law, but no mechanism could compel it. The vagueness is the reason the distribution debate continued for decades, with legislators from underfunded regions pressing the agency to do more and the agency defending merit-based award decisions.

### Q: What fellowship and scholarship programs did the 1950 statute authorize?

The 1950 Act authorized the foundation to award scholarships and graduate fellowships for study and research in the sciences and engineering, creating a national program for the training of new researchers. The fellowships were conceived as portable support for individuals rather than block grants to institutions, so a talented student could pursue training wherever the best opportunities lay. The provisions answered the fourth question Roosevelt had posed to Vannevar Bush in 1944, how the nation should develop its scientific talent for the postwar era, and they made the foundation a patron of scientific careers and of scientific projects. The fellowship programs became one of the agency's most durable legacies, with generations of researchers trained under their support. The scholarship authorities extended the pipeline to undergraduate study. Together with the research grants, the training programs expressed the statute's understanding of scientific capacity as a system: funding investigations while simultaneously cultivating the investigators.

### Q: What was the difference between the Kilgore bill and the Magnuson bill?

The two 1945 bills embodied opposite philosophies of democratic control. Senator Kilgore's S. 1297 proposed a publicly appointed director chosen by the President and advised by a board, required broad geographic distribution of funding, and defined research and development expansively to include practical application, industrial studies, and pilot plants. Senator Magnuson's S. 1285, drafted in consultation with Vannevar Bush, proposed a foundation led by a publicly appointed board of scientists that would select, direct, and supervise the director, supported basic research in the mathematical, physical, and biological sciences without distribution formulas, and left project choice to the scientific community. In short, Kilgore trusted democratic institutions to govern science with public money, while Magnuson trusted scientists to govern themselves. The 1950 statute split the difference on governance, adopting presidential appointment of the director with a policy-setting board, while deferring or softening the other disagreements.

### Q: When was the National Science Foundation Act signed and what is its public law number?

President Harry S. Truman signed the National Science Foundation Act on May 10, 1950, during the second session of the 81st Congress. Its public law number is Public Law 81-507, and its Statutes at Large citation is 64 Stat. 149. The signing came nearly five years after Vannevar Bush delivered Science The Endless Frontier in July 1945 and followed Truman's 1947 veto of the earlier S. 526, making the 1950 measure the second legislative attempt at the same institution. The Act was originally codified at 42 U.S.C. sections 1861 and following, with the establishment provision at section 1861 and the functions provision at section 1862. Later amendments were layered onto this numbering, including the 1968 reauthorization that added the social sciences and applied research. Readers consulting the current code should remember that it reflects decades of amendment; the 1950 text as signed was narrower in its enumerated fields and silent on matters the agency later developed by practice.

### Q: How long is the NSF director's term of office under the statute?

The 1950 Act gives the director a term of six years. The length was a deliberate compromise between democratic accountability and institutional continuity. Because the term exceeds the four-year presidential term, a director normally serves across presidential transitions and cannot be treated as a political appointee serving at the pleasure of the current administration. The security of the fixed term gives the director practical freedom to make scientific judgments, including funding decisions, without calculating their political reception, while the presidential appointment with Senate confirmation preserves the constitutional chain of accountability that Truman's veto demanded. The six-year term also parallels the staggered six-year terms of the twenty-four board members, so the agency's leadership always includes appointees of more than one president. The term length is one of the provisions that made presidential appointment acceptable to the scientific community, which had fought for a board-chosen director and lost.

### Q: Did the 1950 Act authorize applied research or only basic research?

The 1950 Act directed the foundation to initiate and support basic scientific research, placing the agency on the basic-research side of the era's most contested definitional line. The choice followed the Bush report's argument that the government's proper role was to fund fundamental inquiry whose returns industry could not capture, leaving applied development to private firms. The Kilgore bill had blurred the distinction with an expansive definition of research and development reaching into practical application and pilot plants, but the enacted text did not adopt that breadth. The limitation shaped the agency's culture permanently, orienting it toward universities and investigator-initiated inquiry rather than directed development programs. Express authority to support applied research waited until the 1968 amendments, Public Law 90-407, which specifically authorized it alongside the social sciences. The two-stage history shows the 1950 statute as a deliberately narrow charter whose later expansions were enacted by Congress rather than improvised by the agency.

### Q: Why is the National Science Board's role advisory rather than executive under the Act?

The board's advisory rather than executive role is the direct legacy of Truman's 1947 veto. The vetoed bill had given the board the power to appoint the director, making the board the agency's governing principal and shutting the President out of the chain of accountability. Truman rejected that design as incompatible with democratic processes, and the 1950 statute was written to satisfy his objection. Under the enacted design the director, appointed by the President with Senate confirmation, is the chief executive who administers the agency, while the twenty-four member board establishes policies within the statutory framework and advises the director. The division mirrors corporate governance, with the board setting direction and the chief executive executing it, and it gave each side of the five-year fight something essential: the scientific community got a board of eminent scientists shaping policy, while the democratic-control camp got a director answerable to elected authority. The line between advising and choosing is the constitutional heart of the statute.
