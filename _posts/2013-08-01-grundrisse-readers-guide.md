The Grundrisse is the strangest major text in the Marxist canon, because it is a major text that its author never made into one. Spread across seven notebooks written in the winter of 1857 and the spring of 1858, running to several hundred densely covered pages, it is the record of Karl Marx thinking his way through the whole of political economy at speed, for an audience of one. He was not composing a book. He was clearing his own ground, testing formulations, arguing with the economists he had spent a decade reading, and drafting the architecture of a critique he had promised publishers, in one form or another, for years. The notebooks were put away when the immediate project changed shape, and they stayed unpublished for the rest of his life and for more than half a century after his death. When they finally appeared, first in Moscow around 1939 and then, after a wartime silence, in a Berlin edition in the middle of the 1950s, they altered the terms on which Marx himself was read. In English, in the translation by Martin Nicolaus published in the early 1970s, they altered those terms again, and more widely.

That double life, private manuscript and posthumous event, is what a reader's guide has to organise. The Grundrisse defeats the two approaches readers instinctively bring to it. The first approach is reverence: because the notebooks are Marx at full stretch, covering money, capital, labour, pre-capitalist history and the future of machinery in one sustained effort, the reverent reader settles down to read them from the first page to the last, as one reads Capital. The second approach is extraction: because a handful of pages, above all the passage on machinery and the general intellect, have become famous, the extracting reader goes straight to those pages, lifts them out, and treats them as a freestanding prophecy. Both approaches misunderstand the object in front of them. The reverent approach ignores the fact that a working notebook rewards sampling and punishes endurance, since long stretches consist of Marx arguing a position into the ground, repeating himself with variations, or following a category through exchanges that his later published work handled in a tenth of the space. The extracting approach ignores the fact that a passage inside a notebook means something different from the same passage inside a book, because in a notebook nothing has been finally endorsed, and the sentences around the famous ones often qualify, complicate or quietly abandon the claim the extractors celebrate.

This guide is built on a single discipline, which can be given a name at once because names make cautions portable. The notebook warning holds that the Grundrisse is a record of thinking rather than a statement of results, so a position found in it may be one Marx was trying out and later abandoned, and citations from it carry a different evidential weight from citations of published work. Everything that follows serves that warning. The guide gives the manuscript's history, because the history explains the warning. It gives a map of the three passages that carry almost all of the text's modern influence, because a reader with limited time should go where the influence is and know why it is there. It marks the status of every famous excerpt, because status is the thing extraction destroys. And it sets out the live scholarly disputes about what the notebooks are, draft, quarry or alternative architecture, because the honest answer is that the dispute is genuine, the evidence cuts in more than one direction, and a reader who knows the dispute reads every page of the Grundrisse more accurately than a reader who has been handed a verdict.

A boundary should be drawn before the detail begins. This guide owns the manuscript itself: its character, its chronology, its internal geography and its proper use. It does not expound the concept of the general intellect for its own sake, since that concept has its own treatment elsewhere in this series, and it does not retell the argument about the young Marx and the mature Marx, although the Grundrisse's publication history bears on that argument at its most sensitive point. Where those neighbouring questions touch the notebooks, they are touched and linked, not settled. The governing question here is narrower and more practical. Given a manuscript of several hundred pages that nobody was meant to read, which parts should a reader actually read, in what order, with what expectations, and under what cautions? That is the question a reader's guide exists to answer, and it is the question the academic commentary, for all its learning, tends to leave unasked.

## What kind of object the Grundrisse is

Genre is destiny for a manuscript, and the Grundrisse's genre is the private working notebook. Marx filled notebooks throughout his life. He excerpted the books he read, copied passages, interpolated comments, drafted letters he never sent and plans he never executed, and the notebooks of 1857 and 1858 belong to that lifelong workshop practice even though their scale dwarfs the usual excerpt book. They are written in the hand of a man arguing with himself in real time. Sentences begin, swerve, resume. Formulations are proposed, tightened, proposed again in a different vocabulary a page later. Whole trains of thought are pursued because they might lead somewhere, and the reader can watch some of them lead nowhere in particular and be dropped without ceremony. None of this is a defect. It is what thinking looks like before an editor, even a self-editor, has decided what the conclusions are.

The comparison that clarifies the genre is not with other books by Marx but with the laboratory notebook of a scientist. A laboratory notebook records trials, including the failed ones, and its evidential value lies precisely in showing the path taken rather than merely the destination announced. A result copied from a laboratory notebook into a published paper has been selected, repeated and defended. A result still sitting in the notebook has been noticed. The Grundrisse stands to Capital roughly as the laboratory record stands to the paper, with the complication, examined later in this guide, that the relationship between these particular notebooks and that particular book is itself disputed. But the genre point holds under every version of the dispute. Private working papers show a mind in motion. Published works show a mind that has stopped moving on that question long enough to be held responsible for a position. Confusing the two genres produces a characteristic family of errors, and the Grundrisse's modern reception exhibits the whole family.

One error is over-endorsement, treating any striking sentence in the notebooks as Marx's doctrine. Marx in 1857 and 1858 tries formulations the way a locksmith tries keys, and some of the keys did not open anything he later wanted opened. Another error is the opposite, dismissal, treating the notebooks as juvenilia or juvenilia's mirror image, the unbuttoned scribblings of a great man, interesting only to biographers. That error is worse, because the notebooks in fact contain analyses of money, of the capital relation and of pre-capitalist forms that are fuller than anything Marx published in his lifetime, and in a few places fuller than Capital itself. A third error is ventriloquism, in which a later school finds its own programme prefigured in a notebook passage and presents the passage as the programme's charter. The passage is real. The charter status is conferred, not found, and the conferral usually requires detaching the passage from the qualifications standing on its own pages. A reader equipped with the notebook warning can commit none of these three errors in good faith, which is why the warning opens this guide rather than closing it.

The sheer physical character of the manuscript reinforces the genre point. Seven notebooks, conventionally numbered with Roman numerals, containing a continuous but unsectioned torrent of analysis that editors have had to divide, title and order for print. Marx gave almost no headings. The modern reader who opens the Nicolaus translation meets a text that an editorial apparatus has made navigable, with contents pages and part titles that the manuscript itself does not supply in that form. This matters more than it may seem. A book's divisions are part of its argument, since they announce what the author considered a unit. A notebook's divisions in print are an editor's kindness, and a reader who attributes architectural significance to them is reading the editor, not Marx. The three passages map later in this guide uses locations a reader can actually find, the introduction, the section on forms preceding capitalist production, and the machinery pages in the later notebooks, precisely because those locations are recognisable in any serious edition, while finer subdivisions vary.

### Was the Grundrisse meant to be published?

No. It is a set of seven private notebooks written across 1857 and 1858 in which Marx worked out his critique of political economy for himself. It was first published in Moscow around 1939, became generally available in the 1950s, and appeared in English in the 1970s.

The negative deserves its evidence, since the question matters and confident answers circulate in both directions. Marx certainly intended to write and publish a critique of political economy; he had intended it since the middle of the 1840s, announced it repeatedly, and signed contracts for versions of it. The notebooks were raw material for that intention in the way scaffolding is material for a building. But scaffolding is not the building, and there is no sign that Marx regarded these pages as a text anyone else should read. He did not give them a title. The name by which the world knows them, Grundrisse der Kritik der politischen Okonomie, Outlines of the Critique of Political Economy, belongs to the editorial history of the manuscript rather than to any act of presentation by its author. He did not revise them for a printer, did not correct them, did not write a preface for them, and when he did publish the first instalment of his critique, the Contribution of 1859, he worked the material into a different and far more disciplined form. The famous introduction of 1857, the most finished-looking stretch in the notebooks, he deliberately withheld, judging that it would be confusing to anticipate results that still had to be proved. An author who suppresses his own introduction because it runs ahead of the proof is an author who knows his papers are not a book.

## The winter of the notebooks: crisis, urgency and self-clarification

The circumstances of composition explain the manuscript's peculiar energy. In the autumn of 1857 a financial crisis that had begun in the United States crossed the Atlantic and struck European commerce, banking and industry. Marx, who had been waiting for such a crisis for years and who earned money in these years partly by writing on economic affairs for the New York press, believed he might be watching the opening of a revolutionary period. His correspondence with Engels from these months has the tempo of a man working against a deadline set by history itself. He wrote, by his own account, working madly through the nights, because he wanted the outline of his economic argument clear before the storm broke, in case the storm gave the argument an audience and a use. The notebooks are the product of that urgency. Their speed is in the prose, in the pile-ups of clauses, in the willingness to follow an analysis of money for dozens of pages without pausing to make it elegant.

Urgency shaped content as well as pace. A writer who believes events may overtake him does not polish a single volume towards perfection. He tries to get the whole system down, money, capital, landed property, wage labour, the state, foreign trade, the world market, in outline at least, so that the framework exists even if the elaboration must wait. The Grundrisse's famous scope, its reach from the most abstract categories to the history of pre-capitalist societies and forward to machinery and free time, comes from this attempt to fix the whole architecture in one campaign. Later, the campaign's product was mined for smaller, publishable undertakings: the Contribution of 1859, and then, across the following decade, Capital. But in the winter itself, the notebooks were the critique, entire in intention and fragmentary in finish, and reading them with the crisis in mind explains both why they cover so much and why so much of what they cover is covered once, rapidly, and never revisited.

Self-clarification is the second circumstance, and the more important one for readers. Marx used writing as his instrument of thought, and those who have studied his working methods describe a man who understood a subject by drafting it, whose manuscripts are therefore not records of conclusions reached elsewhere but the very scene of the reaching. In such a writer's papers, the distinction between opinion and experiment collapses. A paragraph may state his settled view. The next may state the view he is testing the settled view against, in its strongest form, without a signpost to say which is which. Readers of published Marx are protected by his ferocious self-editing. Readers of the Grundrisse are unprotected, and the first service a guide can perform is to say so plainly, with the consequence spelled out: quotation from these notebooks, detached from the surrounding movement of trial and correction, is the least reliable form of Marx quotation there is, and the most commonly practised, because the notebooks are so quotable. The notebook warning is the name this guide gives to the discipline that fact demands.

There is a third circumstance, quieter than crisis and method, and it is money. Marx in 1857 was poor, dependent on Engels's subsidies and on journalism, harassed by creditors, and aware that his promised book was also his family's economic hope. The notebooks were written by a man in his fortieth year who had been promising the world, and his publisher, and himself, a finished critique of political economy for a decade and a half, and who had produced instead a mountain of excerpts and drafts. The Grundrisse can be read as the moment the mountain began to be organised. That biographical pressure gave the work its comprehensiveness, because the organiser needed the whole design visible at once, and its roughness, because he had neither the time nor the temperament to pretend the organisation was the finished building. A guide should say this without sentimentality, because it returns the manuscript from the realm of pure intellect, where reverence accumulates, to the realm of work, where it belongs.

## Eighty years in a drawer: the publication chronology

The manuscript's journey from desk to public text took the better part of a century, and the journey is not a footnote to its meaning. It is a large part of its meaning, because a text that arrives late arrives into arguments already formed without it, and the Grundrisse arrived into the most consequential argument about Marx's development at exactly the moment when its evidence could do the most damage to the argument's standard form. The broad chronology is settled, even where details of the publishing history reward care. Marx died in 1883 with the notebooks among his papers. They passed with his literary estate through Engels's keeping and then into the custody of the German social democratic movement's archives and its custodians. For decades they were effectively invisible: known to exist, occasionally mentioned by those who had charge of the papers, read by almost nobody.

Publication came in Moscow at the turn of the 1940s, from the institute charged with editing Marx and Engels's collected works, in an edition produced under wartime's approach and then overtaken by war itself. An edition printed in small numbers, in a country about to be invaded, at a moment when the scholarly world was being scattered, could not do the work of availability, and it did not. The Grundrisse existed in print and remained, for practical scholarly purposes, unpublished. General availability waited until the middle of the 1950s, when a Berlin edition put the text into circulation among readers in both German states and beyond, and the first wave of serious engagement began. Readers in the West who worked through it in those years found themselves holding a Marx they had not been prepared for, dialectical, Hegelian in vocabulary, saturated with the language of alienation, and writing at full philosophical stretch in 1857 and 1858, which is to say in the period the ruling periodisation assigned to the mature economist.

The English arrival came in stages, and the stages matter for understanding which parts of the text influenced the Anglophone world first. The section on pre-capitalist economic formations, the Formen, circulated in English before the whole, in an edition prepared by Eric Hobsbawm in the middle of the 1960s, so that historians and anthropologists met that part of the notebooks as a semi-detached text with its own introduction and its own readership. The complete English translation, by Martin Nicolaus, appeared in the early 1970s in the Penguin Marx library, and it is difficult to overstate what that volume did. It placed the entire manuscript, in a readable and energetic English version, into paperback circulation at the moment when universities on several continents were expanding their Marx teaching and when new movements were ransacking the tradition for resources. The Grundrisse's modern fame is inseparable from that conjunction of a complete text and a hungry readership. Whole literatures, on method, on machinery, on pre-capitalist history, on the continuity of Marx's thought, reorganised themselves around a book their central figure had never published.

A reader should register what this chronology does to authority. The canon of Marx's works, as it stood for most of the twentieth century's first half, was a canon of published texts plus the posthumous volumes of Capital that Engels edited, and interpretations built on that canon had the shape the canon gave them. The Grundrisse did not merely add a text to the shelf. It supplied evidence about the workshop in which the published texts were made, evidence of a kind that can revalue everything on the shelf, because a writer's drafts show which of his published formulations were hard-won, which were compressions, and which problems he considered central that his published architecture pushed to the margins. Late arrival therefore gave the manuscript a peculiar power, the power of the discovered diary, and with the power came the temptation to treat it as the secret truth behind the public work. Resist that temptation, says the notebook warning, in its second form: belatedness confers fascination, not seniority. The notebooks are not the real Marx finally unmasked. They are Marx thinking, which is a different and, properly handled, a more valuable thing.

### How did availability arrive in stages?

Availability came in stages spread across four decades, and each stage changed which readers could use the text. A Moscow edition appeared around 1939 but was overtaken by war and reached few scholars. A Berlin edition in the middle of the 1950s then made the notebooks generally usable in German for the first time.

The staged arrival explains a confusion readers often bring to the secondary literature, which is that writers of different generations seem to be discussing different books under one title. A historian formed by the Hobsbawm edition of the Formen met a Grundrisse centred on pre-capitalist societies, communal property and the paths by which those societies dissolved. A philosopher formed by the Nicolaus volume met a Grundrisse centred on method and on the dialectic of capital and labour. An activist formed by extracts in pamphlets and journals met a Grundrisse centred on a few pages about machinery. Each met something real, and each generalisation about the text's character should be checked against the edition that formed it. The lesson generalises beyond this manuscript. Availability is never a single event for a large text. It is a sequence, and the sequence writes itself into the reception.

## Why the chronology damaged the break thesis

No interpretive dispute about Marx has organised more teaching, and more polemic, than the dispute over whether his work divides into a young, humanist, philosophical phase and a mature, scientific, economic phase, with a break between them located in the middle of the 1840s. This guide does not retell that dispute, which has its own full treatment in the article on [young marx vs mature marx](/2011/06/01/young-marx-vs-mature-marx/), but the Grundrisse's place in it belongs here, because it is the cleanest illustration in the whole Marx literature of how publication chronology governs interpretation. The break thesis, in its influential philosophical form, was constructed at a time when the notebooks of 1857 and 1858 were effectively unavailable to its constructor and to most of its audience. The canon on which the periodisation rested contained the early manuscripts, themselves late arrivals, and the published mature works. It did not contain the document that now sits squarely on the boundary's mature side while speaking, page after page, in the vocabulary the periodisation assigned to the young side.

The vocabulary is the evidence. The Grundrisse is saturated with the language of alienation and objectification, with the worker's activity confronting him as an alien power, with wealth measured against human development, with Hegel's logic not merely mentioned but used, worked with, as the instrument of the analysis. A reader who accepts a sharp break must explain this saturation away, as residue, as survival, as a vocabulary retained while its meaning was discarded, and such explanations exist and are argued with skill. A reader who rejects the break points to the notebooks and asks why the explanation-away should be preferred to the plain verdict that the mature Marx remained, in method and in moral vocabulary, continuous with the young one. The point for a reader's guide is not which reader is right. The point is that the dispute could not even be posed in its modern form until the Berlin and English editions put the counter-evidence into general circulation. An argument about a man's development had been conducted, necessarily and innocently, on an archive missing its most awkward document, and the document's arrival is therefore the standing example of a principle this whole series returns to: interpretations of Marx are hostages to the state of his papers, and the state of his papers has changed repeatedly within living scholarship.

Martin Nicolaus, in his foreword to the English edition, drew the moral in the form that made it famous, presenting the Grundrisse as the missing link in the Marx canon, the text that lets a reader watch the economist and the philosopher in the same room at the same desk. That presentation did work beyond the dispute itself. It taught a generation of readers to treat the notebooks as the key to Capital, the place where the published work's intentions stand revealed. There is truth in the teaching, and danger in it, and the danger is the notebook warning again. A missing link in an argument about continuity is evidence. A master key to another book is a licence, and licences get used carelessly. The sections of this guide devoted to the relation between the Grundrisse and Capital take up the question in its full scholarly form, quarry, draft or alternative architecture, because the missing-link presentation, left unexamined, smuggles one answer to that question in under the cover of chronology.

It is worth pausing on the innocence of the original construction, because polemic about the break often forgets it. Scholars who built a periodisation without the Grundrisse were not suppressing evidence. They were doing what scholarship does, generalising from the archive that exists. The manuscript's custodians were not conspirators either. The story sometimes told, that the notebooks were suppressed because they embarrassed an orthodoxy, reverses the actual sequence of obstacles, which were the ordinary, formidable obstacles of a vast Nachlass, an editor's century of competing priorities, war, and the economics of scholarly publishing. Marx himself began the delay by not publishing the notebooks, and every subsequent delay has mundane causes sufficient to explain it. A guide should insist on the mundane story, not from pedantry but because the suppression story does interpretive work, casting the text as forbidden truth, and forbidden truth is read with a credulity that working notebooks, of all objects, cannot bear.

## The notebook warning, stated in full

Every reader's guide makes one claim it would defend against all comers, and this guide's claim has been named since the opening pages. The notebook warning states that the Grundrisse is a record of thinking rather than a statement of results, so a position found in it may be one Marx was trying out and later abandoned, and citations from it carry a different evidential weight from citations of published work. The claim sounds modest. Its consequences are not, because a large share of the manuscript's modern use proceeds as if the warning were false. Pages are quoted as doctrine. Formulations are assigned to Marx as positions, without reference to what the same notebooks say twenty pages later or what Capital says a decade later. Schools build programmes on passages whose author gave them no public standing whatsoever. The warning, taken seriously, does not forbid any of this material from being used. It re-prices the use. A notebook passage can show what Marx was capable of thinking, what problems pressed on him, what solutions he entertained. It cannot, by itself, show what Marx concluded, and arguments that need conclusions must go where conclusions were published and defended.

Three distinctions give the warning its working parts. The first is between exploration and endorsement. In the notebooks Marx explores constantly, following a category, an opponent's argument or a historical possibility as far as it will go, sometimes further than his settled view would sanction, because exploration requires inhabiting the position being tested. The exploratory register is recognisable once a reader looks for it: the hypothetical constructions, the chains of suppositions, the passages where a tendency is followed to its limit in order to see the limit. The second distinction is between evidential and canonical use, a distinction the strongest objection to notebook scholarship will force later in this guide. Evidential use treats the manuscript as testimony about a mind and a project. Canonical use treats it as scripture, a source of propositions whose authority settles disputes. The warning permits the first and refuses the second. The third distinction is between the manuscript and its extracts. Almost nobody outside scholarship meets the Grundrisse whole. It is met in anthologies, in quotation, in a famous section detached for separate publication, and each act of detaching silently upgrades the detached pages, because a passage presented alone presents itself as complete. The warning therefore applies with double force to extracts. The Fragment on Machines, the most extracted passage in the entire Marx corpus, is the standing case, and this guide returns to it with the warning in hand.

A comparison may fix the idea. Philosophers who work on Wittgenstein live with an enormous Nachlass of notebooks and typescripts beside a tiny shelf of published work, and their discipline has evolved conventions for citing the unpublished material that mark its status rather than disguising it. Historians of science handle Darwin's notebooks the same way, quoting the transmutation notebooks as evidence of a theory being born, never as the theory's statement, for which the Origin exists. Marx scholarship possesses the same structure of materials and has not always observed the same conventions, partly because the political stakes attached to Marx's authority made canonical use tempting to friends and enemies alike. The friend who finds a congenial sentence in the notebooks can present it as the master's true voice, freed from the compromises of publication. The enemy who finds an uncongenial one can present it as the master's confession, the position he lacked the courage to print. Both presentations commit the same fallacy in opposite colours, and the notebook warning is simply the fallacy named.

It should be said, against a possible misunderstanding, that the warning cuts the authority of the notebooks without cutting their interest. In several respects the record of thinking is more instructive than the statement of results could be. The notebooks show Marx's method in motion, show the Hegelian instruments being applied to economic material in real time, show historical erudition and economic analysis informing each other on the same page, and show the plan of the critique expanding and contracting under the pressure of the material. A reader who wants to understand how Capital became possible will learn things from the Grundrisse that Capital itself, by its finished nature, conceals. The concealment is what finished works do. They hide their scaffolding. The notebooks are scaffolding left standing, and scaffolding is exactly what a student of the building should study, provided the student never mistakes it for the building's facade. That proviso, in one sentence, is the whole of the notebook warning, and the whole art of reading this manuscript is the art of honouring it page by page.

## Inside the seven notebooks: a working geography

A reader opening the complete text needs a geography before a guidebook, and the geography is simpler than the bulk suggests. After the introduction, which stands apart in character and in Marx's own estimation, the notebooks take up money, then capital in its long central movement, within which the discussions of pre-capitalist forms and of machinery occur as great internal expeditions, and the whole is surrounded by material that reaches towards the topics of the larger plan Marx carried in his head: landed property, wage labour, the state, trade between nations, the world market. Not all of those topics receive their expedition. The plan, which Marx sketched in letters and in the notebooks themselves, envisaged a critique in several books, and the Grundrisse is the general ground-clearing for the whole design rather than the execution of any single book within it. This is the first geographical fact, and it explains the text's heterogeneity. A ground-clearing operation touches everything and finishes nothing, and a reader who expects the finished proportions of the plan will mistake ground-clearing for chaos.

The treatment of money, which opens the main sequence, shows the method of the whole. Marx works through the functions of money and the contradictions of monetary systems at a length his published work never repeated, arguing with the monetary theorists and with Proudhon's followers as he goes. Much of it is the sound of a man making sure of his foundations, and most general readers can sample it profitably rather than inhabit it. The long central analysis of capital is the manuscript's heartland: the transformation of money into capital, the confrontation of capital with labour, the circulation process, the turnover of capital, profit and interest, all developed in a continuous, surging exposition that sometimes anticipates Capital's arguments closely and sometimes diverges from them in order, in emphasis and in substance. It is here that the reader sees the later book being made possible, page by page, and here also that the divergences accumulate which the scholars of the relation between the two texts argue over. The expeditions interrupt the heartland, or rather rise out of it. The discussion of forms preceding capitalist production grows from a question the capital analysis needed, about the historical presuppositions of the capital relation itself. The machinery pages grow from the analysis of the production process and the tendency of capital to press labour time down while resting its whole measure of value on labour time. Nothing in this manuscript is an ornament. Even its most famous detour is a load-bearing detour, which is the geographical answer to the extractor who supposes the famous pages can be lifted without loss.

Two features of the terrain deserve warning signs of their own. The first is repetition with development. Marx in notebook mode circles back. A distinction established in one notebook returns in a later one, restated, applied to new material, sometimes subtly changed. A continuous reader feels the circling as redundancy. It is better understood as the manuscript's real structure of progress, spiral rather than linear, and a strategic reader exploits it, taking the later, more developed statement of a point as the one to annotate while using the earlier statement to see what the point cost to reach. The second feature is the presence of long engagements with other writers, economists and socialists whose names meant more in 1857 than they mean to most readers of the present day. These engagements are not digressions in Marx's economy of work. Refuting a monetary reformer or dissecting a Ricardian socialist was how the categories got tested. But a reader is entitled to move through the polemical thickets at speed, and every honest guide should say so, because the pretence that every page of a working notebook is equally precious is a form of reverence that serves nobody except those who profit from the text's reputation for difficulty.

The famous introduction stands before all of this, dated to the late summer of 1857, slightly before the notebooks proper, and written in a different key: compressed, programmatic, philosophical. It discusses production in general, the relation of production to distribution, exchange and consumption, the method of political economy, and the vexed relation between artistic flourishing and material development, with the example of Greek art that has teased readers ever since. Marx decided not to publish it, on the ground stated earlier, that anticipating results before they are proved confuses readers, and he filed it with the notebooks. Its position at the front of printed editions is an accident of chronology turned into a temptation, because a reader naturally begins there and finds a methodological manifesto of great brilliance, then turns the page into the money chapter and wonders where the manifesto went. The introduction is best treated as the first of the three passages this guide maps, read slowly and reread after the others, and its suppression by its own author is the notebook warning's most elegant proof: even the most finished pages in this manuscript were pages Marx judged unready to stand in public as his position.

## The three passages map

The guide's findable artifact collects the strategy into one view. Three passages carry almost all of the Grundrisse's modern influence, and the map below locates each, states its argument, names the debate it feeds, and gives an honest reading time for a first careful pass. A fourth row covers the remainder, because a strategy that hides the bulk of the book would be a fraud on the reader it claims to help.

| Passage | Where it sits | What it argues | Debate it feeds | Reading time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The introduction on method | Before the notebooks proper, written in 1857 | Political economy must rise from abstract determinations to the concrete, which is a concentration of many determinations, not a starting point | The method of political economy and the Hegel question in Marx's procedure | An evening, then a rereading |
| The Formen on pre-capitalist forms | Inside the capital analysis, as its historical presupposition | Communal forms of property and labour precede capitalism in several distinct types whose dissolution prepares the capital relation | Modes of production, historical paths, and whether history has a single track | Two or three sittings |
| The Fragment on Machines | In the later notebooks, inside the analysis of the production process | Capital presses necessary labour down as machinery and general knowledge become the direct forces of production, straining labour time as the measure of wealth | Automation, knowledge work, and the future of the labour theory of value | One long sitting, with its context |
| The remainder of the notebooks | The money chapters and the long capital analysis surrounding the three passages | The working-out of money, capital, circulation and profit from which the published critique was quarried | The relation between the Grundrisse and Capital, pursued by scholars rather than general readers | Weeks, and only for a purpose |

Read the map as an allocation of attention, not as a canon within the canon. It says where a first reader's hours earn the highest return, and it says so on evidence: these are the passages the scholarship cites, the debates quote, the translations and extracts reproduce. It also says, in the fourth row, what honesty requires about the other several hundred pages. They are the workshop floor. A reader with a research purpose may need to spend weeks on that floor, looking for the moment a category changed, and such work is honourable and sometimes indispensable. A reader without that purpose who attempts the floor out of duty will finish less of the three passages, understand them less well, and quite possibly abandon the manuscript altogether, which is the worst outcome a guide can produce. The map exists to prevent that outcome. It also exists to be argued with, since allocation of attention is a judgement, and readers who finish the three passages and feel the pull of the heartland chapters on circulation will find the map has done its work precisely by being outgrown.

A note on the reading times is owed, because false precision in such matters is a minor dishonesty. The times assume a reader with some economics or some Marx already in hand, reading with a pencil, at the pace the prose demands when it is arguing rather than coasting. The introduction is short and takes an evening because it must be read twice to be read once. The Formen reward slowness for a different reason, the density of historical forms per page. The Fragment is brief, and its sitting is long because the guide's strategy insists on the pages around it, for reasons the next sections give in full. Any reader who moves faster is not failing the map. The map is a default for the uncommitted hour, nothing more, and its times are there to make the commitment feel possible, which is the real obstacle the manuscript sets most readers.

## First passage: the introduction and the ascent to the concrete

The introduction of 1857 is the most read stretch of the Grundrisse after the machinery pages, and the most likely to be the first thing a new reader meets, since editions place it at the front. Its argument, in the space a guide can give it, runs as follows. Political economy, Marx observes, had standardly begun from the concrete, from population, and proceeded by analysis towards ever thinner abstractions, then stopped, leaving a chaotic conception of the whole. The scientifically correct method reverses the return journey. Having reached the simplest determinations by analysis, thought travels back, rising from the abstract to the concrete, and the concrete is now reproduced in thought as what his famous formulation calls a concentration of many determinations, a unity of the diverse. Population, returned to by this route, is no longer an empty word but a rich totality whose classes, wage labour, capital, exchange and money are understood as its articulated moments. The distinction between the order of inquiry, which moves from the concrete given in perception down to abstractions, and the order of presentation, which rises from abstractions back to a concrete now comprehended, is drawn in these pages with a clarity Marx never surpassed, and the fuller treatment of what that distinction does to the reading of Capital belongs to the comparison of [hegel dialectic vs marx dialectic](/2012/08/15/hegel-dialectic-vs-marx-dialectic/), where the method of presentation is the home topic.

Three cautions belong to this passage, and a guide earns its keep by attaching them. The first is the suppression point already made: Marx withheld the introduction because he judged its method talk premature without the results to cash it, so the passage should be read as a worker's memo on procedure, not as a proclamation. The second caution concerns the Hegelian atmosphere. The introduction's language of the concrete and the abstract, and its insistence that the real concrete remains outside thought even while thought appropriates it, has been fought over by every school that fights over Marx and Hegel. Readers who take the passage as proof that the mature Marx was simply a Hegelian must reckon with its explicit warning against confusing the movement of thought with the genesis of the real. Readers who take it as a farewell to Hegel must reckon with how much Hegelian machinery the passage is content to operate. The passage supports a Marx appropriating Hegel's logic for a materialist inquiry, critically and on stated terms, and that middle position, being undramatic, is the one the evidence most plainly supports. The third caution is practical. The introduction also contains the celebrated difficulty about Greek art, the puzzle that art at a high level flourished on a low level of material development and retains its charm in a world that has outgrown its conditions. Generations of aesthetics seminars have lived in those paragraphs. A reader whose business is the critique of political economy may note the puzzle, admire its honesty about the unevenness of development, and move on without guilt.

Why does the introduction feed such large debates from so small a compass? Because it is the only place where Marx sets out his procedure as procedure, at length, in his own voice, near the start of the work that became Capital. Scholars of method therefore cannot leave it alone, and neither can the disputants about Hegel, for whom it is Exhibit A in both directions. Its status, though, is exactly the status the notebook warning describes. It is a memorandum written before the inquiry it describes had been carried out in the notebooks themselves, let alone in print. To treat its formulations as the finished philosophy of the finished work is to invert the order of evidence. The later Afterword to Capital, where Marx describes his method after performing it, and the practice of Capital itself, are the senior witnesses. The introduction is the junior witness with the most interesting testimony, which is a different thing from the deciding vote.

### What does the introduction contribute that Capital does not?

The introduction contributes the method stated as method: the two orders of inquiry and presentation, and the concrete defined as a concentration of many determinations. Capital performs the method without pausing to theorise it, so the notebooks supply the thinker reflecting on his own procedure, in a memorandum he chose not to publish.

Scholars quote the passage constantly for exactly that reason, and careful scholars quote it with its status attached, since a memorandum written before the inquiry it describes cannot certify the philosophy of the finished work.

A reader meeting the introduction for the first time should also know what it is not. It is not a summary of the Grundrisse, since it precedes the notebooks and sketches a programme the notebooks only partly follow. It is not a preface in the publishing sense, since no publication was prepared. And it is not a safe substitute for the economic chapters it introduces, a substitution much practised by readers who prefer philosophy to political economy and find in these twenty pages a Marx they can discuss without learning any economics. The manuscript resists that preference with its whole bulk. The introduction's method is a method for political economy, arrived at by a man in the middle of economic work, and it loses its sense when severed from the work. Read it, by all means, and read it early. Then read it again after the Formen and the Fragment, when its abstractions have acquired the content they were designed to organise.

## Second passage: the Formen and the dissolution of the old world

The section on forms which precede capitalist production, known across the literature by its German short title, the Formen, is the Grundrisse's great historical expedition. Its question grows directly out of the capital analysis. Capital presupposes the free worker, separated from the means of production, compelled to sell labour power, and that presupposition is a historical result, not a natural fact. The Formen asks how human beings were bound to the conditions of production before that separation, and by what processes the bonds dissolved. Its answer surveys forms of communal existence in which the individual belongs to a community, and through the community to the land, which stands as the great presupposition of labour. Marx distinguishes types, an Asiatic form in which a higher unity overlays self-sufficient communities, an ancient form in which communal and private property interlock in the city and its citizens, a Germanic form in which the individual household is the centre and the community exists as a periodic relation among independent proprietors, and further variants and survivals. For each he asks the same disciplined questions: where does property reside, what does the individual have to do to remain a member, and what inside the form works towards its own dissolution?

The scholarly and political charge of the passage comes from its plurality. A reader who comes to the Formen expecting a single ladder of stages through which every society must climb will find material that both feeds and rebukes the expectation. There are sequences here, certainly, and a strong sense that forms succeed one another as their inner limits are reached. But the forms are also presented as divergent responses to common presuppositions, shaped by geography, by war, by the differing weight of the community against the household, and the dissolution of each is its own story. Whether Marx's philosophy of history is a single track or a branching set of paths is one of the durable arguments in the field, and the Formen is its principal battleground, with the treatment of [mode of production explained](/2010/02/15/mode-of-production-explained/) carrying the conceptual side of that argument. The passage also carries the seeds of later controversies about the so-called Asiatic mode of production, a category with a long and politically heated afterlife, and readers should register that the notebook text on that form is exploratory in precisely the notebook sense: suggestive, empirically thin in places, and never revised by its author into a public position.

The Formen's separate English life, in the Hobsbawm edition of the middle 1960s, gave it a readership its context never planned for. Historians of the ancient world, anthropologists, students of peasant societies and of the transition from feudalism to capitalism all took up the passage as a charter for comparative history written from the side of property relations. Hobsbawm's introduction presented it, justly, as evidence that Marx the historian of forms was a larger figure than the caricature of an economic determinist allowed. Detached circulation had its price as well. Read alone, the Formen can look like a finished treatise on pre-capitalist history, a small book with a thesis, and readers formed by the detached edition sometimes import that finish back into the notebooks as a whole. The guide's strategy restores the section to its place: a magnificent expedition mounted from inside an unfinished analysis of capital, whose historical generalisations carry notebook status however magnificent they are. The questions it asks, about community, property and dissolution, are among the best questions historical materialism has ever posed. The answers are trials, several of them never repeated in print, and the difference between the standing of the questions and the standing of the answers is exactly what the notebook warning exists to mark.

### Why does the Formen reward slow reading?

The Formen, the section on forms which precede capitalist production, packs more distinct historical arrangements into fewer pages than any other stretch of the notebooks. Each communal type is distinguished by where property resides and how its members belong to the land, and the distinctions blur if the pages are hurried.

One more quality deserves a paragraph, because it explains why historians keep returning. The Formen is written with a genuine feel for the interior of the forms it describes, for what membership felt like as security and as fetter at once. The communal world is not romanticised. Its members are protected and stunted by the same relations, and the dissolution that eventually frees the worker also ruins him, stripping the protections along with the fetters. That double judgement, dissolution as liberation and as catastrophe in a single process, is among the most characteristic movements of Marx's historical thought, and it appears here in its fullest early form. Readers who know only the published celebrations of the bourgeoisie's revolutionary role, or only the published denunciations of its violence, will find the notebook treatment more complete than either, because it refuses to choose between the roles. The refusal is not indecision. It is the dialectic of the subject itself, caught in a working text that had no public to simplify for.

## Third passage: the Fragment on Machines in its notebook home

Somewhere in the later notebooks, inside the analysis of capital's production process, the prose changes register. Marx is following a tendency that his own value theory makes urgent. Capital, resting on labour time as the measure of wealth, simultaneously drives labour time down, replacing workers with machinery, applying science to production, and building systems of machines in which the worker steps to the side of the process as watchman and regulator rather than standing inside it as its chief actor. Knowledge, social and cumulative, what the passage calls the general intellect, becomes embodied in the machinery and in the organisation of production itself, a direct force of production rather than a mere accompaniment. The passage presses the contradiction to its dramatic limit: production based on exchange value strains against a development of the productive forces that makes labour time an increasingly miserable measure of the wealth being produced, and the theft of alien labour time, on which the existing order rests, appears in the passage's rhetoric as a vanishing foundation beneath an enormous structure. These are the pages the modern world calls the Fragment on Machines.

Every word of the guide's discipline is needed here, beginning with the simple fact of scale. The Fragment occupies a small number of pages within several hundred. It is a stretch of an argument inside a notebook inside an unfinished critique, and its author never returned to publish it, never excerpted it, never, so far as the record shows, singled it out. The passage's modern career, by contrast, is enormous and is treated in the reception sections below. A reader coming to it fresh should first perform the guide's prescribed act of restoration: read the pages around the famous ones. In context, the machinery discussion belongs to an analysis of fixed capital and of the circulation of capital, technical subjects that the extraction tradition leaps over to reach the prophetic paragraphs. The leap changes the meaning. In context, the tendency Marx describes is a tendency internal to capital, operating alongside counter-tendencies he also describes, within a theory of value he is still constructing. Extracted, the same sentences read as a prediction that the labour theory of value has been overtaken by events, a conclusion the passage's admirers and enemies have both, at times, drawn with more confidence than its notebook status permits.

The concept at the passage's centre has its own exposition in the article on [general intellect explained](/2021/01/15/general-intellect-explained/), and this guide observes its boundary by treating the concept only as far as the manuscript requires. What the manuscript requires is the observation that the general intellect here is not a slogan but a term inside an argument about where productive power resides as automation advances: increasingly in the social knowledge objectified in the system of production, decreasingly in the direct labour of the individual worker. That observation, stated plainly, is strong enough. It does not need the ornament of prophecy. It identifies a tension, between wealth's growing dependence on knowledge and its continuing measurement by labour time, that any reader can then watch operating through the later published work, where it reappears in disciplined forms, in the analysis of machinery and large-scale industry, of cooperation, of the working day. Tracing that reappearance is one of the most instructive exercises the Grundrisse affords, and it converts the Fragment from a relic into an instrument. The reader who performs it will also notice what does not reappear, formulations the notebook tried and the published work dropped or transformed beyond recognition, and that negative evidence is the notebook warning's positive yield.

### Where does the Fragment sit inside the notebooks?

The Fragment on Machines sits in the later notebooks, inside the analysis of fixed capital and its circulation, surrounded by technical argument about machinery, turnover and the production process. Its position matters: the famous paragraphs belong to an analysis of a tendency within capital, and they were written as one movement of that analysis, not as a freestanding statement.

A guide should also mark the rhetoric honestly. The Fragment contains some of the most elevated prose Marx ever wrote, sentences that lift off the technical discussion into a register of historical drama, and the elevation is part of its seduction. Elevated prose in a notebook is a sign of a thinker feeling the size of an idea before he has decided its exact place in a system, and the correct response is to share the excitement at a discount, the discount being the warning. Marx's published prose has its own elevations, in the chapter on the working day or the pages on commodity fetishism, and those elevations survived his self-editing, which gives them a standing the notebook's elevations have not earned. None of this diminishes the Fragment's real importance, which the next sections treat seriously, as the founding document of a living literature. It locates the importance correctly, in the passage's afterlife and in the genuine tension it identifies, rather than in an imagined status as the secret climax of Marx's project. The passage is a flare sent up from inside the workshop. Flares illuminate. They are not lighthouses, and navigation by them alone has wrecked more than one interpretation.

## The remainder: money, capital, and the workshop floor

Honesty about the remainder is part of this guide's contract, and the remainder is most of the book. The money chapters work through the theory of money with a patience the published critique compressed, polemicising against monetary reformers and against the confusions of those who thought the evils of the system could be reformed at the level of the currency. The capital chapters, the manuscript's vast centre, develop the concepts that Capital would later organise: the transformation of money into capital through the purchase of labour power, the production of surplus value, the distinction between the labour process and the valorisation process, the circulation and turnover of capital, the metamorphoses of capital through its forms, and the early shapes of the analyses of profit, interest and rent. Around them runs material reaching towards the unwritten books of the grand plan. A reader who has absorbed the three mapped passages and still asks what the rest is for deserves a straight answer in three parts.

First, the remainder is where the published critique came from, and for questions about how specific arguments were reached, modified or abandoned, it is the primary archive. When Capital's compressed formulation of a doctrine leaves a reader wondering what machinery drives it, the odds are good that the machinery lies exposed somewhere in the notebooks, running at full size. Second, the remainder contains analyses that publication never received, on topics the later architecture marginalised, and some of those analyses, on precapitalist survivals, on the state of the world market as Marx then saw it, on monetary questions, retain independent interest for specialists. Third, and most sobering, large parts of the remainder are superseded trials, arguments Marx himself improved past recognition or discarded, and reading them as though they stood level with the published versions wastes the reader's scarcest resource, which is attention. The three answers together describe a quarry with seams of first-rate stone running through serviceable and rubble alike. Nobody works a quarry by carrying away the whole hillside.

There is also a matter of style that any honest account must register. The notebooks' prose, unedited and written at night against a crisis, is frequently magnificent and frequently punishing, sometimes within a single page. Sentences extend across subordinate clauses in the German philosophical manner, qualifications breed inside qualifications, and a reader can lose the grammatical thread of an argument while following its sense perfectly well two paragraphs later. The standard advice of experienced readers is unglamorous and correct: read with a notebook of one's own, summarise each stretch in plain sentences before moving on, and treat the stretches that resist summarising as stretches to revisit rather than as personal failures. A reader keeping structured notes on the passages, the trials and the status of each famous excerpt will find a tool such as the [Marxism study notes companion](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) well shaped to the task, since the whole discipline this guide teaches is, in the end, a discipline of marking what kind of statement one is looking at. The manuscript rewards the annotating reader and quietly defeats the passive one, and the ratio between those outcomes is higher for the Grundrisse than for any other major Marx text.

## Quarry, draft, or alternative architecture: the notebooks and Capital

Ask what the Grundrisse is, relative to Capital, and scholarship offers three answers, each with evidence, none with a monopoly. The guide's task is to lay them out in their strength, since a reader who holds the question open reads both texts better than a reader who has been issued a solution. The first answer is that the notebooks are a draft of Capital in the ordinary sense, the first full version, later revised into the published volumes. The evidence is real. The plan Marx announced in 1858 and 1859 resembles the eventual architecture; central arguments appear in the notebooks before appearing in print; Marx himself mined the manuscript for the Contribution of 1859 and worked in its shadow for years. Against the draft answer stands the scale of divergence: whole categories are ordered differently, topics central in the notebooks shrink in the book, arguments are transformed rather than copied, and the years between were filled with further manuscripts, the vast drafts of the early 1860s, through which any descent from notebook to book must pass. A draft normally stands close to its revision. This one stands at the head of a long manuscript river.

The second answer is that the notebooks are a quarry, a stock of worked material from which the published critique was built selectively and into which Marx returned as needed. The quarry answer explains the divergences, since quarries are not drafts, and it explains the manuscript's heterogeneity, since a quarry holds stone of every grade. It also fits Marx's known habits of excerpting and reusing his own material across decades. Its weakness is a certain flattening. A quarry is passive, and the Grundrisse is not passive reading for anyone who has worked in it. The notebooks possess an argument of their own, a sustained movement from money through capital that is more than a heap of blocks. Calling them a quarry settles the question of status by lowering the text's temperature, and the scholars who resist the word usually resist it on exactly that ground: something with this degree of internal drive, they argue, deserves to be read as a work, with the notebook warning attached, rather than as a warehouse.

The third answer is the boldest: the notebooks embody an alternative architecture for the critique of political economy, broader than Capital's, within which topics the published work compressed or postponed, the state, foreign trade, the world market, the full history of pre-capitalist forms, had assigned and honoured places. On this reading the Grundrisse is not an imperfect Capital but a more complete design, whose reduction to the published volumes cost the tradition subjects it has spent a century re-importing from the notebooks. Michael Heinrich's scholarship on the manuscript character of the whole critique, and the editorial work of the complete edition, have given this family of readings new rigour, while Roman Rosdolsky's classic study, written from the Berlin edition and published in the late 1960s, remains the great demonstration of how much of Capital's structure becomes intelligible when the notebooks' plan is taken seriously. The weakness of the alternative-architecture answer is the mirror of the quarry's strength. Architects finish buildings or at least draw elevations. Marx drew neither for these notebooks, and to call the design complete in conception risks crediting him with a system he spent the rest of his life failing, or declining, to complete.

The guide's own position, offered as a reading discipline rather than a verdict, is that the three answers describe three true relations at once, on different questions. For the genesis of specific arguments, the notebooks behave as a quarry, and researchers should use them so. For the plan of the critique as a whole, they behave as the earliest full draft, and historians of the project should read them so. For the range of topics a complete critique would cover, they preserve an architecture wider than the one publication achieved, and critics of the tradition's narrowings may cite them so, with the warning attached to every citation. What no reader may responsibly do is select whichever relation flatters a present argument and forget the other two. The scholarly literature at its best, Rosdolsky on one side, the editors and Heinrich on another, keeps the relations distinct. The literature at its worst trades on confusing them, and the confusion is detectable whenever a writer calls the Grundrisse simply the first draft of Capital or simply Marx's real book. Both simplicities are marketing. The manuscript is more interesting than either.

### How does the manuscript recalibrate the published volumes?

It recalibrates them in three documented ways. It reveals the plan from which the published volumes grew, so that compressions and postponements in Capital become visible as decisions. It supplies fuller treatments of topics the book treats briefly. And it establishes that the project exceeded the publications, so silences there cannot be read as absences in Marx's thought.

There is a further, subtler change worth naming, which concerns tone. A reader who arrives at Capital from the notebooks arrives with the sound of Marx's workshop in the ears: the arguing, the trying, the sudden clarities. Capital's own surface, finished and ironic and armoured, reads differently once its costs are known. Passages that seemed merely difficult reveal themselves as compressions of much longer trials. Passages that seemed dogmatic reveal the polemical situations they were compressed for. And the famous absences, the state theory never written, the world market book never begun, stop looking like oversights and start looking like the visible edge of a design whose full extent the notebooks preserve. Reading Capital after the Grundrisse is therefore not a luxury for the already converted. It is a recalibration of what kind of object Capital is, from a completed system to the published fraction of a larger, unfinished one. That recalibration, amongst all the manuscript's effects, may be the one its author would most have recognised as just.

## The autonomist reading and the price of detachment

The most consequential modern career of any Grundrisse passage belongs to the Fragment on Machines within the tradition called autonomist Marxism, and a reader's guide must give that career fairly, because fairness to a powerful reading means stating its evidence before its difficulties. The autonomist current, developing in Italy in the 1960s and 1970s around thinkers such as Mario Tronti and Antonio Negri, and carried forward by later writers on knowledge work and cognitive capitalism, found in the Fragment a Marx who seemed to describe their world in advance. If production increasingly rests on social knowledge objectified in machinery rather than on direct labour, and if capital nonetheless continues to measure and organise wealth by labour time, then the contradiction at the system's centre is not a prediction for a distant future but a description of the present's permanent crisis. On this reading the Fragment licenses a politics centred on the refusal of work and on the self-activity of a class whose productive powers have outgrown their capitalist form. Negri's own extended reading of the Grundrisse, delivered as lectures in Paris in the late 1970s and published as a book, treated the notebooks, not Capital alone, as the summit of Marx's revolutionary theory, and read the whole manuscript through the antagonism between capital and living labour.

The evidence for the autonomist emphasis is genuine and should not be minimised by its critics. The Fragment does say that capital works towards its own dissolution as the regulator of production when it makes labour time a shrinking foundation for an expanding wealth. The notebooks as a whole do give the working class's activity an centrality that more economist readings of Marx underplay. And the autonomists' insistence that Marx's categories are categories of a struggle, not of a neutral mechanics, recovered something real that mid-century orthodoxies had embalmed. A guide that dismissed the reading would be failing in neutrality and in scholarship alike.

The difficulties are nonetheless the difficulties this guide exists to name, and they are three. The first is detachment. The Fragment's career in autonomist and post-autonomist writing is largely a career of extracts, and the extracted passage loses the fixed-capital analysis that frames it, in which the tendency towards the displacement of labour is one movement inside capital's own reproduction, generating counter-movements, new employments, new forms of the working day, that the notebooks themselves register. The second difficulty is the measure question. The strong autonomist claim, that the Fragment shows Marx himself concluding that the labour theory of value was being abolished by machinery, reads a strain in the manuscript as a settlement. In the published work Marx continued to build on value measured by labour time while fully aware of automation's advance, and the relationship between the notebook's strain and the book's construction is a problem to be explained, not a verdict to be quoted. The third difficulty is weight. A few pages are asked to reorganise the interpretation of a life's work, against the grain of thousands of pages their author did publish, and the notebook warning prices that request exactly: possible, interesting, and evidentially expensive. Readers who want the contemporary debate in its own terms will find it pursued in the article on [automation ai and marxism](/2020/12/15/automation-ai-and-marxism/), which owns the argument about machinery and work in the present economy. This guide's contribution is the prior discipline: before asking what the Fragment means for that debate, ask what kind of text it is, and let the answer tax every use.

### What did the autonomists find in the notebooks?

Autonomist thinkers found a Marx centred on the antagonism between capital and living labour, and in the Fragment on Machines a description of production resting on social knowledge rather than direct labour. For readers such as Negri, the manuscript offered a Marx of crisis and working-class self-activity against more mechanical readings of the tradition.

## The systematic-dialectic reading and the method question

A second influential reception reads the Grundrisse from the opposite end: not as a charter for a politics but as the key to a method. Writers in the systematic dialectics current, among them Christopher Arthur and Tony Smith, and before them Rosdolsky in his different idiom, argue that the notebooks show Marx constructing his categories in a systematic order derived from the object itself, beginning from the simplest determinations and unfolding the whole as a developing totality, in a procedure consciously modelled on, and critically transformed from, the logic of Hegel's system. The introduction's ascent from the abstract to the concrete is their charter passage, and the notebooks' actual sequence, money to capital to the forms of capital's appearance, is their evidence that the procedure was practised and not merely preached. On their reading Capital is the mature execution of the notebooks' design, and the Grundrisse is where a reader learns to see the design.

This reception has the great virtue of taking the manuscript seriously as a whole rather than as a mine for extracts, and its readings of the order of categories have demonstrably illuminated Capital's architecture for many students. Its difficulties are of the same family as the autonomists', differently distributed. Where the autonomist detaches a passage, the systematic reader risks over-architecting the whole, attributing to a night-written notebook a systematic rigour that its own pages, with their swerves and restarts, frequently contradict. The notebooks show a mind discovering the order of its object by writing, which is a more interesting spectacle than a mind executing a logic it already possessed, and the evidence for discovery, the trials, the abandoned starts, is on every page. There is also a standing counter-position within Marx scholarship, associated with the editorial and historical work around the complete edition, which warns that the very idea of a finished system misrepresents a project its author left radically incomplete, and that reading the notebooks as the system's blueprint repeats, in methodological dress, the old error of treating Marx's work as a closed doctrine. The guide takes no side in this quarrel, because the quarrel is live and its terms are still being refined by editions in progress. It insists only on the discipline both sides need: the notebooks are evidence about a method in formation, and any reading that makes them the certificate of a method completed has stepped past what a private manuscript can certify.

Between the two great receptions, the political and the methodological, stands a third, quieter one, the biographical and historical, which reads the Grundrisse as the record of a season: the crisis winter in which a forty-year-old exile, watching the markets crash, raced to fix his life's argument before events outran it. Historians of Marx's life and of the nineteenth century's economic thought use the notebooks constantly in this register, and their use is the least vulnerable to the notebook warning, since a record of thinking is exactly what a biographer wants. The quiet reception deserves mention in a guide because it models the manuscript's safest and most certain use, as testimony. Everything the notebooks testify to, about their author's concerns, methods, reading and hopes, they testify to with an authority no school needs to inflate. It is when testimony is promoted into doctrine that the warning sounds, and the history of the text's reception might be written as the history of that promotion, repeated by successive generations, each certain that its own promotion was the justified one.

## The strongest objection: building on papers the author never released

The objection must be stated at the strength its best proponents would want, because this guide's whole discipline is implicated in it. Building interpretive claims on private notebooks the author never released is methodologically weak, the objection runs, for reasons that compound. The author never subjected the material to his own standards of finish, so the interpreter is grading ore as though it were coin. The author cannot be consulted about which trials he endorsed, so the interpreter's selection among trials is unconstrained by anything except the interpreter's prior commitments, and the history of Grundrisse interpretation, in which autonomists find a prophet of cognitive capitalism, systematic readers find a flawless architect and continuists find the young Marx alive in 1858, shows each school duly finding itself. Worse, the objection continues, the modern prominence of the machinery fragment in particular reflects not its importance in Marx's own project but its usefulness to arguments about automation and knowledge work that later generations needed a pedigree for. A few pages have been promoted to the centre of a canon because they were convenient, and convenience dressed as scholarship is the precise corruption that philological standards exist to prevent. On the strongest version of the objection, the honest treatment of the Grundrisse is the biographer's: a document for the study of Marx, inadmissible as an authority on Marxism.

The answer begins by conceding the objection's sharpest point without reservation. The fragment's contemporary prominence is disproportionate to the space it occupies in the notebooks, and any honest map, including the one in this guide, registers the disproportion rather than laundering it into significance. Prominence in a reception is evidence about the receivers. It is not, without further argument, evidence about the text's centre of gravity, and the guide's map gives the Fragment its row because readers arrive asking for it, while the surrounding sections have worked to restore its proportions. The concession made, the objection's general form proves too much, and the proof lies in the distinction between evidential and canonical use that the notebook warning builds in. Evidential use of private papers is not a lax substitute for proper scholarship. It is one of its standard instruments, practised wherever drafts, letters and notebooks survive, and its legitimacy does not depend on the author's permission, which the dead cannot give and historians cannot require. What the author's non-release changes is the kind of claim the material supports. Notebooks support claims about development, intention, method in formation, problems perceived and solutions entertained. They do not support, without corroboration from released work, claims about doctrine held. The objection is therefore right against canonical use and wrong against evidential use, and the entire practice this guide recommends is the policing of that boundary, passage by passage.

Corroboration deserves its own paragraph, because it converts the warning from a caution into a method. A notebook formulation that reappears, transformed, in Capital or in the published articles has passed through the author's own filter, and the pair, trial and publication, can be cited together with their relation stated. A notebook formulation that never reappears is not thereby worthless. It may mark a road the author explored and closed, and roads closed are evidence about the shape of the territory. But it must be cited as an exploration, in those words or their equivalent, and the reader who meets a citation without the marker should supply the marker mentally and discount accordingly. Scholarship that observes this convention exists and is the field's best. Scholarship that ignores it, in every school, produces the mirage the objection denounces, a Marx assembled from his most convenient sentences. The objection, properly answered, thus ends up as an ally of this guide rather than its refutation. It is the notebook warning stated from the sceptic's side, and the agreement of the guide and its strongest critic on the central discipline is the surest sign the discipline is correctly drawn.

## Translations, editions, and the reader's practical choices

Practical questions deserve practical answers, and the most practical question a new reader asks is which text to put on the desk. For English readers the complete translation by Martin Nicolaus, made for the Penguin Marx library in the early 1970s, remains the standard door into the whole manuscript: a single volume, vigorous in style, equipped with a foreword that is itself a document in the reception history, and the version through which the Anglophone debates have largely been conducted, which means its page references and terminology are the ones the secondary literature presupposes. Readers of the Formen who want the passage in its historically first English dress, with a major historian's framing, can go to the Hobsbawm edition of the pre-capitalist section, while remembering that its introduction, like Nicolaus's foreword, argues a case. German readers have the text in the collected works and in the historical-critical complete edition, the MEGA, whose editorial standards set the scholarly benchmark and whose apparatus records the manuscript's physical state in detail no translation can carry. A guide should identify translations by translator and period rather than by novelty, because the merit of a translation of a working manuscript lies in its handling of unfinished prose, its consistency of terminology across several hundred pages, and its honesty about gaps and illegibilities, none of which correlates with newness.

### How should a reader choose an edition?

For the complete text in English, the translation by Martin Nicolaus is the standard choice, the version the debates have been conducted through and the one whose terminology the scholarship presupposes. Readers interested chiefly in the pre-capitalist section may prefer the earlier separate edition of that section prepared by Eric Hobsbawm, with its own historian's framing.

Scholars needing the manuscript's exact state should consult the German text in the complete historical-critical edition, where the editorial apparatus records what translation must smooth over.

Terminology is the trap inside the translation question. A working manuscript throws up words whose rendering decides interpretations: the terms for alienation and estrangement, for objectification, for the communal forms of the Formen, for the general intellect itself. Nicolaus's choices shaped a generation's English Marx, and readers comparing passages across translations, or across the secondary literature of different decades, will find the same German sentence wearing different English clothes and supporting different conclusions. The discipline this guide recommends is modest and effective. Settle on one translation for the reading itself, note the contested terms when they carry an argument, and check the German for any sentence on which an interpretation is to be built. Building an interpretation on a translation of a notebook is two removes from the evidence, and the notebook warning, applied consistently, taxes each remove separately.

### Which difficulty should a new reader expect?

A different difficulty from the one Capital sets, which is why the comparison misleads when it expects one answer. Capital is harder in its finished density and its demand that the reader hold a long construction in mind. The Grundrisse is harder in its unfinishedness: no settled divisions, arguments tried and retried, sentences running for half a page.

Readers who fear difficulty should fear the right one. The notebook's difficulty yields to sampling and strategy, while the difficulty of the published volumes yields only to endurance, and the two should not be confused when planning a first approach.

A word belongs here on the extracts industry, since most encounters with this text begin there. Anthologies of Marx's writings standardly include the introduction or the Fragment or a slice of the Formen, and teachers assembling courses reach for the same pieces, so that the mapped passages receive a second and third amplification beyond their place in the manuscript. None of this is culpable. Teaching and anthologising require selection, and these are the right selections. But the amplifications explain a recurrent experience of new readers, who open the complete text expecting the anthology's proportions and find the famous pages surprisingly brief and surprisingly surrounded. The experience is the manuscript correcting the reception in the reader's own hands, and a guide can ask for no better ally. Let the surprise do its work. It is the notebook warning arriving by experience rather than by precept, and it lasts longer that way. Readers planning where this text belongs in the larger architecture of Marx's writings will find the placement question treated in the [marx reading order guide](/2013/06/15/marx-reading-order-guide/), which sequences the whole corpus for a first journey through it.

## A reading strategy for a seven-notebook manuscript

Strategy can now be assembled from the guide's parts into a sequence a reader can actually follow. Begin with the introduction, read slowly, for its method and for its tone, and resist the temptation to master it, since its terms acquire their content only from the work it introduces. Go next to the Formen, which rewards the historically minded reader immediately and which teaches the notebook's central lesson, that Marx's categories are historical through and through, in its most concrete material. Then read the Fragment on Machines, but under the guide's rule: never alone, always with the surrounding discussion of fixed capital that gives its tendency its place among counter-tendencies. Reread the introduction after the Fragment, and notice how much of it has changed from programme into description. At that point the strategic reader has the manuscript's influential core, its method, its history and its most contested pages, in perhaps a week of evenings, and possesses something else as well: a calibrated sense of the text's registers, on which all later sampling depends.

Sampling is the strategy's second phase, and it should be purposeful rather than dutiful. The reader preparing an essay on money goes to the money chapters with the published treatment in the other hand. The reader tracing a category, cooperation, division of labour, the formal and real subsumption of labour under capital, follows that category through the notebooks and into the later drafts, watching it change. The reader with no specific quarry and a general education in view samples the capital chapters at the points where the later book is known to be compressed, and reads the notebooks' versions for the air they let into the argument. What the strategy forbids is only the continuous dutiful march, front to back, undertaken as a penance. Penance produces abandonment or, worse, a skimmed reverence in which the reader has technically covered the pages and actually met none of them. The manuscript's own author worked in it selectively, returning to passages as projects demanded. The reader who works in it selectively is imitating the only reading the text ever received from the one person who fully understood it.

Two habits complete the strategy. The first is the status habit: for every striking passage, the reader asks the warning's question aloud, is this a trial or a result, and looks for the answer in the passage's behaviour, whether it is taken up again, whether it survives into print, whether its neighbours qualify it. The habit takes seconds and changes everything it touches. The second habit is the pairing habit: notebook passages are read, wherever the subject allows, beside their published counterparts, the money chapters beside the Contribution and Capital's opening, the Formen beside the ethnographic and historical writings, the Fragment beside the machinery chapter of Capital. Pairing is the practical form of the corroboration method set out earlier, and it converts the Grundrisse from a rival scripture into what it most valuably is, a commentary its author inadvertently wrote on his own published work, from inside the process of making it. A reader who keeps both habits for a month will find the manuscript has stopped being a monument and become a tool, which is the transformation every reader's guide in this series exists to perform.

## The six-book plan and the shape of the unwritten whole

Behind the notebooks stands a plan, and the plan is part of the manuscript's evidence about itself. In letters to Engels and to his publisher's circle during the very months of composition, and within the notebooks' own pages, Marx described the critique of political economy as a work in several books: capital, landed property, wage labour, then the state, foreign trade, and the world market. The sequence was meant to move from the most internal relations of bourgeois society outward to its political form and its global stage. Of that design, only the first book's territory received its sustained published treatment, and even that treatment, Capital, reached print in one volume in Marx's lifetime with the remainder following posthumously under Engels's editorship. The Grundrisse is the ground plan of the entire six-book city, drawn when only the first district was anywhere near construction, and it is the only document in which the whole design exists in something like its intended proportions.

The plan matters to a reader for two reasons beyond antiquarian completeness. The first is that it explains the manuscript's strange reaches. Passages that seem to wander, towards the state, towards trade between nations, towards crises of the world market, are not wanderings. They are surveyors' marks for books that were never built, and they preserve, in sketch form, positions Marx expected one day to develop at length, on subjects where the tradition that inherited his name has often had to improvise in his absence. Whole later debates, about Marxist state theory above all, are haunted by the plan's ghost, since the book that would have settled the founder's treatment does not exist and the notebooks' sketches are pressed into service as its substitute, under exactly the evidential strain the notebook warning describes. The second reason is that the plan disciplines the quarry-draft-architecture dispute. A draft is of something. The Grundrisse is not, straightforwardly, the draft of Capital, because Capital is the execution of the plan's first book while the notebooks range over all six. The mismatch of scope, once seen, cannot be unseen, and it is the single most concrete reason the simple draft formula fails. What the notebooks draft is a critique of political economy entire, of which Capital as published is the surviving fragment, magnificent and partial at once.

There is a temptation, standing before the plan, to mourn the unwritten books as a library lost, and the temptation should be indulged briefly and then corrected. The unwritten books were not written for reasons internal to the project as well as external to it. The first book's territory expanded under Marx's hands for twenty years, absorbing research, the further manuscript mountains of the 1860s, and revisions driven by events and by his own deepening standards. A writer who could not finish book one to his satisfaction was not prevented from the others by circumstance alone. The plan's survival in the notebooks is therefore best read as a measure of the project's true scale, and of why its author's published legacy has the shape of a torso rather than a statue. Readers of the Grundrisse are looking at the one moment when the whole torso and the whole statue were visible together, the statue as intention, the torso as the notebooks' actual achievement. That is a melancholy spectacle and an extraordinarily instructive one, and it is available nowhere else in the corpus.

## Alienation's vocabulary in a manuscript of the late 1850s

One content question runs under the whole reception history and deserves direct treatment in a guide, because readers will meet it in every commentary. The notebooks are saturated with the vocabulary of the early writings: alienation, estrangement, objectification, the worker's product and activity confronting him as an alien power, human powers developed as ends in themselves, the rich human being as the measure of a society beyond capital. In the Grundrisse this vocabulary is not decorative residue. It does analytical work, inside the theory of money, where the social power of the individual appears as a power of things; inside the theory of capital, where labour's own conditions tower over the labourer as an alien and hostile world; and in the manuscript's most forward-looking passages, where the reduction of necessary labour opens the prospect of free time as the true measure of wealth and the development of human capacities as an end in itself. A reader formed by the break thesis meets these pages with a problem, and the problem's insolubility in its sharp form is what the publication chronology section of this guide was about.

The interpretive options, stated neutrally, are these. One may hold that the vocabulary survived while its philosophical content was discarded, the words retained as rhetoric over a changed, scientific substance. One may hold that the substance survived, that the theory of alienation matured into the theory of fetishism and of the capital relation without ceasing to be itself, so that the notebooks document a continuity the published works' different register conceals. One may hold positions between, distinguishing strands, some continuous, some transformed, some abandoned, and the serious scholarship of the last half century largely lives in that middle territory, mapping the strands one by one. The guide's concern is narrower than the verdict and, for a reader, prior to it. The notebooks are the place where the question can be studied in its richest evidence, because here the allegedly young vocabulary and the allegedly mature economics occupy the same paragraphs, neither pasted over the other. Any course or textbook that teaches the periodisation of Marx without sending students to these paragraphs is teaching a map of territory it has not inspected. And any reading of the paragraphs that ignores their notebook status, treating their eloquence as the author's final word on the human essence, commits the canonical fallacy in its most seductive form, since few pages in Marx are more eloquent or less finished.

The free time passages deserve a specific note, because they attract a quotation traffic second only to the Fragment's. In the notebooks Marx writes that when labour in its immediate form ceases to be the great source of wealth, labour time ceases to be wealth's measure, and the measure becomes disposable time, time for the full development of the individual. Extracted, the sentences circulate as a utopian certificate. In place, they belong to an argument about capital's tendency and counter-tendency, and their register is analytic before it is visionary: Marx is tracing what the development of the productive forces does to the categories, and the vision arrives as the tracing's consequence. The difference matters for use. A politics built on the extracted sentences claims Marx's authority for a destination. A reading built on the passage in place claims his method for analysing a tendency, and must then do the harder work of asking how far the tendency has run and what obstructs it. The notebook warning, once again, is not a deflation of the text's power. It is the price list attached to the power, and readers who pay it get the genuine article.

## Rosdolsky and the scholarly recovery of the plan

The modern scholarly Grundrisse begins with a book about it rather than with the text itself, and a guide should name the fact. Roman Rosdolsky, a Ukrainian Marxist scholar who had worked in the archives and survived the century's catastrophes, studied the Berlin edition and produced, in the years before his death in 1967, the study published in German in 1968 and known in English as The Making of Marx's Capital. Rosdolsky's demonstration was simple to state and laborious to prove: that the structure of Capital, its order of categories, its peculiar inclusions and exclusions, becomes intelligible when read against the plan and the drafts of 1857 and 1858, and opaque when read as a self-sufficient treatise. He followed the notebooks' plan into the later manuscripts, traced the category of money through its transformations, and recovered for scholarship the figure of Marx as a worker in a workshop, whose published masterpiece is the visible portion of a submerged continent of drafting.

The importance of naming Rosdolsky in a reader's guide is not piety towards a predecessor. It is that his book models the manuscript's best scholarly use at full scale. Rosdolsky treats the notebooks as evidence, massively and minutely; he corroborates against the published work at every step; he argues from the plan to the book and from the book back to the plan; and he never confuses the manuscript's authority about Marx's development with an authority over Marx's conclusions. Readers who finish this guide and want the Grundrisse's scholarly literature at its most rewarding should go to Rosdolsky next, with the warning already installed, and watch it operate in the hands of a master. The later waves of scholarship, the editorial labours of the complete edition, Heinrich's arguments about the unfinished and fragmentary character of the critique as a whole, the systematic dialecticians and their critics, all presuppose the ground Rosdolsky cleared, even when they build against his conclusions. Reception histories usually belong to specialists. This one belongs in the guide because the recovery of the plan changed what the manuscript is for every subsequent reader: after Rosdolsky, nobody competent reads Capital innocently of the notebooks, and nobody competent reads the notebooks as a devotional object. The two incompetences survive, of course, in the wild. They survive as the reverent march and the extractors' raid, and this guide has been written against both from its first page.

## Misreadings this guide corrects

Guides earn their authority by correcting errors, and this manuscript attracts a stable set of them, each worth naming in the plainest terms available. The first misreading holds that the Grundrisse is a draft of Capital in the ordinary sense, a first version later polished. The relation is more complex, spanning plan, quarry and wider architecture, and the ordinary sense misleads everyone who brings it to the page. The second misreading holds that the notebooks should be read continuously, as a book, and that anything less is laziness. The reverse is true. Continuous reading is the one approach the manuscript's genre punishes, and strategic reading is the approach its own author, who raided it selectively for decades, exemplified. The third misreading holds that the machinery fragment predicts the end of the labour theory of value, as a settled announcement. That is one contested reading of an exploratory passage, opposed by readers who find the strain in the notebook resolved, in the published work, in favour of the theory the fragment supposedly abandons. The fourth misreading holds that the manuscript was suppressed, hidden by custodians who feared its content. The record shows a Nachlass, a war, and the economics of editions, a duller story that happens to be true. The fifth misreading, the deepest, holds that the Grundrisse is a mature statement, the real book behind the books. It is a working document, and its working character is not a blemish on its greatness but the source of its distinctive value, which is to show a great analysis in the act of existing before it knew what it was.

Each misreading has the same root, and naming the root is the guide's last analytical service. All five treat the manuscript as something other than what it is: as a version, as a book, as a prophecy, as a secret, as a testament. The notebook warning is the refusal of all five substitutions at once. What remains, when they are refused, is not a diminished text but the actual one, seven notebooks of a mind working at its highest pitch under pressure of events, unpublished because unfinished, unfinished because honest about the scale of its undertaking. Readers sometimes fear that genre discipline of this kind is a scholar's trick for withholding enthusiasm. The opposite is the case here. Nothing in the secondary literature's most fervent promotions of the Grundrisse does the manuscript as much honour as the plain description of what it is, held steadily, because the plain description explains the fervour better than the fervour explains itself. A text that can reorganise a century's interpretation of its author, decades after both were buried, does not need to be called his secret masterpiece. It needs to be read, in the right places, in the right order, at the right discount. This guide has tried to specify all three.

## How scholars cite the notebooks, and how readers should audit citations

Since the notebook warning finally cashes out as a practice of citation, the guide ends its argument with the practice stated as rules of thumb a reader can apply to any book, article or lecture that leans on this manuscript. The first rule is to ask what the citation is asked to prove. If it is asked to prove that Marx considered a problem, entertained a formulation, or worked a category in a particular direction, the notebook is strong evidence and the citation stands. If it is asked to prove that Marx held a doctrine, especially a doctrine absent from or contrary to the published work, the citation is carrying weight it cannot bear alone, and the reader should look for the corroboration the writer owes and has or has not supplied. The second rule is to ask whether the passage cited behaves like a trial or a result in its own neighbourhood: whether the notebooks return to it, deepen it, qualify it or drop it. Writers who quote the Grundrisse responsibly tend to answer this question in their exposition, because they have had to answer it for themselves. Writers who quote irresponsibly present the passage as self-evidently monumental, and monumentality, in a manuscript of trials, is a warning sign rather than a recommendation.

The third rule concerns the famous pages specifically. Any argument that rests disproportionately on the Fragment on Machines should be read with its proportions restored, a few notebook pages set against the published corpus, and the reader should ask what the argument would look like if the same weight rested on pages of equal fame in the published work. The exercise does not refute the argument. It prices it, and pricing is the service the warning performs throughout. The fourth rule is gentler and concerns writers rather than readers. The manuscript's eloquence tempts quotation beyond need, and a writer who finds the Grundrisse supplying every third epigraph should suspect the notebooks are being used as an authority perfume, a scent of depth, rather than as evidence. Marx deserves better than his own perfume, and readers deserve citations that discriminate. A literature that observes these four rules would not be smaller. It would be more precise about the single most interesting fact this manuscript embodies, that the difference between thinking and having thought is where interpretation lives.

## The opponents in the room: the polemics and what they are doing

A first-time reader of the notebooks keeps meeting names that the modern world has half forgotten, and the meetings can feel like interruptions. They are not interruptions. They are the testing apparatus. Marx in 1857 wrote against a living field of rivals: the monetary reformers who believed the system's evils could be cured by redesigning its currency, the followers of Proudhon who had made an alternative socialism out of exchange reform, the Ricardian socialists who turned the labour theory of value against the capitalists while keeping its categories unexamined, and the professional economists whose confusions about money and capital he had been annotating for years. The notebooks' opening campaign, the long engagement with the money doctrine associated with Alfred Darimon and the Proudhonist school, is where he clears away the idea that a juster token could abolish the contradictions of a society whose wealth is produced as commodities. The argument matters beyond its target. In demolishing the scheme for labour money and reformed exchange, Marx establishes the principle that runs through everything after: the sphere of circulation cannot be reformed into innocence, because its forms are generated by the production relations underneath, and anyone who treats money as a technical device with accidental defects has mistaken the symptom for the system.

For the strategic reader, the polemics divide into two classes with different reading instructions. The first class is generative polemic, where the refutation builds the positive doctrine as it goes. The Darimon engagement is generative: out of it comes the notebooks' first full statement of why money is a necessary form of the commodity's contradictions rather than a convention. The engagements with the theory of capital's origin, with the economists' accounts of profit, with the population doctrine inherited from Malthus, are generative in stretches. These passages repay the mapped-passage pace, slow and annotated, because the reader is watching foundations being poured. The second class is custodial polemic, where Marx is guarding territory already won, repeating a refutation for the record, sometimes at a length his private purpose did not require him to justify to anyone. Custodial passages can be sampled, their conclusions noted, their detail left for the specialist who needs the history of a controversy. The distinction between the classes is not marked in the text, since a working notebook marks nothing for a reader's convenience. It is exactly the kind of distinction a guide exists to supply, and applying it converts hundreds of daunting pages into a navigable division of labour between what forms the argument and what defends it.

There is a deeper reason the polemics deserve a section of their own, and it returns to the notebook warning from a fresh angle. In published works, an author's opponents are chosen and arranged for public effect, and the arrangement is evidence about the author's strategy. In notebooks, the opponents are simply whoever the argument needed that week, and their prominence on the page measures the state of the author's working problems, not the opponents' objective importance. Readers who reconstruct Marx's intellectual world from the notebooks alone will overpopulate it with monetary reformers and underpopulate it with figures, Ricardo above all, whose influence was so thoroughly absorbed that it operates everywhere without needing daily refutation. The correction is the pairing habit again: the notebooks give the workshop's noise level, the published works and the correspondence give the considered map of allegiances and debts, and a serious account of Marx's sources reads the two registers against each other rather than mistaking either for the whole.

## From the notebooks to the manuscripts of the 1860s

The Grundrisse did not lead straight to Capital, and the road between them is part of what a reader of the notebooks should carry away. Marx's first published instalment, the Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy of 1859, took the notebooks' money analysis as its foundation and issued in a form so compressed and so coolly received that he abandoned the instalment plan it belonged to. Then, in the early 1860s, came a second mountain of manuscript: the vast drafts in which he worked through the history of value theory, the material posthumously known as Theories of Surplus Value, alongside renewed drafts of the capital analysis itself. Only after that second mountain did the Capital of 1867 take its published shape. The notebooks of 1857 and 1858 therefore stand at the head of a decade of drafting, not at the threshold of a printer's shop, and every formulation a reader finds in them had to survive, or fail to survive, two further campaigns of rewriting before it could appear as doctrine.

This long road cuts both ways in the status argument, and a guide should show both edges. On one edge, the road weakens notebook authority further, since even a formulation Marx loved in 1858 had to pass the filters of 1859, of the 1861 to 1863 manuscripts, and of the final drafting, and many did not pass. On the other edge, the road strengthens the notebooks' evidential value about the project, because the categories that do survive the whole decade, transformed but recognisable, can be identified in the Grundrisse as the project's load-bearing members, the ones its author could not do without. The transformation of the value theory is the textbook case. The notebooks' treatment of value and money is powerful and in places unsettled, still arguing with the monetary confusions of the age; the later manuscripts sharpen the distinction between labour and labour power into the form on which the published theory of surplus value depends. A reader who watches that sharpening happen, from notebook to later draft to book, has learned something about how theories are actually made that no finished treatise can teach, because finished treatises are constructed to hide precisely this. The Grundrisse's place at the road's beginning is therefore not a demotion. Beginnings of long roads are where direction is set, and direction, in a project of this scale, is destiny in a way that wording is not.

A related point concerns the introduction's fate along the road. Marx suppressed the 1857 introduction when he published the Contribution, replacing its method talk with a short preface containing the famous summary of the materialist conception of history, the base and superstructure passage that generations learned as the doctrine's charter. The substitution is instructive for readers of the notebooks. The author of the Grundrisse, given one chance to introduce his critique to the public, chose the compressed historical summary over the methodological meditation, judging the second, as he said, a source of confusion before its results existed. Posterity reversed his judgement in its reading habits, canonising the preface's summary and treating the notebooks' introduction as a seminar text, but his judgement stands in the record as the author's own pricing of his private pages. When the writer himself prices a manuscript below his published work as evidence of his position, interpreters who price it higher owe an argument. Some have supplied one. Many have not noticed that one was owed, and noticing it is, once more, the notebook warning doing its work.

## Crisis, credit, and the world market horizon

The notebooks were written inside a crisis, and crisis is not merely their occasion but one of their subjects, treated at a reach the published work never matched. Because the six-book plan reserved its final stages for foreign trade and the world market, and because the events of 1857 were unfolding on exactly that scale, the Grundrisse keeps breaking the frame of a national economy. Money appears in its world-market role, as the form in which nations confront one another. Credit appears as the system's own mechanism for stretching its limits and concentrating its eventual reckoning. The tendency of capital to create the world market, to tear down spatial barriers, to make every nation's production dependent on the whole, is stated in these pages with a sweep that the later compression of the project into its first book pushed to the margins. Readers who know Marx chiefly as a theorist of the factory will find in the notebooks a theorist of the globe, whose unit of analysis was never, even in conception, the single country.

This horizon matters for the guide's strategy because it changes what the remainder of the notebooks is for. Passages on trade, on colonial production, on the differing forms of capital across the world system, which a reader hunting only for Capital's drafts might skip as scaffolding, are on the plan's own terms the survey lines of the unwritten final books, and they preserve positions their author never developed elsewhere. Their status is the most notebook-like of all: intentions recorded, directions marked, arguments sketched in a few dense pages that a lifetime's work elsewhere never cashed. The interpreter's duty towards them is the warning in its strictest form, since a sketch of a book on the world market can ground claims about what Marx noticed and valued, and cannot ground a Marxist theory of the world market in his name. The tradition has not always observed the distinction, and whole constructions rest on these sketches attributed with a confidence their pages do not invite. To say this is not to diminish the sketches. A surveyor's marks across unbuilt ground are precious to everyone who later builds there. They are precious as marks, not as buildings, and the notebooks' crisis and world market material should be read, taught and cited under that description.

There is also the biographical poignancy of the horizon, which a guide may register once without indulging it. The man racing through the notebooks believed the crisis of 1857 might open the revolutionary epoch his whole politics awaited, and he wrote the world market pages as a combatant taking the measure of his moment. The crisis passed. The epoch did not open. The notebooks went into their drawer, and the measure he had taken of the world system waited eighty years for readers. A text's afterlife is rarely just, and this one's injustice is double: it arrived too late for the crisis that provoked it, and it arrived, in the 1970s, in time to be conscripted into arguments its author could not have imagined. Both facts belong to its meaning for a modern reader, who meets it always as a survivor, a document out of its season twice over. Survivors deserve to be questioned about their own time before being enlisted in anyone else's, and that questioning, applied to the Grundrisse, is simply the notebook warning widened from genre to history.

### What happened to the notebooks after Marx's death?

After Marx's death in 1883 the notebooks passed with his literary estate through Engels's keeping into the custody of the German social democratic movement's archives. They were known to exist and occasionally mentioned by their custodians, but for decades they were read by almost nobody at all.

The custody history explains a feature of the reception that puzzles readers who meet it unprepared, which is the confidence with which generations of interpreters, friendly and hostile, pronounced on Marx's development without the single most relevant manuscript. They were not negligent. They were working the archive that existed for them, an archive whose custodians had their own priorities, among which the editing of Capital's remaining volumes and the correspondence stood first, and in which a set of unsectioned notebooks from the 1850s ranked as a remote scholarly desideratum rather than an urgent publication. The lesson the custody teaches is institutional rather than personal. Canons are made by archives, archives are made by custodians with finite lives, and interpretations that present themselves as readings of an author are always, in part, readings of the author's surviving paper as an institution has delivered it. The Grundrisse is the Marx literature's most dramatic case, but the mechanism is universal, and a reader who has grasped it here will read every classic's reception history with new eyes.

Engels's own role in the custody deserves its sentence of justice. He spent his last twelve years editing Marx's manuscripts for publication, completing the second and third volumes of Capital from drafts far more chaotic than posterity usually credits, and the notebooks of 1857 and 1858 were among the papers his labours preserved intact for a future he knew would need them. That the future took another half century to arrive, and arrived by way of Moscow and Berlin rather than London, belongs to the history of the movement that inherited the papers, with its exiles, its splits and its wars. The manuscript that finally emerged into print was thus a survivor of two custodies, the friend's and the movement's, and its survival was never guaranteed. Readers holding the Nicolaus volume are holding the end of a chain of preservation that came close to breaking more than once, and a reader's guide may properly end the historical part of its work by saying that gratitude, like reverence, is a legitimate response to the Grundrisse, provided it is directed at the preservation rather than mistaken for a method of reading.

## Hegel at the desk: the Logic reread in the winter of the notebooks

In January 1858, in the middle of the notebook campaign, Marx wrote to Engels that he had been leafing through Hegel's Logic again and that the exercise had been of great service to him in working through his material. The remark is one of the most quoted sentences in his correspondence, and the notebooks bear it out on their surface. Their movement from the simplest determinations towards ever richer combinations, their treatment of categories as developing out of one another's contradictions, their comfort with the idea that a thing is constituted by its relations, all show a writer whose philosophical instruments had been freshly sharpened on the Logic. This is the biographical root of the systematic-dialectic reception described earlier, and it is also the point at which a guide must be most careful with its boundaries, since the comparison of Hegel's dialectic with Marx's has its own full treatment elsewhere in this series, in the article on [hegel dialectic vs marx dialectic](/2012/08/15/hegel-dialectic-vs-marx-dialectic/). What belongs here is the manuscript fact and its pricing.

The fact is that the Grundrisse is the most Hegelian text Marx ever produced at length, more openly indebted in its procedures than the published Capital, where the debt is confessed in an afterword and practised under the discipline of exposition. The pricing follows from the genre. A notebook written while rereading the Logic is a workshop in which an instrument is being tested on new material, and the tests include excesses: passages where the dialectical machinery runs ahead of the economic content, where a transition is performed because the logic invites it rather than because the evidence compels it. Marx's own later practice pruned such passages. The published work keeps the instrument and drops most of the occasions on which it had merely displayed itself. Readers of the notebooks therefore meet the Hegelian Marx in his least guarded form, which is exhilarating and evidentially treacherous in equal measure. The exhilaration explains the school that takes the notebooks as the method's true home. The treachery is the warning's familiar point: a test of an instrument is not the instrument's certificate, and the distance between the Grundrisse's dialectical exuberance and Capital's dialectical discipline is itself one of the best measures of what publication meant for this author.

There is a second philosophical presence in the notebooks that a guide should register beside Hegel, and it is Aristotle, whose distinctions about exchange, use and the forms of wealth Marx had absorbed and who stands behind the manuscript's treatment of money's ancient history. The presence matters because it corrects a lazy picture in which the notebooks' philosophy is a single German inheritance. Marx's workshop was stocked from two and a half millennia, and the Grundrisse, ranging from Aristotle's household to the joint stock companies of the 1850s, shows the stock being used at full stretch. A reader sampling the manuscript for its philosophy should sample with that range in mind, and should treat the text's philosophical richness as one more reason it cannot be reduced to a preliminary sketch of an economics book. The notebooks are where Marx's whole intellectual formation operated at once, which is their glory and their danger in a single property.

## Labour in the notebooks: the category under construction

If one category can be followed through the notebooks as a tracer, it is labour, and following it teaches the manuscript's method better than any summary. Labour enters the Grundrisse in several states at once. It is the activity presupposed by all production, labour in general, the abstraction the introduction reflects on. It is the historically specific thing the worker sells, labour power, whose distinction from labour itself Marx is in these years forging into the key of the whole critique. It is living labour, the worker's present activity, set against objectified labour, its past products accumulated as capital, in the confrontation that structures the notebooks' central analysis. And it is labour under its future aspect, in the passages where necessary labour shrinks and disposable time grows, where labour as measure gives way to labour as self-development. The notebooks hold all these states simultaneously, sometimes within a few pages, because the manuscript is the scene of their differentiation. In the published work the states arrive separated, defined and assigned their places. In the workshop the reader watches the separations being made, and watches some of them being made twice, differently.

The tracer exercise repays any reader willing to perform it, and the guide prescribes it as the single most instructive sampling project the manuscript offers. Take the distinction between labour and labour power, on which the theory of surplus value turns. In the notebooks the distinction is present and at work, and also not yet the polished instrument it becomes; the terminology moves, the argument circles it, sharpens it against the economists who conflated the two and thereby made profit a mystery or a fraud. Watching the sharpening corrects two opposite misreadings of Marx's development at once. Against the story of a sudden breakthrough, the notebooks show a distinction achieved by labour, over years, against resistance from the material itself. Against the story of a doctrine present from the start, they show how much of the achievement belongs to the winter of 1857 and 1858 and to the further manuscripts that followed. Either way, the notebooks are where the evidence lives, and their evidence is of the kind the warning certifies without discount: evidence about a process, offered by the process's own record.

The same tracer method works for value, for money, for the state, for rent, and a research student could build a sound project out of any one of them. What the method requires is the discipline this guide has repeated until it must sound like a refrain: the notebook occurrence is the beginning of an argument about development, never its end, and the end is always a comparison, notebook against later draft against published text, with the transformations named. Scholars call this genetic reading, and the Grundrisse is its natural home ground in the Marx literature, the text that made genetic reading of Marx unavoidable by surviving. Before its publication, a reader could treat Capital as a monument and be done. After it, the monument had a documented childhood and adolescence, and interpretation became, whether it liked it or not, a branch of biography of a book. That transformation of the whole field's habits is the manuscript's deepest legacy, deeper than any single passage, and it is wholly an evidential legacy, a change in what counts as knowing Marx rather than a change in what Marx is quoted as saying.

## The warning applied: three citations examined

Discipline is best taught by cases, and this guide closes its argument by applying the notebook warning to three citation situations of the kind a reader will meet within a week of entering the secondary literature. The first case is the sentence, encountered in works on automation, that Marx foresaw a production resting on knowledge rather than labour and therefore expected the wage relation to dissolve of itself. The warning's audit asks three questions. Is the sentence a trial or a result in its home? It is a tendency argument inside a fixed capital analysis, flanked by counter-tendencies. Did it survive into print in that form? It did not; the published machinery analysis keeps the tendency and drops the dissolution rhetoric. What work is the citation doing? It is certifying a political expectation with an authority the passage's status cannot supply. Verdict: the citation may be used to show that Marx analysed the tendency with unmatched penetration in 1858. It may not be used to show that he predicted or endorsed the expectation built on it.

The second case is the sentence, met in works on method, that Marx's procedure is the ascent from the abstract to the concrete, full stop, as though the introduction of 1857 were the constitution of the science. The audit asks its questions. Trial or result? A memorandum written before the inquiry, and withheld by its author as premature. Survival? The procedure is practised in Capital and briefly confessed in its afterword, which is the senior witness and should be cited first. Work done by the citation? It converts a worker's memo into a methodological charter, skipping the author's own pricing. Verdict: cite the introduction for the fullest statement of the procedure, cite it as a suppressed memorandum of 1857, and let the afterword and the practice carry the doctrinal weight. The third case is the sentence, met in works on history, that Marx held a doctrine of the Asiatic mode of production as a stage of universal history. The audit finds an exploratory stretch of the Formen, empirically thin, never revised for publication, entangled with journalism Marx wrote for money on deadlines, and promoted by later orthodoxies into a stage the notebooks' own branching treatment resists. Verdict: the Formen grounds claims about the questions Marx asked of pre-capitalist forms. A doctrine of stages in his name must be built, if at all, from sterner material, and honest builders say so.

The three verdicts share a shape that the reader can now apply unaided. In each case the warning did not empty the citation of value. It re-specified the claim the citation can support, usually narrowing the claim from doctrine to development, from prediction to analysis, from charter to memorandum. Narrowing of that kind is not loss. A claim that survives the audit is worth more than the inflated claim it replaces, because it can be defended in the open, against the manuscript itself, without protective vagueness. The literature on the Grundrisse divides, in the end, into writing that welcomes the audit and writing that depends on escaping it, and a reader equipped with the three questions can sort any new contribution in an afternoon. That sorting ability, more than any fact in this guide, is the equipment the guide exists to hand over, because facts about a manuscript date and are revised, while the habit of asking what kind of text one is quoting from is permanent capital for any reader of Marx.

## The prose itself: reading a mind at night

A last word belongs to the experience of the prose, since guides that ignore experience send readers into texts unprepared for their weather. The Grundrisse reads like weather: long fronts of dense argument, sudden clearings where a whole theory stands visible in a paragraph, squalls of polemic, and stretches of calm inventory where Marx is simply making sure of his materials. The sentences are famously long, and their length is functional rather than perverse. A writer holding a totality in mind, the whole circuit of capital with its presuppositions and results, writes sentences that refuse to close before the circuit does, and the reader who surrenders to the rhythm, reading for the completed period rather than word by word, discovers that the prose carries more than its reputation admits. Translation softens this. Nicolaus's English is vigorous and sometimes brisk where the German is deliberate, and readers who find the English notebooks surprisingly readable should credit the translator and adjust their sense of the original accordingly, another small exercise in not mistaking the delivered object for the thing itself.

The prose also has a quality no summary conveys, which is intimacy. Published Marx addresses publics: workers, economists, the readers of a hostile press, posterity. The notebooks address Marx, and the reader overhears. Overhearing a great mind for several hundred pages produces a relationship unlike any other in the literature, part apprenticeship, part trespass, and the relationship explains the manuscript's hold on its devotees better than any doctrine extracted from it. Devotion of that kind is a fact of the reception and even, within limits, a tribute the text has earned. The guide's whole argument can be restated as the ethics of that relationship. The overheard conversation deserves to be listened to with complete attention and quoted with complete honesty about what it was: a man talking his way towards a book, in a winter of crisis, at the height of his powers, not yet knowing which of his sentences the future would be asked to believe. Believe the ones he published. Learn from all of them. That is the notebook warning, and it is also, in the end, simply respect.

## The notebooks in the age of the complete edition

The Grundrisse's scholarly situation has been transformed once more in the decades since the Nicolaus translation, and a reader using the manuscript now works downstream of that transformation whether aware of it or not. The historical-critical complete edition of the works of Marx and Engels, the second MEGA, has been publishing the full manuscript legacy with an apparatus that records cancellations, insertions, the sequence of writing and the physical state of the papers, and its volumes have changed the standing of every text they touch. For the Grundrisse the change is double-edged in an instructive way. On one side, editorial rigour has given the notebooks their most reliable text and their most exact chronology, settling questions about the order of composition that earlier readers could only guess at. On the other side, the same rigour has underlined, with a mass of documentary detail, the manuscript's unfinished character, since an apparatus that shows a writer crossing out, restarting and abandoning is the notebook warning set in type. Scholarship associated with the edition, Heinrich's work most prominently, has drawn the moral for the whole critique: a project that exists substantially as manuscripts in motion should be interpreted as a project in motion, and the search for Marx's final system, in the notebooks or anywhere else, mistakes the character of the legacy.

For the general reader the edition's existence carries a simpler lesson, which the guide states as the last of its chronology lessons. The text on the desk, however venerable the translation, is a representation of a physical object that scholarship is still in the process of fully describing, and fine-grained arguments about single words or the order of passages should be referred to the edition's evidence rather than conducted from a paperback's surface. This counsels humility at exactly the point where interpretation is most tempted by confidence, the close reading of a famous sentence. Close reading remains the glory of Grundrisse study, and the mapped passages repay it as few texts do. But the close reader of a manuscript is reading a scribe's decisions as well as an author's, a translator's as well as a scribe's, and the audit habit this guide teaches applies to the mediation as well as to the matter. Nothing in that caution should frighten a reader away. It should send the reader in with the right instruments, one translation settled on, the contested terms noted, the German checked where an argument turns on a phrase, and the status of every famous passage marked in the margin where it belongs.

## Itineraries: the manuscript for four kinds of reader

The guide's strategy can be particularised, finally, for the readers who most often ask for directions, and the particulars are worth giving because a general strategy still leaves each reader wondering where their own hours go. The reader who comes from Capital, wanting the workshop behind the book, should begin with the notebooks' capital analysis at the points where the published argument is most compressed, the transformation of money into capital and the first working of the labour and labour power distinction, then take the introduction for the method those chapters practise, and leave the Formen and the Fragment for a second expedition. This reader's danger is impatience with divergence, the wish that the notebooks would simply agree with the book, and the cure is the genetic habit: every divergence logged as a finding. The reader who comes from politics, wanting the Fragment and its literature, should be required by honesty to take the fixed capital context with the famous pages, then the Formen for the historical depth the politics presupposes, and then a sceptical pass through the reception, autonomist claims in one hand and their critics in the other, before forming a view. This reader's danger is conscription, the enlistment of a notebook into a programme, and the cure is the warning itself, applied before enthusiasm sets.

The reader who comes from history, wanting the Formen and the pre-capitalist world, has the easiest entry and the subtlest danger. The entry is easy because the Formen rewards the historically literate immediately, with its typology of communal forms and its dissolution stories. The danger is treating the typology as a museum of certified exhibits, when the passage is a working sketch whose empirical filling varies from the substantial to the gestural, and whose own author treated its generalisations as instruments for the capital analysis rather than as a philosophy of history in finished form. This reader should pair the Formen with the later historical writings and with the anthropology Marx read in his last years, where some notebook hunches are deepened and others quietly dropped. The reader who comes from philosophy, wanting the introduction and the Hegel question, has the reverse profile: a short mapped passage of extreme density, and a danger of mistaking the memorandum for the whole manuscript, graduating with a Grundrisse consisting of twenty pages and a rumour. This reader's cure is compulsory sampling of the economic heartland, enough of the money and capital chapters to feel what the method was for, since a method detached from its material becomes, in the notebooks as elsewhere, a style in search of a subject.

Under all four itineraries runs the same river. The manuscript gives each reader the thing that reader came for, and then, if the reader stays, it gives the thing the reader did not know was there: for the economist, history; for the activist, context; for the historian, method; for the philosopher, economics. That chastening generosity is the mark of a great working text, and it is why this guide, for all its warnings, ends in recommendation. Read the Grundrisse. Read it in the mapped places, in the order given, at the discount prescribed, with a pencil in hand and the published work within reach. It will change what its readers think Marx was doing, which is the most any unpublished manuscript has ever done for any author, and it will do so honestly, provided the honesty is reciprocated.

## The silences of the notebooks: what the manuscript does not contain

A guide that maps what a text contains owes its readers a map of the absences too, because readers go to famous manuscripts hunting for things that are not there and return with substitutes. The Grundrisse does not contain a finished theory of crisis. It contains brilliant materials towards one, observations on overproduction, on credit's elasticity and collapse, on the world market as the crisis's true theatre, but the materials are scattered across the plan's territories and were never gathered, in the notebooks or after them, into the treatment the plan promised. It does not contain a theory of the state. The state appears, as taxation, as public debt, as the political form presupposed and passed over, and the sixth book's absence begins here, in a manuscript that keeps deferring the political to a later stage of the design. It does not contain the mature form of the value analysis with which Capital opens, since the opening of Capital was reworked across the following decade, and readers who quote the notebooks' value discussions as the doctrine's foundation are building on the scaffolding of a front wall that was later rebuilt. And it does not contain a sociology of the classes of its own century in anything like finished form, for all its historical brilliance, because the class analysis, like the state, belonged substantially to the design's later books.

The silences are not accusations. A ground-clearing manuscript silences most of what it surveys, and the plan's whole point was sequence: foundations first, the rest in their books. But the silences discipline interpretation in a way readers resist and need. A tradition that possesses a founder's brilliant fragments on a subject and lacks his treatise on it will be tempted, across generations, to upgrade the fragments, to speak of Marx's theory of crisis or of the state as though the treatise existed in dispersed form and only awaits assembly. Assembly is not innocent. The assembler supplies the connective tissue, the priorities, the resolutions of contradiction, and the resulting theory is the assembler's work wearing the founder's name, a ventriloquism the notebook warning was coined to catch. The honest alternatives are two, and the guide names them plainly. Either the later theory is built openly, as construction on Marxian foundations, owned by its builders, or the founder's actual position is reported as what it is: materials, directions, and a plan, powerful and incomplete. Both are respectable. The upgrade is not, and the notebooks' silences, mapped honestly, are the best protection against it that this literature possesses.

There is a positive side to the silences, and it closes the map. What the notebooks do not contain, they frame. The plan tells the reader exactly where the missing treatises would have stood and what questions they would have answered, and a reader who has absorbed the plan reads the entire later tradition differently, as a set of attempts, some magnificent, to occupy surveyed and unbuilt ground. The attempts can then be judged as attempts, by their own merits, rather than as recoveries of a lost whole that never existed. Much of the heat in Marxist theoretical dispute comes from the fiction of the lost whole, the complete doctrine the founder supposedly possessed and the faithful must restore. The Grundrisse is the best antidote to that fiction in existence, because it shows the whole at its most complete moment, the winter when the entire design lay open on one desk, and even then it was a design, not a doctrine. Whoever has seen the design cannot honestly go back to the fiction, and that emancipation from a fiction may be the manuscript's most useful gift to the movement that inherited it.

## Compression as evidence: the first published instalment beside the notebooks

Set the notebooks beside the first thing Marx published from them, and the relation between thinking and having thought becomes measurable. The Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy, issued in 1859, covers the commodity and money, the ground of the notebooks' opening campaign, and it does so in a fraction of the space, with the polemics pruned, the trials resolved, the Hegelian weather cleared, and the argument presented as a sequence of determinations that a reader can test. Whole notebook expeditions shrink to paragraphs. Positions the notebooks entertained for pages are absent without trace. The comparison is the cleanest demonstration available of what this author did between notebook and book, and it runs in one direction with remarkable consistency: towards economy, towards the suppression of everything that served only the writer's own clarification, towards a text that asks to be judged rather than overheard.

The demonstration has a use for every reader of the later Capital, because the same compression operated there at a larger scale, and a reader who has watched it operate once, on material small enough to hold in both hands, will read the great book with calibrated expectations. Passages of Capital that feel abrupt are often compressions of trials the notebooks preserve. Passages that feel exhaustive often mark the places where the author decided the trial was over. And the famous absences, the unwritten books, acquire their exact weight: not oversights, not betrayals by editors, but the residue of a standard of finish so demanding that its author could satisfy it, across a lifetime, for the first book's territory alone. There is a standing temptation to regret that standard, to wish the notebooks themselves had been rushed into print in 1859 so that the whole design might exist in something like the notebooks' form. The Contribution is the answer to the temptation. Marx had the notebooks in his drawer and chose, twice over, not to publish them, choosing instead the labour of compression that consumed his remaining decades. An author is entitled to have his choices about his own papers taken seriously by his interpreters, and taking them seriously is, for the last time in this guide, the notebook warning, now visible as a principle of respect rather than a rule of method.

## A note on the title and the names of the thing

Even the manuscript's name is an editorial inheritance, and a reader alert to mediation should know it. Marx left the notebooks untitled as a work. The German title under which they entered print, Grundrisse der Kritik der politischen Okonomie, Outlines of the Critique of Political Economy, draws on his own vocabulary for the project, his letters speak of outlines and sketches for the critique, but as a book title it belongs to the publishing history rather than to an author's presentation. Scholars consequently use the short form, the Grundrisse, the outlines, as a kind of shelf mark, and the shelf mark has acquired the solidity of a title through sheer use. The solidification is harmless in itself and instructive as a symptom. Everything about this text arrives mediated: title from editors, divisions from editors, famous passages named by readers, the Fragment on Machines being itself a reader's coinage rather than a heading in the manuscript. A reader who notices the layers of mediation at the level of the title has been trained, by the smallest possible example, to notice them at the level of the argument, where they matter. The discipline of this guide is therefore not an external standard imposed on an innocent text. It is the text's own condition, visible from its cover inward, and the reader who accepts the condition reads a different and a truer book from the reader who imagines direct access.

The English title history repeats the lesson in miniature. Outlines of the Critique of Political Economy is a faithful rendering and a dead one; nobody calls the book that. The German word survived translation because the text's identity had already formed around it in the scholarship, and because Grundrisse carries a useful ambiguity, between outline, ground plan and groundwork, that the English equivalents each narrow. The ambiguity is apt beyond the editors' intending. The manuscript is all three things by turns: an outline of the critique's design, a ground plan of its architecture, and the groundwork, the quarrying and foundation digging, from which its published portions rose. Readers who keep the triple sense in mind possess a small, portable version of the argument this guide has made at length about quarry, draft and architecture, since the name itself, properly heard, refuses to choose among the three relations, and the refusal is the correct scholarly position in a single word.

## Profit, interest and rent in embryo: the middle notebooks sampled

A sampler needs one worked example of what the unmapped heartland actually feels like, and the notebooks' treatment of the forms in which surplus value appears, profit, interest and rent, serves the purpose honestly. In the published architecture these forms belong to the critique's later reaches, where capital is shown in its surface shapes, the shapes in which it appears to its agents and to ordinary consciousness. In the notebooks the treatment is earlier, rougher and in one respect more revealing, because Marx is working out, in real time, the principle that the surface forms are necessary appearances of the underlying relation rather than illusions pasted over it. Interest-bearing capital fascinates him in these pages precisely because it is the form in which the relation appears most fetishised, money seemingly breeding money without mediation, and his analysis keeps returning to it as the limiting case that any theory of the forms must explain. Rent appears with the historical material still attached, the ground rent of the notebooks being continuous with the Formen's world of landed property, so that the reader watches an economic category and a historical form being thought together before the published work assigns them to separate volumes.

The sampling lesson is about texture. These stretches are neither the introduction's philosophy nor the Fragment's elevation. They are workmanlike, repetitive in the way of a man testing a joint from several angles, and suddenly, at intervals, they open into general reflections on the nature of appearance in social science that no other Marx text states so freely. A reader who samples them with a specific question, how does the manuscript explain that profit appears as a return on total capital rather than as the surplus extracted in production, will find the question answered in the course of being earned, with false starts preserved. A reader who samples them without a question will find them hard going and will draw the false moral that the heartland is dross. The difference between the two experiences is the whole of this guide's practical teaching in one instance. The notebooks are an instrument that answers questions. They are not a spectacle that rewards attendance. Every section of the heartland behaves like the profit and interest stretches: generous to interrogators, mute to tourists, and the strategy of mapped passages plus purposeful sampling is simply that behaviour, generalised into a reading plan and offered to anyone willing to trade the reverent march for a working relationship with a working text.

## A shelf beside the notebooks: what to read alongside

Companionship improves difficult manuscripts, and the Grundrisse has natural companions that a strategic reader can keep within arm's reach. The correspondence between Marx and Engels from the winter of composition belongs first on that shelf, because the letters date the enthusiasms, record the crisis bulletins that spurred the writing, and preserve judgements about the work in progress that no preface ever gave it. A reader who checks a notebook stretch against the letters of its weeks learns to hear the difference between a position being announced to a trusted collaborator and a possibility being turned over in private, and that difference in register is evidence no apparatus can supply. The Contribution of 1859 belongs beside it for the compression exercise described earlier. The first volume of Capital belongs there for the pairing habit. And a reliable biography belongs there too, not for gossip but for chronology, since arguments about these papers constantly turn on who knew what, and had written what, by a particular spring.

One companion deserves singling out because readers rarely think of it: the excerpt notebooks from the years of economic study that preceded the Grundrisse, in which Marx digested the economists he would later argue with. They survive in the complete edition's fourth section, largely untranslated and forbidding, and no general reader is being sent to them. Their existence, though, completes the workshop picture the guide has drawn. The Grundrisse was not the beginning of the labour but its turning point, the moment a decade of other men's books, excerpted and annotated, was melted down and recast as a system in formation. Knowing that the melting had a long preparation changes how the famous winter is imagined: less a bolt of inspiration, more the visible crest of a submerged decade. Readers who carry that picture will pace themselves correctly for the manuscript's difficulties, since a text that condenses ten years of reading will not surrender to ten evenings of it, and the mapped passages are mapped precisely because they are where the condensation is most rewarding per hour spent.

## The physical object: paper, ink, and a notoriously difficult hand

Scholars who have handled the originals describe a forbidding artefact. Marx wrote in a cramped, hurried script that contemporaries already struggled to decipher, filling the pages margin to margin, layering deletions over insertions, sometimes pursuing a single sentence through several restarts before letting it stand provisionally. Editors preparing the Moscow and Berlin printings had first to perform an act of decipherment, and decipherment involves judgement at every doubtful word, which means the printed Grundrisse rests on thousands of small decisions made by readers working under difficult conditions. Occasional misreadings have been identified by later collators, and a handful of debated passages hinge on words that the handwriting renders genuinely uncertain. None of this unsettles the broad architecture of the text, whose arguments run in channels too wide for a misread noun to divert. It does, however, add a material footnote to the guide's recurring theme, since a quotation balanced on one contested word of a hurried manuscript is a quotation built on several storeys of mediation at once: the author's haste, the decipherer's judgement, the translator's choice, and only then the interpreter's gloss.

There is value in picturing the artefact plainly for another reason. Print lends an air of finality to whatever it touches, and a stout paperback volume called by a single name feels like a book that somebody completed. Picturing loose gatherings of ink-covered paper, unbound, untitled, annotated in more than one campaign, restores the proportions that the strategy sections of this guide have assumed throughout. Nobody binds a quarry. Nobody issues a laboratory bench as a monograph. The physical untidiness of the survival is the genre made visible, and readers who keep the image in mind as they turn the tidy printed pages will find the discipline of status-marking easier to sustain, because the object itself keeps reminding them what sort of encounter they are having.

## For students and exam candidates

For students, the Grundrisse answers a specific family of questions, and the family should be learned as such. Questions on Marx's method send the candidate to the introduction and its ascent from the abstract to the concrete, the one place the procedure is stated as procedure. Questions on the young and the mature Marx send the candidate to the publication chronology and the alienation vocabulary of 1857 and 1858, with the notebook warning ready as the evaluative move: the evidence counts, and its manuscript status prices it. Questions on machinery, automation and the future of work send the candidate to the Fragment, cited with its context in the fixed capital analysis and with the autonomist reading named as a reading. In every case the candidate's advantage lies in knowing what kind of text the Grundrisse is, since examiners reward the status point precisely because most scripts omit it. Quote sparingly, prefer the published work for doctrine, use the notebooks for development, method and the plan, and never present a notebook formulation as Marx's settled view without the marker that it comes from private papers of 1857 and 1858.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

For teachers, the manuscript is a gift with a handling warning, and the handling is the lesson. Set the introduction for method, the Formen for historical range, and the Fragment for the seminar that will run itself, but set each with its status stated on the syllabus and in the room, because the Grundrisse teaches the difference between a text and a testament better than any methodological lecture. A productive seminar exercise follows the guide's pairing habit: students place a notebook passage beside its published counterpart and report what the passage of a decade did to it, what survived, what sharpened, what vanished. The exercise teaches editing as thinking, and it inoculates against the extraction habit more durably than exhortation. Teachers should also make the chronology vivid, the Moscow edition swallowed by war, the Berlin edition detonating under a settled periodisation, since students who grasp that interpretations wait on archives have learned the deepest lesson the humanities offer. The autonomist and systematic readings make excellent opposed set texts at advanced level, provided the class is required to locate, for each, the exact pages on which its case rests and the exact pages it must explain away.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

For researchers, the Grundrisse is primary evidence of the first rank about the formation of the critique of political economy, and its correct use is governed by the distinction this guide has pressed throughout, between testimony and doctrine. Cite it for the plan of the six books, for the genesis and transformation of categories, for Marx's reading and his polemical targets, for the method in formation and for the historical imagination at work in the Formen. Corroborate doctrinal claims against the published corpus, and where the notebooks diverge from it, report the divergence as a finding rather than resolving it silently in either direction. The complete edition's apparatus should be consulted for any passage on which an argument turns, since translation smooths what scholarship must sometimes preserve, including cancellations and the physical sequence of the writing. Historians of economic thought will find the money chapters a record of engagement with the monetary debates of the 1850s unmatched in the published work. Journalists and general writers have a narrower duty and should keep it strictly: the notebooks may be described as Marx's private working papers of 1857 and 1858, unpublished in his lifetime, and any striking sentence drawn from them must be introduced as coming from those papers, never as a pronouncement. The Fragment on Machines, in particular, should never be presented as Marx's prediction about a named later technology. It is an argument about a tendency within capital, made in a notebook, and reporting it as anything more definite misinforms the reader in the direction of sensation. Researchers who observe the status discipline will find the manuscript the most rewarding archive in the whole Marx literature. Those who ignore it will find, as several schools have found, that the notebooks obligingly confirm whatever was brought to them, which is the surest sign that the evidence has stopped being consulted and started being used.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the Grundrisse?

The Grundrisse is the name given to the seven private notebooks in which Marx worked out his critique of political economy across 1857 and 1858. Written during a financial crisis he expected to turn revolutionary, the notebooks range across money, capital, pre-capitalist history and machinery, and they contain the ground plan of the whole critique he intended to write in six books. Marx never prepared them for publication. They are a record of his thinking at full stretch, and almost everything else said about them follows from that single fact about their character.

### Q: Was the Grundrisse meant to be published?

No. Marx intended to publish a critique of political economy, and the notebooks were his private preparation for that project, the scaffolding rather than the building. He gave them no title for the public, wrote no preface for them, and withheld even their introduction on the ground that anticipating results before they are proved confuses readers. When he did publish, in the Contribution of 1859 and then in Capital, he worked the notebook material into entirely different and far more disciplined forms. The notebooks' exploratory character, with positions tried and abandoned side by side, is the mark of papers written for self-clarification.

### Q: When did the Grundrisse become available?

Availability arrived in stages. The notebooks were first published in Moscow around 1939, in an edition overtaken by war and read by very few. A Berlin edition in the middle of the 1950s made the text generally available in German, and the serious engagement with it began then. The section on pre-capitalist forms circulated in English in the 1960s, in an edition prepared by Eric Hobsbawm, and the complete English translation by Martin Nicolaus followed in the early 1970s. Each stage admitted a new readership, which is why writers of different generations can seem to describe different books under the one title.

### Q: Which parts of the Grundrisse should you read?

Begin with the three passages that carry the manuscript's modern influence. Read the introduction of 1857 for the method, the rising from the abstract to the concrete. Read the Formen, the section on pre-capitalist forms, for the historical imagination and the account of how communal forms dissolved. Read the Fragment on Machines for the argument about machinery and the general intellect, but read it inside its surrounding analysis of fixed capital rather than as an extract. Then reread the introduction. The remaining several hundred pages on money and capital are a quarry for specific purposes, not a march to be completed from duty.

### Q: What is the Fragment on Machines?

The Fragment on Machines is the name later readers gave to a short passage in the later notebooks, inside the analysis of fixed capital and the circulation of capital. It argues that as machinery and applied science develop, production comes to rest on the general knowledge embodied in the system of production, the general intellect, rather than on the direct labour time of workers, and that this strains labour time as the measure of wealth on which production based on exchange value depends. It occupies a few pages of several hundred, a proportion its modern fame reverses, and it was never singled out by Marx himself.

### Q: Why do autonomists focus on the Grundrisse?

Autonomist thinkers, including Mario Tronti and Antonio Negri, found in the notebooks a Marx centred on the antagonism between capital and living labour, and in the Fragment on Machines a description of production resting on social knowledge that seemed to anticipate knowledge work and automation. Negri's lectures on the manuscript treated it as the summit of Marx's revolutionary theory. Their focus also has a polemical point: the notebooks resist the mechanical and economist readings of Marx that the autonomists opposed. Critics reply that the reading detaches a few pages from their context and promotes a notebook passage into a programme.

### Q: Is the Grundrisse harder than Capital?

The difficulties are of different kinds. Capital is harder in its finished density, its irony, its architectonic demands on memory and patience. The Grundrisse is harder in its unfinishedness: an unsectioned flow, arguments tried and retried, sentences of great length, and no authorial guidance about what is settled. Many readers find the notebook difficulty more approachable once they abandon continuous reading, since sampling the mapped passages is a fair week's work rather than a season's. Neither text is hard in the manner of a textbook with prerequisites. Both are hard in the manner of arguments that refuse to simplify themselves.

### Q: What are the Formen?

The Formen is the short title for the section of the notebooks on forms which precede capitalist production. It examines communal forms in which the individual belongs to the land through membership of a community, and it distinguishes types, among them Asiatic, ancient and Germanic forms, asking of each how property is held, what membership requires, and what inside the form dissolves it. Its destination is the separation of the worker from the conditions of production, the historical presupposition of capital. The section circulated separately in English before the full translation and has its own readership among historians and anthropologists.

### Q: Does the Grundrisse change how you read Capital?

Yes, in documented ways. The notebooks reveal the six-book plan from which Capital grew as the first book's execution, so compressions and postponements in the published work become visible as decisions rather than mysteries. They supply fuller or earlier treatments of topics Capital treats briefly, including money and pre-capitalist history, and they show categories in formation, which recalibrates how finished the published formulations should be taken to be. They also establish that the project exceeded the publications, so silences in Capital, on the state or the world market, cannot simply be read as absences in Marx's thought.

### Q: Which translation of the Grundrisse should you use?

For the complete manuscript in English, use the translation by Martin Nicolaus, the standard version through which the Anglophone debates have been conducted and whose terminology the scholarship presupposes. For the pre-capitalist section in its first English form, with a major historian's framing, the Hobsbawm edition remains valuable. Scholars working on a passage where exact wording carries an argument should consult the German text in the complete historical-critical edition, where the apparatus records the manuscript's state. Judge a translation of this text by its handling of unfinished prose and consistency of terminology, not by novelty.

### Q: How does the notebook warning change the status of the Fragment on Machines?

The notebook warning holds that the Grundrisse records thinking rather than results, so a passage in it may state a position Marx was trying out. Applied to the Fragment, the warning changes the passage from a prophecy or a verdict on the labour theory of value into an exploration of a tendency, written inside an unfinished analysis of fixed capital. Its claims must then be checked against the surrounding pages and against the published work, where the machinery analysis reappears in disciplined form. The passage loses none of its interest under the warning. It loses the false status of a settled announcement, which its extraction from the notebooks had lent it.

### Q: What should a reader separate between the Grundrisse and the published critique?

A reader should separate trials from results, the plan from its execution, and testimony from doctrine. The notebooks show Marx exploring formulations, sketching all six books of his design, and testifying to his method and concerns in 1857 and 1858. The published critique, the Contribution of 1859 and Capital, contains the positions he finished, defended and allowed to stand in public. Where the two agree, the notebook passage explains the published one's genesis. Where they diverge, the divergence is itself a finding about his development, to be reported rather than silently resolved. Keeping the separation is the whole art of using the manuscript well.

### Q: Why did the manuscript's late arrival reorder debates about Marx's method?

Because the debates had been formed on a canon that excluded the notebooks. The periodisation dividing a young, philosophical Marx from a mature, economic one was constructed while the manuscript of 1857 and 1858, saturated with the vocabulary of alienation and with Hegelian method, sat effectively unavailable. Its arrival in the 1950s and in English in the 1970s supplied counter-evidence on the boundary itself and revealed, through the introduction, a statement of method the published works never contained. Rosdolsky's recovery of the plan likewise reordered readings of Capital's structure. Interpretations built without the archive's most awkward document had to be rebuilt once the document circulated.

### Q: How do the Formen passages complicate a simple stages story?

A simple stages story has every society climbing one ladder of modes of production in one order. The Formen surveys communal forms that differ in structure, in the weight of community against household, in the role of the city and of war, and in the internal process by which each dissolves. The forms share presuppositions, above all the individual's relation to the land through a community, but their paths diverge, and dissolution in one produces slavery's world, in another the independent peasant, in another the conditions capital requires. A reader can extract sequences from the passage, but the passage itself keeps offering branches where the ladder model wants rungs.

### Q: What reading strategy suits a seven-notebook manuscript?

A strategic rather than a continuous reading suits it. Start with the introduction for the method, continue with the Formen for the history, then read the Fragment on Machines inside its context in the analysis of fixed capital, and reread the introduction when its terms have acquired content. After that core, sample the money and capital chapters by purpose, tracing a category or checking how a published argument was reached, and read notebook passages beside their published counterparts wherever a counterpart exists. Throughout, apply the notebook warning by asking of every striking passage whether it behaves as a trial or as a result, and annotate with that status marked.
