Sometime around 1650 BCE, a woman living in the eastern Delta city of Avaris buried a relative. She laid the body in a mudbrick chamber built to an Egyptian plan, set Egyptian pottery beside it for the funerary meal, and pinned the burial shroud with a bronze toggle pin of a type made in the Levant. Outside the chamber door, in a pit cut for the purpose, she or her family buried a pair of donkeys, a rite no Egyptian had ever practiced and every Canaanite would have recognized. She was, on the evidence of that single grave, neither wholly Egyptian nor wholly foreign. She was a resident of a city where foreign rule and Egyptian identity had stopped being opposites and started being neighbors. Her grave is the reason this article exists, because the standard story of the period cannot accommodate her at all.

The standard story says that a horde of Asiatic invaders stormed Egypt, burned its temples, enslaved its people, and held the Nile in a grip of terror for a century until Theban liberators drove them into the sea. The standard story comes almost entirely from Egyptians and Greeks writing centuries after the fact, with reasons to say exactly that. The archaeology of Avaris says something much stranger and much more useful: that Egyptians and their foreign rulers lived alongside each other, borrowed from each other in both directions, worshipped in adjacent precincts, wrote in the same script, and produced a hybrid court culture that was neither purely Egyptian nor purely Levantine. And then the Egyptians who overthrew that arrangement spent the next four centuries telling everyone it had been an atrocity.

![Foreign rule and Egyptian identity under the Hyksos, explained - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-44.webp)

Both things are true, and the gap between them is the subject here. Foreign rule did not erase Egyptian identity. It did something more interesting: it forced Egyptians to state what that identity was. Before the Hyksos, Egyptianness was mostly an unexamined default, the condition of living in the valley under a king who kept order. After the Hyksos, Egyptianness was a claim, an argument, and eventually a program of state policy backed by an army. The thesis this article defends is that Egyptian identity sharpened precisely against the Hyksos, which means foreign rule helped define what Egyptians thought it meant to be Egyptian. Call it the identity-by-contrast thesis. It is not a comfortable finding for anyone who wants a clean story of oppression, and it is not a comfortable finding for anyone who wants a rosy story of multicultural harmony either.

## What foreign rule actually meant in Second Intermediate Egypt

The Second Intermediate Period ran from roughly 1650 to 1550 BCE, and the word "period" flatters it, because for most of that century Egypt was not one thing at all. Three powers held three pieces of the Nile. In the eastern Delta, kings that the Egyptian language called *heqau khasut*, "rulers of foreign lands," ruled from Avaris, the site now excavated as Tell el-Dab'a. Greek writers mangled that title into "Hyksos," and the mangled version stuck so hard that a job description became an ethnicity. In Upper Egypt, Theban kings of the Sixteenth and Seventeenth Dynasties held the valley from somewhere near Cusae southward. And beyond the First Cataract, the kingdom of Kush, ruled from Kerma, held Nubia and had taken over the Middle Kingdom's fortress line. The full political anatomy of that three-way split belongs to [the guide to the Hyksos and the Second Intermediate Age](/2002/07/01/hyksos-second-intermediate-period/), which owns the period's orientation questions; what matters here is what that split did to the people living inside it.

The word "occupation" carries assumptions that the evidence does not support. An occupation implies an outside army arriving, imposing itself on a settled population, and administering a hostile territory through force. The Delta situation was different in kind. The Levantine community at Avaris did not arrive with the Fifteenth Dynasty. It had been there for generations, since at least the Twelfth Dynasty, when Egyptian kings themselves settled Asiatics in the eastern Delta as workers, sailors, soldiers, and craftsmen. Tell el-Dab'a grew from a modest Middle Kingdom settlement with a Levantine population into a large, wealthy port city, and only after that growth did its rulers assert kingship over the surrounding Delta. The takeover mechanism, the settle-then-seize sequence and the collapse of Thirteenth Dynasty central authority that made it possible, is the business of [the account of how the Hyksos took over Egypt](/2002/08/01/how-hyksos-took-over-egypt/). The point for identity is that by the time there was a Hyksos king, there had been a mixed Delta population for a century and a half. Foreign rule in Egypt was not foreigners arriving. It was residents ascending.

### Was all of Egypt actually under foreign rule?

No. Firm Hyksos control covered the eastern Delta and the northern approaches, with looser authority or vassal arrangements through Middle Egypt to roughly Cusae. Thebes ruled the south independently, and Kush held Nubia. Most Egyptians never saw a Hyksos official, and a Theban peasant's life changed less than the propaganda suggests.

That geographical fact governs everything else. When a Theban king later shouted that a foreigner sat in Avaris while a Nubian sat in Kush and he himself sat squeezed between them, he was describing a real political humiliation, not a real experience of subjection. He was not paying a house tax to an Asiatic. He was sharing a river with one. The distinction sounds pedantic until you notice how much of the later rhetoric depends on collapsing it. The Egyptian memory of the Hyksos speaks of an entire land defiled. The archaeology and the geography speak of a partitioned country in which the northern third was ruled by a dynasty of Levantine descent and the southern third was ruled by a dynasty that resented it.

Even inside the Hyksos zone, "rule" meant something specific and limited by the standards of the pharaonic state. The Fifteenth Dynasty did not attempt to remake Egyptian society. It did not replace the language of administration, abolish the priesthoods, redistribute land on ideological lines, or install a foreign legal code. It taxed, it traded, it built, it recorded, and it did all of that in Egyptian. A Delta farmer in 1600 BCE paid his grain to an assessor whose title was Egyptian, whose seal was Egyptian, and whose script was Egyptian, on behalf of a king whose throne name invoked the Egyptian sun god Re. What had changed at the top had barely changed at the bottom. This is precisely why the period is so hard to see: foreign rule that governs through the existing machinery leaves very little foreign residue in the record of ordinary life.

### Who were the Hyksos kings, and what did they call themselves?

They were kings of Levantine descent ruling from Avaris, and they called themselves pharaohs. Names like Khyan and Apepi carry non-Egyptian roots, but their throne names were fully Egyptian formations built on the god Re, written in cartouches, with the traditional titles of Egyptian kingship attached.

Khyan's prenomen was Seuserenre. Apepi carried Aauserre and, at other points in a long reign, other Re-compounded throne names. These are not the names of men trying to advertise their foreignness. They are the names of men claiming the oldest and most prestigious job title in the ancient world and performing that claim in its own vocabulary. A Hyksos king wrote "Son of Re" beside his name. He wrote "the Good God." He wrote "given life, stability, and dominion." He commissioned scarabs bearing his cartouche in the same formulas a Twelfth Dynasty king would have used. The specific question of where these kings and their people came from, and whether "invasion" or "immigration" describes their arrival, is decided in [the assessment of whether the Hyksos were invaders or immigrants](/2003/03/01/were-hyksos-invaders-or-immigrants/), which owns that debate. Here the relevant fact is narrower and sharper: they governed as Egyptians governed, in Egyptian, and they wanted the record to say so.

## Where Egyptians sat in the Hyksos order

Every account of foreign rule eventually has to answer the question of what happened to the ruled. The Egyptian answer, delivered centuries later, was that the ruled were crushed. The evidence answers differently, and the difference is not a matter of interpretation but of what is physically present in the ground and on the objects.

Start with the administration. The Hyksos state ran on Egyptian bureaucratic forms, which means it ran on Egyptian bureaucrats or on people trained exactly as Egyptian bureaucrats were trained. Sealings and scarabs from Avaris and from Hyksos-period levels elsewhere carry Egyptian administrative titles: treasurer, chancellor, steward, overseer. The best-attested of these officials is a treasurer named Har, whose name and title appear on a substantial number of scarabs, and whose function was the classic Egyptian one of managing the royal storehouse. Some officials bear Egyptian names, some bear Semitic names, and some families appear to shift from one to the other across generations. There is no sign of a purge of the Egyptian administrative class. There is every sign that the Fifteenth Dynasty needed those people and kept them.

### Did Egyptians hold real positions under the Hyksos?

Yes. Hyksos-period sealings carry the standard Egyptian administrative titles, and the state was staffed by scribes trained in Egyptian conventions. Whether an individual official was ethnically Egyptian or Levantine is often impossible to determine, because names and titles crossed the line in both directions within single families.

That last point deserves more weight than it usually receives. Onomastic evidence, the study of personal names, is the closest thing the period offers to a census of identity, and it is a terrible census. Names are chosen, not inherited biologically. A man with a Semitic name may name his son Sobekhotep, and a man with an Egyptian name may name his daughter something Canaanite. What the name data shows at Avaris is not two sealed populations but a name pool that mixes, with Egyptian theophoric names appearing in families whose earlier generations carried Levantine ones. Names track fashion, ambition, and belonging. They do not track blood. Anyone who tries to sort the Hyksos-period Delta into Egyptians and foreigners using names alone will produce a chart that looks precise and means very little.

Now consider the class structure itself. Egypt under the Fifteenth Dynasty retained the fundamental shape it had held since the Old Kingdom: a king at the apex, a small literate administrative and priestly elite beneath him, a broader stratum of skilled craftsmen, soldiers, and minor officials below that, and a vast agricultural population at the base whose labor produced the grain surplus everything else consumed. Foreign rule did not invert this pyramid. It changed the ethnic composition of its top layer and left the rest structurally intact. The comparison worth making is with what a genuinely transformative conquest looks like. There is no Hyksos-period land redistribution visible in the record, no new category of subject population defined in law, no displacement of Egyptian temple estates, and no evidence of a legal disability attached to being Egyptian.

### Were Egyptians reduced to slaves under foreign kings?

No. No text or excavation shows a general enslavement of Egyptians under the Hyksos. Egyptian officials held office, Egyptian temples continued, and Egyptian burials continued in Egyptian forms. Slavery existed in Egypt in the period as it had before, but it was not an ethnic condition imposed on Egyptians by their Delta rulers.

The claim of mass enslavement enters the record through Josephus, the Jewish historian of the first century CE, who quoted the Egyptian priest Manetho, who wrote in the third century BCE, roughly thirteen centuries after the events. Josephus quoted Manetho because he wanted to argue that the Hyksos were the Israelites and that his people had ruled Egypt before being expelled. Manetho wrote his account under Ptolemaic patronage for readers who wanted Egypt's history packaged as a sequence of dynasties with moral shape. Neither man had access to Fifteenth Dynasty archives. What Josephus preserves is a Greek-language summary of a Ptolemaic-era summary of a Ramesside-era memory of a Seventeenth Dynasty grievance. Treating it as testimony about daily life under Apepi is like reconstructing a medieval village from a nineteenth-century novel about it.

## Avaris: the city where two ways of living shared a street

Tell el-Dab'a is the single most important site for this question, because it is the one place where the two cultures can be watched interacting stratigraphically, layer by layer, over generations. Decades of excavation there, most extensively under the Austrian mission led by Manfred Bietak, have produced a picture of the Hyksos capital that no ancient text prepared anyone for.

The city was large, prosperous, and organized on Egyptian urban lines: mudbrick housing in blocks, a harbor connected to the Pelusiac branch of the Nile, storage facilities, workshops, cemeteries interleaved with the housing rather than separated from it. The scale of the port and the volume of imported material make Avaris one of the great commercial cities of the eastern Mediterranean in its era, and the commercial machinery behind that wealth is the subject of [the study of the trade networks of Hyksos Egypt](/2003/01/01/trade-networks-of-hyksos-egypt/). What concerns identity is the texture of the place: what people built, where they buried their dead, and which rites they performed while doing it.

The housing at Avaris is a study in convergence. Early levels show Levantine house plans, the Middle Bronze Age "Mittelsaal" type with a central hall, standing alongside Egyptian plans. Over time the distinction blurs. Houses take on mixed features. The architectural signature of foreignness fades not because foreigners left but because the two building traditions merged in the hands of builders who knew both. The same happens in pottery. The earliest Levantine levels are dominated by imported and locally made Middle Bronze forms. Later levels show Egyptian forms made by potters who had learned Levantine shaping, and Levantine forms made in Egyptian fabrics. Ceramics are the most conservative material culture there is, because cooking habits change slowly. When the pots merge, the kitchens have merged.

### What did daily life look like in the Hyksos capital?

Mixed. Residents of Avaris lived in mudbrick housing that combined Egyptian and Levantine plans, cooked in vessels of both traditions, worked in a port trading across the Mediterranean, and buried their dead with rites drawn from both cultures. The city was neither an Egyptian town with foreigners in it nor a Canaanite colony on the Nile.

Burial is where the mixing is most visible, because burial is where people are least willing to compromise. The Avaris cemeteries contain tomb chapels built to Egyptian designs, holding bodies laid out in Egyptian positions, accompanied by Egyptian funerary pottery, and equipped with Egyptian-style offering arrangements. They also contain equipment and rites with no Egyptian precedent at all. Weapons appear in male graves, a warrior-burial practice standard in the Middle Bronze Levant and alien to Egyptian funerary custom, where a man's tomb goods signaled office and provision rather than martial identity. Donkeys are buried in pairs at tomb entrances, a Levantine rite associated with status and possibly with the animals that carried a merchant's wealth. Servant burials appear beside elite graves. Toggle pins, a Levantine dress fastening, turn up in graves furnished otherwise like an Egyptian's.

The correct reading of these graves is not "foreigners buried in Egypt" or "Egyptians adopting foreign rites." It is that the people of Avaris had built a funerary practice of their own by selecting from two menus. A family could bury a father with a dagger in an Egyptian chapel and see no contradiction, because the chapel expressed one thing about the dead man and the dagger expressed another. Identity in that cemetery was not a single dial set between Egyptian and Levantine. It was a set of choices made rite by rite.

Isotope work on burials from the site has sharpened this further. Strontium isotope analysis, which reads the geological signature of the water a person drank during childhood from their tooth enamel and bone, has been applied to Tell el-Dab'a burials and has found a substantial non-local component in the population, with a notably higher proportion of non-local signatures among women than among men. Two conclusions follow that no text would have given us. First, the population of Avaris was drawing people in from outside the Nile Valley across a long span, including before the Fifteenth Dynasty existed, which fits the settle-then-rule model rather than a sudden conquest. Second, a great deal of that inward movement was female, which points to marriage as one of the mechanisms of population mixing rather than to armies.

### Did Egyptians and the Hyksos population intermarry?

The evidence points that way. Isotope analysis of Avaris burials shows a high proportion of non-local individuals, especially women, consistent with marriage networks drawing partners across the Delta and the Levant. Names within single families shift between Egyptian and Semitic forms across generations, which fits mixed households.

Marriage is the quietest of all historical mechanisms and the most consequential. Armies leave destruction layers. Treaties leave inscriptions. Marriage leaves grandchildren with mixed names and a household that cooks two cuisines. If the mixing at Avaris was driven substantially by marriage, then the Hyksos period was not a foreign elite floating on top of an Egyptian sea. It was a genuinely creole society producing children who would have found the later Egyptian question "are you one of us or one of them" not offensive but incoherent.

## Family, gender, and the life course under a divided country

Reconstructing family life in the Second Intermediate Period is harder than for almost any other era, because the period produced few of the tomb biographies and household archives that make Middle and New Kingdom family life legible. What exists is skeletal, funerary, and artifactual, which means the picture is real but coarse.

The basic shape of the Egyptian household did not change under the Hyksos. Marriage remained a social and economic arrangement rather than a state-registered contract with religious sanction. Property moved with women in ways that would have startled most contemporary Bronze Age societies, and Egyptian women retained the capacity to own, inherit, and dispose of property. There is no evidence that the Fifteenth Dynasty legislated on any of this, and no reason it would have. A dynasty that adopted Egyptian titles, Egyptian script, and Egyptian administrative forms had no motive to disturb Egyptian family law.

What did change was the mixing described above, and the range of what a household could look like. In the Delta, a household might combine Egyptian and Levantine practice in food, dress, and rite. In Upper Egypt, under Theban rule, households looked as they had under [the Middle Kingdom](/2001/09/01/middle-kingdom-of-egypt-guide/), poorer and more militarized than in the kingdom's confident years but culturally continuous.

Skeletal evidence from the period sketches a life course that was hard everywhere. Adult stature and dental health among the non-elite population reflect a diet heavy in coarse-ground grain, with the characteristic tooth wear that sand-laden bread produced across Egyptian history. Childhood mortality was high, as in every pre-industrial society. Healed fractures and degenerative joint changes appear in patterns consistent with lifelong manual labor. Nothing in the skeletal record suggests that the Delta population under foreign kings was systematically worse fed or worse used than the Theban population under Egyptian ones. The famine and hardship rhetoric that later texts attach to the era is not visible in the bones.

### How did women's lives change under foreign rule?

Very little in structure, more in composition. Egyptian women retained property rights and household standing, and the Hyksos state did not legislate on family matters. The visible change is demographic: a large share of the non-local population at Avaris was female, which suggests women moved into the Delta through marriage networks in significant numbers.

Elite women are slightly more visible. A woman named Tany, identified in the record as a king's sister and connected to Apepi, is attested on objects from the site, which tells us that the Hyksos royal house maintained the kind of titled female relatives an Egyptian royal house maintained, and used Egyptian terminology to describe them. That is a small fact with a large implication. When a Hyksos king's sister carries an Egyptian court title, the court has adopted not just Egyptian administration but Egyptian ideas about how a royal family should be shaped and named.

## Work, food, housing, and health in a Delta under foreign kings

The material conditions of ordinary life in Hyksos Egypt were set by the Nile, not by the ethnicity of the man in the palace, and the Nile did not care who wore the double crown. Grain agriculture on the flood plain remained the engine of everything. The annual inundation deposited silt, the fields were sown after the water withdrew, the harvest came in spring, and assessors measured it and took the state's share. That cycle had run for two thousand years before the Fifteenth Dynasty and would run for two thousand after it.

What foreign rule did change, in the Delta specifically, was the range of work available and the goods that passed through people's hands. Avaris was a port with reach. Cypriot pottery arrives in quantity. Levantine storage jars arrive with their contents, which means oils, wine, and resins moving in bulk. Metals move. The workshops of the city produced bronze work at a scale and technical level that made Delta metalworking a distinct industry rather than a service to agriculture. A Delta family in the Hyksos century had a better chance of a livelihood in transport, warehousing, shipbuilding, or metalwork than a family in Middle Egypt did, and a much better chance than a family in the Theban south, whose economy had contracted with its political horizon.

Food shows the same pattern of mixture that the pots do. The staples did not change: emmer wheat and barley, bread and beer, onions, lentils, fish from the Nile and the Delta lakes, fowl from the marshes. To that base the Levantine connection added, in the Delta, a stronger presence of imported oils and wine, and a set of cooking vessels suited to Levantine methods. The archaeobotanical and faunal record from the site shows sheep and goat playing a larger dietary role than they typically did in Upper Egyptian sites, which is what one would expect of a population with Levantine pastoral roots living in a Delta where the grazing supported it. Diet is the most intimate index of culture there is, and at Avaris the diet had merged.

### What did people eat in Hyksos-period Avaris?

Bread and beer from emmer and barley formed the base, as everywhere in Egypt. To this the Delta added Nile and lake fish, marsh fowl, and a larger share of sheep and goat than Upper Egyptian sites show, plus imported oils and wine arriving through the port. The cooking equipment mixed Egyptian and Levantine forms.

Housing, as noted, converged. Health, so far as bone can testify, was unremarkable for the Bronze Age: hard, short, marked by labor, with the specific insults that Egyptian life delivered, including the parasitic burden of a population living beside irrigation water and the joint damage of lifting and carrying. There is no Hyksos-period health catastrophe in the skeletal record, and no Hyksos-period prosperity boom in it either. Ordinary bodies wore out at the ordinary rate.

The important negative finding is that no site in the Hyksos zone shows the archaeological signature of a conquered population. There is no widespread destruction horizon at the transition. There are no burned temples where later texts claim burned temples. There is no abrupt population replacement, no abandonment of Egyptian settlements, no sudden impoverishment of Egyptian grave goods. Whatever happened to Egypt when the Fifteenth Dynasty rose, it did not happen the way sacked cities happen.

## Religion: Seth of Avaris and the arithmetic of gods

Religion is the sharpest test of the two-way blend, because religion is where an ancient state declared what it was. If foreign rule had meant cultural imposition, the gods would show it first.

The Hyksos kings worshipped Seth. That fact has been used to argue both that they were rapacious foreigners who elevated Egypt's god of disorder, and that they were thoroughly Egyptianized rulers who adopted an Egyptian god. Both readings miss the mechanism. What happened at Avaris was identification: the Levantine storm god, the deity Canaanites called Baal or Hadad, was recognized as the same divine power the Egyptians called Seth, and worshipped under a name that satisfied both. Seth was the obvious Egyptian counterpart. He was the god of storms, of the desert, of foreign lands, of violent power. His iconography could take Levantine attributes, and the Seth of Avaris appears in forms carrying the tall conical headdress and the streamers of a Syrian god.

This is not a foreign god replacing an Egyptian one, and it is not an Egyptian god swallowing a foreign one. It is *interpretatio*, the standard ancient practice of treating your neighbor's god as your own god under a different name. It runs in both directions by definition. A Canaanite in Avaris could pray to Baal and call him Seth. An Egyptian in Avaris could pray to Seth and see nothing foreign in it, because Seth had been in the Egyptian pantheon since the Predynastic era, had been a royal patron under the Second Dynasty king Peribsen, and had been the divine partner in the Osiris myth that structured Egyptian ideas about kingship and death. The Hyksos did not import a god. They found a shared one.

### Did the Hyksos suppress the Egyptian gods?

There is no evidence of it. The Fifteenth Dynasty elevated Seth as its dynastic patron, but Seth was an Egyptian god with a history stretching back to the Predynastic era. Other Egyptian cults continued, Egyptian religious formulas appear on Hyksos royal objects, and no temple destruction layer has been found matching the later Egyptian accusation.

The continuity of the Seth cult at Avaris after the Hyksos fell is the single most telling piece of evidence, and it comes from an unexpected source. The Ramesside kings of the Nineteenth Dynasty, whose family came from the eastern Delta, honored Seth of Avaris and counted the establishment of his cult there as an event worth commemorating. The monument known as the 400 Year Stela, set up by Ramesses II, marks four centuries since the founding of that cult, placing its origin squarely in the era of Levantine settlement in the Delta. Read that carefully. The dynasty of Seti I and Ramesses II, whose very names honored Seth, treated the Delta Seth cult as venerable heritage rather than as foreign contamination, four hundred years after the era the same culture's propaganda described as a defilement. Egypt's official memory of the Hyksos was not merely biased. It was inconsistent with Egypt's own religious practice.

Egyptian religious formulas appear on Hyksos royal material as a matter of course. A Hyksos scarab wishing its owner life, stability, and dominion is invoking Egyptian concepts through Egyptian words in Egyptian script. A Hyksos king styling himself Son of Re is making a theological claim about solar kingship that only makes sense inside Egyptian religion. If the Fifteenth Dynasty had wanted to be understood as conquerors imposing an alien order, they had every tool available to say so and used none of them.

What the Hyksos did add was a Levantine religious presence with its own architecture. The temple precinct at Tell el-Dab'a includes broad-room temple plans of Middle Bronze Levantine type, with the offering practices that went with them, standing in the same city as Egyptian cult installations and Egyptian mortuary chapels. The gods of the two traditions were housed in the same town, in buildings each culture would have recognized. That is the physical form of a religious accommodation, and it is the sort of thing that later polemic cannot describe without collapsing.

## Script, scribes, and the mathematics of accommodation

If the Hyksos had wanted to erase Egyptian identity, script was the lever. Language and writing carry a culture's memory, its law, its literature, and its administrative capacity. Conquerors who mean to remake a people start there.

The Fifteenth Dynasty did the opposite. It wrote in Egyptian, in hieroglyphs for monuments and in hieratic for documents, using Egyptian formulas, Egyptian titles, and Egyptian dating by regnal year. It did more than tolerate Egyptian scribal culture. It patronized it. The clearest single proof is the Rhind Mathematical Papyrus, the most substantial mathematical text to survive from ancient Egypt. Its scribe, Ahmose, states that he copied it in the thirty-third regnal year of a king whose name is that of the Hyksos ruler Apepi, and that his exemplar was an older text of Middle Kingdom date. Sit with what that sentence contains. A scribe working under a Hyksos king, dating by that king's reign, copied a Twelfth Dynasty mathematical treatise so that Egyptian scribal training could continue. The transmission of Egyptian mathematics across the Second Intermediate Period ran directly through the Hyksos court.

### Why did the Hyksos write in Egyptian?

Because Egyptian was the language of the state they had taken over, and because writing in it was the way to be a legitimate king of it. Hyksos administration used Egyptian script, titles, and regnal dating. Court patronage extended to Egyptian scholarship, including the copying of the Rhind Mathematical Papyrus under Apepi.

The Rhind Papyrus is not an isolated case, merely the loudest one. Egyptian literary transmission across the period shows the same continuity. The corpus of Middle Kingdom literature survived to be read and recopied in the New Kingdom, which means it survived the century of division, which means someone was copying it. Copies made in the Second Intermediate Period are part of that chain. A culture whose script was being suppressed does not hand its classical literature intact to the next era.

This has a consequence for the identity question that is easy to miss. Because the Hyksos wrote Egyptian, they are almost invisible in the written record as foreigners. Their own inscriptions do not say "we are Levantines ruling Egypt." They say what Egyptian royal inscriptions say. If we had only the texts the Fifteenth Dynasty itself produced, and not the archaeology of Avaris and not the polemic of their enemies, we would struggle to know they were foreign at all. Which means the foreignness of the Hyksos, as a fact of the historical record, is largely a fact supplied by their opponents and their excavators, not by themselves. That asymmetry is the deepest source of the distortion in every popular account of the period.

## The court: pharaonic titulary in foreign mouths

A Hyksos king's throne room is a useful place to stand, because it is where the two identities were performed at once. He held the office of pharaoh with its full theological apparatus: the god's son, the lord of the Two Lands, the one who gives offerings, the guarantor of order against chaos. He also held a Levantine dynastic identity legible to his own kin and to the Canaanite rulers he corresponded with.

The reach of that court was real. Objects bearing the name of Khyan have been found far outside Egypt: at Knossos in Crete, at Hattusa in Anatolia, and at sites in the Levant and Mesopotamia. The precise mechanism behind each find is debated, and the objects are portable, so the distribution shows contact rather than empire. But the pattern is consistent with a Delta court plugged into the diplomatic and commercial circuits of the eastern Mediterranean in a way no Egyptian court had been before. The Middle Kingdom had traded with Byblos and mined in Sinai. The Hyksos court sat inside the Levantine world as a member of it rather than as an occasional visitor to it.

This mattered for Egyptian identity in a way the Egyptians would only understand later. The Fifteenth Dynasty demonstrated that Egypt could be run as a Levantine power, connected northward and eastward, oriented toward the sea and the trade routes rather than inward toward the valley. That was a live alternative model of what Egypt might be, and the Theban kings who destroyed the Hyksos would go on to adopt a version of it while denying that they had. The imperial New Kingdom, projecting force into Syria and treating the Levant as a sphere of Egyptian interest, is unthinkable without the geographic and technological reorientation the Hyksos century produced. The military side of that inheritance, the chariot, the composite bow, the khopesh, and the tactical system that came with them, is the province of [the account of how the Hyksos changed Egyptian warfare](/2003/04/01/how-hyksos-changed-egyptian-warfare/), and the technology itself is treated in [the article on the chariot and Bronze Age Egypt](/2002/09/01/chariot-and-bronze-age-egypt/).

### Did the Hyksos kings claim to be pharaohs?

Yes, completely. They wrote their names in cartouches, took Egyptian throne names built on the sun god Re, used the traditional royal titles including Son of Re and the Good God, dated documents by their own regnal years, and had scribes address them in the standard formulas of Egyptian kingship.

The court was, in short, a hybrid institution that presented an Egyptian face and possessed a Levantine spine. Nothing about it suggests a program of cultural domination. Everything about it suggests a dynasty that wanted to be accepted as Egyptian royalty and worked at it. The tragedy for the Hyksos, and the making of the Egyptian identity that followed, is that the effort failed. They spoke Egypt's language fluently and Egypt never conceded that they had learned it.

## The foreign-rule interaction table

The blend was not uniform. Some areas of life merged almost completely, some merged partially, and a few stayed stubbornly separate. Sorting them out is the difference between saying "the cultures mixed" and saying something a reader can use. Two patterns fall out of that table immediately, and both are counterintuitive.

The first is that the Hyksos adopted far more from Egypt than Egypt adopted from the Hyksos in the sphere of high culture, which is exactly the reverse of what the label "foreign rule" leads people to expect. In kingship, script, administration, religion's official face, and burial architecture, the Fifteenth Dynasty moved toward Egypt and Egypt did not move toward them. The rulers assimilated harder than the ruled.

The second is that in the one sphere where Egypt did adopt aggressively, military technology, the adoption was total, permanent, and transformative. The chariot did not merely enter Egyptian warfare; it restructured Egyptian aristocracy, Egyptian royal iconography, and Egyptian foreign policy. Every New Kingdom pharaoh depicted charging in a chariot, from Thutmose III to Ramesses II, is riding a Hyksos-era inheritance. Egypt's response to the humiliation of foreign rule was to arm itself with the tools of the people who had humiliated it. That is the shape of the whole relationship in one object.

## Law and the state's reach into ordinary life

The Egyptian state had always touched daily life through three instruments: the assessment and collection of the harvest, the corvee obligation to labor on state projects, and the local administration of disputes by officials whose authority came from the king. Under the Hyksos, all three continued in recognizable form, because all three were the machinery by which any Egyptian government fed itself.

Taxation ran on grain. The Fifteenth Dynasty's assessors used Egyptian measures, Egyptian sealing practice, and Egyptian scribal record-keeping, which is why their titles survive on scarabs and why their existence is not in doubt. Whether the rate was heavier or lighter than under the Thirteenth Dynasty is unknown and probably unknowable; no tax registers from the period survive. What can be said is that a state which invested in port infrastructure, temple building, and a large scribal apparatus was extracting a surplus efficiently, and that the population of the Delta appears to have grown rather than fled during the century, which is not what happens under ruinous extraction.

Corvee labor and state building projects are attested by the fact of the buildings. Avaris was constructed and expanded, its harbor maintained, its defenses raised. Someone did that work under someone's direction. The construction record does not distinguish Egyptian from Levantine hands, which is itself an answer of a kind: the workforce was the Delta workforce, mixed like the rest of the population.

### How far did Hyksos authority reach into an Egyptian village?

Through the same channels any Egyptian government used: grain assessment, labor obligation, and local officials with royal authority. Villages in the firm Hyksos zone dealt with Egyptian-titled officials writing Egyptian records. In Middle Egypt, arrangements were looser, run through local rulers who owed Avaris allegiance rather than through direct administration.

That last arrangement matters more than its brevity suggests, and it is the source of one of the period's most awkward facts. Middle Egypt was held not by Hyksos garrisons but by Egyptian local rulers who accepted Hyksos overlordship. When Kamose of Thebes drove north, he attacked Neferusi, and his account names the man holding it as an Egyptian, Teti son of Pepi, described as holding the town for the Asiatics. Kamose's rhetoric against him is not the rhetoric one uses against a foreign enemy. It is the rhetoric one uses against a traitor.

Consider what that implies about identity in 1550 BCE. Egyptians in Middle Egypt had made a rational political accommodation with the Delta power, as provincial elites have done under every distant overlord in history. From Avaris, they were loyal vassals. From Thebes, they were collaborators who had sold Egypt to foreigners. The category "Egyptian" was doing no work at all in predicting whose side a man was on, and the Theban kings had to invent a version of that category in which it did. Which is precisely what they set about doing.

## Thebes: being Egyptian in the half of Egypt that foreigners did not rule

The southern kingdom is the other half of this story, and it is where the identity in question was actually manufactured.

Materially, Theban Egypt in the Second Intermediate Period was a diminished thing. Its royal tombs were small. Its building program was modest. Its coffins, the distinctive rishi type with their feathered decoration, are the work of a craft tradition making do. Its kings were buried at Dra Abu el-Naga in a necropolis whose scale would have embarrassed a Twelfth Dynasty nomarch. The Theban state controlled the valley from around Cusae to the First Cataract and had lost the Nubian trade routes to Kush, which meant it had lost gold, ivory, ebony, and the leverage that came with them. Compared to the wealth of Avaris, Thebes was poor.

Ideologically, Thebes was doing something no Egyptian state had done before. It was constructing a theory of Egypt as a thing that could be lost, and therefore a thing that had to be defined and defended. The Old Kingdom had not needed such a theory, because no one had ever taken Egypt. The Middle Kingdom had not needed it either; its reunification was a matter of internal factions, Egyptians against Egyptians, and the winner's propaganda described the loser as a rebel rather than as an alien. Thebes in the Seventeenth Dynasty faced a rival who could be described as neither an Egyptian rebel nor a foreign neighbor. Apepi was in Egypt, ruling Egyptians, calling himself pharaoh, and writing in hieroglyphs. The only way to make him an enemy was to make foreignness itself the disqualification.

### What did being Egyptian mean to a Theban in the Seventeenth Dynasty?

It meant something new: a claim of exclusive right to the land against people who lived in it and ruled part of it. Theban royal texts defined Egypt as a unity that a foreigner could not legitimately hold, whatever titles he took. That definition was not a description of an existing consensus. It was an argument being made.

The Kamose stelae are where the argument gets its voice. Kamose, the last Theban king before the war's end, records his complaint in terms that no earlier Egyptian king would have used: he sits as ruler while an Asiatic holds one part and a Nubian another, each man with his slice of Egypt, and he cannot pass by them as far as Memphis. The rhetorical move in that passage is subtle and enormous. Kamose is not saying his enemies are strong or wicked. He is saying that the arrangement itself, the sharing, is intolerable because of who the sharers are. The offense is not conquest. The offense is partition with foreigners.

His court is recorded as having advised against the war, on the sensible grounds that the arrangement worked, that the Theban cattle grazed in peace, and that a fight would risk everything for a principle. Whether the recorded council debate happened as written or is a rhetorical construction, its presence in a royal text is revealing. A king who has to answer the argument "but we are doing fine" is a king whose war is ideological rather than defensive. Kamose's answer is that he will not sit beside an Asiatic and be called a ruler alongside one. The war he launched, and that his successor finished, is narrated in [the account of the war that drove the Hyksos from Egypt](/2002/10/01/war-that-drove-out-the-hyksos/), and the king who completed it and founded the dynasty that followed is treated in [the profile of Ahmose I](/2002/11/01/ahmose-i-founder-of-new-kingdom/).

Here is what matters for identity. The Theban kings did not fight the Hyksos because Egyptians were suffering under them. They fought because a definition of Egypt that they had constructed made the Hyksos intolerable in principle. And when they won, that definition became the official self-understanding of the most powerful state Egypt would ever build.

## The identity-by-contrast thesis

Every account of this period has to explain a paradox, and most do not even notice it is there. The paradox is this: the century of foreign rule produced a two-way cultural blend of unusual depth, and the aftermath of foreign rule produced the most aggressively self-defining, most militarized, most foreigner-conscious Egyptian state in the whole pharaonic record. Blending should produce openness. It produced its opposite. Why?

The identity-by-contrast thesis is the answer offered here. Egyptian identity, before the Second Intermediate Period, was largely tacit. It consisted of living in the valley, speaking the language, honoring the gods, and being subject to the king who kept order against the chaos outside. It did not require articulation, because nothing had contested it. There were foreigners in Egyptian ideology, arranged in the Nine Bows beneath the king's feet and cursed in the Execration Texts, but they were outside, and their outsideness was the whole content of the idea. Egyptian identity was a description of a default condition, not a claim.

The Hyksos broke the default. They were foreigners who were not outside. They lived in Egypt, ruled Egyptians, took Egyptian titles, wrote Egyptian script, worshipped an Egyptian god, married Egyptian women, and produced Egyptian-named children. Every marker that had implicitly defined Egyptianness, they possessed. If Egyptianness was a matter of practice, they were Egyptian. And so Egyptianness could no longer be a matter of practice. It had to become a matter of something else, something the Hyksos could not acquire by living well in Egypt.

What the Theban kings invented, under that pressure, was a definition of Egyptian identity based on origin and on exclusive right to the land. Not what you do, but where you and your ancestors come from. Not how you rule, but whether you are entitled to rule at all. That definition was manufactured because it was the only one that made the war necessary. And it was manufactured against a specific group of people whose only real disqualification was descent.

### Why did foreign rule make Egyptian identity stronger rather than weaker?

Because it forced a tacit identity to become an explicit one. As long as no foreigner ruled Egypt, Egyptianness needed no definition. When Levantine kings took Egyptian titles and governed in Egyptian, the Thebans had to define Egyptianness as something a foreigner could not acquire, and the definition they chose was descent and exclusive right to the land.

The thesis has three testable consequences, and all three are visible in the record.

The first is that Egyptian ideological hostility to foreigners should intensify sharply after the expulsion rather than during the occupation. It does. The Hyksos century itself produces no surviving anti-foreign polemic from within the Hyksos zone, for the obvious reason that the state there was not producing any. The polemic comes from Thebes as the war approaches, and then explodes in the New Kingdom, where the smiting of foreigners becomes the single most repeated image in Egyptian royal art, and where kings who never fought a Levantine campaign are nevertheless shown clubbing Asiatics by the fistful.

The second is that the hostility should be strongest in official ideology and weakest in practice, because it was constructed by the state for the state's purposes rather than growing out of popular experience. It is. The New Kingdom that shouted loudest about wretched Asiatics also absorbed more foreigners, more foreign gods, more foreign words, and more foreign goods than any Egyptian state before it. Levantine deities took residence in the Egyptian pantheon. Semitic loanwords entered the language in quantity, concentrated in exactly the technical domains where the Hyksos era had transferred expertise. Foreigners rose in the Egyptian administration and army. Royal harems held Levantine women by treaty. The contradiction between the ideology and the practice was never resolved because it was never noticed; the ideology was doing political work, not describing the world.

The third is that the memory should get worse over time rather than better, because a memory that serves a purpose grows to fit the purpose. It does, and that is the next section.

## The contested memory: how Egypt rewrote the Hyksos

The Egyptian account of the Hyksos is not one account. It is a sequence of accounts, each further from the events and each angrier than the last. Laid end to end, the sequence is a case study in how a state manufactures a founding grievance.

The earliest layer is the Kamose stelae, composed at the very end of the period by a participant. Kamose is hostile, but his hostility has a specific and limited content. He objects to sharing Egypt. He calls Apepi a chieftain rather than a king, which is a denial of status rather than an accusation of atrocity. He does not say the Hyksos burned temples. He does not say they enslaved Egyptians. He does not say they destroyed anything. He says they are there and should not be, and that he intends to remove them. The Kamose texts are propaganda, but they are propaganda about legitimacy, not about crimes.

The second layer is Ahmose-era and early Eighteenth Dynasty material, of which the autobiography of Ahmose son of Ibana, inscribed in his tomb at Elkab, is the most valuable. He was a marine who served at the siege of Avaris and then at Sharuhen in southern Canaan, and he records what he did and what he was given: prisoners, gold of valor, land. His account is a soldier's account, concerned with plunder and promotion. It contains no atrocity narrative about Hyksos rule, because he was not writing one. He was writing a career record.

The third layer arrives about a century later, with the Speos Artemidos inscription of Hatshepsut. Here the story has changed. Hatshepsut claims to have restored what was ruined, and refers to the time when the Asiatics were in Avaris and roaming hordes were among them overthrowing what had been made. This is the first appearance of the destruction narrative, and it appears roughly a hundred years after the events, in the mouth of a ruler with an obvious motive: a queen with a contested claim to the throne, presenting herself as the restorer of order against the memory of the deepest disorder Egypt could name. Hatshepsut had no more access to the reign of Apepi than any writer has to events a full century before their own birth, and she had considerably less reason to be accurate about them.

### Did the Hyksos destroy Egyptian temples?

The archaeology does not show it, and the accusation appears only long after the events. Kamose, writing at the time, makes no such claim. The destruction narrative first surfaces in Hatshepsut's Speos Artemidos inscription roughly a century later and grows in the retelling. No excavated destruction horizon matches the charge.

The fourth layer is Ramesside literature, where the Hyksos have become characters in a story. The tale conventionally called the Quarrel of Apepi and Seqenenre, surviving on a papyrus of Nineteenth Dynasty date, opens with Apepi sending a complaint to the Theban king that the hippopotami in the pool at Thebes are keeping him awake in Avaris, some five hundred miles away. It is a folk tale, and probably a comic one, built on an absurd pretext for war. It breaks off before its ending. Its evidentiary value for the Second Intermediate Period is zero and its value for the history of memory is enormous: by the Ramesside era, the Hyksos had become a genre.

The fifth layer is Manetho, writing in Greek in the third century BCE under the Ptolemies, at a distance of roughly thirteen centuries. Manetho supplies the version everyone knows: a sudden invasion by men of obscure race, cities burned, temples razed, the population massacred or enslaved, the children of the land carried off. He is not reporting; he is systematizing a tradition that had been growing for a millennium into the shape a Hellenistic history required, with an origin, a villain, and a deliverance.

The sixth layer is Josephus, writing in the first century CE, who quotes Manetho at length in Against Apion and adds his own argument that the Hyksos were the ancestors of the Jews and their expulsion the Exodus. This is not history at all. It is Josephus using an Egyptian legend to make a claim about Jewish antiquity in a Greco-Roman literary quarrel. The identification has no support in the archaeology or the Egyptian record, and the Exodus narrative belongs to a different question entirely.

Every popular account of the Hyksos as ravaging invaders descends from layers five and six. Almost nothing in those layers descends from layer one. The story most people know about foreign rule in Egypt was assembled by a Ptolemaic priest and a Roman-era Jewish apologist out of a Ramesside folk tale and an Eighteenth Dynasty queen's legitimacy campaign, and the participant who was actually there did not mention any of it.

### Why did the Egyptian account of the Hyksos get worse over time?

Because the memory served the state that inherited it. The New Kingdom justified its empire, its standing army, and its aggressive Levantine policy as prevention of another Hyksos. A worse Hyksos justified a stronger response, so the story grew. Each retelling had more reason to exaggerate than the one before.

## Was Egyptian hostility to foreigners born here? The longer record

A thesis that says foreign rule shaped Egyptian attitudes toward foreigners has to answer an obvious objection: Egyptians disliked foreigners long before the Hyksos, and the evidence for it is abundant. The objection is correct, and meeting it honestly sharpens the thesis rather than sinking it.

The Nine Bows, the conventional set of nine foreign peoples represented as bound captives beneath the king's feet or on the soles of his sandals, appear in Egyptian royal iconography from the Old Kingdom. The image is older than the pyramids of Giza in its essentials. Its message is that the king's function includes the subjugation of the outside world, and that message is structural to Egyptian kingship rather than a response to any particular event.

The Execration Texts, in which the names of foreign rulers and peoples were written on pottery or figurines that were then smashed and buried, are a Middle Kingdom practice. They list Nubian and Levantine rulers by name and consign them to ritual destruction. This is a state technology for handling the foreign, and it was running centuries before Avaris was a city.

Middle Kingdom literature carries the same theme in more sophisticated form. The instruction text addressed to Merikare characterizes the Asiatic dismissively as a wretched creature of hard country, always fighting, never announcing a day of battle, never conquering and never being conquered. The Prophecy of Neferti, set in the Fourth Dynasty but written in the Twelfth to praise Amenemhat I, imagines a nightmare Egypt in which Asiatics have entered the Delta and describes a king who will build a Wall of the Ruler to keep them out. Egyptian ideology had a category for the foreign long before any foreigner ruled.

So what did the Hyksos change? Three things, and each one is precise.

They changed the referent. Before the Second Intermediate Period, the Asiatic in Egyptian rhetoric was a generic figure, a category filled by whoever happened to be on the eastern desert edge. The bows were a set, not a memory. After the Hyksos, the Asiatic had a face and an address. Egyptian anti-foreign rhetoric acquired a specific historical grievance to point at, which converted an abstract ideological posture into a felt one. Abstractions do not motivate armies. Grievances do.

They changed the stakes. The older ideology said that the outside must be kept out because the king's role is to maintain order. The Hyksos experience said that if the outside gets in, Egypt itself stops existing as Egypt. That is a categorically different claim, and it converts a ritual duty into a security doctrine. The Nine Bows under Sneferu's sandals were a statement about cosmic order. The Nine Bows under Thutmose III's sandals were also a war plan.

They changed the policy. Middle Kingdom Egypt built a wall and a fortress line and stayed home. New Kingdom Egypt did not build a wall. It went north and took Canaan, then Syria, then fought for the Orontes. That reversal, from defensive containment to forward projection, is the single largest strategic change in Egyptian history, and it happened directly after the expulsion, executed by the dynasty the expulsion created. The reasoning was explicit in New Kingdom royal texts: the frontier is not a line to be held but a distance to be extended, so the enemy fights far from Egypt or does not fight at all. Empire as a buffer. The imperial state that logic produced is mapped in [the complete guide to the New Kingdom of Egypt](/2003/05/01/new-kingdom-of-egypt-guide/), which owns that period's orientation.

### Did the Hyksos invent Egyptian xenophobia?

No. Egyptian hostility toward foreigners predates them by more than a thousand years, visible in the Nine Bows imagery from the Old Kingdom and the Middle Kingdom Execration Texts. What the Hyksos supplied was a specific referent, higher stakes, and a policy: the New Kingdom turned an old ritual posture into an imperial security doctrine.

The distinction between inventing a prejudice and weaponizing one is not a technicality. An ideology that has always existed and never governed policy is a decoration. An ideology that acquires a historical trauma to justify itself becomes an engine. What the Hyksos century did was hand Egypt's oldest ideological reflex a story it could use, and Egypt used it for five hundred years.

## What the evidence lets us see, and what it hides

Every claim above rests on a specific kind of evidence with specific limits, and a reader who wants to argue this material rather than repeat it needs to know where the ground gives way.

The archaeology is strong and lopsided. Tell el-Dab'a is exceptionally well excavated and exceptionally informative, but it is one site. It is the capital, which means it is the least typical place in the Hyksos realm: the richest, the most cosmopolitan, the most likely to show mixture because mixture concentrates in ports and courts. Generalizing from Avaris to the whole Delta is a move that has to be made carefully. Other Delta sites of the period are less well published or less well preserved, and the Delta's wet conditions destroy organic material that survives in the desert south. Egyptology's picture of the Second Intermediate Period is a picture of one city plus fragments, and the city is atypical by construction.

The texts are weak and hostile. There are no Hyksos chronicles, no Hyksos royal annals, no Hyksos administrative archives. What exists from the Fifteenth Dynasty is inscriptional and glyptic: scarabs, sealings, a small body of monumental material, much of it reused or usurped later. The Hyksos are almost silent about themselves. Everything narrative about them comes from their enemies or from writers separated from them by centuries. This is the fundamental asymmetry of the period, and it means every statement about Hyksos intentions is an inference from behavior rather than a report of a stated purpose.

Skeletal and isotopic evidence is powerful but narrow. Isotope analysis can establish where a person spent their childhood; it cannot establish what language they spoke, which god they prayed to, or whether they thought of themselves as Egyptian. A local isotope signature at Avaris does not make a person Egyptian in any sense that person would have recognized. Identity is a matter of self-understanding and social recognition, and neither of those leaves a strontium trace. The isotope data is decisive against the invasion model and silent on the identity question.

Onomastics, the study of names, is the most tempting and least reliable index. Names are the only direct handle on how individuals presented themselves, which is why every treatment of the period reaches for them, and they are contaminated by fashion, patronage, and aspiration. A man named Sobekhotep may have been the son of a man named Yaqub. The name tells us about the naming decision, not the household.

### How do we know what daily life under the Hyksos was like?

Mainly from excavation at Avaris, which supplies housing, pottery, burials, diet, and isotope data across generations. Egyptian texts are late, hostile, and thin on ordinary life. The picture is therefore strong on material practice at one great city and weak on the rest of the Delta and on what people believed.

The honest position is that the material record settles the question of whether foreign rule was a violent imposition, and it settles it in the negative. The material record does not settle the question of how Egyptians felt about it, and no evidence available will. We know that Egyptians and Levantines shared streets, kitchens, cemeteries, and probably beds at Avaris. We do not know whether a Theban peasant, hearing of it, felt outrage, indifference, or nothing at all. The rhetoric that survives tells us how the Theban court wanted people to feel, which is not the same thing and is often the opposite.

One further limit deserves naming, because it is the one most often ignored. The absence of a resistance record is not evidence of contented acquiescence. Peasant resistance in a Bronze Age state does not produce documents. Foot-dragging, evasion of assessment, flight from the fields, and quiet non-cooperation leave no trace at all in a record composed entirely of royal inscriptions and elite burials. The claim defended here is narrow: no organized Egyptian resistance movement against Hyksos rule is attested inside the Hyksos zone, and no destruction or upheaval consistent with one appears in the ground. Whether individual Egyptians resented their rulers is beyond recovery.

### Did ordinary Egyptians resist Hyksos rule?

There is no evidence of organized resistance inside the Hyksos zone. Middle Egyptian rulers accommodated Avaris, and the war came from Thebes as a royal project rather than as a popular rising. Whether individuals resented foreign kings cannot be recovered, since the record preserves royal voices and elite burials only.

## The New Kingdom inheritance: an identity with an army behind it

The consequences of the identity manufactured at Thebes were not confined to rhetoric. They restructured the Egyptian state, and it is worth being concrete about how, because this is where the Hyksos century's real historical weight sits.

Egypt after 1550 BCE maintained a standing professional army for the first time in its history. The Middle Kingdom had raised levies and garrisoned forts; the New Kingdom kept regiments, chariot corps, a career officer class, and a system of rewards including the gold of valor and grants of land and captives to soldiers. That apparatus existed because the state had decided that the security problem was permanent. Ahmose son of Ibana's tomb autobiography, which lists what he was given for his service, is the founding document of a new social type: the Egyptian whose family fortune came from fighting foreigners.

Egypt after 1550 BCE also treated the Levant as a zone of Egyptian interest rather than a foreign country to be walled off. Egyptian garrisons occupied Canaanite towns. Canaanite princes' sons were taken to Egypt to be raised at court, educated as Egyptians, and returned as loyal vassals. That policy is the strangest artifact of the whole identity story: a state that defined itself against Levantine rule turned around and manufactured Egyptianized Levantines by the generation, because it had learned from the Hyksos exactly how well that worked.

Egypt after 1550 BCE built its royal ideology around the smiting scene to a degree earlier eras had not. The image of the king with a mace raised over a kneeling foreigner is Predynastic in origin, visible on the Narmer Palette, but its frequency and prominence in the New Kingdom is unmatched. Pylons carry it at colossal scale. It becomes the default statement of what a pharaoh is.

And Egypt after 1550 BCE absorbed the Levant into itself at a rate that would have been unthinkable before. Baal and Reshep received Egyptian cult. Astarte and Anat entered the pantheon and acquired Egyptian associations, including a connection with Seth that runs directly back to the Delta identification. Semitic vocabulary entered Egyptian, concentrated in military, maritime, and technical language, exactly the domains where the Hyksos century had done its transferring. Ramesses II built a capital, Pi-Ramesses, on the site of Avaris, in the Delta, oriented toward the Levant, honoring Seth.

That is the shape of the New Kingdom's relationship to the Hyksos in a single fact. Egypt's greatest imperial dynasty built its capital on the ruins of the foreign capital, worshipped the foreign capital's god, used the foreign capital's military technology, spoke a language salted with the foreign capital's vocabulary, and told everyone that the foreign capital had been an abomination. Identity by contrast does not require consistency. It requires only a contrast.

### Why does the Hyksos period matter for understanding the New Kingdom?

Because the New Kingdom's defining features are responses to it. The standing army, the chariot corps, the forward empire in the Levant, the intensity of the smiting ideology, and the practice of Egyptianizing foreign princes all descend from the Second Intermediate Period. The empire was built to prevent a repetition and used the tools of the thing it feared.

## The two stories that both fail: total oppression and total assimilation

Popular treatments of this period reach for one of two shapes, and neither survives contact with the material.

The first shape is total oppression. In this version, the Hyksos are conquerors, the Egyptians are a subject people, the century is an occupation endured, and the expulsion is a liberation. It has the advantage of matching the ancient sources, and it fails on almost every point of evidence. There is no conquest horizon in the archaeology. There is no subject-people legal category. There is no ethnic disability. There is no impoverishment of the Egyptian population under Hyksos rule, and no flight from the Hyksos zone; the Delta population grew. There is no resistance record. There is no destruction of the temples the sources claim were destroyed. And the liberators themselves, in the earliest surviving account, complain about partition rather than about atrocity.

The version also fails a simpler test. If the Hyksos had governed as brutal foreign occupiers, they would not have needed Egyptian scribes, Egyptian titles, Egyptian gods, and Egyptian throne names. Occupiers who despise the occupied advertise the fact. The Fifteenth Dynasty spent a century advertising the opposite.

The second shape is total assimilation, and it has become the fashionable correction. In this version, the Hyksos were merely Egyptians of Levantine descent, the whole business was a domestic dynastic quarrel dressed up as an ethnic one, the cultural blend was complete, and the later hostility is pure invention with no basis in anything real. This version is closer to the evidence and still wrong, in three ways.

It is wrong about the Levantine material. The Hyksos zone retained a distinct Levantine practice that never merged out of existence: the warrior burials, the donkey burials, the broad-room temples, the toggle pins, the dietary profile. Those are not the traces of a fully assimilated population. They are the traces of a population that kept a second identity alive for generations while acquiring an Egyptian one. Assimilation was real and it was partial.

It is wrong about the political reality. The Fifteenth Dynasty was not just an Egyptian dynasty with unusual grandparents. It was oriented toward the Levant, connected to Levantine and Aegean circuits, and holding a piece of Egypt that other Egyptians claimed. The Thebans were not hallucinating a difference. They were exaggerating a difference that existed.

It is wrong about the memory. Treating the later hostility as pure invention makes the New Kingdom's ideology inexplicable. Ideologies are built out of something. Kamose's grievance about sharing Egypt with an Asiatic is a real grievance about a real situation, expressed in a rhetoric that was already available. What the later tradition did was inflate it, not fabricate it from nothing.

### Were the Hyksos really foreign, or were they Egyptians by then?

Both, in the way that matters. They were Egyptianized in kingship, script, administration, and official religion, and they remained Levantine in burial rite, temple architecture, dietary practice, and diplomatic orientation. The Thebans exaggerated a real difference rather than inventing one.

The picture that survives both failures is the awkward one. Foreign rule in Egypt was a genuine political fact that produced a genuine cultural blend, and the blend was neither so complete that the difference vanished nor so shallow that the difference was only rhetoric. The Hyksos century was a real experiment in what Egypt could be, run in the Delta for about a hundred years, and it was destroyed not because it failed but because a rival king decided that its success was intolerable.

## Language, script, and what people actually spoke

The linguistic situation of Hyksos Egypt is one of the more instructive corners of the problem, because language sits closest to identity and the evidence for it is strangely indirect.

The state wrote Egyptian. That is beyond doubt and has been covered. What people spoke is a different question, and the honest answer is that the Delta was multilingual and the proportions are unrecoverable. A Northwest Semitic language, the ancestor stock behind Canaanite, was in use among the Levantine population; the personal names in the record are the evidence, since names are the surviving fossils of a spoken language. Egyptian was in use among the Egyptian population and among the administration. Which language a given household used at dinner is not knowable, and in a city like Avaris the answer for many households was probably both.

The traffic between the two languages is measurable, and it runs mostly one way. Egyptian acquired a body of Northwest Semitic loanwords, and the acquisition is concentrated in the Second Intermediate Period and the New Kingdom. The vocabulary that entered clusters in revealing places: military equipment and practice, horses and chariotry, ships and the sea, certain trades and materials. This is the linguistic fingerprint of technology transfer. When a culture borrows a word, it is usually borrowing the thing along with it, and the things Egypt borrowed were the things that made an empire possible.

### What languages did people speak in Hyksos Egypt?

Egyptian and a Northwest Semitic language, in a Delta that was functionally bilingual. Administration ran entirely in Egyptian script and language. Personal names show Semitic speech alive among the Levantine population. Egyptian absorbed Semitic loanwords in exactly the domains where technology moved: chariots, horses, weapons, ships, and trades.

Nothing moved the other way at the level of the state. No Semitic administrative language established itself. No Hyksos king issued a decree in anything but Egyptian. This is the sharpest possible demonstration of where the cultural gravity lay. The rulers were foreign and the state was Egyptian, and the state won the argument about language before the argument was ever held.

There is a further point about script that is worth stating, since it is the sort of thing that survives an exam question. Egyptian writing was not a neutral technology. Hieroglyphs carried a theology; they were the god's words, and their use implied a whole cosmology about the power of names and images. A Levantine dynasty writing hieroglyphs was not merely adopting a convenient notation. It was accepting a theory of how the world worked. That is why the Hyksos adoption of Egyptian script is stronger evidence of accommodation than any of their political gestures. Titles can be cynical. Cosmology is harder to fake for a century.

## The social change foreign rule actually produced

Setting rhetoric aside, what concrete changes did the century of foreign rule leave in Egyptian society? Four are defensible on the evidence, and they are worth separating from the many changes that are asserted and cannot be shown.

The first is a permanent shift in the Delta's demographic and cultural profile. The eastern Delta after the Second Intermediate Period was a region with deep Levantine roots, a Seth cult of Syrian coloring, and a population whose ancestry was mixed. It stayed that way. The Ramesside kings came out of it. Pi-Ramesses rose on Avaris. The Delta's difference from Upper Egypt, which had always existed geographically, acquired a cultural dimension that persisted for the rest of pharaonic history.

The second is the militarization of Egyptian elite society. Before the Second Intermediate Period, the Egyptian gentleman was a scribe or an official; military service was a phase or a duty rather than an identity. After it, a career military class existed with its own rewards, its own tomb biographies, its own path to land and status. Ahmose son of Ibana's tomb is the visible beginning of a social type that would dominate Egypt for centuries and would eventually put generals on the throne. Foreign rule produced Egyptian militarism, and Egyptian militarism produced a new elite.

The third is the entry of a large foreign population into Egypt by force, which is the darkest consequence and the one least often stated plainly. The New Kingdom's response to having been ruled by foreigners was to conquer foreigners and bring them to Egypt as captives, as labor, as temple endowments, and as soldiers. Ahmose son of Ibana lists the people he was given. The temples of the New Kingdom held foreign workforces. The Egyptian army recruited from the peoples it defeated. Egypt's population became more foreign after the expulsion than it had been under the Hyksos, by policy, at the point of a spear. The state that defined itself against foreign presence imported foreigners at industrial scale.

The fourth is the hardening of a category. Before the Hyksos, "Egyptian" described a way of living. After them, it was a claim about descent and right, and it carried a political charge. That charge would be picked up by every later Egyptian generation that faced foreign rule, and Egypt would face a great deal of it: Libyans, Kushites, Assyrians, Persians, Macedonians, Romans, and beyond. Every one of those episodes would be understood through a framework first built in the Seventeenth Dynasty to deal with a dynasty in the Delta.

### What changed in Egyptian society because of foreign rule?

Four things stand up: the eastern Delta kept a permanently mixed, Levantine-inflected culture; a career military class emerged with its own path to wealth and land; the New Kingdom imported foreign captives at scale; and Egyptianness hardened from a description of how one lived into a claim about descent and rightful possession of the land.

Notice what is absent from that list. The Hyksos did not change Egyptian law, Egyptian religion's core, Egyptian family structure, Egyptian agriculture, Egyptian language, or Egyptian administrative practice. The lasting changes came not from what foreign rule did to Egypt but from what Egypt did in response to it. This is the deepest form of the identity-by-contrast thesis. The Hyksos century's effects were produced almost entirely by the Egyptian reaction, not by the Hyksos themselves. A foreign dynasty that governed conservatively and left almost nothing behind became, through the memory Egyptians built of it, the most consequential foreign presence in pharaonic history.

## The other foreign rule that nobody remembers

There is a control case sitting in plain view, and running it exposes the Theban argument for what it was.

While Levantine kings held the Delta, the kingdom of Kush held Nubia from Kerma, and Kush had taken over the Middle Kingdom fortress line at the Second Cataract. Egyptians lived in those fortresses. Egyptian garrison commanders and their descendants at Buhen and elsewhere transferred their service to the Kushite ruler and continued in place, and they left inscriptions saying so, in Egyptian, with Egyptian titles, describing themselves as serving the ruler of Kush. Egyptian families in Nubia served a Nubian king for generations and did not treat it as a catastrophe. They treated it as employment.

That fact should be set beside the Theban rhetoric and left there to do its work. Kamose complains that a Nubian holds a piece of Egypt, and his complaint is symmetrical with his complaint about the Asiatic. But the Egyptians actually living under Kushite rule made no complaint at all that survives. They accommodated, exactly as the Egyptians in Middle Egypt accommodated Avaris. Two separate foreign rulers, two separate Egyptian populations, and in both cases the Egyptians on the spot adjusted and got on with it while the Egyptians at a distance found the situation unbearable.

The pattern is instructive because it isolates the variable. Outrage at foreign rule correlates with distance from foreign rule and with proximity to a throne that wants a war. It does not correlate with the experience of being ruled by foreigners. The people who had that experience mostly did not object. The people who objected mostly did not have that experience. Any account of the Hyksos period that treats Egyptian hostility as the natural response of a subjugated people has to explain why the subjugated people were the ones not responding.

### Did Egyptians serve foreign rulers willingly?

The record says yes, in two places at once. Egyptian officials staffed the Hyksos administration in the Delta, and Egyptian garrison families in Nubia transferred their service to the king of Kush and recorded it proudly in Egyptian inscriptions. Accommodation was the normal response of Egyptians who actually lived under foreign rule.

## Counting the Hyksos, and why the number matters

One of the most consequential uncertainties in the period is the simplest: how many Levantines were there?

Manetho's tradition supplies a figure of hundreds of thousands, which is the kind of number ancient sources produce when they mean "a lot" and which no one should treat as data. The archaeology gives a range rather than a count. Avaris at its height was one of the largest cities in Egypt, and the Levantine component of its population was substantial across many generations. Other Delta sites show Levantine material in varying proportions. The best that can be said is that the community was large enough to sustain its own material culture and religious architecture over a century and a half without being absorbed, and not so large as to displace Egyptian population anywhere.

The number matters because it decides between two models of what a Hyksos king was. If the Levantine population was small, the Fifteenth Dynasty was a foreign elite ruling an Egyptian society, and the identity question is about elite legitimacy. If it was large, the Fifteenth Dynasty was the government of a genuinely mixed region and the identity question is about what happens when part of a country becomes demographically different from the rest. The evidence favors the second reading in the eastern Delta specifically and the first reading for the wider zone of Hyksos authority, which is why the period resists a single description. Avaris was a mixed society with a mixed government. Middle Egypt was an Egyptian society with a distant foreign overlord and Egyptian intermediaries. Those are different situations under one crown, and Egyptians in each of them had different reasons to care.

Both situations feed the identity problem from different sides. In the Delta, the problem was that the difference was dissolving. In Middle Egypt, the problem was that the difference did not seem to matter. Either way, the Theban answer had to be a rule that operated regardless of what people did or how they lived, and descent is the only such rule available. Thebes did not arrive at a descent-based definition of Egyptianness because Egyptians had always thought that way. It arrived there because every other definition would have let Apepi in.

## What a divided Egypt felt like at the seam

The frontier between the two Egypts sat near Cusae in Middle Egypt, and the seam is where the abstractions become concrete.

Cusae is roughly halfway between Memphis and Thebes, and the Kamose texts treat it as the edge of Theban authority. North of it, the towns owed Avaris. South of it, they owed Thebes. That line ran through a continuous stretch of Nile valley in which nothing changed physically at all. The same fields, the same villages, the same language, the same gods, the same families in many cases with relatives on both sides. Foreign rule at the seam was not a wall or a garrison line separating two worlds. It was an administrative fact dividing one.

River traffic crossed it. Kamose's second stela records the interception of a messenger carrying a letter from Apepi to the ruler of Kush, proposing coordinated action against Thebes. Set aside the diplomatic content, which is a set piece for another article, and notice the logistics. A courier was moving from Avaris toward Kush, which meant traveling the length of Theban territory, which he was evidently expected to be able to do, and which Kamose treats as an outrage precisely because it was routine enough to be attempted. The two Egypts were not sealed from each other. They were adjacent administrations on a shared river, with traffic between them that only became treasonous once a king decided to make it so.

The recorded objection of Kamose's own council makes the same point from inside the Theban court. Their position, as his text preserves it, was that the arrangement was tolerable: Theban cattle grazed in the Delta marshes, Theban fields were worked, nobody was starving, and a war would risk all of it for a principle. Whether or not those words were spoken, the text's author thought that argument needed answering, which means the argument was in circulation among people who mattered. Not every Theban wanted the war. The identity that made the war necessary was contested within Thebes itself before it was imposed on Egypt.

### Was the Egyptian border with the Hyksos a hard frontier?

No. The line near Cusae divided two administrations on the same river, not two worlds. Trade and traffic crossed it, Theban herds are described grazing in the Delta, and a Hyksos courier could attempt the journey south through Theban territory. The frontier was a political fact rather than a physical or cultural barrier.

## Monuments, usurpation, and the erasure that was not

There is one more body of evidence that bears directly on the memory question, and it is the fate of Hyksos monuments after the expulsion.

Hyksos royal material is scarce, and part of the reason is deliberate. Their monuments were reused, their names removed, their statuary appropriated. This looks at first like the physical counterpart of the textual damnation: the New Kingdom erasing its enemies from the record. But the practice needs to be read in context, because Egyptian kings usurped each other's monuments constantly, and the reuse of a predecessor's stone was standard economics rather than a statement about that predecessor's legitimacy. Middle Kingdom statuary was reused by Hyksos kings, then reused again by Eighteenth Dynasty kings, then reused again by Ramessides. The stones circulated.

What is genuinely distinctive is the absence rather than the erasure. The Fifteenth Dynasty left strikingly little monumental material to begin with, compared to Egyptian dynasties of comparable length and wealth. They were rich; the port and the trade prove it. They did not convert that wealth into the vast temple and tomb complexes that Egyptian kings converted wealth into. That is a genuine cultural difference and it is a useful corrective to any account that makes the Hyksos indistinguishable from Egyptian kings. They took the titulary and the script and the god. They did not take the building compulsion, which was arguably the most Egyptian thing about Egyptian kingship.

There is an alternative explanation worth naming honestly: Delta conditions destroy monuments. The water table is high, the mudbrick dissolves, the stone was carted away for later construction, and Pi-Ramesses was built on the site, which means a millennium of quarrying and reuse over the top of whatever the Hyksos left. Absence in the Delta is weak evidence. The comparison with contemporary Theban royal building, which was also modest, suggests the Second Intermediate Period as a whole was a low-monument era, and the Hyksos may not be exceptional within it. The honest statement is that the Hyksos monumental record is thin, that some of the thinness is deliberate destruction, some is Delta geology, and some may be a genuine difference in royal priorities, and that the three cannot be separated with the evidence available.

### Did the Egyptians erase the Hyksos from the record?

Partly, and less systematically than the textual hostility suggests. Hyksos names were removed and their statuary reused, but monument usurpation was routine among Egyptian kings and not a special punishment. The bigger reason their record is thin is that they built little to begin with and the Delta preserves badly.

## What a student should be able to argue

A reader who has followed the material this far should be able to do five specific things, and they are worth stating because they are what separates understanding this period from having heard of it.

Distinguish the layers of the source tradition and weigh them. Kamose is contemporary and limited; Ahmose son of Ibana is contemporary and non-ideological; Hatshepsut is a century later and motivated; the Ramesside tale is folklore; Manetho is thirteen centuries later and systematizing; Josephus is using Manetho for an argument of his own. Any exam answer or essay that treats these as a single body called "the sources" has already failed. Any answer that names the sequence and explains why the story worsens along it has already passed.

Explain why the archaeology of Avaris is decisive against the invasion model and silent on the identity question. The material record settles what happened to buildings, bodies, pots, and diets. It does not settle what people thought they were. Knowing which questions the evidence can answer is the whole of historical method.

State the two-way flow accurately in both directions and know which direction was stronger in which domain. The Hyksos moved toward Egypt in kingship, script, administration, official religion, and burial architecture. Egypt moved toward the Levant in military technology, and later in language and pantheon. The interaction table above holds the whole of it.

Defend or attack the identity-by-contrast thesis on evidence. The case for it: the hostility intensifies after the expulsion rather than during the occupation, it is strongest in state ideology and weakest in practice, and it grows over time. The case against it: Egyptian anti-foreign ideology is a thousand years older, and the New Kingdom's militarism has other causes, including the simple fact that chariot warfare made offense cheaper than defense. A serious answer takes the objection seriously and argues that the Hyksos supplied a referent, a stake, and a policy rather than the prejudice itself.

Refuse both easy stories. Not oppression, not harmony. A real political subordination, a real cultural blend, a real Theban grievance, a manufactured memory, and a set of consequences produced almost entirely by the Egyptian reaction rather than by the foreign rulers. The century's meaning lies in what Egypt did about it afterward, which is why the period matters far out of proportion to what actually happened during it.

## The honest picture

Strip out the propaganda from both directions and the following is defensible.

For about a century, roughly 1650 to 1550 BCE, the eastern Delta and its dependencies were ruled by kings of Levantine descent whose community had lived in Egypt for generations before it took power. They governed through Egyptian institutions, in the Egyptian language, using Egyptian titles, patronizing Egyptian learning, and worshipping a god the Egyptians already had. Their capital, Avaris, was a wealthy, mixed, cosmopolitan port where two material cultures interpenetrated to the point that the archaeology of its houses, its pots, its diet, and its graves shows a hybrid rather than two populations side by side. Marriage moved people across the boundary in quantity, especially women moving in. Egyptian officials served them. Egyptian towns in Middle Egypt accepted their overlordship. There is no evidence of a violent conquest, a subject-people status, a program of cultural erasure, or a destroyed temple.

Simultaneously, the kings of Thebes, ruling a poorer and smaller Upper Egypt, constructed a case that this arrangement was an intolerable violation of what Egypt was. That case did not describe the world; it built one. It required defining Egyptianness as something a foreigner could not acquire, which meant defining it by descent and by exclusive right to the land, which meant defining it for the first time as an explicit political claim rather than an unexamined condition. They fought a war on that basis, they won it, and their definition became the operating ideology of the most powerful state Egypt ever built.

The New Kingdom then behaved in a way that made the ideology look absurd and never noticed. It built its capital on Avaris. It honored Avaris's Seth. It fought with Avaris's weapons. It filled its language with Avaris's vocabulary. It imported foreigners in numbers the Hyksos never approached. And it decorated its temples with images of pharaohs clubbing Asiatics.

The honest verdict is that foreign rule did not weaken Egyptian identity. It created the argued version of it, the version that is a claim rather than a description, and it created that version by giving Egyptians something to define themselves against. Egyptian identity as a self-conscious, defended, argued thing is a product of the Hyksos century, and it is a product of the century's aftermath rather than of the century itself. The people who blended at Avaris did not build Egyptian identity. The people who refused to blend at Thebes did, and they built it out of a refusal.

That is a finding with a long reach, and it should be handled carefully. It would be a serious error to read the Second Intermediate Period as a nationalist episode in a modern sense. There was no Egyptian nation in the sense of a people claiming political sovereignty as a right of peoplehood. There was a theology of kingship in which the king maintained order against chaos, and the Theban kings solved a political problem by classifying their rival as chaos. That is not nationalism. It is a religious argument put to political use, and the difference matters because the modern reading imports a whole apparatus of ideas about ethnicity, nation, and self-determination that did not exist and would have been unintelligible in 1550 BCE.

What can be said, without importing anything, is that a people who had never had to say what they were said it, and said it against someone. That is the mechanism the identity-by-contrast thesis names. It is not unique to Egypt and it is not modern. It is one of the oldest attested instances of a pattern that has repeated ever since, and it is visible here with unusual clarity because the archaeology of Avaris and the rhetoric of Thebes survive side by side, contradicting each other, in the same century.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How did foreign rule affect Egyptian identity?

It made an unexamined identity into an explicit one. Before the Hyksos, being Egyptian was the default condition of living in the valley under a king who kept order, and it needed no defining because nothing had contested it. The Fifteenth Dynasty broke that default by being foreign without being outside: Levantine kings living in Egypt, ruling Egyptians, taking pharaonic titles, writing hieroglyphs, and worshipping Seth. Every practical marker of Egyptianness they possessed. So Egyptianness could no longer be a matter of practice, and the Theban kings redefined it as a matter of descent and exclusive right to the land, because that was the only definition that made their war necessary. Foreign rule did not weaken Egyptian identity. It forced Egyptians to state it, and the statement they chose shaped the next five hundred years of Egyptian policy.

### Q: How did Egyptians view the Hyksos?

That depends entirely on which Egyptians and when. Egyptians in the Delta lived alongside them, married into their community, and served in their administration. Egyptians in Middle Egypt accepted their overlordship through local rulers, and Kamose calls one such man, Teti son of Pepi at Neferusi, a holder of the town for the Asiatics, which is the language used against a traitor rather than a foreign enemy. Egyptians at Thebes, and specifically the Theban royal court, regarded them as an intolerable violation of what Egypt was. Kamose's own council is recorded as arguing against the war on the grounds that the arrangement worked. The hostile view was a court position that became a national one only after the court that held it won the war and built an empire on it.

### Q: Did the Hyksos adopt Egyptian culture?

Extensively, and in exactly the areas where a foreign dynasty seeking legitimacy would. They wrote their names in cartouches and took throne names built on the sun god Re, such as Seuserenre for Khyan and Aauserre for Apepi. They used the traditional royal titles including Son of Re and the Good God. They ran their administration in Egyptian script with Egyptian titles and dated by their own regnal years. They patronized Egyptian scholarship, and the Rhind Mathematical Papyrus was copied from a Middle Kingdom original under Apepi. They built Egyptian-style tomb chapels and buried their dead with Egyptian funerary pottery. What they kept was Levantine burial rite, temple architecture, dress fastenings, and diet. The adoption was deep but not total, and it ran much harder from Hyksos toward Egypt than the reverse.

### Q: How did the Hyksos treat Egyptian religion?

They joined it rather than suppressing it. Their dynastic patron was Seth, an Egyptian god with roots reaching back to the Predynastic era, whom they identified with the Levantine storm god Baal. That identification is the standard ancient practice of recognizing a neighbor's god as your own under another name, and it ran in both directions. Egyptian cult formulas appear on Hyksos royal objects as a matter of course. No excavated destruction horizon matches the later Egyptian charge that they razed temples, and that charge first appears roughly a century after the events, in Hatshepsut's Speos Artemidos inscription. The strongest counterevidence is Ramesside: the Nineteenth Dynasty honored Seth of Avaris as venerable heritage and Ramesses II commemorated four centuries of that cult on the monument known as the 400 Year Stela.

### Q: What did Egyptians think of foreign rulers?

The evidence splits along a line that has nothing to do with ethnicity and everything to do with proximity. Egyptians who actually lived under foreign rulers accommodated them. Egyptian officials staffed the Hyksos administration. Egyptian garrison families in Nubia transferred their service to the king of Kush and recorded the fact proudly in Egyptian inscriptions with Egyptian titles. Egyptian towns in Middle Egypt accepted Hyksos overlordship through their own local rulers. The outrage came from Thebes, from a court that was not ruled by foreigners and that needed a justification for a war. That pattern is the single most important corrective to the standard account, because it means Egyptian hostility to foreign rulers was a political construction rather than the natural cry of a subjugated people.

### Q: How did foreign rule change Egyptian society?

Four changes are defensible on evidence, and all four came from Egypt's reaction rather than from Hyksos policy. The eastern Delta kept a permanently mixed, Levantine-inflected culture that produced the Ramesside kings and their capital at Pi-Ramesses. A career military class emerged with its own rewards, tomb biographies, and route to land and status, beginning visibly with Ahmose son of Ibana. The New Kingdom imported foreign captives at industrial scale as labor, temple endowment, and soldiers, making Egypt more foreign after the expulsion than during the occupation. And the category "Egyptian" hardened from a description of how a person lived into a claim about descent and rightful possession of the land. The Hyksos changed almost nothing directly. What Egypt did about them changed everything.

### Q: Were the Hyksos hated by the Egyptians?

By some Egyptians, at some times, for reasons that were political rather than experiential. The earliest surviving hostile voice is Kamose, and his objection is narrow: he will not sit as a ruler while an Asiatic holds one part of Egypt and a Nubian another. He calls Apepi a chieftain rather than a king, denying status rather than alleging atrocity. He does not accuse the Hyksos of burning temples or enslaving Egyptians. Hatred in the full sense, the atrocity narrative with destruction and massacre, appears about a century later under Hatshepsut and grows across the Ramesside period, Manetho, and Josephus. The people best placed to hate the Hyksos, the Egyptians living under them, left no record of hating them at all.

### Q: How did the Hyksos period shape Egyptian attitudes to foreigners?

It supplied three things to an attitude that already existed. A referent: the generic Asiatic of Middle Kingdom rhetoric acquired a face, a name, and an address in the Delta, which converted an abstract ideological posture into a felt grievance. A stake: the older ideology said the outside must be kept out to maintain order, while the Hyksos experience said that if the outside gets in, Egypt stops being Egypt. And a policy: Middle Kingdom Egypt built a wall and stayed home, while New Kingdom Egypt went north and took Canaan and Syria, on the explicit reasoning that a distant frontier means the enemy fights far from Egypt. Empire as a buffer against a repetition. The prejudice was a thousand years older. The engine was new.

### Q: What was daily life like at Avaris?

Mixed, prosperous, and busy. Residents lived in mudbrick housing whose plans began as distinctly Egyptian or distinctly Levantine and merged into hybrid forms over generations. They cooked in vessels of both traditions, ate emmer bread and barley beer like every Egyptian, and consumed a larger share of sheep and goat than Upper Egyptian sites show, alongside Nile and lake fish, marsh fowl, and imported oil and wine arriving through the harbor. Work meant agriculture, but also transport, warehousing, shipbuilding, and a bronze industry operating at a serious technical level. Burials sat interleaved with the housing. The city was a Mediterranean port that happened to be on the Nile, and it was neither an Egyptian town with foreigners in it nor a Canaanite colony.

### Q: Did Egyptians and Hyksos intermarry?

The evidence points that way, though it is circumstantial rather than documentary. Strontium isotope analysis of burials at Tell el-Dab'a, which reads the geological signature of childhood drinking water from tooth enamel, has found a substantial non-local component in the population, with a notably higher proportion of non-local signatures among women than among men. That pattern fits marriage networks drawing partners into the Delta rather than armies arriving. It also appears across a long span, including before the Fifteenth Dynasty existed. Personal names within apparent family groups shift between Egyptian and Semitic forms across generations, which fits mixed households. Marriage is the quietest historical mechanism and the most consequential: it leaves no destruction layer, only grandchildren with mixed names.

### Q: What languages were spoken in Hyksos Egypt?

Egyptian and a Northwest Semitic language, in a Delta that was functionally bilingual. The state wrote and administered exclusively in Egyptian, in hieroglyphs for monuments and hieratic for documents, and no Semitic administrative language was ever established. Semitic speech among the Levantine population is attested indirectly through personal names, which are the surviving fossils of a spoken tongue. The traffic between the languages is measurable and runs mostly one way: Egyptian acquired a body of Northwest Semitic loanwords concentrated in the Second Intermediate Period and the New Kingdom, clustering in military equipment, horses and chariotry, ships and the sea, and certain trades. That vocabulary distribution is the linguistic fingerprint of technology transfer.

### Q: Were Egyptians enslaved under the Hyksos?

No evidence supports it. No text from the period and no excavation shows a general enslavement of Egyptians or any legal disability attached to being Egyptian. Egyptian officials held office under Hyksos kings, Egyptian temples continued, Egyptian burials continued in Egyptian forms, and the Delta population grew rather than fled. The mass-enslavement claim enters the record through Josephus in the first century CE, quoting Manetho from the third century BCE, roughly thirteen centuries after the events, and Josephus quoted it because he wanted to argue that the Hyksos were the Israelites. Slavery existed in Egypt across the period as it had before, but it was a legal and economic condition rather than an ethnic sentence imposed on Egyptians by their Delta rulers.

### Q: Did Egyptian scribes and officials serve Hyksos kings?

Yes, and the Hyksos state could not have functioned otherwise. It ran on Egyptian bureaucratic forms, which means it ran on people trained exactly as Egyptian bureaucrats were trained. Sealings and scarabs from the period carry the standard Egyptian administrative titles: treasurer, chancellor, steward, overseer. The best-attested official is a treasurer named Har, whose name and title appear on a substantial body of scarabs and whose function was the classic Egyptian one of managing the royal storehouse. Some officials bear Egyptian names and some Semitic ones, and families appear to shift between them across generations. There is no sign of a purge of the Egyptian administrative class and every sign that the Fifteenth Dynasty needed it and kept it.

### Q: Did ordinary Egyptians resist Hyksos rule?

No organized resistance is attested inside the Hyksos zone, and no destruction or upheaval consistent with one appears in the ground. Middle Egyptian rulers accommodated Avaris and administered their towns on its behalf. The war came from Thebes as a royal project, and Kamose's own council is recorded arguing against it on the grounds that the existing arrangement worked. That said, the absence of a resistance record is not proof of contentment. Peasant resistance in a Bronze Age state, meaning foot-dragging, evasion of assessment, flight from the fields, and quiet non-cooperation, leaves no trace in a record composed entirely of royal inscriptions and elite burials. What can be said is narrow: no movement, no rising, no destruction. What individuals felt is beyond recovery.

### Q: What evidence shows Hyksos and Egyptian cultures mixed?

The stratified excavation of Tell el-Dab'a, layer by layer over generations, supplies almost all of it. Housing plans begin as recognizably Levantine or Egyptian and merge into hybrid forms. Pottery merges likewise, with Egyptian shapes made in Levantine techniques and Levantine shapes in Egyptian fabrics, which matters because ceramics are conservative and track cooking habits. Burials combine Egyptian tomb chapels, body positions, and funerary pottery with Levantine warrior burials, paired donkey burials at tomb entrances, servant burials, and toggle pins. Temple precincts hold Middle Bronze Levantine broad-room plans beside Egyptian cult installations. Diet shows a Levantine sheep and goat component in an Egyptian grain economy. Isotope data shows sustained inward movement of non-local people, weighted toward women, across a long span.
