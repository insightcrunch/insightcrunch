Native rule in Egypt did not fade out. It was cut off at a high point, and that is the single most misunderstood fact about the last native pharaohs. The standard picture has a tired civilization drifting toward its inevitable end, its last kings presiding over decay until Persia finally put it out of its misery. The evidence describes something else entirely: sixty years of restored independence in which Egyptian kings repelled full Persian invasions twice, ran an aggressive diplomatic operation across the Aegean, and financed a temple building program that stands comparison with the New Kingdom. Nectanebo II, the last of them, was still building when he lost his kingdom.

That contrast is what this article is about, and it supports what this series calls the last-flourish thesis: the final native dynasties mounted a genuine cultural and military revival, and the end came from an external force overwhelming a functioning state rather than from internal exhaustion. Getting that right matters for more than fairness to some fourth-century kings. It changes what the end of pharaonic Egypt means. A civilization that collapses under its own weight and a civilization that is conquered while still producing are different stories, and only one of them is what happened.

## Who the Last Native Pharaohs Were

The phrase covers three dynasties in Manetho's numbering, the Twenty-eighth, Twenty-ninth, and Thirtieth, spanning roughly 404 to 343 BCE. They occupy the gap between the two Persian occupations, and they represent the last period in which Egypt was ruled by Egyptians until the twentieth century CE.

The Twenty-eighth Dynasty consists of one man. Amyrtaeus of Sais led the revolt that won independence in circa 404 BCE and ruled for roughly five years before being deposed. Manetho counts him as a dynasty in his own right, which tells you something about how thoroughly the Delta political landscape had fragmented into competing houses.

The Twenty-ninth Dynasty ruled from Mendes in the eastern Delta from circa 399 to 380 BCE. Its succession was violent and contested, and its most substantial reign belonged to Hakor, known to Greek writers as Achoris, who held Egypt through a sustained Persian effort to retake it.

The Thirtieth Dynasty ruled from Sebennytos from circa 380 to 343 BCE and produced three kings: Nectanebo I, his son Teos, and his grandson Nectanebo II. This is the dynasty that matters most, both because it lasted longest and because it built most, and Nectanebo II is the figure the phrase last native pharaoh normally denotes.

### Who were the last native pharaohs of Egypt?

The kings of Dynasties 28 to 30, ruling circa 404 to 343 BCE between the two Persian occupations: Amyrtaeus of Sais, the Mendesian kings of Dynasty 29 including Hakor, and the Sebennytos house of Dynasty 30, made up of Nectanebo I, Teos, and Nectanebo II. Nectanebo II is conventionally the last.

There is a complication to that conventional answer involving a rebel king named Khababash, addressed later in this article, and it is a genuine complication rather than a technicality.

Read the achievement column and the picture that emerges is not decline. Four of these six reigns include either a successful defense against a major power or a substantial building program, and two include both. Read the final column and the pattern of failure becomes visible too: three of the six lost power to internal rivals rather than to Persia, which is the real structural weakness of this period and is discussed below.

## Amyrtaeus and the Winning of Independence

Egyptian independence in circa 404 BCE was won by timing rather than by force of arms, and the timing was excellent.

Darius II died in that year, and the Achaemenid succession was immediately contested between Artaxerxes II and his younger brother Cyrus the Younger, whose bid for the throne would culminate in the battle of Cunaxa and the long march home of the Greek mercenaries who had served him. With the empire absorbed in a dynastic war at its center, its western province had an opening, and Amyrtaeus of Sais took it.

He is a shadowy figure. Egyptian material naming him is sparse, and much of what we have comes from Aramaic documents at Elephantine, where the garrison's dating formulas shift from Persian kings to Amyrtaeus, and from the Demotic Chronicle, a later Egyptian text that comments on the reigns of this period in prophetic and moralizing terms. He appears to have carried a Saite name and to have claimed continuity with the great Twenty-sixth Dynasty, which by then had been gone for over a century but retained enormous prestige as the last era of successful native rule.

What is notable about the Elephantine evidence is how gradually his authority reached the far south. Documents there continue dating by Persian reigns for a period after the Delta revolt, which indicates that independence spread down the country over time rather than arriving everywhere at once. That is a useful corrective to the impression of a single national uprising.

His fall came from within. The Demotic Chronicle indicates that he was condemned and executed, and his successor Nepherites I came from Mendes rather than from Sais, so the transition was a change of house rather than a succession. Egypt had won its freedom and immediately reproduced the Delta rivalry that had characterized its politics for centuries.

## The Twenty-ninth Dynasty and the Politics of Survival

The Mendesian dynasty ruled for roughly twenty years and spent them doing two things: fighting over the throne and building an alliance system that kept Persia out.

Nepherites I established the house and, on the evidence of Greek sources, entered into alliance with Sparta against Persia, sending grain and naval materials. The alliance was not entirely successful in execution, with a consignment reportedly intercepted, but the strategic instinct was correct and became the dynasty's standing policy: Egypt could not defeat Persia alone, so Egypt would ensure Persia always had another war to fight.

The succession after Nepherites I was disputed, with a short-lived claimant named Psammuthis appearing in the record, and Hakor emerging as the winner. Hakor, called Achoris by Greek writers, ruled for roughly thirteen years and is the most substantial king of the dynasty.

His achievement was survival against a determined effort. Persia mounted a sustained campaign to retake Egypt in the 380s BCE, and Hakor met it with a combination of hired professionals, fortification, and alliances. He engaged the Athenian general Chabrias, one of the ablest commanders of the age, and supported Evagoras of Salamis in Cyprus, who was in revolt against Persia at the same time. The strategy of funding a second front is visible here in its clearest form. Persian forces failed to break into Egypt across several campaigning seasons, and Hakor died in office with the country still free.

He also built, at Karnak, at Medinet Habu, and elsewhere, which is worth noting because it establishes that the Thirtieth Dynasty's construction program had a precedent rather than appearing from nowhere.

His son Nepherites II lasted a matter of months before being displaced by Nectanebo I, a general from Sebennytos. Once again the throne changed by internal coup rather than by foreign action.

### What was the 30th Dynasty?

The Thirtieth Dynasty was the last native Egyptian ruling house, based at Sebennytos in the Delta and ruling from circa 380 to 343 BCE. It comprised Nectanebo I, his son Teos, and his grandson Nectanebo II. It repelled two full Persian invasions, ran an aggressive Aegean diplomacy, and produced the largest temple building program of the Late Period.

## Nectanebo I: The Path to Power

Nectanebo I, whose Egyptian name was Nakhtnebef, came to the throne in circa 380 BCE from a military background in Sebennytos, displacing the brief Nepherites II. The details of the coup are not recorded, and what the sources give us is the outcome rather than the mechanism.

That origin matters for how he governed. He was the third house in twenty-five years to take the Egyptian throne by force, and he took it in a country whose independence rested on Persian distraction that could end at any moment. His problem was therefore identical in structure to the one Psamtik I had faced three centuries earlier: a ruler without hereditary claim, in a country needing defense, whose legitimacy had to be manufactured. And his solution was the same solution the Saites had pioneered, which was to build.

He also secured the succession in a way his predecessors had not, associating his son Teos with the throne during his own reign and establishing a family line rather than a personal rule. That the Thirtieth Dynasty produced three kings across four decades when the two preceding dynasties had produced five in twenty-five years is a measure of how much better he managed the internal problem.

## The Persian Invasion of 373 BCE

The defining military event of Nectanebo I's reign was the largest Persian attempt to retake Egypt since the revolt, and its failure bought Egypt another two decades.

Artaxerxes II assembled a substantial force under the Persian satrap Pharnabazus, with the Athenian general Iphicrates commanding a large body of Greek mercenaries in Persian service. The arrangement is worth pausing on: an Athenian general led Persian troops against an Egyptian king who was himself employing Greek professionals. The mercenary market of the fourth century BCE served everyone, and national allegiance had almost nothing to do with who fought for whom.

The invasion force reached the Delta and, rather than assaulting the fortified eastern approaches at Pelusium directly, entered through the Mendesian mouth of the Nile, achieving initial success against local defenses. What followed is one of the better-documented command failures of the period. Iphicrates urged an immediate push upriver to Memphis while Egyptian forces were still concentrating, arguing that speed was the whole advantage. Pharnabazus refused, insisting on waiting for the full force to assemble before advancing. The delay allowed Nectanebo I to reinforce, and the Egyptians recovered the ground they had lost.

Then the Nile did the rest. The annual flood began, the Delta became progressively impassable to a large army, and the Persian force withdrew without having reached Memphis. The invasion had failed, and the recrimination between Pharnabazus and Iphicrates was severe enough that the Athenian left Persian service.

Two lessons come out of this episode and both shaped the following decades. The first is that Egypt's defensive geography was still formidable, provided the defender could delay an invader past the start of the flood. The second is that Persian invasions of Egypt in this era were repeatedly hobbled by divided command between Persian satraps and their Greek professional subordinates, a structural weakness Artaxerxes III would eventually fix.

### How did Egypt repel Persian invasions in the fourth century BCE?

Through fortified Delta approaches, hired Greek professional troops, and the Nile flood. Egyptian defense aimed to delay rather than to destroy: if an invader could be held until the inundation made the Delta impassable, the campaign collapsed on its own. Divided Persian command between satraps and Greek mercenary generals repeatedly helped.

## Nectanebo I as Builder

The construction record of Nectanebo I is the strongest single piece of evidence against the decline narrative, because it is physical, datable, and distributed across the whole country.

At Philae, near the First Cataract, he began the temple complex of Isis that later generations would extend into one of the most complete religious sites in Egypt. The kiosk and the earliest surviving structures on the island belong to his reign, and the choice of location is itself a statement: a king building at the southern frontier is a king asserting control of the entire country.

At Karnak he raised the first pylon, the massive western gateway that remains the entrance to the temple complex and is the largest pylon at the site. That it was never fully completed is often noted; that a fourth-century king was building on that scale at the greatest temple in Egypt is the more significant fact.

Beyond those, his work appears at Memphis, Saqqara, Bubastis, Hermopolis, Edfu, El-Kab, and across the Delta, generally in the form of temple enclosure walls, gateways, birth houses, and additions to existing sanctuaries. The birth house, or mammisi, a subsidiary chapel celebrating the divine birth of the god's child and by extension the king's own divine legitimacy, becomes a standard feature of Egyptian temple complexes in this period, and its promotion under the Thirtieth Dynasty is not accidental for a house that needed exactly that argument made.

The most informative document of his reign is not a building but a decree. The Naucratis stela, dated to his first regnal year, records a grant assigning a tenth of the customs revenue on imports through the Canopic mouth of the Nile, and a tenth of the production of Naucratis itself, to the temple of Neith at Sais. A near-identical stela was later found at the sunken port site of Thonis-Heracleion, showing the decree was published at the point of collection as well as at the beneficiary temple.

That document tells us several things at once. Egypt was running a taxed and monitored international trade through defined ports. The crown had the administrative reach to assign a revenue stream to a specific temple and to publish the arrangement in two places. And a new dynasty from Sebennytos was directing money to the temple of the Saite dynastic goddess at Sais, which is a legitimacy transaction as much as a pious one: the new house buying continuity with the last great native dynasty.

## Teos and the Cost of Ambition

Teos succeeded his father in circa 362 BCE and lost the throne within two years, and his reign is the clearest demonstration of the constraint every Late Period king operated under.

His strategic reasoning was defensible. Persia in the 360s BCE was distracted by a widespread revolt of its western satraps, and an Egyptian offensive into the Levant might have converted Egypt from a besieged defender into a regional power with strategic depth. Teos assembled an unusually strong command for the effort: Agesilaus II, the king of Sparta, brought a Spartan contingent, and Chabrias of Athens, the same general who had served Hakor, handled naval affairs.

The problem was money. A Levantine campaign with foreign professionals required cash on a scale Egyptian revenues did not casually produce, and Teos raised it by taxing the temples. Greek sources credit Chabrias with proposing the fiscal measures, which included levies on temple income and on priestly personnel, alongside a currency arrangement in which precious metal was requisitioned against later repayment.

This was politically fatal, and the reason is structural rather than incidental. Temples in Egypt were not merely religious bodies. They were the largest landowners outside the crown, major employers, and the manufacturers of royal legitimacy, since the rituals that made a man pharaoh were performed in them by their personnel. A king who taxed them heavily was extracting from the institution whose endorsement he required. Teos went abroad on campaign and left behind a priesthood with a grievance and a family with ambitions.

The coup came from inside his own house. His brother Tjahapimu, left in charge in Egypt, put forward his own son, the future Nectanebo II, who was with the army in the Levant. Agesilaus, whose Spartan contingent was the decisive military factor on the spot, backed the younger man. Teos found himself abandoned by his commanders and his family simultaneously, and fled, ending his life at the Persian court, which is a bleak irony for a king who had marched to fight Persia.

The lesson every subsequent Egyptian ruler could draw was unambiguous. Egypt could defend itself indefinitely on its own frontier and could not attack anyone without destabilizing its own base. That constraint is why the Thirtieth Dynasty's remaining years were spent building temples rather than campaigning, and it is one of the deeper reasons native Egypt could never break the strategic stalemate with Persia.

## Nectanebo II: The Path to Power

Nectanebo II, whose Egyptian name was Nakhthorheb, came to the throne in circa 360 BCE through the coup against his uncle, backed by his father Tjahapimu and by Agesilaus of Sparta. He had to fight off at least one rival claimant, a Mendesian challenger who briefly contested the throne with substantial support, before his position was secure, and Agesilaus' military advice appears to have been decisive in that contest as well.

He inherited a country that had just been through a failed offensive, a fiscal raid on its temples, and a dynastic coup, and his first task was to repair all three. His method was to reverse Teos' policy completely. Where Teos had taken from the temples, Nectanebo II gave, and gave on a scale no Egyptian king had matched for centuries.

That is not merely a pious gesture. It is the single most coherent political program of any Late Period reign, and it addressed his legitimacy problem, his uncle's grievance-generating fiscal legacy, and the need to bind the country's most powerful institutions to a house that had now taken the throne twice by force.

### Who was Nectanebo II?

Nectanebo II, Egyptian name Nakhthorheb, ruled from circa 360 to 343 BCE and was the last native Egyptian pharaoh. He came to power through a coup against his uncle Teos, repelled a Persian invasion in circa 351 BCE, and funded the largest temple building program of the Late Period before being defeated by Artaxerxes III and fleeing south.

## The Building Program of Nectanebo II

The scale of the construction under Nectanebo II is the fact that most reliably surprises readers who arrive expecting a twilight reign.

Work appears at more sites than under any other Late Period king. At Saqqara he invested heavily in the Serapeum, where the Apis bulls were buried, and in the wider Sacred Animal Necropolis, the vast complex of catacombs and associated temples serving the votive animal cults. At Behbeit el-Hagar in the Delta he began or substantially advanced a temple of Isis constructed in hard stone, a project of exceptional ambition that the Ptolemies continued. At Philae he continued his grandfather's work. At Bubastis, Hermopolis, Edfu, Karnak, Memphis, Tanis, and elsewhere his name appears on gateways, chapels, enclosure walls, and additions.

The quality is as notable as the quantity. Sculpture and architectural relief from his reign show the archaizing precision that had characterized Egyptian official art since the Saites, executed with the technical control the period was capable of at its best. The colossal falcon statues associated with him, with a small royal figure sheltered between the bird's feet, are among the more striking surviving objects of the era and express the relationship between the king and the god Horus in a single compact image.

His unused sarcophagus survives, a large hard-stone container covered in funerary texts, prepared for a burial that never took place because he lost his kingdom. It was later removed from Egypt and, at one stage of its long afterlife, served as a basin in a mosque in Cairo before being identified. Few objects state the shape of a reign so economically: an enormous investment in eternity, abandoned unused.

The animal cult investment deserves particular attention because it is easy to misread. Funding the burial of sacred bulls and the catacombs of mummified ibises can look like superstition consuming a state's resources. It was in fact an efficient political instrument. The votive animal cults were where ordinary Egyptians participated in religion, since the great temple rituals were closed to the public, and the industry around them generated substantial independent income for temple institutions. A king who funded them was buying popular religious legitimacy and institutional loyalty at the same time, and doing it in a way visible to every pilgrim who came to dedicate a mummified bird.

## The Persian Attempt of 351 BCE

Artaxerxes III came to the Persian throne determined to recover Egypt, and his first attempt failed.

The campaign of circa 351 BCE was launched after he had consolidated his position at home, and it broke on the same defenses that had stopped his predecessors: fortified Delta approaches, Egyptian forces stiffened by Greek professionals, and the constraints of season and terrain. Nectanebo II held.

The consequences were larger than the campaign itself. A Persian failure in Egypt encouraged revolt elsewhere in the western empire. Sidon and the Phoenician cities rose, and Cyprus followed, and Nectanebo II supported them with troops, sending a substantial force of Greek mercenaries to Sidon under the command of Mentor of Rhodes. For a brief period the Egyptian strategy of funding somebody else's war against Persia was working better than it ever had.

Then it collapsed. Sidon was betrayed to the Persians by its own king, and the city was destroyed with great loss of life. Mentor of Rhodes, the Greek commander Egypt had sent, changed sides and entered Persian service, where he became one of Artaxerxes III's most capable officers. The strategic instrument Egypt had used successfully for sixty years, the outsourced second front, had been turned into a source of expert knowledge for the enemy.

### How did Nectanebo II fight to save Egypt?

By defending the Delta approaches with fortifications and Greek mercenary troops, supporting anti-Persian revolts in Phoenicia and Cyprus to divide Artaxerxes III's attention, and repelling a full invasion in circa 351 BCE. The strategy worked until Persia committed overwhelming force with competent Greek commanders in circa 343 BCE.

## The Fall: The Campaign of 343 BCE

Artaxerxes III returned in circa 343 BCE with everything his earlier attempt had lacked, and the differences are what explain the outcome.

The first difference was force. He came in person with a large army rather than delegating to a satrap, which removed the divided-command problem that had wrecked the invasion of 373 BCE. The second was expertise. Mentor of Rhodes, who had commanded Egyptian troops at Sidon, was now in Persian service and knew Egyptian dispositions, defenses, and the character of the Greek contingents on the other side. Two other experienced Greek commanders, Lacrates of Thebes and Nicostratus of Argos, led divisions, and the eunuch official Bagoas held a senior command. The third was money, and it was decisive in a way that requires spelling out.

Egypt's defense had rested for sixty years on hired Greek professionals holding fortified positions. Persia now employed Greek professionals in larger numbers on better terms. When the campaign turned, the mercenaries in Egyptian service acted as mercenaries do: the garrison at Pelusium, commanded by a Greek officer, negotiated terms and was allowed to depart with its possessions, and other contingents followed suit. A defense system built on the mercenary market fails at the precise moment the opponent outbids you, and that is what happened.

The tactical course of the campaign followed from all this. Pelusium was contained and bypassed rather than stormed. Persian forces landed and advanced through the Delta at several points, the fortified line was outflanked rather than broken, and Egyptian forces found themselves defending a position that had been rendered irrelevant. Nectanebo II withdrew to Memphis with the intention of holding the interior, then judged the position untenable and went south.

The occupation that followed was harsh. Greek and Egyptian sources describe heavy exactions, the demolition of defensive walls, and the stripping of temple treasuries, and the sacred-animal outrages that hostile tradition had wrongly attached to Cambyses two centuries earlier were attributed to Artaxerxes III with considerably more contemporary complaint behind them. Egypt became the Thirty-first Dynasty in Manetho's numbering, the Second Persian Period, and it lasted about eleven years.

### When did native Egyptian rule end?

In circa 343 BCE, when Artaxerxes III defeated Nectanebo II and Egypt returned to Persian control as the Thirty-first Dynasty. Apart from a possible brief rebel reign under Khababash a few years later, that date ends roughly three thousand years of rule by Egyptian kings, and Egypt would not be governed by Egyptians again until the twentieth century CE.

## What Happened to Nectanebo II

The honest answer is that nobody knows, and the way the sources handle that gap is itself instructive.

He withdrew from Memphis and went south, and the contemporary record stops there. Later Greek writers say he fled to Ethiopia, meaning Kush, the Nubian kingdom upriver, which is a plausible destination since it lay outside Persian reach and had ancient ties to Egypt. Whether he lived there for years, attempted a return, or died soon after is unrecorded. No burial is known, and the sarcophagus prepared for him was never used.

What filled that vacuum was legend, and the legend became one of the most widely circulated stories of the ancient world. In the tradition known as the Alexander Romance, which took shape in the centuries after Alexander's death and was translated and adapted across dozens of languages, Nectanebo II is a master magician who foresees the Persian conquest, flees to Macedon in disguise, becomes court astrologer to Philip II, and by magical deception fathers Alexander on Olympias. Alexander is thereby made the son of the last Egyptian pharaoh, and his conquest of Egypt becomes a homecoming rather than an invasion.

The story is demonstrably fiction, and its function is transparent. It solves a legitimacy problem for Macedonian and Ptolemaic rule in Egypt by making the conqueror an Egyptian heir, and it solves an emotional problem for Egyptians by making the last native pharaoh the father of the man who ended Persian rule. It is a piece of political mythology doing useful work for everyone involved, which is why it spread so far.

There is a related Egyptian text, surviving in fragments, in which Nectanebo II receives a dream vision concerning a temple and its decoration, and it belongs to the same broad tradition of narrative literature attaching to his name. Egyptian storytellers of the Ptolemaic and Roman periods returned repeatedly to the last native king, which tells us he retained a hold on Egyptian imagination long after his kingdom was gone.

### What happened to Nectanebo II?

He withdrew from Memphis in circa 343 BCE and fled south, probably to Nubia, and the contemporary record ends there. No burial is known and the sarcophagus prepared for him was never used. The elaborate later story making him a magician and the true father of Alexander the Great belongs to the Alexander Romance and is fiction.

## The Khababash Complication

Calling Nectanebo II the last native pharaoh is the standard position and it has a genuine qualification, which most short accounts omit.

A ruler named Khababash appears in the record as an Egyptian king during the Second Persian Period, probably in the late 330s BCE, between the reigns of Artaxerxes III and the arrival of Alexander. He is attested by the Satrap Stela, a document set up under Ptolemy in the late fourth century BCE, which refers to his actions in connection with land granted to the temple of Buto and describes him inspecting the Delta marshes against a Persian naval threat. He is also attested by a small number of other objects including an Apis burial reference, which places him at Memphis and shows him performing the royal religious functions.

What this means is that after Nectanebo II fled, at least one further Egyptian claimed and to some degree exercised the kingship, was recognized in Egyptian religious contexts, and organized resistance to Persia. His reign appears to have been short and to have ended in Persian suppression, and he is not counted in Manetho's dynastic scheme.

Whether that makes him the last native pharaoh depends on what the phrase is doing. If it means the last Egyptian recognized as king by Egyptian religious institutions, Khababash has a claim. If it means the last king of a native dynasty ruling a united and independent Egypt, Nectanebo II is the answer. The second is the more useful sense and is the reason the convention exists, but a reader who encounters Khababash in a specialist account should know that the convention is a convention rather than an oversight.

The Satrap Stela itself is worth a note. It was erected under the early Ptolemaic administration and it invokes Khababash approvingly while condemning Persian rule, which is Ptolemaic propaganda positioning the new Greek regime as the restorer of Egyptian rights against Persian depredation. A rebel Egyptian king was useful to that argument, which is a reminder that even the evidence for Egyptian resistance often survives because somebody later found it politically convenient.

## Contemporary and Later Judgments

How these kings were seen divides sharply by source, era, and interest.

Greek writers of the fourth century BCE treated Egypt primarily as a theater in the long conflict with Persia and as an employer of Greek soldiers. Their interest in Nectanebo I and II is largely instrumental, concerned with campaigns, alliances, and payments, and their assessments of Egyptian generalship are not flattering, though it should be noted that Greek military writing rarely flatters non-Greeks. Diodorus Siculus, writing much later but preserving fourth-century material, provides most of the narrative for these reigns and is the source for the Iphicrates and Pharnabazus dispute and for much of the 343 BCE campaign.

Egyptian religious sources are wholly positive, as one would expect from temple contexts funded by the kings in question. Nectanebo II in particular appears in temple inscriptions as a conventional pious pharaoh, and the volume of his building means his name is one of the more commonly encountered royal cartouches of the Late Period.

The Demotic Chronicle, a later Egyptian composition, takes a more evaluative line, commenting on the reigns of this period in terms of whether kings governed rightly, and it is one of the few Egyptian sources that judges rather than merely praises. Its treatment reflects priestly perspectives and should be read as a temple-derived assessment rather than as neutral history.

Later Egyptian storytelling, as discussed, made Nectanebo II a magician and Alexander's father, which is affectionate rather than analytical but tells us he was remembered.

Modern scholarship has moved considerably. Nineteenth and early twentieth century accounts, working within a framework that treated everything after the New Kingdom as decline, described these reigns as a feeble last gasp. That verdict has not survived the systematic study of the building record, which established that Nectanebo I and II constructed at more sites than most New Kingdom rulers, nor the reassessment of the military record, which established that Egypt repelled full Persian invasions twice.

The current position is more interested in mechanisms than in verdicts: how the mercenary economy actually worked, how temple funding related to royal legitimacy, why internal coups were so frequent when external defense was so effective, and what the Delta political landscape looked like beneath the dynastic surface. Those are researchable questions rather than moral gradings, and the shift toward them is the surest sign that the decline framing has been retired.

## Why Native Rule Collapsed

The question deserves a structural answer rather than a narrative one, and four factors account for it.

The first is the resource asymmetry, which is the deepest cause and the least avoidable. Persia could lose a campaign in Egypt and return a decade later with a fresh army. Egypt could lose once. That is not a statement about Egyptian competence; it is arithmetic, and it applied equally to every medium-sized state facing the Achaemenid empire. Sixty years of successful defense against an opponent with that advantage is a considerable achievement in itself, and the eventual failure required only that Persia eventually get one campaign right.

The second is the mercenary dependence. Egypt could not generate heavy infantry of the required standard from its own population and economy, so it bought it, and the market it bought in was open to its enemy. Phanes' defection had illustrated the vulnerability in 525 BCE, Mentor's defection illustrated it again after Sidon, and the Pelusium garrison's negotiated departure in 343 BCE closed the circle. The problem was not that mercenaries were unreliable in some moral sense. It was that Egypt's defensive model gave a wealthier opponent a purchasable route to victory.

The third is the fiscal constraint on offensive action. Egypt could defend cheaply, using terrain, fortification, and the flood, and could not attack without raising sums that meant taxing temples, which meant losing the domestic support that made a king legitimate. Teos proved this decisively. A state that can only ever defend eventually loses, because the initiative always belongs to the other side, and Egypt was structurally incapable of taking the initiative without destabilizing itself.

The fourth is the pattern of internal succession by coup. Between 404 and 343 BCE, three dynasties held the throne and at least four transitions were violent or contested. Amyrtaeus was deposed and executed, Nepherites II was displaced within months, Teos was overthrown by his own family, and Nectanebo II had to fight off a rival claimant. Every one of those episodes consumed attention, money, and elite cohesion at a moment when the country needed all three pointed outward. Egyptian independence in this period was never threatened by internal division in the sense of the country splitting apart, but it was persistently weakened by competition for who would lead it.

### Why did native Egyptian rule collapse?

Because Persia could absorb repeated defeat and return while Egypt could not lose once, and because Egyptian defense rested on hired Greek professionals whom a wealthier opponent could outbid. Frequent internal coups drained elite cohesion, and the fiscal cost of any offensive made temple taxation necessary, which was politically fatal.

What is not on that list is decadence, exhaustion, or loss of will, and their absence is the point. Nectanebo II was funding the largest temple program of the era and supporting rebellions across the eastern Mediterranean in the years immediately before his defeat. This was not a state that had given up.

## The Last-Flourish Thesis

The argument this article advances can be stated in three propositions, each of which is testable against the record.

The first is that the sixty years of restored independence were a period of genuine achievement rather than a holding action. The evidence is the building program, which places Nectanebo I and II among the more prolific temple builders in Egyptian history, and the military record, which includes the repulse of full Persian invasions in circa 373 and circa 351 BCE. A state under terminal decline does not do either of those things, let alone both.

The second is that the achievement was driven by the same mechanism that had driven the Saite revival: a legitimacy deficit answered through cultural and religious investment. Every ruling house of this period took the throne by force from another Egyptian house. None could claim unbroken descent. All of them therefore had to demonstrate that they were the correct successors of Egypt's ancient kingship, and the demonstration took the form of temples, archaizing art, and lavish funding of the cults ordinary Egyptians participated in. Nectanebo II's building program is not incidental to his political position; it was his political position.

The third is that the end was external and abrupt rather than internal and gradual. The campaign of 343 BCE succeeded because Artaxerxes III fixed the specific problems that had defeated earlier invasions, committing sufficient force, unifying command under himself, employing commanders who knew Egyptian defenses from the inside, and outbidding Egypt for the professional troops the defense depended on. Nothing in that list describes an Egypt that had weakened. It describes a Persia that had learned.

The thesis makes a prediction worth noting: if the revival had been mere display over a hollow state, the country should have folded quickly and stayed folded. Instead Egypt produced at least one further claimant, Khababash, who organized resistance and was recognized by religious institutions, and then received Alexander a decade later with an enthusiasm that indicates a population with clear opinions about who should govern them. The state was functioning to the end and beyond it.

## Myths and Misconceptions to Correct

Several beliefs about this period are widely repeated and do not hold.

The first is that the last native pharaohs were weak rulers presiding over a spent civilization. The building record and the military record both contradict it directly, and the contradiction is not subtle.

The second is that Nectanebo II was Alexander's father. This belongs to the Alexander Romance, a literary tradition that took shape centuries later and served the political interests of Macedonian and Ptolemaic rule in Egypt. It is one of the most successful pieces of political fiction in ancient literature and it is not history.

The third is that Egypt fell in 343 BCE because Egyptians would not fight. Egyptian forces fought in 373 BCE, in circa 351 BCE, at Sidon by proxy, and in 343 BCE itself. What failed at Pelusium in 343 BCE was a Greek mercenary garrison negotiating terms, which is a fact about the mercenary market rather than about Egyptian morale.

The fourth is that the Thirtieth Dynasty was a Theban or Upper Egyptian revival. It was Delta-based throughout, ruling from Sebennytos, and its building in Upper Egypt was an assertion of national reach rather than a sign of southern origin.

The fifth is that native rule ended in a single clean moment. The Khababash episode shows that Egyptian kingship was claimed and religiously recognized again after Nectanebo II fled, which complicates any tidy endpoint.

The sixth is that 343 BCE ended ancient Egyptian civilization. It ended native political rule. Egyptian religion, language, art, and temple institutions continued and in some respects expanded for centuries afterward, and the temples these kings began were completed by Ptolemaic and Roman rulers who continued their programs.

## What the Last Native Pharaohs Left Behind

Their legacy is more visible today than that of most Egyptian dynasties, for a reason that is easy to miss.

The Ptolemies inherited their building projects and finished them. The Isis temple at Philae, begun under Nectanebo I, became one of the great religious centers of Greco-Roman Egypt and remained in use for the better part of a thousand years, hosting some of the latest hieroglyphic inscriptions known. The Iseum at Behbeit el-Hagar was continued under Ptolemy II and Ptolemy III. The birth house form that the Thirtieth Dynasty promoted became a standard element of Ptolemaic temple architecture. When a visitor to Egypt today looks at a Greco-Roman period temple, a good deal of what they are seeing was begun by or modeled on the work of the last native kings.

The animal cult infrastructure they funded at Saqqara and elsewhere operated for centuries after them, and the votive economy around it remained a major part of Egyptian religious life into the Roman period.

The archaizing artistic idiom they maintained passed directly into Ptolemaic royal representation. Greek kings of Egypt had themselves depicted in Egyptian temple relief in exactly the conventions the Thirtieth Dynasty used, because those conventions were what an Egyptian audience recognized as kingship.

And the memory itself proved durable. Egyptian storytellers of the Ptolemaic and Roman eras returned repeatedly to Nectanebo II, and prophetic literature circulating in temple circles looked forward to the expulsion of foreigners and the return of proper rule, which is a tradition with the last native pharaohs standing behind it. The idea that Egypt had once governed itself and might again was politically live in Egyptian religious circles for centuries, and these kings are the last historical instance it could point to.

## Governing Egypt Between the Occupations

The administrative story of these sixty years is thinly documented compared with the building record, and what can be reconstructed suggests continuity rather than reinvention.

The machinery these kings inherited was the machinery Persia had inherited from the Saites and had largely left in place: nome administration, temple estates, assessment of agricultural land, Demotic legal instruments, and a scribal culture running in two registers. Independence did not require rebuilding any of it. What changed was who sat at the top and where the revenue went, and the second of those changes was substantial, since tribute that had flowed to an imperial treasury now stayed in Egypt.

That redirection is almost certainly what funded the building program, and it is the simplest explanation for why the Thirtieth Dynasty could construct on a scale the Persian period had not. A province paying imperial assessment has less to spend at home than an independent kingdom of the same productive capacity. Nectanebo I and II were not necessarily richer than their Persian-period predecessors; they were keeping more of the same income.

Taxation appears to have followed traditional lines, flexing with the flood as Egyptian practice had always done, which is one of the concrete improvements independence brought to ordinary taxpayers over the fixed imperial quota. The Naucratis decree of Nectanebo I shows customs revenue on international trade being assessed at defined points and assigned by royal decree, which indicates a functioning fiscal administration with real reach into commerce.

The relationship with temples was the central political fact of internal governance, and the Thirtieth Dynasty's approach was investment rather than extraction. Grants of revenue, construction, endowment of cults, and the funding of animal necropolis operations all flowed from the crown to religious institutions. Teos' brief departure from that policy cost him his throne within two years, which established the boundary with unusual clarity for everyone who came after.

Delta cities retained significant local weight throughout. Sais, Mendes, Sebennytos, Bubastis, and Tanis were all substantial centers with their own elites and their own histories of producing rulers, and the frequency of dynastic change across this period reflects that landscape. Egypt was administratively unified and politically competitive, which is a different condition from the fragmentation of the Third Intermediate Period and should not be confused with it.

Upper Egypt was governed rather than merely claimed. Building at Karnak, Edfu, El-Kab, and Philae under both Nectanebos demonstrates crown resources reaching a thousand kilometers up the Nile, and the Theban establishment appears to have cooperated without producing the semi-independent power center it had been in earlier centuries. The God's Wife of Amun office, which had been the instrument of Saite control of the south, had faded from significance by this period, and the south was integrated through more ordinary administrative means.

## The Greek Connection

No account of the last native pharaohs makes sense without the Greeks, who appear in this period as soldiers, generals, allies, enemies, and eventually as the successors to everything.

The military relationship is the most consequential. Egyptian armies of this era combined native machimoi levies with hired Greek heavy infantry, and Egyptian kings sought out the best available Greek commanders. Chabrias of Athens served Hakor and later Teos. Agesilaus II, the Spartan king, brought a contingent to Teos' Levantine campaign and then determined the outcome of the succession dispute that replaced him with Nectanebo II. Mentor of Rhodes commanded Egyptian troops at Sidon before changing sides.

The pattern across all of these engagements is that Greek professionals were a market commodity rather than allies in any national sense. The same men served Egypt and Persia at different points, sometimes within a few years, and their decisions followed pay, opportunity, and personal circumstance. Iphicrates of Athens commanded Persian mercenaries against Egypt in 373 BCE while Chabrias of Athens had recently served the Egyptian king. Both were prominent Athenians. Athens itself alternated between supporting Egypt against Persia and accommodating Persia depending on its own situation.

The diplomatic relationship followed the same logic in reverse. Egyptian foreign policy for these sixty years consisted almost entirely of ensuring that Persia had something else to worry about. Alliances with Sparta, with Athens, with Evagoras of Salamis in Cyprus, and support for the Phoenician revolt under Nectanebo II were all instruments of that policy. It worked remarkably well for a long time and failed catastrophically once, when the Sidonian revolt collapsed and handed Persia both a lesson and a commander.

The commercial relationship continued from the Saite arrangement. Naucratis remained the regulated point of Greek trade, and Nectanebo I's decree assigning a tenth of its production and of Canopic customs to the temple of Neith shows that commerce running through it was substantial enough to be worth a royal grant. Greek pottery, coinage, and goods circulated in the Delta, and Egyptian grain moved outward.

What all of this means for the fall of 343 BCE is straightforward. Egypt had built a defensive and diplomatic system that depended entirely on the Greek world, and the Greek world was available to Persia on the same terms. When Artaxerxes III decided to spend what was necessary, the system Egypt had relied on for sixty years became the mechanism of its defeat.

## How We Know: The Sources

The evidence for these reigns is uneven in a way that shapes what can be said about them, and being explicit about it is part of an honest account.

The building record is the strongest material and the reason the decline narrative failed. Temples, gateways, chapels, enclosure walls, and statuary carrying the cartouches of Nectanebo I and Nectanebo II survive across the whole country, are datable, and are physically inspectable. Nobody has to take a text's word for the scale of the Thirtieth Dynasty's construction.

Royal decrees and stelae supply the administrative and fiscal detail. The Naucratis stela of Nectanebo I, and its twin from Thonis-Heracleion, are the outstanding examples, giving a dated revenue assignment with specified sources. The Satrap Stela, though set up under Ptolemy, preserves information about Khababash and about land grants that would otherwise be lost entirely.

Greek narrative history supplies the campaigns. Diodorus Siculus, writing in the first century BCE but drawing on fourth-century sources, provides most of the connected account of the 373 BCE invasion, Teos' Levantine expedition, the succession dispute, and the campaign of 343 BCE. He is our main narrative and he is at several removes from events. Xenophon and other Greek writers supply detail on Agesilaus and on Athenian generals. All of this material is written from a Greek perspective, focused on Greek actors, and interested in Egyptian affairs mainly where they intersect with Greek ones.

Egyptian literary and prophetic material, above all the Demotic Chronicle, gives an Egyptian evaluative perspective but was composed later and reflects temple viewpoints and the concerns of its own time rather than contemporary reporting.

Aramaic documents from Elephantine continue into the early independence period and show how the transition registered at the southern frontier, chiefly through changes in dating formulas.

The gaps are as important as the material. Egyptian administrative papyri from these decades are scarce. Internal politics beyond the succession disputes is largely invisible. The social and economic texture of the period is reconstructed by inference from the periods on either side. And the fate of Nectanebo II, the single detail readers most want, is simply absent. A responsible account of these reigns says clearly which statements rest on physical evidence, which on a Greek narrative at three removes, and which on inference, and this article has tried to keep those distinct.

## Sites and Objects Worth Knowing

Certain places and objects carry most of the evidentiary weight for these reigns, and knowing which ones makes the rest of the reading easier.

Philae, at the First Cataract, holds the beginnings of the Isis complex under Nectanebo I, including his kiosk, and it demonstrates crown reach to the southern frontier. The site's later Ptolemaic and Roman expansion grew directly from what he started.

Karnak holds the first pylon, the enormous western gateway raised under Nectanebo I and never fully finished, which remains the entrance to the temple complex and is the largest structure of its kind at the site.

Saqqara holds the Serapeum and the Sacred Animal Necropolis, both heavily funded by Nectanebo II, and the dated Apis burials there anchor the chronology of the period as they do for the whole Late Period.

Behbeit el-Hagar in the Delta holds the ruins of the hard-stone Iseum begun under Nectanebo II and continued by the early Ptolemies, an exceptionally ambitious project now collapsed but with substantial decorated blocks surviving.

Hermopolis, Bubastis, Edfu, Tanis, and Memphis all carry Thirtieth Dynasty work in the form of gateways, chapels, and additions, and the geographic spread is itself the argument for the dynasty's administrative reach.

The unused granite sarcophagus of Nectanebo II, covered in funerary texts and prepared for a burial that never happened, is the single most eloquent object of the period. So are the colossal falcon statues associated with him, in which a small figure of the king stands sheltered between the feet of the god Horus, an image of royal legitimacy expressed through divine protection that a king who took the throne by coup had particular reason to commission.

Thonis-Heracleion, the sunken port site in the Bay of Aboukir, produced the twin of the Naucratis decree and a body of material illustrating the trade regime the decree taxed.

## Comparing the Two Nectanebos

Grandfather and grandson are often collapsed into a single figure in short accounts, and the differences between them are instructive.

Nectanebo I was the consolidator. He took the throne in a country that had changed ruling houses three times in twenty-five years, and his central achievement was making the change stick. He secured the succession by associating his son with the throne. He defeated the largest Persian attempt of the era. He launched the building program and issued the decree that shows his administration reaching into international commerce. His reign of roughly eighteen years is the longest of the period and the most stable.

Nectanebo II was the maximizer. He inherited a functioning state that had just been shaken by his uncle's failed offensive and fiscal raid, and he pushed every instrument his grandfather had established to its limit. Where Nectanebo I built substantially, Nectanebo II built more. Where Nectanebo I defended Egypt, Nectanebo II defended Egypt and funded revolts across the eastern Mediterranean to keep Persia occupied. Where Nectanebo I supported temples, Nectanebo II poured resources into the animal cults on a scale that made them a national institution.

The contrast in outcome is not a contrast in competence. Nectanebo II faced an opponent who had solved the problems that defeated Nectanebo I's opponent, and no amount of building or diplomacy answers a unified Persian command with sufficient force and better-paid Greek troops. What the comparison does show is a dynasty that escalated rather than coasted. The last native pharaoh was doing more, not less, than his predecessors in the years before he lost.

There is a third figure between them, and Teos is the necessary contrast to both. He tried the one thing neither Nectanebo attempted, which was offensive war, and his removal within two years defined the boundary of Egyptian royal action for the rest of the period. A dynasty is often best understood through the member who broke its rules, and Teos broke the rule that Egyptian kings did not tax temples.

## The Delta Landscape Behind the Dynasties

Underneath the dynastic sequence sits a political geography that explains why the throne changed hands so often, and it is worth setting out because it makes the period's instability legible.

The Egyptian Delta by the fourth century BCE contained several substantial cities with long histories, entrenched local elites, major temples with independent resources, and in some cases a memory of having produced kings. Sais had given Egypt the Twenty-sixth Dynasty and produced Amyrtaeus. Mendes produced the Twenty-ninth. Sebennytos produced the Thirtieth. Bubastis and Tanis had their own earlier royal associations. These were not provincial towns; they were competing centers with the resources and the standing to make a bid.

That landscape had been created by the Third Intermediate Period, when Libyan-descended dynasts established themselves across the Delta as local rulers, and it never fully dissolved. The Saites had suppressed the competition for a century and a half by being conspicuously successful. Persian rule had suppressed it by removing the prize. When independence returned in 404 BCE, so did the competition.

The consequence is that Egyptian internal politics in this period looks less like a national monarchy with occasional palace intrigue and more like a contest among Delta houses for a throne all of them could plausibly claim. Every transition between 404 and 360 BCE was a change of city as well as a change of family, which is not the pattern of ordinary dynastic succession.

Two qualifications keep this from being overstated. First, the country was administratively unified throughout, and none of these houses attempted to rule only their own region. The prize was Egypt, not a Delta principality. Second, the competition did not visibly weaken frontier defense, which functioned effectively across the whole period. What it consumed was elite cohesion and royal attention, and those costs are real but harder to see than a lost battle would be.

The Thirtieth Dynasty's relative durability, three kings across nearly forty years, suggests Nectanebo I had found at least a partial answer, most likely a combination of associating his heir with the throne during his lifetime and buying institutional support through the building and endowment program.

## After the Fall: Eleven Years and Alexander

What happened between 343 and 332 BCE is properly the subject of other articles in this cluster, and a short account here completes the story of native rule.

The Second Persian Period, Manetho's Thirty-first Dynasty, ran under Artaxerxes III, then briefly under Artaxerxes IV, and finally under Darius III. It is remembered as the harshest phase of Persian rule in Egypt, and the complaints preserved about it are more numerous and more contemporary than those attaching to the first occupation. Walls pulled down, treasuries stripped, and religious offense are the standing charges. Khababash's rising, discussed above, belongs to this decade.

Then in circa 332 BCE Alexander of Macedon arrived, having defeated Darius III at Issus and reduced Tyre and Gaza on the way south. The Persian satrap Mazaces surrendered Egypt without a fight. Alexander sacrificed to the Apis bull at Memphis, was recognized as pharaoh, founded Alexandria on the coast, and made the difficult journey to the oracle of Amun at Siwa.

The contrast with 343 BCE is the clearest available evidence about Egyptian attitudes. A foreign conqueror at the head of a foreign army was received without resistance and with apparent enthusiasm, eleven years after another foreign conqueror had been fought at every stage. The difference was not nationality. It was that Alexander performed Egyptian kingship in public, honored the temples, and did the things Persian rulers had increasingly declined to do.

That is the final vindication of the last native pharaohs' central strategy, arriving too late to help them. Nectanebo I and Nectanebo II had understood that ruling Egypt required funding and participating in Egyptian religion, and had built their entire domestic program on that understanding. Alexander understood the same thing within days of arriving, and it delivered him a country that had spent sixty years resisting the alternative.

## The Army of the Last Native Pharaohs

The military instrument these kings commanded is worth describing directly, because the story of their fall turns on its composition.

The Egyptian component remained the machimoi, the hereditary warrior stratum of largely Libyan descent who held land in exchange for service and had constituted the numerical bulk of Egyptian armies since the Saite period. By the fourth century BCE they were thoroughly Egyptianized in language, religion, and self-identification, and they were a political constituency as much as a military one. Greek writers give them numbers that cannot be verified and treat them dismissively, which reflects Greek assumptions about non-hoplite troops more than any careful assessment.

The professional component was Greek and, to a lesser extent, Anatolian, hired in the open mercenary market and paid in silver. These men fought in the heavy armored style that decided battles in this era, and Egyptian kings recruited not only soldiers but commanders, engaging Chabrias, Agesilaus, and Mentor at various points. The proportion of hired troops in Egyptian armies of this period appears to have been substantial, and their concentration at the critical defensive points is what made the Pelusium garrison's negotiated departure in 343 BCE so decisive.

Fortification was arguably the most important arm of all. Egyptian defensive doctrine in the fourth century BCE was built around holding the Delta approaches, and the archaeological and textual evidence indicates serious investment in walls, water obstacles, and garrison positions along the eastern edge and at the Nile mouths. Diodorus describes the defenses Artaxerxes III faced as formidable, which is a Greek source crediting an Egyptian achievement and therefore worth taking seriously.

The navy was real but limited. Egypt operated ships and controlled its own river and coastal waters much of the time, and naval forces mattered in the Delta where waterways were the highways. Against Persian access to Phoenician and Cypriot fleets, however, Egypt could not contest the eastern Mediterranean as a whole, and its maritime role was defensive and riverine rather than strategic.

Cavalry remained the permanent gap, as it had been in 525 BCE. Egypt's land and economy did not produce horsemen in the numbers the Iranian plateau did, and this is one reason Egyptian defense was so committed to terrain and fortification. On prepared ground with obstacles, the cavalry disadvantage matters much less; in open field battle it is close to decisive.

Put together, the Egyptian military of these decades was a competent defensive instrument, well suited to the specific task of making an invasion of the Delta expensive and slow, and structurally incapable of offensive operations or of surviving the loss of its purchased professional core.

## Religion Under the Thirtieth Dynasty

Religious policy was the domestic centerpiece of these reigns, and it was policy in the full sense rather than merely piety.

The building program discussed above was its most visible expression, but the endowments and the operational funding mattered as much. Grants of revenue, like the tenth of Naucratis trade assigned to Neith at Sais, gave temples predictable income streams. Support for cult operations, festivals, and the personnel who ran them tied institutional interests to the reigning house. And the promotion of the birth house, the subsidiary chapel celebrating a divine child's nativity, made a theological argument that mattered to a dynasty of usurpers: the king is the god's son, born divine, whatever his human ancestry.

The animal cults received the heaviest investment, particularly under Nectanebo II. The Serapeum at Saqqara, where the Apis bulls were interred, and the wider Sacred Animal Necropolis with its catacombs of ibises, hawks, and baboons, functioned as the point where ordinary Egyptians touched institutional religion. A pilgrim could purchase a mummified animal, dedicate it, and secure the god's attention, in a religious system whose central temple rituals had always been closed to the public. Royal funding of that machinery reached a constituency that temple architecture alone did not.

There is an economic dimension that should not be separated from the religious one. The votive animal industry generated substantial revenue for the institutions that ran it, and those institutions had been living on reduced state support through the Persian period. A king who funded the operations and expanded the facilities was restoring temple finances after a century and a half of imperial squeeze, which is a concrete political benefit distinct from any spiritual one.

Theologically the period is one of continued codification. Temple inscriptions grow more elaborate and more systematic, laying groundwork for the encyclopedic cosmological texts that Ptolemaic temples at Edfu and Dendera would carry. Late Period priests were organizing their religion in writing, and the Thirtieth Dynasty's construction gave them the wall space to do it on.

Nothing about this constitutes religious innovation in the sense of new doctrine. It is intensification, standardization, and expanded access, which is exactly what the era's political conditions would predict and exactly what the Saites had established as the pattern.

## Society and Economy in the Independence Years

The documentary record for these six decades is thinner than for the periods on either side, and what can be said rests substantially on continuity from the Saite and Persian eras and forward into the Ptolemaic one.

Agriculture remained the occupation of the overwhelming majority, and the flood cycle set the year. Grain assessment moved through an administration whose basic logic had been continuous for two thousand years. The most concrete improvement independence brought to ordinary taxpayers was the return of flexible assessment: Egyptian practice adjusted to the height of the inundation, whereas the imperial tribute of the Persian period had been fixed regardless of the harvest. In a poor year that difference was the difference between hardship and ruin.

Legal life continued in Demotic, on the forms established under the Saites. Contracts, leases, loans, marriage settlements, and litigation records follow the same conventions before, during, and after the independence period, and the fact that the paperwork does not change when the kingship does is itself a finding: Egyptian personal and commercial law operated below the level at which conquest and liberation registered. Women continued to appear as parties in their own right, owning, inheriting, selling, and suing without a male guardian, exactly as they had done for centuries.

Trade continued through the regulated Delta ports. The Naucratis decree establishes that Greek commerce through the Canopic mouth was substantial enough to make a tenth of it a worthwhile temple endowment, and the material recovered from Thonis-Heracleion illustrates the traffic. Silver continued to enter Egypt through that trade and continued to be the currency in which professional soldiers were paid, which links the commercial and military stories directly: Egypt's ability to defend itself depended on its ability to sell grain.

Foreign communities remained a feature of specific places rather than a general condition. Greek merchants at Naucratis, Greek and Carian soldiers in garrison towns and Delta camps, and the older established communities on the southern frontier all continued through the transition.

Priestly families continued their long rise in social weight. Temple careers had been the most durable form of status through the Persian occupation precisely because they did not depend on royal favor, and the Thirtieth Dynasty's enormous investment in temple institutions made those careers more attractive still. The deep priestly genealogies recorded on Late Period statues, tracing descent through many generations, remain characteristic of the period and express the same instinct visible in royal archaism: authority grounded in demonstrable continuity when the political present could not supply it.

## Egypt in the Fourth Century World

Placing these reigns against what else was happening around the eastern Mediterranean clarifies both why Egypt survived as long as it did and why it eventually did not.

The fourth century BCE was a period of exhaustion and realignment among the Greek states. The Peloponnesian War had ended in 404 BCE, the same year Egypt regained independence, and the following decades saw Spartan hegemony, Theban ascendancy, and Athenian recovery succeed one another without any of them producing a stable order. One consequence was a large pool of experienced soldiers available for hire, which is the supply side of the mercenary market both Egypt and Persia drew on. Another was that Greek states were persistently short of money and persistently willing to take Persian or Egyptian subsidies.

Persia in the same decades was wealthy, large, and internally troubled. The revolt of the western satraps in the 360s BCE, the succession from Artaxerxes II to Artaxerxes III, and the recurring problems in Anatolia and the Levant all consumed imperial attention. Egyptian independence was sustained in the gaps those troubles created, and Egyptian diplomacy worked hard to widen them.

Macedon, meanwhile, was becoming something nobody had anticipated. Philip II took the Macedonian throne in 359 BCE, a year or two after Nectanebo II took the Egyptian one, and spent the following two decades building the army and the state that his son would use to destroy the Achaemenid empire. The last native pharaoh and the architect of Macedonian power were near contemporaries, which is a useful thing to hold in mind: the world that ended Egyptian independence was itself about to be overturned.

The Phoenician cities and Cyprus were the pivot where these strands met. Egypt's support for Sidon and for Cypriot resistance was the most direct expression of its strategy of dividing Persian attention, and the collapse of the Sidonian revolt was the hinge that turned the strategic situation against Egypt, both by freeing Persian forces and by delivering Mentor of Rhodes into Persian service.

Seen in that frame, the sixty years of Egyptian independence were possible because of a particular configuration of Persian distraction and Greek availability, and they ended when Artaxerxes III resolved his other problems and turned the same Greek availability against Egypt. The world beyond the Nile determined the outcome, as it had in 525 BCE and would again in 332 BCE.

## The Thirtieth Dynasty and the Saite Precedent

The last native dynasty behaved so much like the Twenty-sixth that the resemblance is worth setting out, because it demonstrates that the Saite response to a legitimacy crisis had become the standard Egyptian playbook.

Both dynasties came from Delta cities with no hereditary claim to national kingship. Both took power in a country recently under or immediately threatened by foreign rule. Both faced a strategic situation they could not resolve militarily and therefore invested in legitimacy instead. Both answered the legitimacy problem with archaizing art, temple building, endowment of cults, and conspicuous performance of traditional royal function. Both relied on Greek professional soldiers paid from trade revenue. Both regulated foreign commerce through defined Delta ports and taxed it. Both ended in Persian conquest.

The differences are instructive too. The Saites had a century and a half; the Thirtieth Dynasty had under forty years. The Saites reunified a fragmented country; the Thirtieth Dynasty inherited a unified one and had to defend it. The Saites built the archaizing program from something close to scratch, with the scholarly infrastructure that implied; the Thirtieth Dynasty inherited a mature idiom and could deploy it immediately, which is part of why its building output was so rapid.

The most significant continuity is the temple relationship, and Teos is the exception that proves it. Every successful ruler in both dynasties funded temples and was funded in return with legitimacy. The one king who reversed the flow lasted two years. That is about as clear a demonstration of a political rule as ancient evidence permits.

What this comparison establishes for the wider argument of this cluster is that the pattern is structural rather than personal. Egyptian rulers in the last four centuries of native rule faced a recurring problem, and they converged on the same solution because the solution worked. The revival under siege that characterizes the Saite era is the same phenomenon visible in the Thirtieth Dynasty, running at higher speed against a shorter clock.

## The Sarcophagus and the Falcon: Two Objects That Explain a Reign

Two surviving objects associated with Nectanebo II carry more interpretive weight than any text about him, and they are worth reading closely.

The first is his sarcophagus. It is a large container of hard dark stone, covered inside and out with funerary texts and figures drawn from the corpus of underworld literature, executed to the highest standard the period could produce. It was prepared for his burial and never used, because he lost his kingdom before he died and never returned to Egypt to be interred. Its later history is its own commentary: removed from its intended context, it survived in Egypt for centuries and at one stage served as a basin for ritual ablutions in a mosque in Cairo, before being identified in the modern era and removed to a museum.

Every element of that object states something about the reign. The quality confirms the technical capacity of the era and the resources the king commanded. The completeness of the funerary program confirms that Egyptian royal religion was operating in full at the moment of the conquest, with no sign of decay or abbreviation. And the fact that it was never used confirms the abruptness of the ending. A king planning his eternity on that scale did not expect to lose.

The second is the group of colossal falcon statues associated with him, in which a hard-stone image of the god Horus as a falcon stands with a small figure of the king sheltered between its feet, protected by the god's presence. The composition is not new in Egyptian art, but its scale and prominence under this king are notable.

The image makes an argument. A king who took the throne by coup against his own uncle, in a dynasty that had itself taken power by coup, could not appeal to unbroken descent. What he could do was show himself as the god's protected one, small before the divine and legitimated by that relationship rather than by ancestry. The birth houses his dynasty promoted make the same argument in architecture: the king is the god's child, born divine, whatever his human line.

Read those two objects together and the reign becomes legible without a single narrative source. An enormous investment in the traditional apparatus of Egyptian kingship, an argument for legitimacy conducted through divine protection rather than descent, executed at the highest technical standard available, and cut off before completion. That is the last native pharaoh in two objects, and it is a considerably more accurate picture than the decline story.

## What a Visitor Sees Today

For readers who encounter this period in a museum or on a site rather than in a book, it is worth naming where the Thirtieth Dynasty is actually visible, because the answer is: almost everywhere in Egypt.

At Karnak, the first pylon that visitors walk through to enter the temple complex is Nectanebo I's work, along with much of the enclosure wall surrounding the precinct of Amun. Most people passing through it have no idea they are entering by a fourth-century gateway.

At Philae, the kiosk and the earliest elements of the Isis complex are his, and the site as a whole is the clearest demonstration of how Thirtieth Dynasty foundations became Ptolemaic and Roman monuments.

At Saqqara, the Serapeum galleries and the Sacred Animal Necropolis carry Nectanebo II's investment, and the sheer scale of the underground complexes is the most direct experience available of what the animal cult economy actually meant in practice.

In museum collections worldwide, Late Period hard-stone sculpture, healing statues, block statues, naophorous statues of officials, and royal images from these reigns are abundant, because the durability of the material and the volume of production have together ensured good survival.

At Behbeit el-Hagar in the Delta, the collapsed Iseum presents a harder but rewarding sight: enormous decorated hard-stone blocks lying where the building fell, giving a sense of the ambition of a project that a king with fifteen years and a Persian invasion pending chose to begin anyway.

That distribution is itself the argument this article has been making. A dynasty visible at that many sites, in that much stone, across forty years, was not a dying regime, and the physical record is available to anyone who wants to check it.

## The Problem of Counting an Ending

Historians who work on this period run into a difficulty that is worth naming, because it recurs whenever a long institution stops: deciding exactly when something ends is harder than it looks, and the choice of endpoint carries an argument inside it.

Several candidate dates exist for the end of native Egyptian rule. Circa 343 BCE, when Nectanebo II fled, is the standard answer and marks the fall of the last recognized native dynasty. The late 330s BCE, when Khababash claimed the kingship and performed royal religious functions, would extend it by a few years and would define native rule by religious recognition rather than by dynastic continuity. Circa 332 BCE, when Alexander arrived, is when Egypt passed permanently out of the Persian orbit and into a Greek one, and some accounts treat that as the meaningful terminus. And a case can be made for the death of Cleopatra VII in circa 30 BCE, on the grounds that Ptolemaic rulers were pharaohs of an independent Egypt even if they were Greek-speaking, so the end of Egyptian sovereignty rather than the end of Egyptian ethnicity in the kingship is what matters.

Each of those choices asserts something. Choosing 343 BCE asserts that ethnic and dynastic continuity is what defines native rule. Choosing 332 BCE asserts that the relevant change was the shift from Persian to Hellenistic domination. Choosing 30 BCE asserts that independence matters more than the ruler's origin. None is simply wrong, and readers who encounter different dates in different books are meeting different definitions rather than different facts.

This article uses 343 BCE because the question it is answering is about native pharaohs specifically, and because the removal of an Egyptian ruling house is a discrete, datable event with consequences that followed from it. But the qualification is real, and the honest position is to state the criterion rather than to present the date as self-evident.

There is a related question about whether the ending was recognized as such at the time, and the answer appears to be no. Egyptians in 343 BCE had lived through a Persian occupation and its reversal within living memory. The obvious expectation, from three thousand years of Egyptian history, was that foreign rule would eventually be thrown off as it always had been. Khababash's rising is exactly what that expectation looks like in action. Nobody in 343 BCE knew they were witnessing a permanent change, and the finality of the moment is entirely a judgment made afterward, by people who could see that the recovery never came.

That is worth holding onto, because it protects against the most common error in reading this period. The last native pharaohs were not living at the end of Egyptian history. They were living in what they had every reason to believe was another difficult stretch in a very long story, and they behaved accordingly, building for centuries they assumed would follow.

## The Ptolemaic Inheritance

The dynasty that followed the last native pharaohs was Greek, and it inherited more from them than is usually acknowledged.

Ptolemy and his successors took over a country whose temple institutions had just received four decades of heavy royal investment, whose religious infrastructure was in good order, and whose priestly establishment had recent experience of a monarchy that funded rather than extracted. That was a substantial asset for a foreign dynasty needing Egyptian recognition, and the Ptolemies used it. They continued the building programs, completed projects the Nectanebos had begun, had themselves depicted in the same archaizing conventions, and maintained the animal cult funding that bound ordinary Egyptians to temple institutions.

They also inherited the political lesson. Teos had demonstrated what happened to a ruler who taxed temples, and the Ptolemies, who had every incentive to extract as much revenue as possible from their new possession, were consistently careful to route substantial resources back into Egyptian religion and to appear in Egyptian temple relief performing the offerings a pharaoh performed. Whether they believed any of it is beside the point; they understood what the position required, and the last native pharaohs had established the terms.

The physical continuity is the most visible part. The Isis complex at Philae, the Iseum at Behbeit el-Hagar, and temple work at Edfu, Dendera, and elsewhere either began under the Thirtieth Dynasty or followed its architectural conventions, including the birth house form that dynasty had promoted. A visitor to a Greco-Roman period temple in Egypt is largely looking at the completion of a program the last native kings started. The full account of that dynasty and its Egypt belongs to [the guide to Ptolemaic Egypt](/2007/07/01/ptolemaic-egypt-complete-guide/).

There is one final continuity worth naming, and it is the memory itself. Egyptian priestly and literary circles under the Ptolemies kept returning to Nectanebo II, in the dream narrative that survives in fragments and in the vast Alexander Romance tradition that made him Alexander's father. A foreign dynasty ruling Egypt found it useful to have the last Egyptian king absorbed into its own origin story, and Egyptians found it consoling. That the last native pharaoh ended up as a character in the legend of his own successor's successor is a strange fate, and it is a measure of how much he still mattered centuries after he disappeared upriver.

## Studying the Last Native Pharaohs

For a reader working toward an examination answer, a lecture, or an essay, this topic rewards a compact structure rather than the full narrative.

Hold the three dynasties and their seats: Amyrtaeus at Sais as Dynasty 28, the Mendesian kings as Dynasty 29, and the Sebennytos house as Dynasty 30. Hold three dates: circa 404 BCE for the recovery of independence during a contested Persian succession, circa 373 BCE for the repulse of the major Persian invasion, and circa 343 BCE for the end under Artaxerxes III. Hold three names: Hakor for the successful defense of the Twenty-ninth Dynasty, Nectanebo I for consolidation and building, and Nectanebo II as the last native pharaoh.

Be able to state the last-flourish argument and support it with a specific example, because the ability to name the first pylon at Karnak or the Serapeum funding at Saqqara is what turns an assertion into an answer. Be able to explain the four structural reasons native rule ended, since the question is usually asked in that form and a narrative of the 343 BCE campaign alone will not answer it.

Be able to say what the Alexander Romance is and why the Nectanebo paternity story exists, because examiners like the distinction between record and legend and this is one of the cleanest cases available. And know that Khababash exists, because being able to qualify the phrase last native pharaoh accurately signals genuine reading rather than a summary.

## The Honest Verdict

Nectanebo II was not a failed king. He was a competent ruler of a functioning state that lost a contest it could not win indefinitely, and the distinction matters.

The record supports that assessment on every measurable dimension. He built more than any other Late Period ruler and at more sites. He repelled a full Persian invasion. He ran a foreign policy that kept the Achaemenid empire occupied for most of his reign. He reversed his predecessor's damaging fiscal policy and repaired the crown's relationship with the temple establishment. He was preparing a royal burial in a sarcophagus of the first quality when the campaign that ended his kingdom began.

What defeated him was an opponent who fixed the problems that had defeated earlier Persian invasions, and a defensive model whose central component was purchasable by whoever paid most. Neither of those is a personal failure, and neither indicates a civilization in decline.

The larger verdict concerns what 343 BCE means. It is the end of three thousand years of native kingship, and calling it anything less understates it. It is not the end of Egyptian civilization, which continued producing temples, texts, art, and religious scholarship for centuries under Ptolemaic and Roman rule, much of it completing what these kings had begun. And it is not the culmination of a long decline, because there was no long decline. There was a state that kept working, kept building, and kept fighting until a larger one finally got the campaign right.

That is a harder ending than the romantic one, in which an exhausted civilization lies down. It is also the one the evidence describes, and the last native pharaohs are better served by it than by the sympathy the decline story offers.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who were the last native pharaohs of Egypt?

The kings of Manetho's Twenty-eighth, Twenty-ninth, and Thirtieth Dynasties, ruling from circa 404 to 343 BCE in the gap between the two Persian occupations. The Twenty-eighth consists of one man, Amyrtaeus of Sais, who won independence during a contested Achaemenid succession. The Twenty-ninth ruled from Mendes and included Nepherites I and, most substantially, Hakor, who held Egypt against a sustained Persian effort. The Thirtieth ruled from Sebennytos and comprised Nectanebo I, his son Teos, and his grandson Nectanebo II. Nectanebo II is conventionally called the last native pharaoh, with the qualification that a rebel king named Khababash claimed and exercised kingship briefly after him during the Second Persian Period.

### Q: Who was Pharaoh Nectanebo I?

Nectanebo I, Egyptian name Nakhtnebef, ruled from circa 380 to 362 BCE and founded the Thirtieth Dynasty, taking the throne from Sebennytos after displacing the short-lived Nepherites II. His reign of roughly eighteen years was the longest and most stable of the independence period. He repelled the major Persian invasion of 373 BCE, which failed after divided command between the satrap Pharnabazus and the Athenian general Iphicrates delayed the advance until the Nile flood made the Delta impassable. He launched the era's building program, including the first pylon at Karnak and the earliest structures of the Isis complex at Philae, and issued the Naucratis decree assigning trade revenue to the temple of Neith at Sais.

### Q: Who was Nectanebo II?

Nectanebo II, Egyptian name Nakhthorheb, ruled from circa 360 to 343 BCE and was the last pharaoh of a native Egyptian dynasty. He came to power through a coup against his uncle Teos, backed by his father Tjahapimu and by Agesilaus II of Sparta, and had to defeat a rival claimant before his position was secure. He repelled a Persian invasion in circa 351 BCE, supported anti-Persian revolts in Phoenicia and Cyprus, and funded the largest temple building program of the Late Period, including major investment in the Serapeum and Sacred Animal Necropolis at Saqqara and the Iseum at Behbeit el-Hagar. Artaxerxes III defeated him in circa 343 BCE and he fled south.

### Q: When did native Egyptian rule end?

In circa 343 BCE, when Artaxerxes III defeated Nectanebo II and Egypt returned to Persian control as Manetho's Thirty-first Dynasty. That date closes roughly three thousand years of rule by Egyptian kings beginning with the unification of Upper and Lower Egypt. There is one qualification: a rebel named Khababash claimed the kingship during the Second Persian Period, probably in the late 330s BCE, and is attested performing royal religious functions, so Egyptian kingship was claimed and recognized once more before Alexander arrived. Egypt then passed to Macedonian, Ptolemaic, Roman, Byzantine, Arab, Ottoman, and British rule and was not governed by Egyptians again until the twentieth century CE.

### Q: What was the 30th Dynasty?

The Thirtieth Dynasty was the last native Egyptian ruling house, based at Sebennytos in the Delta and ruling from circa 380 to 343 BCE. It comprised three kings: Nectanebo I, who founded it and defeated the Persian invasion of 373 BCE; Teos, who ruled about two years and was overthrown after taxing temples to fund a Levantine offensive; and Nectanebo II, who ruled roughly seventeen years and lost Egypt to Artaxerxes III. Its defining characteristics were an enormous temple building program spanning the whole country from the Delta to Philae, an aggressive diplomacy aimed at keeping Persia occupied elsewhere, and a deliberate policy of investing in temple institutions rather than extracting from them.

### Q: How did Nectanebo II fight to save Egypt?

Through three connected strategies. Defensively, he maintained fortified positions across the Delta approaches, garrisoned with Greek professional troops alongside Egyptian levies, relying on terrain and the annual flood to defeat any invader who could be delayed long enough. Diplomatically, he funded and supported revolts against Persia elsewhere, sending a substantial mercenary force to Sidon during the Phoenician rising and backing Cyprus, on the principle that Persia distracted was Persia harmless. Domestically, he bound the temple establishment to his rule through unprecedented building and endowment, securing the institutional support that made a king legitimate. The combination repelled a full Persian invasion in circa 351 BCE before failing in 343 BCE.

### Q: Why did native Egyptian rule collapse?

Four structural reasons rather than any single event. Persia could lose a campaign and return a decade later while Egypt could not afford to lose once, which is arithmetic rather than a judgment on Egyptian competence. Egyptian defense depended on hired Greek heavy infantry, and a wealthier opponent could outbid Egypt in the same market, which is exactly what happened at Pelusium in 343 BCE. Any Egyptian offensive required taxing temples, which was politically fatal as Teos discovered, so Egypt could only ever defend and never seize the initiative. And frequent coups between competing Delta houses drained elite cohesion at moments when it was needed outward.

### Q: What happened to Nectanebo II?

He withdrew from Memphis in circa 343 BCE as Persian forces advanced and fled south, probably into Nubia, which lay beyond Persian reach. The contemporary record stops there. No burial is known, no death date is recorded, and the large granite sarcophagus prepared for him was never used, surviving instead as one of the more poignant objects of the period. Later Greek writers say he fled to Ethiopia, meaning Kush, but add nothing reliable. The vacuum was filled by legend: the Alexander Romance made him a magician who fled to Macedon and fathered Alexander the Great on Olympias by magical deception, a story that is demonstrably fiction.

### Q: Was Nectanebo II really the father of Alexander the Great?

No. That story belongs to the Alexander Romance, a body of legendary material that took shape in the centuries after Alexander's death and circulated in dozens of languages across the medieval world. In it, Nectanebo II foresees the Persian conquest, escapes to Macedon disguised as an astrologer, and fathers Alexander through magic. The story's function is transparent: it made the Macedonian conqueror an Egyptian royal heir, which served Ptolemaic legitimacy in Egypt, and it gave Egyptians a version of events in which their last king fathered the man who ended Persian rule. It is one of the most successful pieces of political fiction in ancient literature and it is not history.

### Q: Who was Khababash?

Khababash was an Egyptian who claimed and to some degree exercised kingship during the Second Persian Period, probably in the late 330s BCE, after Nectanebo II had fled and before Alexander arrived. He is attested chiefly by the Satrap Stela, a document erected under Ptolemy in the late fourth century BCE, which describes him granting land to the temple of Buto and inspecting the Delta marshes against a Persian naval threat, and by a small number of other objects including an Apis burial reference placing him at Memphis. His reign appears to have been short and to have ended in Persian suppression. He is not counted in Manetho's dynastic scheme, which is why the conventional last-native-pharaoh title goes to Nectanebo II.

### Q: How long was Egypt independent between the two Persian occupations?

Roughly sixty years, from circa 404 BCE to circa 343 BCE. That interval is easy to overlook in summary accounts and is longer than many well-known historical periods. Within it, Egypt repelled at least two full Persian invasions, in circa 373 BCE under Nectanebo I and circa 351 BCE under Nectanebo II, mounted one offensive into the Levant under Teos, ran a sustained alliance policy across the Aegean, and financed the largest temple construction program since the New Kingdom. Three ruling houses held the throne during those decades, with several violent transitions, but the country remained administratively unified and its frontier defense functioned effectively throughout.

### Q: What did the last native pharaohs build?

More than almost any other Late Period rulers, and across the whole country. Nectanebo I raised the first pylon at Karnak, the largest gateway at the site and still its entrance, and began the Isis complex at Philae near the First Cataract. Nectanebo II funded the Serapeum and Sacred Animal Necropolis at Saqqara heavily, advanced the hard-stone Iseum at Behbeit el-Hagar in the Delta, and left his name on gateways, chapels, birth houses, and enclosure walls at Bubastis, Hermopolis, Edfu, Tanis, Memphis, El-Kab, and elsewhere. The Ptolemies inherited and completed many of these projects, so a great deal of what visitors see at Greco-Roman period sites was begun by these kings.

### Q: Why was Teos overthrown?

Because he taxed the temples to fund an offensive war. Teos took the throne in circa 362 BCE and, judging that Persian distraction offered an opening, mounted a campaign into the Levant with Agesilaus II of Sparta and Chabrias of Athens. Financing foreign professionals on that scale required cash Egyptian revenues did not casually produce, so he levied on temple income and priestly personnel and requisitioned precious metal. Temples in Egypt were the largest landowners outside the crown and the manufacturers of royal legitimacy, so extracting from them meant attacking the institutions whose endorsement made him pharaoh. His brother Tjahapimu organized a coup at home, Agesilaus backed the young Nectanebo II, and Teos fled to Persia.

### Q: How did Artaxerxes III finally conquer Egypt?

By fixing the specific problems that had wrecked earlier Persian attempts. He came in person with a large army, which removed the divided command between satraps and Greek mercenary generals that had ruined the invasion of 373 BCE. He employed experienced Greek commanders, including Mentor of Rhodes, who had led Egyptian troops at Sidon and knew Egyptian defenses from the inside, along with Lacrates of Thebes and Nicostratus of Argos. And he committed enough money to outbid Egypt for professional troops, so the Greek garrison at Pelusium negotiated terms and departed rather than fighting. The fortified line was outflanked rather than stormed, and Nectanebo II withdrew and then fled.

### Q: Were the last native pharaohs weak rulers?

The evidence says no, and the belief that they were is a survival of an older framework that treated everything after the New Kingdom as decline. These kings repelled full Persian invasions in circa 373 and circa 351 BCE, ran a diplomacy that kept the Achaemenid empire occupied across the eastern Mediterranean for decades, and financed temple construction at more sites than most New Kingdom rulers managed. Nectanebo II was still building on an enormous scale and still funding foreign revolts in the years immediately before his defeat. What ended their rule was an opponent with overwhelming resources who eventually ran a competent campaign, not internal exhaustion.

### Q: Did the fall of Nectanebo II end ancient Egyptian civilization?

No. It ended native political rule, which is a different thing entirely. Egyptian religion, language, art, law, scribal practice, and temple institutions all continued functioning under Persian, Macedonian, Ptolemaic, and Roman administration. The temples these kings began were completed by Greek and Roman rulers who continued their architectural programs and had themselves depicted in the same Egyptian conventions. The Isis complex at Philae, begun under Nectanebo I, remained in use for the better part of a thousand years afterward and carries some of the latest hieroglyphic inscriptions known, dating to the fourth century CE, roughly seven centuries after native rule ended.

### Q: Where was the capital of Egypt under the last native dynasties?

In the Delta, and it moved with the ruling house. Amyrtaeus ruled from Sais in the western Delta, the Twenty-ninth Dynasty from Mendes in the eastern Delta, and the Thirtieth Dynasty from Sebennytos, also in the Delta. Memphis retained its importance as the practical administrative center and the site of major religious functions, including the Apis cult, and it was where Nectanebo II withdrew before fleeing. Thebes remained a great religious center receiving substantial royal building, including the first pylon at Karnak, but produced no kings. The northward orientation reflected where Egypt's threats and trading opportunities now arrived from.

### Q: What sources tell us about the last native pharaohs?

Four kinds, with very different strengths. The building record is the strongest and is physically inspectable: temples, gateways, and statuary carrying the cartouches of Nectanebo I and II across the whole country. Royal decrees supply administrative detail, above all the Naucratis stela of Nectanebo I and its twin from Thonis-Heracleion, along with the later Satrap Stela which preserves the Khababash material. Greek narrative history, chiefly Diodorus Siculus drawing on fourth-century sources, provides almost all the campaign detail and is written from a Greek perspective at several removes. Egyptian literary material such as the Demotic Chronicle gives a later temple-derived evaluation rather than contemporary reporting.
