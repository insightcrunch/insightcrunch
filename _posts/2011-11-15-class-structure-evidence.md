Ask how large the working class is within the class structure of a developed economy and the answers arrive with confidence and without agreement. One source reports a working class that forms a shrinking minority, overtaken by a broad middle. Another reports a working class that remains the large majority of everyone who works for a living. A third declines to use the phrase at all and offers instead a ladder of occupational groups, numbered from the top, in which no boundary is drawn where a Marxist would draw one. The disagreement is usually treated as a dispute about society, as though one side must have miscounted the population. The argument of this article is that the disagreement is principally about measurement, and that once the measurement decisions are made visible, most of the mystery dissolves. Class has been measured, seriously and at scale, on explicitly Marxist criteria, in comparative surveys that asked respondents about ownership, about authority over others at work, and about credentials. What those surveys found is instructive twice over. They found class structures that differed between countries at similar levels of development, which no theory of a uniform modern occupational order would predict. And they demonstrated, in their own internal variations, that the measured size of a class depends more on how a schema treats authority and credentials than on anything respondents actually reported. That second finding deserves a name, and this article gives it one: the asset-inclusion rule.

The claim at stake is testable in a way that much writing about class is not. Marxist theory does not merely assert that class matters; it specifies what class is. A class position, on the relational account developed in the [marxist theory of class guide](/2011/09/01/marxist-theory-of-class-guide/), is defined by a person's relation to the means of production, by whether they own productive assets sufficient to live without selling their capacity to work, by whether they employ others or are employed, and by the authority and autonomy their position carries inside production. If that specification means anything, it should be possible to take a population, ask the relevant questions, sort the population accordingly, and report the resulting distribution. It should also be possible to say what would count against the enterprise: a schema whose categories could be redrawn at will to absorb any result, a distribution that changed only because the coding rules changed, or a set of categories that predicted nothing about the lives of the people sorted into them. This article follows the data and evidence discipline of stating the claim in measurable form before admiring it, and it keeps the same standard when the evidence turns awkward, because the awkward parts of this record are among the most informative.

## The claim stated in a form that could be measured

A measurable version of the Marxist claim about class structure has three components, and separating them prevents a great deal of confusion later. The first component is distributional: in a capitalist economy, the population divides, on relational criteria, into a small class that owns productive assets and employs labour, a large class that owns no such assets and must sell its labour power to live, and a set of intermediate positions whose character the theory itself treats as mixed or contradictory. The second component is comparative: because ownership structures, welfare states, educational systems and labour law differ between countries, the distribution should differ between countries in ways that track those institutions rather than converging on a single modern shape. The third component is consequential: the positions so defined should be associated with differences in income, in control over working life, in attitudes towards distribution and collective action, and in exposure to insecurity, and those associations should not vanish entirely when occupation and education are taken into account, since if they did vanish the relational apparatus would be redundant decoration on an occupational classification.

Each component can fail separately, and the empirical literature is most useful when it is read as three tests rather than one verdict. The distributional claim is the easiest to examine and the hardest to interpret, because a distribution is an output of a coding scheme as much as a portrait of a society. The comparative claim is the strongest of the three when it succeeds, because institutional variation supplies something close to a natural experiment: if two economies at similar income levels, surveyed with the same instrument, show markedly different class structures, then class structure is a real variable and not a relabelling of development. The consequential claim is where rival traditions compete most directly, because occupational schemas also predict outcomes, often impressively, and a fair assessment has to ask not whether the Marxist schema predicts anything but whether it predicts anything its rivals miss, and whether it illuminates the questions, above all exploitation and authority, that its rivals were never designed to address.

It is worth stating plainly what measurement cannot do for this theory. Counting class positions does not by itself demonstrate exploitation, because exploitation is a claim about the transfer of surplus labour inside production, not a claim about how many people occupy each box. Counting does not demonstrate class consciousness, because consciousness concerns what people in a position come to believe and do together, which is a further process with its own conditions. And counting does not refute the theory if the count comes out differently from a textbook expectation, because the theory predicts intermediate positions and historical variation rather than a pristine two-class census. What measurement can do is discipline the vocabulary. After the surveys examined here, no one is entitled to speak of the size of the working class without stating a criterion, and no one is entitled to quote an official occupational distribution as though it were a finding about ownership. That discipline, modest as it sounds, is the article's central deliverable, and it is the same discipline that the parallel problem in [measuring rate of exploitation](/2011/03/01/measuring-rate-of-exploitation/) imposed on a different magnitude, where a classification decision about productive and unproductive labour moved the estimate more than the data did.

## Two ways of sorting a population

Every class schema is an answer to a prior question: what about a person's situation is supposed to matter? The gradational answer, which dominates official statistics and much sociology, says that what matters is rank. People are sorted by the standing, income, skill or prestige attached to their occupation, and classes appear as bands on a scale, higher professional above lower professional, skilled manual above unskilled. The picture is a ladder, and the interesting questions concern movement up and down it, the distance between rungs, and the chances of children starting on a different rung from their parents. The relational answer, which is the Marxist one, says that what matters is not rank but relation. People are sorted by their connection to productive assets and to other people inside production: whether they own, whether they employ, whether they command the work of others, whether they must accept direction in order to earn. The picture is not a ladder but a structure of positions defined against each other, in which one position, the employer of labour, exists only because another position, the seller of labour power, exists to meet it.

The distinction sounds abstract and becomes concrete the moment a difficult case arrives. Consider a senior hospital consultant and a self-employed plumber. On a gradational schema the consultant sits far above the plumber, and the distance between them summarises differences in education, income, prestige and security that are perfectly real. On a relational schema the ordering scrambles. The plumber may own tools, a van, premises and goodwill, may employ an apprentice, and may be unable to be dismissed by anyone; the consultant, for all her salary and status, owns no part of the hospital, employs no one in her own right, and sells her labour power to an employer who can restructure her working life. A Marxist schema does not deny the consultant's advantages; it refuses to let those advantages settle the class question, because its question is about the structure of production rather than the distribution of rewards. Whether that refusal is illuminating or perverse is an empirical and theoretical argument, and it is precisely the argument the survey evidence allows us to have with data in hand rather than by intuition.

A second difficult case exposes the other flank. Consider a call centre team leader who hires no one, owns nothing, earns modestly, yet spends the day allocating tasks, monitoring calls and initiating discipline for a dozen agents. Gradational schemas place her close to the agents she supervises, because occupation, pay and qualifications are similar. A relational schema that takes authority seriously must separate her from them, at least partially, because she exercises a function of capital inside the labour process, however junior her version of it. Erik Olin Wright built his entire measurement programme around such cases, naming them contradictory class locations, positions that share characteristics of two classes at once. The existence of a large zone of such positions is not an embarrassment the programme discovered by accident; it is one of its substantive findings, and the size assigned to that zone turns out to be one of the main levers moving every published total.

### Can an occupational classification measure ownership at all?

Strictly, no. An occupational classification records the kind of work a person does and infers standing from the typical rewards and qualifications of that work. Ownership enters, if at all, only through the separate question of employment status. Two people with identical occupational codes may stand on opposite sides of the property relation, and the classification cannot see the difference.

That answer needs immediate qualification, because occupational classifications are not useless for class analysis; they are useful for different questions. A well-built occupational schema tracks the structure of skill, the division between manual and non-manual work, the growth of professional employment, and the intergenerational transmission of advantage with great precision. Generations of mobility research rest on it, and its predictions about life chances are among the most replicated findings in sociology. The error is not using such a schema; the error is the silent inference from its results to conclusions about ownership, exploitation or the disappearance of a working class defined relationally. When a commentator announces that the working class has shrunk to a minority because routine and manual occupations have declined as a share of employment, a gradational fact has been dressed as a relational one. Routine non-manual work, the clerical, retail and care jobs that replaced much manual employment, is performed overwhelmingly by employees who own no productive assets, and on relational criteria its growth enlarges rather than shrinks the class that must sell its labour power. The entire debate canvassed in [does the working class still exist](/2011/11/01/does-the-working-class-still-exist/) turns on keeping those two sentences apart.

## The official schemas and what they were built to do

Government classifications deserve to be described on their own terms before they are criticised for failing a test they never sat. The British National Statistics Socio-economic Classification, universally shortened to NS-SEC, is the standard example and the most carefully theorised of the family. It was constructed for official use at the turn of the century, replacing older Registrar General's classes and the earlier Hope-Goldthorpe scale, and it sorts the population into categories built from occupation together with employment status and the size of the employing organisation. Its intellectual foundation is the class schema associated with John Goldthorpe and his collaborators, itself a development of a Weberian rather than Marxist tradition, in which the key distinctions concern employment relations: whether a person works under a service relationship, with salary, security, autonomy and a career ladder, or under a labour contract, with wages, close supervision and payment for discrete effort, or in the intermediate forms between. The schema's categories, from higher managerial and professional occupations through intermediate and small employer groupings to lower supervisory, semi-routine and routine occupations, are therefore relational in a limited sense, but the relation they encode is the form of the employment contract, not ownership of productive assets.

The comparative European descendant of the same tradition, the European Socio-economic Classification, was designed to harmonise this approach across national statistical systems, so that a French, a German and a British distribution could be placed side by side without each country's categories defeating comparison. In the United States the official apparatus looks different again. American statistics lean on the Standard Occupational Classification for the structure of jobs and on income, education and wealth distributions for stratification, and the Census Bureau does not publish a class schema in the European sense at all, a fact that itself expresses a national statistical culture more comfortable with continuous distributions than with named classes. Labour force surveys in most countries, coordinated in Europe and paralleled elsewhere, collect the raw materials from which many schemas can be built: occupation, industry, employment status, supervisory responsibility in some form, size of workplace, hours, tenure and earnings. That common raw material is important for what follows, because it means the contrast between official and Marxist measurement is rarely a contrast between datasets. It is a contrast between coding decisions applied to substantially similar information.

Three features of the official family matter for the argument. First, its classes are designed for stability and administrative usability: categories must be assignable from survey responses by coders, must behave sensibly in small samples, and must serve many government purposes at once, from health inequalities monitoring to educational planning. Second, its validation standard is predictive and descriptive performance on outcomes such as mortality, morbidity, educational attainment and income, and by that standard it performs well, which is a genuine achievement that any rival must acknowledge rather than wave away. Third, and decisively, ownership is not a dimension of the schema except in the residual visibility of employers and the self-employed, who appear because employment status is collected, not because the schema theorises them. A large employer and a one-person business with no employees are near neighbours in some versions of the classification, while a finance director employed by a corporation sits in the top occupational class without owning the enterprise she directs. For the purposes the schema serves, none of this is a defect. For a reader who wants to know the distribution of the population across positions in the relations of production, it means the official table is answering a neighbouring question, and its authority, which is considerable, does not transfer.

The Goldthorpe tradition should also be given its due as an intellectual rival rather than a bureaucratic convenience, a point developed in the comparison of [marx vs weber on class](/2011/12/15/marx-vs-weber-on-class/), where the Weberian emphasis on market situation and life chances is set against the Marxist emphasis on production relations. Goldthorpe's employment-relations schema was built to explain mobility and class formation, and its architects were explicit that classes are aggregates of positions within labour markets and production units, defined by the terms on which work is exchanged. That is a serious theory of class, with serious evidence behind it. The debate between the traditions is therefore not a debate between theory and mere counting. It is a debate between two theories that sort the same population differently because they think different features of a position do the causal work. Survey evidence cannot crown one theory by itself, but it can show where each theory's sorting earns its keep, and it can expose when a finding attributed to social change is actually an artefact of switching from one sorting to the other.

## Wright's programme: class asked as a set of questions

The Marxist alternative to the official schemas is not a speculation. It is a research programme with instruments, datasets and a publication record stretching over decades, and its central figure is the American sociologist Erik Olin Wright. Beginning in the late 1970s, Wright set out to do for Marxist class analysis what the mobility tradition had done for occupational class: to define categories precisely enough that a survey could assign real people to them, to field that survey in more than one country with a common instrument, and to test whether the resulting map explained anything worth explaining. The full intellectual biography of the schema, its revisions and its place in analytical Marxism, belongs to the dedicated study of [erik olin wright class analysis](/2020/02/15/erik-olin-wright-class-analysis/); what matters here is the measurement design and what it produced.

The design begins from the classical criterion and immediately complicates it. Ownership of the means of production remains the primary axis: a respondent who owns a business, employs others and can live from the proceeds occupies a capitalist position; a respondent who owns nothing productive and works for a wage or salary occupies a working class position; a respondent who owns a small business and works in it without employing others, or with only marginal help, occupies the petty bourgeois position that the classical theory already recognised. So far the schema could be built from employment status alone. Wright's decisive move was to argue that in advanced capitalist economies two further assets distribute class-relevant advantage inside the population of employees, and that a schema ignoring them would misclassify the most contested part of the structure. The first is authority: the capacity, attached to a job, to direct the work of others, to hire and dismiss, to set budgets and to sanction subordinates. The second is credentials and scarce skills: qualifications whose holders can extract a premium because their labour power cannot be quickly replaced or cheaply trained. Employees possessing substantial authority became, in Wright's vocabulary, contradictory locations between the bourgeoisie and the proletariat, since they remain propertyless sellers of labour power while exercising functions of domination that belong to capital. Employees possessing scarce credentials became contradictory locations between the petty bourgeoisie and the proletariat, since their asset is not a business but a certified capacity that behaves, in the labour market, a little like property.

The resulting maps, in their best known versions, sort employees along these dimensions into a grid of locations, commonly summarised as a twelve-location scheme in the mature comparative work: capitalists subdivided by scale, small employers, the petty bourgeoisie, and then expert managers, non-managerial experts, skilled managers, unskilled managers, expert non-managers, and the strata of skilled and unskilled workers with and without authority, the exact labels varying between versions as Wright revised the instrument. The revisions matter methodologically. Across the programme's life the schema changed: an earlier formulation organised around domination and a more orthodox exploitation-centred reformulation later, in which the asset language was made explicit and class locations were defined by exploitation relations grounded in unequal distribution of productive assets of several kinds, labour power itself, means of production, organisational assets and skill assets. A hostile reading treats each revision as an admission that the previous map was wrong. A fairer reading, which the evidence section of this article will return to, treats the revisions as the normal history of a measurement instrument being sharpened against its own results, provided the changes are published, motivated and applied consistently thereafter, which in this programme they were.

Fielding the instrument required survey questions that ordinary omnibus surveys did not ask. Respondents had to report not only their occupation and employment status but whether they supervised others, whether that supervision included real decision power over pay, promotion and discipline or merely the passing on of instructions, whether they participated in organisational decisions about budgets and products, whether they were formally credentialed, and how autonomous their own work was from direction by others. The comparative class analysis project fielded such modules in the United States first and then, with national collaborators, in a set of other advanced economies including Canada, Britain, Sweden, Norway, Denmark, Finland, Japan, Australia and New Zealand, with later extensions and related national studies widening the net. The point of the comparative design was not tourism. It was to discover whether class structures, measured identically, were identical, and the answer was that they were not. Sweden's structure, with its compressed wage differentials, strong unions and large public sector, did not match the American structure; Japan's did not match either. Class structure emerged from the project as a variable with institutional causes, which is exactly what a relational theory should want and exactly what a purely developmental account of occupational change would not predict.

### What did cross-national comparison add that a single country study could not?

It separated the schema from the society. A national study can always be accused of building local peculiarities into its categories. When one instrument, applied across economies at similar income levels, yields different distributions and different patterns of attitudes, the differences belong to the countries and their institutions, not to the questionnaire, and class structure becomes a comparative fact.

## The asset-inclusion rule

We can now state the article's namable claim with the precision it needs. The asset-inclusion rule holds that the measured size of the working class is driven by whether workplace authority and credentials are counted as class-relevant assets, and that any published figure that does not state its treatment of those two dimensions is uninterpretable. The rule is not a complaint about other people's arithmetic. It is a claim about where the action is in every class count, demonstrable by holding a single population fixed and varying only the schema applied to it. Count as working class everyone who owns no productive property and must sell labour power, and the class is a large majority of the employed population in every advanced economy the comparative project examined. Then treat substantial managerial authority as an asset that lifts its holder out of the working class into a contradictory or middle location, and the working class shrinks by the entire managerial stratum, which in a modern economy is a substantial slice of employment. Then treat scarce credentials the same way, and it shrinks again by the professional and expert strata. Nothing in the population changed between the three counts. No one gained or lost a job, a qualification or a subordinate. The totals moved because the definition of an asset moved, and the movement is large, larger than most of the historical change that class commentary usually debates.

The rule cuts in both directions, which is what makes it a rule rather than a slogan. A Marxist who quotes the largest available figure for the working class owes the reader the information that the figure counts senior managers and highly credentialed professionals as working class because they are propertyless employees. A critic who quotes the smallest figure owes the reader the information that the figure was produced by treating authority and credentials as class assets, a theoretical decision that requires defence and is not a datum. And an official statistician publishing an occupational distribution owes the reader, if class language is used at all, the information that the schema contains no ownership dimension, so that its middle and working categories are gradational bands and not positions in production relations. In each case the number is honest as an output and misleading as a headline, and the difference between the two is disclosure. Researchers who work with these schemas know this and routinely report sensitivity: the same dataset coded under variant assumptions, the totals compared, the conclusions that survive the variation separated from those that do not. The public discussion of class almost never inherits that discipline, and rebuilding it is a large part of what an evidence article can contribute.

There is a close parallel, worth pausing on, with the classification problem in exploitation measurement. There, the decision about which labour counts as productive determined the level of the estimated rate of exploitation more powerfully than the underlying national accounts did, and the mature literature responded by reporting ranges, naming conventions and treating the classification as part of the result. Class measurement has reached the same maturity inside the specialist literature and the same immaturity outside it. The asset-inclusion rule is the class case of a general law of this series: when a Marxist magnitude is measured, the methodological decision is not a preliminary to the finding. In large part it is the finding, and suppressing it converts an argument into a number with a false air of having been simply observed in nature.

### How big is the working class?

It depends on the criterion applied. Counting everyone who must sell labour power and owns no productive property gives a large majority in most developed economies. Treating substantial workplace authority or scarce credentials as assets that remove their holders reduces the figure considerably. No single published figure is authoritative on its own.

Two clarifications keep that answer from being misread as evasion. The first is that dependence on criteria is not dependence on whim. The criteria in dispute correspond to genuine theoretical disagreements about what does the causal work in class relations, disagreements that can be argued, tested for their consequences and revised, as Wright's programme argued and revised them in public over decades. The second is that the range produced by defensible criteria is not infinitely wide. No defensible relational schema makes employers of labour a majority, none makes propertyless employees a small minority, and none dissolves the intermediate zone whose size is the real variable. A reader who absorbs the asset-inclusion rule gains something better than a number: a way to interrogate any number, by asking which assets it counted, and a correct expectation that the answer to that question will explain most of the distance between competing headlines.

Reading the table vertically teaches the article in miniature. The first column shows that every schema is a theory wearing a coding frame. The second shows that the number of classes is a presentational choice as much as a discovery, since each family can be collapsed or expanded. The third shows that the relational and gradational logics cross-cut the political labels readers attach to them, since the Goldthorpe tradition is relational about contracts while remaining silent on ownership. The fourth is the discipline: no instrument is indicted for failing at a task outside its design, and no instrument is licensed to pronounce outside it either. A health researcher using NS-SEC to model mortality gradients is doing sound work. A polemicist using the same table to declare the working class abolished is committing a category error, and the table exists so that a reader can say exactly which error, in one sentence, with the criterion named.

## What the comparative surveys found

Findings should be reported at the strength the designs support, which in this literature means designs, directions and ranges rather than decorative precision. Four findings recur across the comparative programme and its surrounding literature, and each survives retelling without invented percentages. The first is sheer scale: on relational criteria, the working class, defined by propertyless employment without substantial authority or scarce credentials, together with the contradictory locations adjacent to it, accounts for the overwhelming bulk of employment in every country studied, while unambiguous capitalists, owners employing labour on a scale that frees them from working themselves, form a small single-figure stratum wherever the question is asked in this form. That pattern is sometimes reported as though it were a discovery about the persistence of an old world. It is better understood as the arithmetic of the criterion: in economies where most people work for organisations they do not own, a relational census will return a propertyless majority under any variant of the schema, and the interesting variation lies in the composition of the zones around that majority.

The second finding is national difference. Class structures measured with one instrument differed between the countries of the project in ways that patterned with institutions: the size of the managerial and expert contradictory zones, the weight of the petty bourgeoisie, the share of employment in public services and the distribution of authority inside workplaces all varied, and the variation was not a simple function of wealth, since rich countries differed from each other as much as any of them differed from less rich ones. Wright and his collaborators also found that the relationship between class location and class attitudes, measured through questions about distribution, unions, and the legitimacy of inequality, varied in strength between countries, being notably weaker in the United States than in Sweden in the classic comparisons. That second-order finding matters for theory, because it suggests that the translation of position into consciousness is institutionally mediated, a conclusion that supports rather than embarrasses a sophisticated historical materialism, in which identical positions inside different political and organisational environments are not expected to generate identical beliefs.

The third finding concerns the contradictory locations themselves. They were not a residual smear between two pure classes; they were large, they were internally structured, and their occupants' incomes, attitudes and behaviour tended to fall between the classes they contradicted, or to lean towards one pole on some measures and the other pole on others, in ways the asset logic could often anticipate. Managers with genuine organisational power resembled capitalists in attitudes more than managers who merely relayed instructions resembled anyone in particular. Credentialed experts without authority occupied a distinct position whose market advantages were real but whose lack of command over others separated them from management. These are the kinds of textured results that justify a complicated schema: the complications predicted distinguishable patterns, and the patterns appeared. Where they did not appear, in particular where some fine-grained locations behaved indistinguishably from their neighbours, the programme's own analysts collapsed categories and said so, which is the correct scientific response and, again, a matter of published record.

The fourth finding is about change over time, and it must be handled with the most care because the comparative project's repeated cross-sections were sparser than its initial cross-national sweep, and because national follow-up studies used instruments that evolved. The broad direction reported across the literature is not the disappearance of the working class but its recomposition: manual industrial employment contracted, routine and skilled service employment by propertyless employees expanded, the credentialed expert zone grew with mass higher education, and the managerial zone grew with the organisational complexity of firms and states. On gradational schemas these shifts register as embourgeoisement, a swelling middle. On relational schemas the same shifts register as a change in the working class's occupational clothing together with an expansion of the contradictory zones. Both descriptions use real data. They differ because the schemas ask different questions, and the asset-inclusion rule tells us to expect exactly this divergence and to refuse the journalistic shortcut in which one description is reported as having refuted the other. Readers assembling notes across this cluster will find that keeping the two descriptions in separate columns, a habit easily maintained when notes are organised by criterion rather than by headline in a tool such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html), prevents the commonest error in essays on this subject, which is quoting a gradational trend against a relational claim as though they occupied the same axis.

## The employment status proxy: the most useful crude instrument in social statistics

Buried in the argument so far is the practical point the brief for this article singled out, and it deserves a section of its own because it converts the whole discussion from commentary into method. Virtually every labour force survey in the world asks respondents whether they work as an employee, are self-employed, run a business with employees, or work unpaid in a family enterprise. That distinction, collected for administrative reasons and published in headline tables by national statistical offices and international agencies, is a relational distinction. It sorts the population by their position with respect to ownership and the employment of others, however crudely, and it is almost never used as a class measure by the commentators who quote the same surveys' occupational tables. A reader with access to published tables, let alone microdata, can therefore produce a defensible first approximation of a relational class structure for a great many countries and years from material that is already public, free and methodologically documented.

The approximation works as follows in prose. Employers, those who own a business and employ others, form the closest available proxy for the capitalist class, with the caveat that the category reaches down to very small businesses whose owners work alongside one or two employees and live more like the petty bourgeoisie of classical theory. Own-account workers, the self-employed without employees, proxy the petty bourgeois position, with the caveat, examined in the next section, that the category now contains both genuine small proprietors and dependent contractors whose independence is legal rather than real. Employees proxy the great propertyless zone, inside which the proxy can say nothing further: the chief executive and the cleaner are both employees, and separating them requires exactly the authority and credential information that Wright's instruments were built to collect. Unpaid family workers, a small category in advanced economies and a large one in agrarian and informal economies, mark a position the classical schema handles awkwardly and the proxy at least renders visible. Even in this crude form the exercise has bite. It shows at a glance that in advanced economies employees constitute the overwhelming majority of the employed, employers a small minority, and the self-employed a fluctuating band between, and it does so on relational grounds that a Marxist, a Weberian and a statistician can all inspect, because the categories are defined by the survey rather than by the analyst's theory.

The proxy also disciplines historical and comparative claims. Because employment status has been collected for decades in many countries, a researcher can trace the broad relational skeleton over periods for which no Wright-style instrument exists, and can compare countries whose bespoke class studies are incommensurable. The limits must be stated with the results every time: the proxy cannot measure exploitation, cannot see inside the employee category where most modern class argument lives, and inherits the survey's own boundary decisions about informal work, agriculture and the public sector. Used within those limits it is the single most actionable instrument in this article, and the habit of reaching for it before reaching for an occupational ladder would improve most public discussion of class structure overnight. Guidance on locating the underlying series and their documentation is gathered in the companion study of [researching marxism sources](/2021/10/01/researching-marxism-sources/), which treats statistical sources with the same suspicion of unexamined categories that this article applies to schemas.

## The self-employed: one category, several classes

No category in class measurement conceals more heterogeneity than self-employment, and no category better illustrates why schema decisions are findings. At one pole stand proprietors whose businesses own significant productive assets, employ others or plainly could, and whose incomes derive substantially from the assets and the labour of employees; at the other pole stand workers who are self-employed only in the sense that a contract says so, who work for one client under that client's direction, supply little beyond their labour, and bear risks that an employer has externalised onto them. Between the poles lie tradespeople with tools and goodwill, professionals with practices, shopkeepers, farmers, artists, consultants between posts, and the vast informal proprietors of economies where wage employment is not the norm. A schema that counts all of them as one petty bourgeois class is making a strong claim disguised as a coding convenience. A schema that reassigns the dependent contractors to the working class is making the opposite claim, equally in need of statement.

The empirical literature handles the problem in three broad ways. The first is thresholding: using number of employees, turnover, or incorporation status to split owners who employ on a meaningful scale from own-account workers, a split the employment status proxy already gestures towards. The second is dependence testing: asking or inferring whether a nominally independent worker has one dominant client, works set hours under direction, and cannot substitute another person for themselves, the tests labour law itself uses when it polices disguised employment. The third is trajectory analysis: examining whether self-employment in a given sector functions as a destination with asset accumulation or as a waiting room and fallback for wage workers between jobs, since the same legal status means different things in the two cases. Each approach moves measured totals, and the movement is concentrated exactly where contemporary argument is hottest, in platform and gig work, in construction, in care and in creative industries. The honest practice, again, is disclosure: a published figure for the petty bourgeoisie or the self-employed middle should state which of these treatments it embodies, and a reader meeting a figure that does not state it should apply the asset-inclusion rule's logic and treat the figure as uninterpretable until the treatment is known.

There is also a historical warning embedded here. Self-employment's share of employment fell across the advanced economies for much of the twentieth century as agriculture and small retail contracted, and that fall was widely read, by Marxists and their critics alike, as the classical petty bourgeoisie dissolving into the two great classes, much as the nineteenth-century theory expected. Its stabilisation and partial revival in new forms since then has been read, with equal haste, as the theory's refutation. The measurement record supports neither haste. What dissolved was a specific agrarian and small-commodity stratum; what revived is a legally defined status spanning genuinely different relations, some of them new wine in the old category and some of them wage relations with the label changed. Classifying by legal form in a period when legal forms are actively used to reorganise relations is the surest way to measure the form and miss the relation, and the better surveys and studies increasingly supplement status questions with the dependence and control questions that detect the difference.

## Managers: the stratum that moves every total

If the asset-inclusion rule has a single largest application, it is the treatment of managers. Managerial and supervisory employees are numerous enough in modern economies that assigning them to the working class, to a middle class, or to a set of contradictory locations swings the headline distribution more than any other single decision, and the theoretical stakes match the arithmetic. In Marx's own writing the labour of supervision has a double character: it is necessary coordination, required by any combined labour process, and it is also a function of capital, the work of extracting effort and surplus from workers on the owner's behalf, the two aspects fused in a single job and separable only analytically. Wright's authority asset is the operational descendant of that insight. A schema that counts authority must then decide its degrees: the executive who controls budgets, strategy and hiring across an organisation; the middle manager who allocates work and appraises staff within parameters set elsewhere; the supervisor who monitors a line or a shift and can recommend but not decide discipline; the senior worker whose seniority gives informal influence without formal power. Each threshold, drawn at a different height, produces a different class map from identical responses.

The survey solutions are instructive about the difficulty. Wright's instruments distinguished decision-making authority over organisational policy, direct authority over subordinates' work and sanctions, and mere supervisory responsibility, precisely because early analyses showed that counting everyone with a subordinate as a manager inflated the contradictory zone with people whose experience of work, income and attitudes resembled those they supervised. Later work in the tradition, and critical work outside it, pressed further questions: whether authority should be measured by job description or by exercised discretion, whether professional autonomy is a form of the same asset or a different one, and whether authority in public sector organisations, where the surplus logic is absent even though hierarchy is present, should count identically to authority in a firm. None of these questions has a purely empirical answer, because each encodes a claim about what authority is for in the theory. But their consequences are empirical and reportable: a study can show its totals under a broad and a narrow definition of managerial authority, and the better studies do.

For the general reader the lesson is portable. When a class figure is quoted, the question what did they do with the managers will usually explain more of the figure than any enquiry into sampling or response rates. A schema in which all employees with supervisory duties join a middle class will report a modest working class and a dominant middle. A schema in which only ownership removes a person from the working class will report the opposite shape from the same population. Neither is a lie; each is a theory with a threshold. The threshold is the argument, and an evidence culture worthy of the name would print it next to the number.

## Households, partners, retirement and the edges of the employed population

Class schemas built on individual jobs meet a stubborn fact: people live in households, pool incomes, and spend large parts of their lives outside employment. The decisions taken about these facts move totals as surely as the asset decisions do, and they expose theoretical commitments of their own. The oldest convention in mobility research assigned a household the class of its male head, a practice whose assumptions about whose position defines a family's situation were dismantled by feminist critics over several decades, in one of the most consequential methodological arguments in the field. The alternatives each carry implications. Classifying individuals by their own current job leaves non-employed adults unclassified or classified by a partner or a previous job. Classifying households by the dominant position among their members reintroduces a hierarchy of significance between partners' jobs. Classifying individuals by their own job where they have one and treating non-employment as a set of positions in its own right, student, retired, caring, unemployed, disabled, multiplies categories but respects the reality that a retired machinist, a student from a professional family and a long-term unemployed carer do not stand in the same relation to production merely because none of them holds a job this week.

Wright's programme confronted the issue by distinguishing direct class locations, held through one's own job, from mediated locations, held through household relationships, and by analysing both rather than pretending the second did not exist. The distinction proved fertile: it allowed the programme to ask how a person's attitudes and interests are shaped jointly by their own position and by the positions they are attached to, and it found, as one would hope from a theory that takes material life seriously, that both matter, with direct location generally the stronger predictor and mediated location adding measurably to the account. Official schemas have their own household conventions, typically analysing individuals of working age with their own occupations and treating the retired through last occupation, practices that are serviceable for health and mobility research but that quietly define away the question of what class a person belongs to when their relation to production runs through a pension, a benefit, a partner or an inheritance.

The edges matter for headline figures in a specific, checkable way. A distribution computed over employed adults only, a distribution over all adults using last or partner's occupation, and a distribution over households will give three different sizes for every class, and public argument slides between these bases without signalling the change of denominator. The retired population alone, large and growing in rich countries, can shift a working class share by a wide margin depending on whether pensioners are counted by former occupation, excluded, or treated as a class location defined by dependence on transfer income. None of the choices is absurd; all of them belong in the methods note. Their collective effect reinforces the article's refrain. Before disputing a class total, establish its universe: individuals or households, employed or all adults, own job or household position. A disagreement that survives the alignment of universes is a real disagreement. Most do not survive it.

## The Great British Class Survey and the Bourdieusian turn

Any account of class measurement that stopped with Wright and Goldthorpe would miss the most publicly successful class study of recent decades and the misunderstandings it generated. The Great British Class Survey, conducted in the early 2010s by researchers associated with the BBC's Lab UK platform and led sociologically by Mike Savage and colleagues, approached class through the French theorist Pierre Bourdieu rather than through Marx or Weber. Its premise was that class advantage travels in several currencies at once: economic capital in income, savings and property; cultural capital in education, tastes and cultural participation; and social capital in the range and standing of a person's contacts. A large web survey measured these resources, a latent class analysis grouped respondents by their profiles of capitals, and the study announced seven classes, from an elite at the top through established and technical middle classes, new affluent workers and traditional working class, to an emergent service sector group and a precariat at the bottom, characterised by insecurity across every currency.

The study's influence on public language was real: precariat entered ordinary commentary, and the image of class as a bundle of resources rather than a rung or a relation entered journalism. Its methodological standing requires the criticism the brief for this article insists be given plainly. The main survey was self-selected, recruited through a broadcaster's website and publicity, and its respondents were dramatically unrepresentative of the British population, heavily skewed towards the educated and culturally engaged; the research team, to its credit, published a smaller representative comparison survey alongside it and discussed the distortion openly, but the seven classes and their estimated population shares were derived substantially from the unrepresentative instrument, and the shares cannot be treated as population estimates in the way headline coverage treated them. A schema whose sampling cannot support its percentages is in a different evidential position from Wright's probability samples or the official classifications built on census and labour force data, and conflating them in a league table of rival class counts is an error this article explicitly refuses.

The deeper point is that the Bourdieusian instrument measures a different object. Clusters of capitals describe the distribution of resources and tastes, and they illuminate cultural distinction, the way advantage signals and reproduces itself, with genuine power. They do not describe positions in production relations, and their working class categories are defined by low volumes of capital rather than by the sale of labour power, which is why the study's traditional working class appears small and ageing while its emergent service workers, propertyless employees in insecure service jobs, appear as a separate class beneath it. A relational reading reunites what the capital profile divides; a Bourdieusian reading replies that the division captures real differences in security, culture and connection that the relational category flattens. Both replies can be true because the instruments answer different questions, and the episode as a whole is the asset-inclusion rule's cultural cousin: the totals were driven by the decision to define class by accumulated resources, a decision invisible in most of the coverage the study received.

## Wealth, ownership and the capitalist class

At the top of the structure the measurement problem reverses. For the working class the difficulty is an excess of plausible boundaries; for a capitalist class the difficulty is that the most available evidence, wealth statistics, measures something adjacent to the theoretical object. Tax records, rich lists, household wealth surveys and the distributional research built on them establish beyond serious dispute that ownership of financial and business assets is extremely concentrated, with small fractions of populations holding large shares of privately held wealth, and with business equity among the most concentrated categories of all. What such figures demonstrate directly is concentration of assets. The Marxist claim about a capitalist class is stronger and more specific: that there exists a class whose members control means of production, appropriate surplus produced by others, and reproduce that control across time and generations. Concentration is evidence towards that claim, especially where the assets are controlling stakes in productive enterprises, but the inference needs the middle steps supplied, because a distribution of wealth also contains housing, pension entitlements and small savings whose concentration profile differs from that of productive control, and because a wealthy individual may be a rentier, an executive paid in equity, an heir, or an operating owner, positions a relational analysis distinguishes.

The better literature supplies the middle steps from several directions. Studies of corporate control map interlocking directorships and block shareholdings to show how a relatively small network of firms and owners sits at the centre of an economy's productive assets. Occupational and employment data identify the employer stratum directly, small in number even before any wealth threshold is applied. Inheritance and wealth transmission research addresses reproduction, the feature that turns rich people into a class in the strong sense. And Wright's schemas identify capitalists relationally at the survey level, owners employing labour, a category whose small size in every country studied is itself a finding, since it refutes the lazy symmetry in which classes are imagined as comparable blocs. Assembled, the evidence supports the existence of a small, asset-controlling class far more strongly than any single wealth statistic does, while also showing why the slogan that a fixed tiny percentage owns a fixed vast percentage, quoted without asset definitions, is not the strong version of the argument. The strong version names the assets, distinguishes control from mere value, and shows the mechanisms of reproduction; it is slower to say and harder to headline, and it is the version that survives scrutiny.

A corresponding caution applies at the boundary between executives and owners. Senior managers of large corporations often hold substantial equity and receive incomes that place them among the wealthy, while owning no controlling stake and remaining dismissible by boards and shareholders. Relational schemas divide on their treatment: by employment status they are employees, by wealth they resemble owners, by function they direct the labour of thousands and appropriate salaries partly resembling a share of surplus. Wright's framework places them among contradictory locations near the capitalist pole when their organisational assets and authority are extensive, a judgement that fits the theory's functional insight while conceding that the legal form says employee. The wealth data alone cannot arbitrate this, because the question is not how much they have but in what capacity they hold and exercise it. Once again the schema decision, stated or unstated, is doing the work that a headline attributes to the data.

## Can labour force surveys carry a Marxist analysis?

The raw material question deserves a direct answer, because scepticism about data is sometimes used to excuse never attempting the measurement at all. Labour force surveys, the rotating household surveys through which most countries monitor employment, collect occupation, industry, employment status, hours, earnings in some designs, tenure, workplace size, and in a number of national versions, supervisory status and educational credentials. That is enough to build the employment status proxy exactly and a simplified Wright-style schema approximately, provided the analyst accepts that authority will be measured by supervisory proxies of varying quality and that credential assets will be proxied by qualifications, which capture scarcity only roughly. It is also enough to build the official occupational schemas in their published forms, which is how statistical offices themselves proceed. Census data add small-area and long-run dimensions at lower frequency and with fewer labour process variables. Dedicated academic surveys, general social surveys and election studies periodically field richer modules, and panel studies that follow the same individuals over years allow the analyst to watch locations change, which converts class structure from a snapshot into a process and exposes how much apparent structural change is individuals moving between positions as they age, qualify, are promoted, lose jobs or start businesses.

The practical obstacles are real and should be catalogued rather than dramatised. Occupation coding changes between classification revisions, and long series break at each revision unless bridge coding is done. Supervisory questions differ in wording between countries and waves in ways that move the measured managerial share, a fact cross-national analysts ignore at their peril. Self-employment income is notoriously misreported, credentials are recorded as qualifications held rather than scarcity in the market, and workplace authority inside the public sector maps imperfectly onto instruments designed with firms in mind. Sample sizes that are generous for national totals become thin once a twelve-location schema is crossed with region, ethnicity or gender, so that the most interesting sub-group claims carry wide uncertainty that honest reporting must show. None of this is unique to Marxist measurement; identical obstacles confront the official schemas and the mobility tradition, which have developed conventions for handling them. The difference is that a Marxist analysis cannot borrow the conventions silently, because several of them, household headship conventions and occupational prestige orderings among them, encode the rival theory. Building the analysis from microdata with stated decisions is laborious, and it is also the entire point: the decisions are the analysis, made auditable.

### Where should a first-time researcher begin with public microdata?

Begin with employment status. Published labour force tables already separate employers, the self-employed and employees for many countries and years, and that three-way split is a relational skeleton requiring no special access. Add supervisory status and qualifications only after the skeleton is stable, document every coding choice, and report how the totals move when a choice changes.

That sequence, simple as it sounds, inverts the habit most commentary starts from, which is to open an occupational table and begin interpreting. Beginning relationally and adding gradational detail afterwards keeps the theoretical question in view and makes each added refinement visible as a decision. It also produces work that other researchers can replicate and attack productively, because the coding path from raw responses to class totals is the part of class analysis where genuine cumulative progress occurs. Wright's programme left codebooks and instruments that successors could criticise item by item; official schemas publish derivation algorithms for the same reason. A class analysis whose derivation cannot be inspected is, by the standards of this field, not yet a result at all, whatever its totals claim.

## Performance: what each schema predicts, and what prediction proves

The most serious empirical conversation between the traditions concerns performance. If classes are real features of the social structure rather than filing systems, membership should be associated with the things the theories say flow from class: income and its security, health and mortality, children's educational outcomes, attitudes and political behaviour, exposure to unemployment and control over one's work. Both great traditions can point to shelves of such associations. Occupational schemas predict health and mortality gradients with an elegance that has made them indispensable to epidemiology; Goldthorpe-style classes structure mobility tables and educational inequality in study after study; income rises across their categories in broadly the order the theory expects. Wright's locations likewise pattern income, and in the comparative project's analyses they patterned attitudes towards class-relevant questions, perceptions of class conflict and support for redistribution, with the contradictory locations falling instructively between the poles their assets implied. On several standard outcomes the two families perform comparably once their categories are matched for fineness, a result this article reports without flinching, because it is what the comparative analyses broadly found and because pretending otherwise would violate the evidential standard the series exists to model.

Comparability, however, is not identity, and the residual differences are where the theories earn their separation. Three domains stand out. The first is authority itself: outcomes tied to control over work, autonomy, monitoring and the experience of command and subordination are, by construction, better organised by a schema that measures authority than by one that infers everything from occupation, and studies of workplace control consistently find location in authority structures carrying information that occupational class does not duplicate. The second is exploitation-adjacent magnitudes: where researchers have linked class locations to the appropriation of surplus at the firm or economy level, or to the distribution of productivity gains between wages and profits within sectors, the relational categories are the ones that map onto the mechanisms, because the mechanisms run through ownership and control rather than through occupational prestige. The third is the shape of interests on specifically class-political questions, where the contradictory locations behave as the asset theory anticipates, divided and context-dependent, rather than as a simple middle band on a ladder behaves. None of this shows the occupational tradition to be false; it shows the two schemas illuminating different causal systems that happen to overlap in the population they sort. A ladder predicts how far advantage travels. A relational map predicts who commands, who complies, and where the surplus goes. A complete social science wants both, and a polemic that uses the ladder's success at the first task to deny the map's object is overclaiming its evidence.

### Does a schema that predicts health outcomes thereby prove its theory of class?

No. Predictive success shows that a sorting captures something real about the distribution of advantage, and several incompatible sortings can do that at once, because advantage is overdetermined by occupation, education, income and authority together. Prediction arbitrates between schemas only on outcomes where their theories genuinely diverge, and class research is most informative exactly at those points of divergence.

This is also the place for honesty about null and awkward results within the Marxist programme. Some analyses found class location adding little to the explanation of particular attitudes once education and income were controlled; some locations in the finer schemas were empirically indistinguishable neighbours and were merged; the association between location and voting, in particular, proved weaker and more nationally variable than a simple class politics model would suggest, and the programme's own publications reported these disappointments rather than burying them. A measurement tradition that reports its failures is more, not less, credible for them, and the contrast with public class commentary, in which every schema is brandished only when it agrees with the speaker, is stark. The archive of this debate is one of the best available educations in how empirical social science actually proceeds, which is why the researcher persona section below treats learning to read it as a skill in its own right.

## The strongest objection: categories that can always be redrawn

The objection this article is required to state at full strength has two forms, one about payoff and one about falsifiability, and they are stronger together than apart. The payoff form, stated earlier in the brief for this article and pressed by many sociologists outside the Marxist tradition, holds that relational class analysis has produced comparatively little empirical return for the enormous effort of its instruments: that neo-Weberian occupational schemas predict income, health and mobility outcomes at least as well with categories statistical offices already publish, and that the Marxist schema's survival owes more to the theoretical commitments of its adherents than to victories in open predictive competition. The falsifiability form cuts deeper. It holds that a schema whose boundaries can be moved whenever results disappoint, in which authority can be counted or not, credentials included or excluded, managers reassigned and the self-employed split or lumped, is not a measurement at all but a vocabulary in search of confirmation; that the asset-inclusion rule, which this article has presented as a finding, is better described as a confession, since a quantity whose value depends this heavily on the measurer's choices has not been shown to exist independently of them; and that the proliferation of contradictory locations functions as an immunising device, a zone into which any anomalous group can be filed so that the two-class core of the theory is never itself at risk.

Stated that way, the objection deserves its prominence, and parts of it land. The concession on schema sensitivity must be made plainly and without varnish: the measured size of every class in this literature is sensitive to schema decisions, sometimes dramatically; there is no schema-free class total waiting in the data to be discovered by a sufficiently honest analyst; and any Marxist who quotes a class share without its schema is doing exactly what the objection accuses the tradition of doing, presenting a construction as a fact of nature. It must also be conceded that on the standard outcome variables of income, health and mobility, the relational and occupational schemas often perform comparably, that the comparative project's attitudinal findings were variable in strength, and that the tradition's popularisers claim a solidity for its numbers that its own researchers, Wright prominently among them, did not claim. The objection's portrait of a programme aware of its sensitivities and publishing them is, ironically, a fair description of the programme at its best.

The reply begins by denying that sensitivity to stated decisions is the same as unfalsifiability. A measurement is unfalsifiable when no result could count against it; the class programme fails that description at several points. Its schemas were published in advance of analyses, applied by critics as well as adherents, and revised under pressure of results in ways that were themselves published and criticised, including revisions that reduced the theory's claims, collapsing locations that did not behave distinctly and abandoning formulations that did not survive contact with data. Its central expectations were risky in identifiable ways: if contradictory locations had behaved identically to the classes they supposedly contradicted, if authority had shown no patterning of attitudes or control outcomes, if cross-national structures measured identically had come out identical despite different institutions, the programme would have had to report the failure of its own architecture, and the fact that its critics can name these counterfactuals shows the architecture was exposed to evidence. Redrawing is also not the free move the objection imagines. Each redrawing forfeits comparability with previous results, invites the charge of opportunism in print, and must justify itself by improved performance somewhere specifiable; the history of the schemas shows those costs being paid and sometimes refused, as when Wright resisted simpler schemas that performed nearly as well on some outcomes because they could not represent the authority dimension the theory cared about, a refusal a pure opportunist would not have made.

On payoff, the reply identifies the questions on which the relational schema does work its rivals cannot do, and asks that the competition be judged there as well as on the shared ground. Questions about exploitation require categories built from ownership and control; no occupational schema can state them, let alone answer them. Questions about workplace authority, who exercises it, in whose interest and with what effects on those subject to it, require an instrument that measures authority, and the occupational tradition largely declines to build one. Questions about the direction of class interests, including why some employee groups reliably align with employers on some issues and with workers on others, are the questions the contradictory locations were built to formulate, and their intermediate behaviour is a finding, not a fudge. Judged across the full range of questions class theory asks, the payoff ledger looks different from the version computed on health and mobility alone. Judged even on that restricted ground, comparable performance from a schema measuring different features is not redundancy, because it corroborates that both sortings track real structure. The honest verdict is therefore double: the objection succeeds against careless uses of class numbers, including many made in the theory's name, and it fails against the programme conducted at its documented best, where sensitivity is reported, revisions are costed, and the questions a schema cannot answer are named alongside those it can.

## One population, three totals: a worked passage

Abstractions about sensitivity become persuasive when a single population is followed through three schemas in sequence, so consider a stylised employed population described once, in terms no schema disputes. Out of every hundred employed people, a small handful own businesses employing others on a scale that lets them live mainly from the business; a larger band work for themselves without employees, some thriving as proprietors, some plainly dependent on single clients; the great remainder are employees, among whom a substantial minority supervise others, a smaller minority hold scarce professional credentials, some hold both, and the majority hold neither. Every number in what follows is an illustration of arithmetic, not a statistic about a real country, and the article's rule against asserting national shares as fact applies to its own examples.

Apply the official occupational logic first, and the population re-sorts by the standing of jobs: higher professional and managerial occupations form a top class, intermediate occupations a middle, routine occupations a bottom, with employers and the self-employed redistributed by the size and type of their enterprises. The results that public commentary loves appear immediately: a large middle, a working class identified with routine manual and service jobs that looks like a minority, and a story about occupational upgrading over time as routine manual work contracts. Apply the strict ownership logic next, counting only property and the employment of others, and the shape inverts: employers are the small handful, the self-employed band stands between, and employees, chief executives and cleaners together, form an overwhelming propertyless majority whose internal differences the schema deliberately brackets. Apply Wright's asset logic last, and the employee majority differentiates: those with extensive organisational authority move towards the capitalist pole as contradictory locations, credentialed experts without authority form their own contradictory band, supervisors of a narrow kind sit closer to the workers they oversee, and the working class proper, employees with neither asset, emerges smaller than the propertyless majority yet still the largest single location in most of the comparative studies, with the contradictory zones together rivalling it. Three totals, three middles, three working classes, one population. The example is the asset-inclusion rule enacted slowly, and a reader who has followed it possesses the critical skill this article exists to teach: the ability to ask of any class figure, before believing or disputing it, which of these machines produced it.

The same discipline resolves most apparent contradictions between studies. When two reports disagree about whether the working class is growing or shrinking, the first enquiry is not about the society but about the instruments: whether the boundary around managers moved, whether dependent contractors were reassigned, whether the universe was employees or all adults, whether a classification revision redrew occupational codes between the two dates. Genuine structural change certainly occurs, and the comparative record shows it, but it announces itself by surviving these alignments, appearing under several schemas at once in the directions each would predict. Change that appears under one schema and reverses under another is, until proven otherwise, a measurement event, and the history of class commentary is littered with measurement events that were debated for years as though they were transformations of society.

## Class structure over time: movement inside the categories

A further layer of evidence concerns what happens to locations over time, and it cautions against reading any single cross-section as the theory's portrait of an epoch. The industrial working class of the classical texts was defined as much by its concentration, in large workplaces, in homogeneous conditions, in neighbourhoods built around the plant, as by its propertylessness, and the measurement record of the past half century is in large part a record of that concentration dissolving while propertylessness persisted and spread. Employment moved from manufacturing to services, from large plants to fragmented supply chains and small units, from single-employer careers to sequences of jobs and statuses, and each movement altered the raw material from which schemas are built. Service employment is not a class; it contains surgeons and porters in the same hospital. Fragmentation is not a class trend either; a cleaner employed by a contractor rather than by the firm whose offices she cleans has changed statistical industry and employment unit without changing her relation to production at all, though her bargaining position may have worsened considerably. Analysts who track only occupational distributions register these changes as class transformation. Analysts who track relations register continuity where the occupational analyst sees rupture and rupture, in the growth of credentialed and managerial zones, where the occupational analyst sees only upgrading. Panel data, following individuals, add the biographical dimension: many people occupy several locations across a working life, entering as unskilled employees, acquiring credentials, holding supervisory posts, enduring unemployment, finishing in own-account work, so that the class structure at a moment is a still photograph of moving lives. That mobility within a lifetime neither refutes the structure nor leaves it untouched; it specifies the sense in which positions, rather than persons, are the theory's units, and it warns every schema against treating a location as a destiny.

Two long-run comparisons repay attention because they were attempted with unusual care about exactly these traps. The first is the repeated measurement of class structure in individual countries using held-constant instruments, where researchers re-ran earlier coding on later data or fielded the same module twice, finding, in the British and American cases most often discussed, stability in the broad relational shape alongside marked occupational change: the propertyless majority did not shrink as the manual share fell, the expert and managerial zones grew, and the employer class stayed small. The second is the comparison between class structure and income distribution trends, where the finding that income inequality can widen sharply while relational class shares move slowly is sometimes paraded as a paradox. It is no paradox on the theory's own terms, since shares of positions and distances between positions are different variables: a structure can hold its shape while the rewards attached to its locations polarise, which is precisely what several decades of wage dispersion within a stable employment structure look like. Keeping those variables separate is another dividend of the measurement literature, and it protects the reader against a family of headlines in which a change in incomes is reported as a change in classes or the reverse.

## Criticism from inside the Marxist tradition

The comparative programme was criticised from outside sociology and also, instructively, from within Marxism, and an evidence article should report the internal criticisms because they identify real weaknesses that the replies only partly met. One line of criticism held that Wright's locations were defined by assets and domination in a way that drifted from the exploitation core of the theory, so that the schema measured stratification inside production more faithfully than it measured exploitation itself; Wright's reformulation of the framework in explicitly exploitation-centred terms was his response, and whether the reformulation succeeded or merely relabelled the assets remains a live scholarly dispute that readers of the primary texts can judge. A second line held that the entire survey enterprise mistook the nature of class, which on some readings is not a set of positions to be counted at a moment but a historical relationship that exists in struggle and formation over time, in the spirit of the historian E. P. Thompson's insistence that class is a happening rather than a thing. On that view a cross-sectional survey photographs the residue of class relations while missing the relations themselves, which live in conflicts, organisations and episodes. The reply available to the measurement tradition is modest and partly concessive: a distribution of positions is not a history of class formation, and was never offered as one, but histories of formation require knowing the distribution of the positions among which formation occurs, and the surveys supply the map without which the narrative floats. A third internal criticism targeted the treatment of gender and household, arguing that class locations assigned through individual jobs or male breadwinners rendered the labour of reproduction invisible, a criticism the programme absorbed through the direct and mediated location distinction while critics continued to argue that absorption was not enough. A fourth targeted the national frame itself, noting that a class structure bounded by a nation state truncates relations that run through global production chains, a point with growing force as the comparative project's countries outsourced exactly the manual employment whose decline at home was being measured.

These criticisms share a pattern worth naming: each says the instrument measures something real but narrower than its name suggests. That is, in the end, the correct general verdict on all the schemas in this article, official and Marxist and Bourdieusian alike, and it is why the disclosure discipline matters more than loyalty to any instrument. A schema named for its actual operation, Wright's locations described as positions defined by ownership, authority and credentials in national employed populations, the NS-SEC described as an employment-relations and occupational classification, the Great British classes described as clusters of capitals in a self-selected sample, would generate far less confusion than the single word class carrying all of them by turns. The instruments are better than the word. Much of this article has been an exercise in letting each instrument be judged under its operational name.

## Class attitudes and the mediated path from position to politics

Because the comparative project measured attitudes alongside locations, it produced the most cited body of evidence on a question that theory alone had argued about for a century: how tightly does class position bind political outlook? The findings, reported at the level of generality the literature supports, run against both simple determinism and simple dismissal. Class location, measured relationally, was associated with attitudes towards inequality, redistribution, unions and class conflict itself, with employers and workers at the poles and the contradictory locations distributed between in patterned ways, managers leaning toward employer positions on many items, credentialed experts divided by issue, and the strength of the whole gradient varying by country in the institutional pattern already noted. At the same time the associations were moderate rather than overwhelming, substantial minorities within every location dissented from its central tendency, and other identities and experiences, education, sector, union membership, religion and nation, structured attitudes alongside class rather than beneath it. Researchers in the programme drew the conclusion the data offered: class interests are real enough to pattern responses across thousands of interviews and several countries, and they are not scripts; between position and politics stand organisations, parties, media and biography, doing work that no distribution table can do for them.

That measured conclusion has a use in public argument that exceeds its academic interest. Claims that class no longer matters politically are often supported by the observation that voting and attitudes do not align neatly with any class schema, as though neatness were the standard. The comparative evidence sets the standard correctly: alignment was never neat even in the industrial era the nostalgia invokes, the relational gradient in attitudes persists where it is looked for with appropriate instruments, and its variability between countries is itself a datum about institutions rather than an embarrassment for the concept. Conversely, claims that a measured class structure dictates a political future are equally unsupported, since the same evidence shows mediation everywhere. The position-to-politics path is the best-documented weak force in this literature, and articles that represent it as either absent or automatic are both misreporting the record.

## The public sector, the professions and other hard territories

Several territories of modern employment resist every schema and deserve individual mention, because the way an analyst handles them is a reliable diagnostic of the analyst's care. Public sector employment is the largest. A nurse in a state hospital, a teacher in a state school and a civil servant administering benefits are employees without a capitalist employer; their labour is funded from taxation, their managers exercise authority inside organisations that do not appropriate surplus value in the market sense, and yet their positions exhibit hierarchy, credential assets, security gradients and relations to the state that class analysis cannot responsibly ignore. Wright's schema classifies them by the same ownership, authority and credential axes, treating the state as an employer whose locations parallel those in firms while flagging the theoretical difference; critics within Marxism have argued for treating state employment as a distinct terrain, since the exploitation relation that anchors the schema is absent or transformed there. Either way the size of the sector, a fifth to a third of employment in many European economies, guarantees that the choice moves totals, and comparative work that ignores the sector's different weight between, say, Scandinavian and liberal economies will misread national differences in class structure that are substantially differences in where services are provided.

The professions pose the credential problem in its purest form. Medicine, law and academia combine scarce certified skills, organised closure of entry, high autonomy at senior levels and, in private practice, direct ownership, so that a single occupational label spans the full relational range from partner-owner to salaried junior working under intense direction. Schemas that assign professions wholesale to a top class are measuring prestige; schemas that disaggregate by employment status and authority recover the internal spread at the cost of sample size. Agriculture, though shrunken in rich economies, remains the sector where family labour, tenancy, seasonal wage work and ownership interlock most intricately, and where the employment status proxy is most treacherous, since a family farm's members may be simultaneously owners, workers and employers of harvest labour. Informal economies, dominant in much of the world, strain the survey frame itself: where employment is unregistered, multiple and intermittent, the questions that presuppose a main job with a definite status return unstable answers, and class measurement in such settings requires instruments adapted to livelihoods rather than jobs, a frontier where the comparative tradition built on advanced economies has least to say and should say it least confidently. A global class structure, if the phrase is to mean anything measurable, will be built by confronting these territories rather than by exporting one country's coding frame, and the existing literature's honesty about its national bases is again to its credit.

## What a careful class figure looks like: a disclosure standard

The constructive conclusion of the measurement literature can be stated as a standard that any publisher of class figures, academic, official or journalistic, could adopt tomorrow, and stating it converts this article's criticisms into a checklist. A careful class figure names its schema and its theoretical family, so the reader knows whether the sorting is relational, gradational or capital-based. It states its universe, employed individuals, all adults or households, and its treatment of the non-employed, the retired and students. It states its asset decisions: whether authority counts and at what threshold, whether credentials count and how scarcity was proxied, whether the self-employed were split and by what dependence or scale test, and how managers and public sector employees were placed. It reports, where feasible, the sensitivity of its headline total to the largest of those decisions, or at minimum acknowledges the direction in which an alternative decision would move it. And it separates the figure from claims the figure cannot carry: a distribution of positions is not a measure of exploitation, of consciousness, of mobility or of income inequality, each of which needs its own evidence. Figures published to this standard can disagree fruitfully, because their disagreements can be located in named decisions. Figures published below it can only collide.

Judged against that standard the traditions rank in an order that may surprise partisans of each. The official schemas disclose superbly: their derivation algorithms, category definitions and revision histories are public documents, and their failure is not concealment but the misreading of their outputs by others, a failure their documentation explicitly warns against in places. Wright's programme discloses to an academic standard, with instruments, codebooks and revision rationales in print, and its failure mode is the opposite: a scholarly apparatus so extensive that its findings enter public discussion only in mutilated summary. The Bourdieusian survey disclosed its sampling weakness in its own methodological writing and then watched its population shares circulate without the warning, a case study in how disclosure fails when it is not attached to the number itself. Journalism, with honourable exceptions, discloses least, because the headline form punishes qualification, and the standard above is therefore aimed most hopefully at the intermediaries, textbook writers, teachers, report authors and encyclopedias, who transmit class figures from the literatures to the public and who can carry the methods note with the number if they choose.

## Mobility, income and the life-course evidence

A class structure is a distribution at a moment, but the traditions that built the schemas were equally interested in movement, and the mobility evidence forms a second empirical front on which the schemas can be compared. Intergenerational mobility research asks how strongly a person's class destination is predicted by the class of their parents, and its central findings are among the most robust in sociology: origin class substantially predicts destination in every country studied, absolute mobility rose during the expansion of professional and managerial employment in the postwar decades, and relative mobility, the fairness of the competition once the changing shape of the structure is allowed for, moved far less and differs between countries in patterned ways. The Goldthorpe tradition built much of this edifice, and its class schema was refined in the course of it, since a classification intended for mobility tables must be stable in meaning across generations whose occupational worlds differ markedly. That requirement exposes a relational difficulty the mobility literature handles with varying candour: a father's location as a skilled manual employee in a nationalised industry and a daughter's location as a skilled technician in a private service firm may share an occupational band while differing in security, authority and relation to ownership, and the table records them as immobile or mobile depending on coding conventions about sector and status that rarely reach the published summary.

The Marxist contribution to mobility evidence is thinner as a dedicated literature and thicker as an implication. If positions are defined by assets, then mobility is not only movement between jobs but acquisition and loss of assets: credentials gained through education, authority gained through promotion, property gained through inheritance or business formation, and the corresponding losses through redundancy, business failure or deskilling. Wright's framework implies that the educational system functions in part as a machine for distributing the credential asset unequally, a claim that connects class measurement to the sociology of education and that empirical work on graduate premiums and professional closure supports in outline: certified scarcity commands rents, entry to certification is socially patterned, and the premium persists even where the underlying skill could be more widely taught. The implication cuts against a purely meritocratic reading of occupational mobility, because a ladder climbed by acquiring a scarce asset looks different from a ladder climbed by talent alone once the scarcity itself is socially manufactured and defended by the asset's holders. None of this displaces the mobility tables; it reframes what their movements mean, and it directs attention to the distribution mechanisms, inheritance, credentialing, promotion ladders, that a purely occupational account treats as background.

Income evidence adds a necessary complication in the opposite direction. Within every class location in every schema, incomes spread widely, and over recent decades the spread within locations has grown in many countries even where the spread between locations grew more slowly. A measurement practice that reports only the class distribution therefore misses the dimension along which inequality has most visibly moved, and critics of class analysis as a whole have used within-class dispersion to argue that class categories are losing their grip on economic life. The fair conclusion is narrower. Class locations were never income bands, and their theoretical work concerns relations and assets rather than pay packets; but a class analysis that cannot connect its map to the changing distances between and within its locations has surrendered the economic plot to other frameworks. The strongest contemporary work in both traditions therefore reports structure and dispersion together, treating the class map as the skeleton and the income distribution as the changing flesh upon it, and the reader should be suspicious of any class figure offered as a complete account of inequality in either direction.

## Gender, ethnicity and the intersections measurement must not flatten

The feminist criticism of class schemas, already noted for its demolition of the male headship convention, developed into a broader measurement lesson that every schema in this article has had to absorb. Women's employment is disproportionately part-time, interrupted by caring responsibilities that the surrounding society assigns unequally, and concentrated in sectors, care, education, retail, health support, whose relational character is easy to miscode: a schema built around full-time continuous careers will systematically misplace women, and a schema assigning class through a partner will systematically erase them. The corrective adopted across the field, classifying individuals by their own employment wherever possible and treating non-employment and part-time status as data rather than as absence of data, improved measurement for everyone, since men's interrupted and part-time trajectories were also being misread by the older conventions. Wright's mediated locations added a second corrective, allowing a person's class situation to be analysed as a compound of their own position and the positions of those whose resources they share, an approach that respects household reality without dissolving individuals into it. The remaining frontier concerns unpaid labour itself: the work of reproduction that sustains labour power daily and generationally is performed overwhelmingly outside employment, and no schema of employed positions can see it, a limitation that Marxist-feminist scholarship has pressed as theoretical rather than merely technical, since a map of production that excludes reproduction is partial by design. An evidence article can register that limitation honestly without pretending a coding fix exists: the surveys measure class in the sphere of paid production and employment, and claims beyond that sphere need other instruments.

Ethnicity and migration expose a parallel set of measurement traps. Migrant workers frequently occupy positions at odds with their credentials, the engineer driving a taxi and the doctor working as a carer being the emblematic cases, so that a schema coding by occupation and a schema coding by credential asset return different locations for the same person, and both are telling a truth about different aspects of their situation: their market treatment follows the job, their frustrated asset follows the certificate the host labour market refuses to honour. Schemas that cannot represent that dissonance will understate the class disadvantage of migrant populations or misattribute it. Similarly, ethnic segmentation of labour markets, in which particular groups are concentrated in particular niches of self-employment, small retail, garment work, transport, by networks of recruitment and capital access as much as by choice, means that a single category such as own-account worker contains ethnically specific class realities that aggregate figures flatten. The comparative tradition's national frames aggravate the problem, since migration is precisely a relation between national structures. None of these difficulties is a reason to abandon measurement; they are reasons the better studies disaggregate, report the dissonance cases explicitly, and resist the temptation to let a clean national total stand in for a population whose class experience is jointly structured by origin, status and reception. A reader applying the disclosure standard should add one more item to it for this terrain: state how migrants, part-time workers and those outside employment were classified, because in these groups the coding decision is frequently the finding.

## Authority and credentials under the microscope

Since the asset-inclusion rule makes authority and credentials the hinge of the whole measurement, the validity of their measurement deserves scrutiny of its own. Authority in survey instruments is typically captured by questions about supervising others, participating in decisions, and influencing pay, promotion and policy, and validation work comparing responses with organisational records and with job analysis data has found the self-reports serviceable at the level of broad strata and noisy at fine grain, which is one reason the programme's analysts preferred collapsed locations for many purposes. A subtler problem is that authority is partly a property of organisational context rather than of the job title: the same supervisory role carries budgetary and disciplinary power in one firm and only message-passing in another, flat organisations distribute decisions that hierarchies concentrate, and public sector authority operates under political and legal constraints unlike those in a firm. Instruments respond by asking about exercised discretion rather than formal level, and the trend in the literature is towards treating authority as a graded scale that schemas then cut at stated thresholds, which is the honest architecture: the data supply a continuum, the theory supplies the cut, and the publication supplies the sensitivity of results to where the cut fell.

Credentials raise a different validity problem, because the theory's asset is not the certificate but the scarcity and closure the certificate organises. Qualifications held are easy to count and are what surveys count; scarcity is a market property that varies by field, place and time, so that the same degree commands a large premium in one labour market and little in another, and a fixed coding of degree holders as asset owners mismeasures both. The empirical tradition has handled this pragmatically, using occupation-linked expertise and measured earnings premiums to identify which credentialed groups actually extract rents, and critics have noted the circularity risk when premiums both define the asset and validate its importance. The defence is that the circle is not fully closed: the schema predicts non-income consequences, attitudes, autonomy, security, from the location, and those consequences can fail to appear. Still, the credential asset remains the least cleanly measured element of Wright's apparatus, and readers should hold its fine-grained results more loosely than the ownership results, which rest on the survey's firmest questions. That graded confidence, firm on ownership, moderate on authority, tentative on credential scarcity, is itself an evidence judgement of the kind this article exists to teach, and it is preferable in every way to the uniform confidence or uniform scepticism with which class figures are usually traded.

## Reading a class table without being misled: a short apprenticeship

The skills this article has exercised can be compressed into the order in which an experienced reader interrogates an unfamiliar class table. The first question is always about the sorting principle: what property of a person placed them in this row, occupation, assets, resources, legal status? Until that is known the row labels, which often share words like middle and working across incompatible schemas, mean nothing portable. The second question concerns the universe and the denominator, because a percentage is a claim about a share of something and the something is frequently where the surprise is hiding. The third concerns the known levers for that schema family: for relational schemas, the treatment of managers, the self-employed and the public sector; for occupational schemas, any classification revision and the handling of the non-employed; for capital-based schemas, the sample and the clustering method. The fourth is about the claim being hung on the table: does the conclusion require ownership, exploitation, consciousness or mobility, and is this the instrument that measures that thing, or a neighbouring thing with a persuasive label? Most public misuse of class statistics fails at the fourth question after passing the first three, which is why the apprenticeship ends there. A table can be impeccably built and still be evidence about life chances being quoted in an argument about exploitation, and no amount of statistical quality repairs a mismatch of object.

Worked habits follow from the interrogation. Keep the employment status skeleton beside any elaborate schema as a sanity check, since a result that contradicts the skeleton without explanation usually signals a coding event rather than a social one. Prefer studies that publish their derivation to studies that publish only their results, and prefer ranges and sensitivity statements to point figures wherever the literature offers them. When two sources disagree, align their universes and asset decisions before theorising their difference, and treat the residual, the disagreement that survives alignment, as the genuinely interesting object, because it is there that either the world or a theory is misbehaving in an instructive way. And when writing, transmit the methods note with the number as a matter of professional honour: the extra sentence costs little and is the difference between contributing to an evidence culture and contributing to the fog this article was written to disperse.

## Health, mortality and the gradational stronghold

The occupational schemas possess one fortress of evidence that any honest comparison must enter respectfully: the social gradient in health. Study after study, in Britain most famously through the Whitehall investigations of civil servants and through routine mortality statistics by NS-SEC, finds health and life expectancy ordered by occupational class with a regularity that survives adjustment for smoking, income and education, each step down the classification carrying worse average outcomes. This is among the best replicated findings in social epidemiology, and it demonstrates something real about how advantage and disadvantage accumulate in bodies over lifetimes. A Marxist reading of the gradient is available and partly persuasive: the ordering tracks control over work, exposure to hazard and insecurity, and the material resources that positions command, all of which correlate with relational locations too, so that when researchers have modelled health by employment relations and by authority the relational variables also perform. But the fortress holds on its own terms because the gradational instrument was designed and validated exactly here, and pretending the Marxist schema outperforms it on mortality would be advocacy, not evidence. The correct conclusion is the one this article has drawn repeatedly: strong prediction on an outcome validates a schema for that outcome's causal neighbourhood. Health is produced by material conditions, psychosocial control and behaviour patterned by resources, a neighbourhood where rank and relational position overlap heavily. Exploitation is produced inside production relations, a neighbourhood where only the relational instrument looks. Each tradition's temptation is to export its home victory across the whole map, and the evidence licenses neither export.

## The platform economy as a live measurement problem

The growth of platform-mediated work offers a contemporary laboratory in which every difficulty in this article appears at once, and it is worth examining as method rather than as news. A driver or courier engaged through an application may be classified by the platform as an independent contractor, by a labour court as a worker, by a tax authority as self-employed, by an occupational coder as a transport operative, and by a relational analyst as either a petty proprietor who owns a vehicle or a propertyless employee whose apparent asset is a consumer durable irrelevant to the relation's real control structure, which lives in the application's allocation of work, pricing and discipline. Each classification is defensible from its own criterion, and the population counts of the gig economy vary accordingly by factors, not by fractions. The asset-inclusion rule predicts exactly this chaos and supplies the questions that organise it: does the worker's vehicle count as means of production when access to customers, the genuinely scarce asset, is owned by the platform and rented back through commission? Does algorithmic direction count as authority exercised over the worker, and if so whose authority is it in schema terms, the platform's managers or the system they own? Do ratings function as a credential asset, accumulated, non-transferable and capable of being withdrawn, a new species of the genus Wright identified in certified skills? Serious measurement work on platforms increasingly builds bespoke instruments for these questions rather than forcing the standard categories, which is the field learning its own lesson in real time. For the reader the episode's value is diagnostic: when a workforce can be made to appear and disappear as an employee class by legal and coding decisions alone, the claim that class totals are schema outputs stops being a methodological subtlety and becomes the only serious description of what is happening.

## What census and survey categories structurally cannot see

Alongside the difficulties that better instruments could in principle repair stand limits that belong to the form of the census and the survey themselves, and cataloguing them prevents the measurement programme from overpromising. A survey of individuals at a moment cannot see the firm as a unit: the ownership networks, supply relations and internal labour processes through which class relations actually operate are properties of organisations and chains, recoverable only by linking individual data to enterprise data that confidentiality regimes guard fiercely. It cannot see surplus: no questionnaire asks how much value a respondent produced or how much was appropriated, and the exploitation that anchors the theory must be inferred from positions and from separate economic data, never observed in the class instrument itself. It cannot see history: a location is a present tense, while class in the strong theoretical sense includes trajectory, organisation and memory, the difference between a workforce with a union tradition and an identical distribution of positions without one being precisely the difference between a class as counted and a class as an actor. And it cannot see across borders: the national frame that makes surveys fundable and comparable truncates the relations of workers whose employers, owners and product markets sit in other jurisdictions, so that the measured structure of a rich country silently excludes the offshore locations its firms command. Naming these limits is not a retreat from measurement. It is the boundary that keeps measurement honest, and it explains why the most thoughtful practitioners, Wright among them, presented class structure analysis as one component of class analysis, alongside studies of formation, organisation and struggle, rather than as the whole of it. The distribution is the map; the territory includes the journeys, and confusing the two is the final measurement error this article warns against.

## The pattern across magnitudes: classification as the recurring finding

Readers who have followed this series through its evidence articles will recognise the shape of the present findings, and the recognition is worth making explicit because it is itself a result. In the measurement of the rate of exploitation, a classification decision about productive labour moved the estimate more than the data. In class measurement, classification decisions about assets move the totals more than the population. The repetition is not a coincidence of two badly designed fields; it is what happens whenever a theoretical category built to name a relation is operationalised into countable boxes, because the relation does not arrive pre-boxed and every boxing encodes the theory again. The mature response, visible in the best work of both literatures, is neither to abandon quantification nor to worship it, but to practise it as an argued translation: state the theory, state the translation, publish the sensitivity, and let critics attack the translation where it is weakest. That practice is slower than the production of headline numbers and it is the only practice under which numbers deserve belief. Class structure and the evidence, in the end, names both halves of that phrase with equal emphasis. The evidence is real, comparative and instructive. The structure is the thing the evidence is about only through decisions that the evidence itself cannot make for us, and intellectual honesty consists in keeping both halves in view at once.

## Choosing a schema for a question: decision guidance

Because this article has refused to crown a single instrument, it owes the reader practical guidance on choosing among them, question by question. If the question concerns health inequalities, educational access or the intergenerational transmission of advantage, the official and Goldthorpe-style schemas are the instruments with the deepest validation, the longest series and the most developed conventions, and choosing them is not a theoretical surrender but a matching of tool to task. If the question concerns exploitation, workplace authority, the interests attached to positions in production, or the comparison of class structures as structures of relations, the relational schemas are the only ones that contain the relevant variables, and their sensitivity profile must be carried into the report as part of the answer. If the question concerns taste, cultural distinction, lifestyle clustering or the symbolic boundaries between groups, the Bourdieusian instruments illuminate what the others flatten, provided their sampling supports the inference being drawn, which in the famous British case it does only with qualifications the original team itself published. If the question is a first reconnaissance over an unfamiliar country or period, the employment status proxy is the correct opening move, cheap, relational and honest about its crudity, with the elaborate schemas reserved for the questions the reconnaissance shows to be worth their cost. And if the question is journalistic, which is to say a claim about society offered to a general audience, the governing rule is the disclosure standard: whichever schema supplied the figure must travel with it into print, together with its universe and its largest asset decision, because a number detached from its machine is not a finding but a rumour with decimals.

This guidance also settles, in practice, most of the territory war between the traditions. The war persists in public because each side's partisans ask only their own questions and then announce that the rival instrument failed to answer them. In research practice the traditions coexist in the same departments and often in the same papers, occupational schemas carrying the mobility and health analyses, relational categories carrying the analyses of authority, interests and exploitation, capital-based measures carrying the analyses of distinction. The comparative surveys this article has examined are best honoured by that division of labour rather than by a verdict that one sorting is class and the others are not. What the Marxist programme uniquely contributed, beyond its own tables, was the demonstration that the relational questions could be asked with survey rigour at all, and the asset-inclusion rule that now disciplines every answer given to them. What the official tradition uniquely contributed was a standard of documentation and validation that any programme, Marxist included, is better for meeting. The evidence base as a whole is richer than any of its owners, and the reader who can move between its instruments with the criteria in view has acquired the skill that this cluster of the series, from its theory of class to its disputes about the working class's existence, has been building towards throughout.

One last historical perspective steadies the whole account. Class measurement is young. The occupational schemas were systematised within living memory of the oldest working sociologists, Wright's comparative project is younger than the careers of many who now use its results, and the Bourdieusian survey belongs to the present century. A field this young should expect its instruments to be revised, its totals to be sensitive and its categories to be argued about, and should regard those conditions not as scandal but as the normal adolescence of a measurement science. Physics envy has damaged social research before, tempting it to hide its constructions behind the rhetoric of discovery. The class literature at its best resists that temptation in print, and its best is good enough to learn from: schemas published, decisions argued, sensitivities tabled, failures reported. If the next generation of instruments, built for platform work, informal economies and global chains, inherits that discipline, the totals of the future will be no less constructed than those of the past, and considerably more honest about it, which is the only kind of progress measurement can honestly promise.

## For students and exam candidates

Cite class statistics the way this article has tried to model: never quote a class share without its criterion attached. In an essay, the sentence that earns its place names the schema, the universe and the asset decision before it trusts the number, and distinguishes the gradational fact that routine manual employment has declined from the relational question of how many people must sell their labour power. Examiners reward the distinction between position and consciousness, and between a distribution of locations and evidence about exploitation, because both distinctions show the candidate handling the literature rather than reciting it. When a question asks whether the working class has disappeared, answer through the schemas: show what each machine reports from the same population, identify the asset-inclusion decision driving the difference, and conclude that the dispute is substantially methodological with a real residue about the growth of managerial and credentialed zones. Keep the worked example of one population under three schemas in reserve; it converts an abstract methods point into visible arithmetic in a handful of sentences, and it is the fastest route to the evaluative marks. Above all, avoid the two symmetric errors of treating an official occupational table as a finding about ownership, and of treating a relational total as a measurement of exploitation, since questions in this area are usually designed to catch exactly those confusions.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

This topic teaches best as an exercise before it is taught as a doctrine. Give the seminar a single population description, a hundred employed people characterised by ownership, employment status, supervisory power, credentials and sector, and let groups code it under an official-style occupational logic, a strict ownership logic and an asset-inclusive logic, then compare the three working class totals the room has honestly produced from identical facts. The argument that follows teaches the asset-inclusion rule more durably than any lecture, because students discover their own coding decisions as the source of the divergence. Follow the exercise with the disclosure standard as a marking tool: set students to find a class figure in journalism or a textbook and to grade its methods note, or its absence, against the checklist. The Great British Class Survey rewards classroom use as a paired text, since its own methodological appendix supplies the criticism and students can watch a research team disclose a weakness that its headlines then lost. Keep the political temperature manageable by insisting throughout on the operational names of schemas; a room that argues about whether authority should count as an asset is having a better seminar than a room arguing about whose side the numbers are on, and the first argument contains the second in a form that can actually be settled in places.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

For researchers, the working sequence this article recommends begins with the employment status skeleton from labour force microdata, adds authority and credential dimensions only with documented instruments, and reports sensitivity to the managerial threshold and the self-employment split as a matter of routine. Postgraduates building a first analysis should replicate before they innovate: reproduce a published Wright-style distribution or an official derivation on a familiar dataset, because replication exposes every hidden decision in the original and calibrates the replicator's own. Historians should treat schema change as a source problem, since a series that crosses a classification revision is two series wearing one label, and should prefer held-constant recoding, however laborious, to spliced official totals. Journalists carry the heaviest responsibility per word: a class figure in a headline rests, as this article has shown, on a schema, a universe and at least two asset decisions, and the honest minimum is to name the schema and to refuse the conversion of occupational change into class disappearance. Ask of any study the questions from the disclosure standard, what was counted as an asset, who is in the denominator, how were the self-employed and managers treated, and if the answers are unavailable, report the figure as the study's construction rather than as a social fact. The comparative archive rewards the effort: few topics offer a clearer view of how social statistics are made, and a reporter who understands class measurement understands something about every official number that crosses the desk thereafter.

## What is settled, what is sensitive, and what remains open

The state of the evidence can be closed honestly in three registers. Settled, to the standard anything in this field is settled: class has been measured on Marxist criteria in large comparative surveys with published instruments; the resulting distributions show a small employer class, a large propertyless employee population and substantial intermediate zones in every advanced economy examined; structures differ between countries in institutionally patterned ways; and official classifications, whatever their considerable merits, do not measure ownership and cannot be quoted as though they do. Sensitive, and to be reported as sensitive in every use: the exact size of the working class and of every intermediate location, which moves with the treatment of authority, credentials, managers, the self-employed, households and the non-employed, to a degree that dwarfs sampling error and most historical change. Open, and genuinely so: whether the relational schema's advantages on questions of authority, exploitation and interests outweigh its comparable performance on standard outcomes sufficiently to justify its complexity in general-purpose research; how class measurement should be rebuilt for informal and globally chained production; and how positions in a structure increasingly organised through platforms, credentials and state employment will be coded by the next generation of instruments. A reader who can hold those three registers apart, settled, sensitive and open, possesses the finding of this article in its most portable form.

The closing statement on the evidence is therefore not a number. It is a rule for handling numbers, the asset-inclusion rule, together with the demonstration that the rule operates everywhere in this field: in the official tables that omit ownership, in the Marxist schemas that include assets by explicit decision, in the Bourdieusian clusters that substitute capitals for relations, and in the headlines that inherit all three without distinction. Marxist class analysis asked to be judged as an empirical programme, and in Wright's hands it became one, with instruments, findings, revisions and reported disappointments that entitle it to be argued with on evidence rather than dismissed as doctrine or embraced as revelation. Its most durable contribution to the wider culture may prove to be the discipline it forces on everyone who touches its subject: say what you counted, say what you treated as an asset, say who is in your denominator, and let the number carry no more than its construction can bear.

## Validation studies and the European comparative exercise

A schema earns scientific standing not when its author defends it but when independent teams test its derivations on data it was not built to fit, and both major traditions have passed through that discipline with instructive results. The European socio-economic classification was subjected to a dedicated validation programme in which national teams applied a common derivation to different countries' labour force data and compared the resulting classes against criterion variables including income, unemployment risk, health and educational outcomes, publishing the points at which the harmonised categories behaved differently in different labour markets. The exercise found the schema broadly serviceable and specifically strained where national institutions, training systems and welfare regimes bent the employment relations the categories assumed, a result that parallels the Wright programme's cross-national attitude findings from the other tradition: in both cases the instrument travelled, and in both cases the friction of travel was itself the most interesting evidence, because it located the institutional joints at which class structures are actually assembled. Validation of the NS-SEC followed a similar path through official reviews, with its categories tested against the outcomes government most needed it to order, and its derivation revised in public when anomalies accumulated.

The Marxist schemas underwent a parallel process less centrally organised but no less real, as researchers outside Wright's own team applied his instruments to new countries and populations, adapted the locations to different labour markets, and published comparisons in which the schema's predictions were sometimes confirmed, sometimes flattened and sometimes beaten on particular outcomes by simpler occupational rivals. Especially valuable were the studies that applied several schemas to a single dataset and reported the contest openly, since a same-data comparison controls for everything except the instrument and converts the traditions' argument into a set of local, answerable questions: on this outcome, in this population, which sorting organises the variation better, and by how much. The cumulative picture from those contests is the one this article has maintained throughout. No instrument sweeps the board. The relational schema's distinctive victories cluster where its theory says they should, around authority, control and class-relevant attitudes; the occupational schema's victories cluster around the gradients of health, education and mobility; and the contests themselves have improved both instruments, forcing the relational tradition to simplify locations that never earned their complexity and the occupational tradition to state its employment-relations theory more explicitly when challenged. A reader who wants the strongest available evidence about class measurement should read the validation and comparison literature rather than any programme's own summary of itself, because it is the one place where every tradition's claims are examined by people with an interest in finding their limits.

There is a final lesson in how these literatures age. Instruments that publish their derivations can be re-run on new data by sceptics, and several of the schemas discussed here have now been re-estimated across decades, so that arguments about their performance can be settled by accumulation rather than by authority. Instruments that exist only as a famous set of percentages, detached from any re-runnable derivation, age into folklore. The disclosure standard proposed earlier is therefore not etiquette; it is the difference between joining a cumulative science and issuing a pamphlet with tables. Class analysis, Marxist and otherwise, has produced enough of both that a reader can learn to tell them apart on sight, and that discrimination, practised consistently, is the real deliverable of the evidence tradition this article has surveyed.

## A note on sources for the counts cited in classrooms

Teachers and students meeting this subject through textbooks will encounter a small canon of endlessly recycled figures: a working class share from a Wright study of one country in one decade, an NS-SEC distribution from a census year, the seven Great British classes with their sample percentages, each travelling from book to book with its methods note progressively amputated. The scholarly sources behind those figures are recoverable, and part of the research skill this series exists to build is the habit of recovering them: returning from the textbook figure to the study, from the study to its instrument, and from the instrument to its coding decisions, at which point the figure can be quoted with its conditions attached or replaced by a better one. Wright's distributions belong with the version of his schema that produced them, since the locations changed between versions and a total from one version cannot illustrate another. Official distributions belong with their classification revision, for the same reason. The Great British percentages belong with their sampling warning, permanently. Quoting any of them otherwise is not a venial shortcut; it is the precise mechanism by which the fog this article describes is manufactured, one unconditioned number at a time, and the classroom is where the habit of resisting it is cheapest to form.

The same habit protects the reader in the opposite direction, against the sceptic who dismisses every class figure as invented because each is constructed. Construction is the common condition of social statistics, from unemployment rates built on definitions of seeking work to poverty lines built on conventions about equivalence scales, and the correct response to a constructed number is never blanket refusal; it is inspection of the construction, which is possible exactly when the constructor has disclosed it. The class literature, at its documented best, discloses more of its construction than most fields that use its results. Meeting it halfway, by reading the methods before quoting the totals, is the smallest courtesy a serious reader can pay it, and the largest single improvement available to public argument about class.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you measure class structure empirically?

You define a schema, obtain survey or census data containing the variables the schema needs, code each respondent into a class location, and report the resulting distribution with its decisions disclosed. A relational measurement needs, at minimum, employment status and ownership information; a fuller Marxist measurement adds questions about supervisory and decision-making authority and about credentials. An occupational measurement needs reliably coded occupations and, in the Goldthorpe tradition, employment status and employer size. In every case the empirical work is inseparable from the coding: derivation rules must be published, borderline groups such as managers, the self-employed and public sector staff must be assigned by stated rules, and the totals should be shown, where possible, under the main alternative assignments. Measurement is therefore a chain of argued translations from a theory to a table, and its quality is judged by the transparency and consistency of the chain as much as by the size of the sample.

### Q: What official class classifications do governments use?

Governments mainly use occupational classifications built for statistical purposes, of which the British NS-SEC is the most theorised example, sorting people by occupation together with employment status and employer size into classes derived from the Goldthorpe tradition's account of employment relations. A comparative European version harmonises a similar approach across countries. These classifications serve health monitoring, education planning and social reporting, and they are validated against outcomes such as mortality and attainment, at which they perform well. The United States takes a different path, publishing occupational distributions and income and wealth statistics without an official class schema in the European sense. The crucial point for readers is what all of these instruments share: none of them is built on ownership of the means of production. Employers and the self-employed appear because employment status is collected, but a senior employee and an owner can share a top category. Official class statistics are therefore evidence about occupational structure and life chances, not about the distribution of productive property.

### Q: Did Erik Olin Wright measure class on Marxist criteria?

Yes, and his programme remains the most extensive attempt to do so. From the late 1970s Wright designed survey instruments that sorted respondents by ownership of the means of production first, asking whether they owned a business and employed others, and then by two further assets among employees: authority over subordinates and organisational decisions, and scarce credentialed skills. Employees with substantial authority or credentials occupied what he called contradictory class locations between the main classes. The comparative class analysis project fielded versions of this instrument in the United States and in a range of other advanced economies including Canada, Britain, Sweden and Japan, using probability samples and published questionnaires. The programme revised its schema over time, moving towards an explicitly exploitation-centred formulation, and published its instruments, codebooks and internal criticisms. Whatever verdict a reader reaches on the schema's categories, the existence of a large, documented body of class data collected on Marxist criteria is a matter of record rather than of interpretation.

### Q: How big is the working class by relational criteria?

By relational criteria the working class is large, but its exact size is a function of the schema's asset decisions, and honesty requires giving the logic rather than a single figure. Count every propertyless employee as working class and the class forms an overwhelming majority of the employed in the advanced economies the comparative surveys examined. Treat substantial managerial authority as an asset removing its holders into contradictory locations, and the class shrinks by the managerial stratum; treat scarce credentials the same way, and it shrinks again by the professional and expert strata. In Wright's published analyses the working class so defined remained the largest single location or among the largest, with the contradictory zones together forming a substantial share. No defensible relational schema makes employers more than a small minority or propertyless employees less than the largest bloc. A figure quoted without its treatment of authority and credentials should be treated, under the asset-inclusion rule, as uninterpretable.

### Q: Why do class schemas disagree about the size of classes?

Because they sort by different criteria, over different universes, with different decisions about the groups in between. An occupational schema counts by the standing and skill of jobs, so its working class tracks routine employment and shrinks as routine manual work declines. A relational schema counts by ownership, authority and credentials, so its working class tracks propertyless employment and can grow while manual work shrinks. Schemas also differ over whether the unit is the employed individual, all adults or the household, and over how managers, the self-employed, public sector staff and the retired are placed, each decision moving the totals. Sampling and clustering choices add further distance in capital-based schemas. The disagreements are therefore mostly located in stated or unstated construction decisions rather than in rival observations of the same quantity. Align the criteria, the universe and the asset decisions, and much of the disagreement dissolves; what survives alignment is the genuine dispute about which features of a position do the causal work.

### Q: Is self employment a usable proxy for class position?

It is a usable starting point and a treacherous resting place. Employment status is collected almost everywhere, and separating employers, own-account workers and employees gives a crude relational skeleton that no class analysis should ignore. But the self-employed category spans genuinely different positions: proprietors with assets and employees, independent tradespeople and professionals, and dependent contractors whose self-employment is a legal form placed over what functions as wage labour for a single client. Treating all of them as one petty bourgeois class mistakes a legal status for a relation. The literature's repairs are to split owners by whether and how much they employ, to test dependent contractors for single-client dependence and direction, and to watch whether self-employment in a sector accumulates assets or merely fills gaps between jobs. A published figure should state which of these treatments it used. Used with those disclosures, the proxy earns its place as the first approximation; used without them, it measures the label rather than the position.

### Q: What did the Great British Class Survey find?

The survey, run in the early 2010s through a broadcaster's online platform by a team led by Mike Savage, measured economic, cultural and social capital in Bourdieu's sense and grouped respondents into seven classes, running from a small wealthy elite through established and technical middle classes and new affluent workers to a traditional working class, an emergent service sector and a precariat defined by insecurity across all three capitals. It put the vocabulary of capitals and the word precariat into wide public circulation. Its evidential limits are equally part of the finding: the main sample was self-selected and heavily skewed towards the educated and culturally engaged, so the classes describe profiles of resources in that sample, and their population shares cannot be treated as national estimates, a weakness the team documented through a smaller representative comparison study. The schema also defines class by accumulated resources rather than by production relations, so its categories answer questions about distinction and advantage, not about ownership or exploitation.

### Q: Does wealth data show a capitalist class?

Wealth data show one necessary component of a capitalist class and leave the rest to be demonstrated. Tax records, wealth surveys and distributional research establish that ownership of financial and business assets is highly concentrated, and that business equity is among the most concentrated categories, which is consistent with a small class living substantially from productive property. But concentration of assets is not identical to control of means of production: household wealth includes housing, pensions and savings with different distributions, and wealthy individuals include rentiers, heirs and highly paid executives as well as operating owners. The stronger case assembles several evidences together: the small size of the employer stratum in employment data, studies of corporate control and interlocking ownership showing how productive assets are commanded, and research on inheritance showing how positions reproduce across generations. Wealth statistics open the argument and give it scale; they do not complete it, and quoting a top wealth share as though it were itself a class analysis skips the steps on which the conclusion depends.

### Q: Can labour force surveys support Marxist class analysis?

Yes, within limits that should be stated alongside any result. Labour force surveys collect occupation, industry, employment status, hours, workplace size and often qualifications, and a number of them ask about supervisory responsibility, which is enough to build the employment status proxy exactly and a simplified relational schema approximately. Statistical offices themselves build the official classifications from the same raw material. The limits are practical and conceptual: supervisory questions vary in wording between countries and waves, occupation coding changes between revisions and breaks long series, self-employment income is poorly reported, credentials are recorded as qualifications held rather than scarcity in a market, and samples thin quickly once a many-location schema is divided by region or group. Panel surveys add the ability to follow people between locations over time. None of these obstacles is unique to Marxist analysis, but a Marxist analysis must surface its own decisions rather than inherit the survey's defaults silently, because several defaults encode rival theories of what class is.

### Q: Which criteria change the measured size of a class most?

Two criteria dominate all others, and together they form the asset-inclusion rule. The first is authority: whether employees who direct the work of others are counted inside the working class or moved into intermediate or managerial locations changes the totals by the whole supervisory and managerial stratum, which is large in modern economies, and the threshold within authority, between deciding policy and merely relaying instructions, moves them again. The second is credentials: whether scarce certified skills count as a class asset changes the totals by the professional and expert strata. Behind these come the universe decision, employed individuals against all adults or households, and the treatment of the self-employed, the public sector and the retired. Sampling error, by contrast, is usually a minor contributor in the large surveys. This ordering is the practical core of the subject: disputes about class size are won and lost at the criteria, not at the counting, and criticism should be aimed accordingly.

### Q: What should a researcher disclose when publishing a class size estimate?

Everything another researcher would need to rebuild the figure and to move it. Name the schema and its theoretical family, and publish the derivation from raw variables to locations, including the exact questions or codes used for ownership, authority and credentials. State the universe, whether employed individuals, all adults or households, and the treatment of students, the retired, the unemployed and carers. State the asset decisions and their thresholds: who counted as a manager, how credential scarcity was proxied, whether and how the self-employed were split, and how public sector employees were placed. Report the sample's design and any weighting, and, where the data allow, show the headline total under the main alternative decisions so readers can see the sensitivity for themselves. Finally, separate the distribution from claims it cannot carry about exploitation, consciousness or mobility. Disclosure of this completeness is what distinguishes a measurement from a number, and it is the standard the best work in every tradition already meets.

### Q: How do household-level and individual-level measures change class counts?

They change both the totals and the meaning of the categories. An individual measure classifies each person by their own job or status, which leaves non-employed adults to be excluded, classified by a former job, or classified through a partner, each convention producing different class shares from the same population. A household measure assigns one class to a shared living unit, usually from the position judged dominant among its members, which raises the measured size of whichever classes contain the partners whose jobs dominate the convention and hides dual-position households entirely. The retired are the largest single swing group, since counting pensioners by former occupation can restore a substantial working class that an employed-only universe excludes. Wright's distinction between direct locations, held through one's own job, and mediated locations, held through household ties, is the tradition's most useful tool here, because it lets an analysis report both instead of letting a convention choose silently. Any comparison of class figures should first check that the universes match.

### Q: Why does the treatment of managers move the totals of a class schema so much?

Because managers and supervisors are numerous, and because their position is genuinely double in the theory itself. Managerial employees form a large stratum in modern economies, so assigning them wholesale to a middle class, to the working class, or to contradictory locations shifts the headline distribution more than any other single coding decision. The theoretical ground for the sensitivity is Marx's own observation that supervision combines necessary coordination of combined labour with a function of capital, the extraction and direction of others' work, so any schema must decide how much exercised authority over budgets, hiring and discipline makes an employee a bearer of capital's function rather than simply a senior worker. Instruments divide authority into policy-making, sanctioning and merely supervisory levels precisely to control this lever, and careful studies report totals under more than one threshold. When two class figures disagree sharply, the treatment of managers is the first place to look, and a figure that does not state it has withheld its most consequential decision.

### Q: What can census categories never capture about class in Marx's sense?

Census categories record persons and their stated occupations at a moment, and several things central to class in Marx's sense lie outside that form altogether. They cannot capture the firm or the production chain as a unit, so the ownership networks and supply relations through which class power operates remain invisible. They cannot capture surplus or exploitation, since no census asks how much value a worker produced or how much was appropriated, which must be inferred from positions and separate economic data. They cannot capture trajectory and organisation: two identical distributions of positions may belong to populations with entirely different union traditions, memories and capacities for collective action, and that difference is the difference between a class as an aggregate and a class as a historical actor. They cannot capture unpaid reproductive labour, which sustains the workforce outside employment. And their national frame cannot capture relations that run across borders. These are limits of form, not of diligence, and they mark where class measurement ends and the wider class analysis begins.

### Q: How should a journalist report two class surveys that disagree?

By reporting the disagreement as information about method before treating it as information about society. Establish what each survey sorted by, ownership and authority, occupation, or accumulated resources; who each counted, employed people, all adults or households; and how each treated managers, the self-employed and the non-employed, since those decisions usually explain most of the gap between the headlines. Name the schema beside any figure quoted, and resist converting an occupational trend, such as the decline of routine manual jobs, into a claim about a relational class disappearing. Where a study's sample limits its shares, as with self-selected online surveys, carry the qualification in the same sentence as the number. If the studies still disagree after their constructions are aligned, that residue is the story, because it marks a real dispute about which features of a position matter. A report that gives readers the criteria equips them to judge every future class figure they meet, which is worth more than adjudicating one pair of totals.
