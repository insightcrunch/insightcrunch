Lenin and Leninism form the rare examination topic that sits in two schoolrooms at once, examined down one corridor as history and down another as political theory, and the candidate who prepares for only one of the two rooms walks into the other already beaten. In the history paper the subject is a man moving through events: an exile reading newspapers in Zurich library, a sealed train, a government falling twice in a single year, a new state fighting for survival. In the politics paper the same name denotes a body of doctrine: a theory of the party, a theory of imperialism, a theory of the state, each stated in texts that can be quoted, compared and judged. The examiners in the two rooms reward different things, punish different things, and read the same sentences with different eyes. A narrative that earns warm praise from a historian can look like evasion to a theorist, and a conceptual distinction that delights a theorist can strike a historian as bloodless. This guide exists because almost no revision resource admits that the two rooms exist, and so candidates keep carrying history answers into politics questions and theory answers into history questions, losing marks in both directions for a fault that has nothing to do with how much they know.

![Lenin and Leninism exam guide, biography and doctrine in the two-discipline answer matrix - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-73.webp)

The remedy taught throughout this guide is the two-discipline answer. The phrase names a habit rather than a trick: hold the biography and the doctrine apart as separate disciplines, each with its own evidence, its own authorities and its own characteristic errors, and then, inside a single answer, show both at work without confusing them. The biographical discipline asks what the man did, when he did it, under what pressure and with what knowledge available to him at the time. The doctrinal discipline asks what the texts claim, whether the claims cohere, how they relate to Marx, and whether later practice honoured or violated them. A strong examination answer keeps those questions visibly distinct even while it connects them, because the connection is precisely where the analytical marks live. A weak answer melts everything into a single story in which the life explains the ideas and the ideas excuse the life, and markers in both disciplines have been trained to spot that melt and to cap the band it can reach.

Organisation matters as much as separation, and the organising device of this guide is a date. Almost every question set on this subject turns, whether the setter admits it or not, on the transformation that overtook the man and the doctrine in the spring of the revolutionary year, when an exile who had spent decades insisting that Russia must pass through a bourgeois stage arrived in Petrograd and demanded that his bewildered party skip the stage altogether. Before that moment and after it, positions on the party, the soviets, the peasantry, the war and the state itself differ so sharply that an answer which quotes the earlier text against a question about the later period, or the reverse, is answering a question nobody asked. The sections that follow build the whole method out of these two commitments: separation of the disciplines, and dating of every position. Master those, and the facts a candidate already possesses begin to earn the marks they always deserved.

## Lenin and Leninism in two disciplines

The history discipline examines the Russian revolutions as events situated in time, and its currency is evidence handled with discipline. Its questions ask how the Provisional Government fell, why the Bolsheviks prevailed in the civil war that followed, whether the seizure of power in October deserves the name of revolution or of coup, and how far one man's decisions bent outcomes that structures had prepared. Its authorities are historians arguing from archives: Richard Pipes presenting October as a coup carried out by a determined minority, Sheila Fitzpatrick reading the revolution as a social process that overflowed every party's intentions, Orlando Figes tracing the tragedy through the collapse of an old order's legitimacy, Edward Hallett Carr building the documentary record volume by volume. A candidate in this room is rewarded for weighing sources against each other, for knowing what a document can and cannot establish, and for treating causation as a problem rather than a storyline.

The politics discipline examines Leninism as a doctrine situated among doctrines, and its currency is the concept handled with precision. Its questions ask whether the vanguard party amends or abandons democratic centralism as Marx left it, whether the theory of imperialism extends or distorts the analysis of capital, how the account of the state in the writings of the revolutionary summer relates to the state actually constructed afterwards, and whether the road from the founder to his successor was continuity or rupture. Its authorities are the texts themselves and their critics: the pamphlet of 1902 on party organisation, the notebook study of imperialism written in wartime exile, the hurried chapters on the state composed in hiding, and the replies they provoked from Rosa Luxemburg, Karl Kautsky, Julius Martov and Leon Trotsky. A candidate in this room is rewarded for defining terms before deploying them, for distinguishing what a text says from what later regimes claimed it said, and for evaluating a doctrine against its own stated aims as well as against its rivals.

Confusion between the rooms produces a recognisable family of failed answers. The politics candidate who narrates the storming of the Winter Palace at length, in place of analysing the doctrine of the party, has written travel writing where an argument was required. The history candidate who spends three pages on the theory of the dictatorship of the proletariat, in place of weighing the evidence for how power was actually seized, has brought a sermon to an archive fight. Both candidates may know a great deal. Both are marked down, and both leave the hall believing the paper was unfair, when in truth the paper was two papers and they revised for the wrong one. The first service this guide performs is therefore diagnostic: before any content is revised, the candidate learns to identify which room a question comes from, because the room decides what counts as a good sentence.

Identification is easier than candidates fear, because question wording betrays its discipline. A question containing a date range, an event, a government or a named battle belongs to history, however theoretical its phrasing sounds. A question containing a doctrine word, a text title, a comparison with Marx, or the suffix ism belongs to politics, however narrative its examples may be. Some questions straddle the corridor deliberately, and those are the gift questions for a candidate trained in the two-discipline answer, since they reward exactly the candidate who can move between rooms while announcing each move. The straddle questions are also where the costliest confusions occur, because a candidate who drifts between rooms without noticing writes an answer whose evidence and whose argument belong to different examinations.

Each room also keeps its own register of unforgivable errors, and the registers differ in an instructive way. The historian's unforgivable error is the unsourced number, the casualty figure or crowd size asserted from memory, because the discipline's whole claim to rigour rests on the provenance of quantities. The theorist's unforgivable error is the undated text, the quotation deployed as though its author held one position across forty years, because the discipline's whole claim to rigour rests on the development of ideas through time. Notice that the two errors are mirror images: one treats quantities as timeless, the other treats ideas as timeless. The two-discipline answer is, at bottom, a refusal of both timelessnesses. Everything the man wrote has a date, and everything the regime did has a source, and the candidate who supplies date and source as a matter of reflex has internalised the method before writing a word of content.

There is a third presence in both rooms that candidates forget at their cost, namely the successor. Stalin enters history questions as the inheritor of a state and politics questions as the claimant to a doctrine, and in both rooms the relationship between founder and successor is an open question with marks attached to its openness. The candidate who writes as though the succession were a simple continuation has answered the question in the introduction without earning the marks for arguing it, and the candidate who writes as though the succession were a simple betrayal has done the same thing in the opposite direction. What examiners reward is the candidate who treats the relationship as the problem the question says it is, assembling the continuities and the ruptures side by side and letting a judgement emerge from the assembly. That habit, of letting judgement emerge rather than announcing it, recurs throughout this guide because it recurs throughout good examining.

### What is the biggest mistake in exam answers on Lenin?

Treating Leninism and Stalinism as one thing is the biggest mistake, because the relationship between them is itself an examinable question, so assuming the answer forfeits the marks for arguing it. The second commonest error is quoting What Is to Be Done? as the permanent programme of a man who later revised its emphases himself.

The snippet answer above deserves expansion, because both errors are really the same error wearing two costumes. Each treats a development as a constant. The first treats the doctrine of 1902, or the practice of 1918, as identical with the system of the 1930s, erasing two decades of civil war, succession struggle and deliberate reconstruction. The second treats a polemical pamphlet written for a factional fight inside a small exiled movement as a constitution for all seasons, erasing its author's own later description of its formulations as bent too far in one direction to straighten an opposite error. In both cases the candidate has been defeated not by ignorance but by stillness: the material moved and the answer did not. Every technique in this guide, from the dating discipline to the answer matrix, exists to keep the answer moving at the same speed as its subject.

## The hinge and the habit of dating

Every long revolutionary career contains a moment after which the earlier writings must be read differently, and in this career that moment is so sharp that examiners have learned to listen for whether a candidate can hear it. The 1917 hinge is the name this guide gives to the turn: before April of the revolutionary year, the doctrine's Russian application rested on a two stage scheme inherited from the older generation of Russian Marxism, in which a bourgeois democratic revolution would first clear away tsarism, landlordism and the remnants of serfdom, and only after a period of capitalist development could a socialist revolution be placed on the agenda. The formula of the democratic dictatorship of the proletariat and the peasantry, worked out during the revolution of 1905, expressed that scheme exactly: the workers and the peasants together would complete the bourgeois revolution, and the question of socialism belonged to a later act of the drama. Generations of candidates have memorised the formula. Far fewer have registered that its author abandoned it, publicly and angrily, in the middle of the event it was designed to describe.

The abandonment was prepared in exile, and understanding the preparation is what separates an answer that narrates the hinge from an answer that explains it. During the war years in Switzerland, three bodies of work quietly rebuilt the foundations. The study of imperialism argued that capitalism had entered a stage in which the world was divided among monopolies and great powers, that development was uneven by law rather than by accident, and that a chain of imperialist countries could therefore break at a link that was not its most advanced point. The philosophical notebooks on Hegel, kept in the same years, deepened a dialectical impatience with fixed stages and mechanical sequences. The writings on the national question conceded self determination up to secession, a concession that loosened the centralised assumptions of the older movement. None of this yet amounted to a new Russian strategy, but together it dissolved the premises of the old one, so that when the February revolution destroyed the tsarist state and left a dual power of Provisional Government and soviets, the exile reading the news in Zurich was intellectually equipped to draw a conclusion his own party found scandalous.

The April Theses, delivered on his return to Petrograd in April 1917, drew that conclusion in ten short points. No support for the Provisional Government, because the war remained imperialist on its side too. No parliamentary republic as the goal, but a republic of soviets of workers, agricultural labourers and peasants. Confiscation of the landed estates and nationalisation of the land, a single national bank under soviet control, and soviet control of production and distribution as immediate steps. The party's own name was to change, its programme to be revised, its tactics rebuilt around the argument that the bourgeois stage in Russia was already complete because power had in fact passed into hands that could not hold it. The theses were received by many senior Bolsheviks as the ravings of a man out of touch with Russian reality, and the party's Petrograd committee voted them down. Within weeks, after an internal fight conducted in meetings and in the party press, the conference adopted the new line. The episode matters for examinations far beyond its drama, because it is the clearest documented case in the whole subject of a doctrine being changed by argument inside a party, against the resistance of its own leadership cadre, at the moment of supreme opportunity.

### Why does the date of a position change what the position means?

A position in this subject is an answer to a situation, and the situations changed violently. The pamphlet written for an exiled faction, the theses written for a collapsing empire and the decrees written for a besieged government speak in different voices because they faced different problems, so the date tells the reader which problem a sentence was solving.

The dating habit that follows from the hinge is mechanical enough to be learned in an afternoon and valuable enough to transform a grade. Attach a year to every position the moment it enters a notebook: not Marxism in general, not Leninism in the abstract, but the pamphlet of 1902 written for a movement of exiles, the theses of April 1917 written for a revolution in progress, the book on the state written in hiding during the summer of 1917, the pamphlet against Kautsky written in the first winter of the new regime, the trade union argument of the last active winter. An answer built from dated positions cannot commit the timelessness errors, because its own materials resist them. The marker reading such an answer encounters a candidate who appears to think in the same tense as the subject, and markers reward that appearance heavily because it is so rare.

Dating also disciplines the use of the most quoted and most abused text in the whole field. What Is to Be Done?, published in 1902, argued that the working class left to itself develops only trade union consciousness, that socialist consciousness arrives from outside the immediate economic struggle, and that a centralised organisation of professional revolutionaries was the instrument the Russian underground required. For decades, hostile and friendly readers alike treated the pamphlet as the permanent constitution of Leninism. Its author complicated that reading himself, remarking in 1907 that the pamphlet had bent the stick sharply in one direction to correct an opposite bend, and that its formulations belonged to a specific polemic against the economist trend. After the hinge the complication deepens: the man who wrote the pamphlet spent the revolutionary year insisting that the soviets, organs nobody had designed, were the state form of the future, and that the party must win a majority inside them by persuasion rather than substitute itself for them. A candidate who quotes the pamphlet against a question about 1917 without registering any of this has not made a small slip. The candidate has missed the subject.

### Why is the April reversal a theoretical event rather than a change of mood?

Because what changed in April was a stage theory held for decades, namely the claim that Russia had to complete a bourgeois revolution before socialism could be discussed. Abandoning a stage theory rearranged the party's tasks, alliances and attitude to the state at once, which is a theoretical act with consequences, not a temperamental swing in one man's thinking.

The hinge also reframes the succession question, which is another reason examiners value it. If the doctrine of the revolutionary year placed its confidence in soviets as organs of a majority, in the withering of the state on the Commune model, and in the international spread of revolution as the condition of survival, then the system constructed in the following decade, with its single party monopoly, its expanding bureaucracy and its doctrine of socialism in one country, cannot be described as the simple unfolding of the doctrine without argument. Equally, if the practice of the civil war years had already normalised command, requisition and one party rule before the founder's death, then the successor's system cannot be described as a simple betrayal without argument either. The hinge does not settle the continuity question. It arms the candidate to argue it, by fixing the point against which both continuity and rupture must be measured. An answer that can say where the hinge sits, and can test a claim against both sides of it, has already done the analytical work that the top band exists to reward.

## The two-discipline answer matrix

A method that cannot be consulted under pressure will not survive contact with an examination hall, so the two-discipline answer is compressed here into a single findable artifact. The two-discipline answer matrix sets the five question types that between them cover nearly everything setters ask on this subject against the four things a candidate must decide before writing: which discipline is asking, what content leads, which evaluation lifts the answer into the top band, and which trap waits inside the question for the unprepared. The matrix is a planning instrument, not a script. Its rows are read across, in the two minutes a wise candidate spends planning before the pen moves, and its last column deserves the most attention, because examinations are lost to traps far more often than they are lost to missing knowledge.

| Question type | Discipline asking | Content to lead with | Evaluation that lifts the band | Trap inside the question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Explain Leninism as a development of Marxism | Politics and theory | The dated texts in sequence, from the party pamphlet to the wartime study of imperialism | Weigh the founder against Marx on the state and the peasantry, then judge continuity point by point | Quoting the pamphlet of the exile years as a lifelong programme |
| Assess whether the October seizure of power was a coup | History | The evidence for soviet majorities and garrison politics in the capital during the autumn weeks | Set the coup reading of Pipes against the social revolution reading of Fitzpatrick and judge the evidence for each | Treating the question as settled in either direction and arguing only one camp |
| Evaluate the vanguard party | Politics and theory | The conditions clause: underground, illegality and a movement in formation, stated before the model itself | Bring in the critique Luxemburg wrote while the model was new, and the founder's own later bend of the stick | Describing a party of a new type as though the phrase were his own doctrinal term |
| Assess the theory of imperialism | Politics and theory | The five features and the argument about uneven development, tied to the war that produced the book | Test the theory against Kautsky and against the survival of capitalism it failed to schedule | Reciting the features as a list detached from the strategic conclusion they were built to carry |
| Discuss whether the founder led to his successor | Both disciplines | The hinge: doctrine and practice on either side of the revolutionary spring, measured separately | Assemble continuities and ruptures in parallel columns and let the judgement emerge from the assembly | Announcing the verdict in the introduction and spending the essay defending the announcement |

Reading the matrix downwards teaches a lesson that reading it across can miss. The trap column, taken as a whole, describes a single candidate: one who treats the subject as settled, static and singular, who has a verdict ready before the question is read and a quotation ready before the period is fixed. Every other column is a defence against that candidate's habits. The discipline column forces the room to be identified. The content column forces the period to be fixed. The evaluation column forces a named opponent into the answer, because an evaluation without an opponent is merely an opinion with footnotes. Candidates who revise with the matrix open beside their notes find that their practice essays reorganise themselves: the openings shorten, the dates multiply, the critics acquire names, and the verdicts migrate to the ends of essays where they can be afforded.

### What does a history marker reward that a politics marker does not?

A history marker rewards the weighing of evidence whose provenance has been stated, the document tested against the archive and the witness tested against an interest. A politics marker rewards the definition tested against a rival definition. The first asks where a claim comes from; the second asks what a claim excludes, and answers know which question is being asked.

One further use of the matrix deserves emphasis, because it concerns the straddle questions that setters love and candidates dread. When a question asks, for example, how far the theory of the party explains the outcome of the revolution, both columns of the matrix are in play at once, and the answer must run on twin tracks: a doctrinal track stating the model with its conditions and its date, and an evidential track testing the model against the garrison votes, the soviet congresses and the provincial reports. The twin track answer is not two half answers stitched together. Its analytical force comes from the junctions, the sentences where the candidate states plainly whether the evidence confirms the doctrine, embarrasses it, or shows the doctrine being improvised under pressure. Three or four such junction sentences, honestly written, will lift an answer further than three additional pages of content in either discipline alone.

## Writing the biography answer

Biography earns marks in examinations only when it is deployed as evidence, and the full life is best learned through the companion study of [the intellectual biography of Lenin](/2014/03/01/lenin-intellectual-biography/), which traces the formation of the mind in the detail the subject deserves. What the examination candidate needs from the life is narrower and more functional: a sequence of episodes, each attached to the argumentative work it can do inside an answer. An episode that cannot change an argument is decoration, and decoration is the first thing a marker's pen learns to skip. The episodes that follow are the load bearing ones, presented in the order a candidate should hold them, with the use of each stated as plainly as the fact.

The formation years supply the answer to any question about origins. Born in Simbirsk in 1870 into a family of the educated service class, Vladimir Ulyanov lost his father while still a schoolboy and then, in 1887, saw his elder brother Alexander hanged for a plot against the tsar. The biographical tradition has sometimes turned the brother's execution into a single key that unlocks the whole life, and candidates should handle that key with suspicion, because the scholarship treats it more cautiously: the episode marked the family, closed respectable careers to the younger brother after his own expulsion from Kazan University for student protest, and pushed him towards the revolutionary circles he then entered by study as much as by grief. The Samara and St Petersburg years gave him the lawyer's training he barely practised, the Marxist reading he pursued with ferocious system, and the first arrest and Siberian exile that completed his education in the underground's realities. Deployed properly, the formation years let a candidate discuss radicalisation as a documented process rather than a legend, and to test claims about the man's later ruthlessness or patience against the record of how slowly his own mind was made.

The exile decades supply the answer to questions about the doctrine's birthplace. From the founding of the newspaper Iskra in 1900, through the second congress of 1903 at which the party split into Bolshevik and Menshevik wings over a clause of the membership rules, the life was lived in emigre rooms in Munich, London, Geneva, Krakow and Zurich, in poverty, polemic and print. Candidates often apologise for the sheer disproportion between the smallness of that world and the size of its later consequences, and the apology wastes words. The disproportion is the point an answer should make: the doctrine of the party was designed by men whose entire experience of politics was clandestine publication and factional congress, which explains both its genius for survival and its blindnesses about open politics, and a candidate who states that connection has converted biography into analysis in a single move. The 1905 revolution interrupted the exile and educated its subject in soviets, insurrection and defeat, and the long reaction afterwards returned him to the emigre rooms with the lessons filed rather than forgotten.

The revolutionary year converts the exile into a head of government, and it is the year in which biography and doctrine collide most violently, which is why the hinge was built from it. The sealed train across Germany, arranged with the knowledge of the German authorities who hoped the returning exiles would rot the Russian war effort, is an episode candidates must handle with evidential care: the arrangement is documented, the purposes of each side are documented, and the leap from arrangement to agency is a leap the evidence does not support and markers penalise when it is made carelessly. The April Theses followed within days of arrival. The July Days nearly destroyed the party, drove its leader into hiding in Finland, and produced, from that hiding, the book on the state that stated the doctrine's most utopian commitments weeks before its author had to govern. October placed him at the head of a government that almost nobody, including most of his own central committee, had expected to survive the winter. Each of these episodes carries a double use, biographical and doctrinal at once, and the two-discipline answer is precisely the technique of stating both uses without letting either swallow the other.

The governing years supply the record questions, and they are reserved for their own section below because examinations treat them with a special discipline. For the biography answer, their outline suffices: a capital moved to Moscow under the threat of German advance, a separate peace signed at Brest Litovsk at a staggering territorial price, a constituent assembly dispersed, a civil war fought against White armies and foreign intervention, a famine of enormous scale, a mutiny at the Kronstadt naval base suppressed by the government it had once defended, a retreat into the New Economic Policy, and a series of strokes that removed the founder from power while his lieutenants manoeuvred around his sickroom. The final documents, dictated in the winter of 1922 and 1923 and known as the Testament, warned the party about the danger of a split and about the character of its general secretary, and were suppressed by the leadership that inherited them. A candidate who can place the Testament accurately, neither inflating it into a deathbed conversion nor dismissing it as a forgery, demonstrates the evidential temper that history markers exist to reward.

### How much biography belongs in a theory answer?

Enough to date and locate the doctrine, and no more. A politics answer needs the exile room, the war and the hiding place as coordinates for the texts, because doctrines written under illegality carry illegality inside their assumptions. It does not need the childhood, the brother or the train, whose charm is what makes them expensive in a theory paper.

Two symmetrical faults govern the biography answer, and naming them helps candidates avoid both. The first is the cradle to grave recital, in which the candidate retells the life in order and hopes the question will eventually be met by some part of the story, a technique that spends the examination's scarcest resource, which is time, on the material least likely to be rewarded. The second is the anecdote answer, built from vivid episodes, the train, the balcony speeches, the assassination attempt by Fanny Kaplan in August 1918 that wounded him and helped ignite the terror that followed, arranged for colour rather than for argument. Episodes earn their place by the inference they support. The Kaplan shooting supports an inference about the escalation of repression in a besieged state, contested as that inference remains, and on those terms it belongs in an answer. Used as a dramatic pause, it belongs in a novel. Markers can tell the difference in a paragraph, and so, with practice, can candidates.

## Writing the doctrine answer

Doctrine answers fail in the opposite way to biography answers: not by recital but by suspension, the doctrine presented as a system floating above its occasions, each tenet stated in the present tense as though it had been held unchanged from the first pamphlet to the last decree. The corrective is the dating discipline applied tenet by tenet, and the reward is considerable, because a doctrine presented as a development can be evaluated as a development, which is the operation the politics paper exists to test. The question of whether the development continued Marx or broke with him is itself a set question in this field, treated at length in the companion examination of [whether Leninism broke with Marx](/2014/05/15/was-leninism-a-break-with-marx/), and the candidate who has absorbed that treatment will find the tenets below arranging themselves into an argument rather than a catalogue.

The party tenet comes first because the whole architecture leans on it. Stated with its date, it runs as follows: in a country where open politics was a police matter, the movement required an organisation of professional revolutionaries, centralised, disciplined and capable of surviving illegality, able to bring political consciousness to a working class whose spontaneous struggles reached only the level of trade union demands. Stated with its conditions, the tenet is an argument about a situation, open to challenge on its facts and its logic alike. Stated without them, it becomes the caricature that critics and defenders share, a blueprint for dictatorship drawn up in advance. The fullest treatment of the model and its mutations is reserved to the companion study of [the vanguard party explained](/2014/04/01/vanguard-party-explained/), and the examination candidate needs from it chiefly the habit of asking, whenever the party is mentioned, which party at which date is meant: the faction of exiles, the mass party of the revolutionary year that grew by hundreds of thousands, or the ruling apparatus of the civil war's aftermath, three organisations sharing a name and a lineage and very little else.

### How should an answer begin when the question concerns the vanguard party?

Begin with the conditions clause: a small movement, working illegally under an autocracy, in formation rather than in power. Stating the conditions first prevents the model from floating free of its setting, and it prepares the evaluation, because most criticisms of the model are criticisms of what happened when the conditions vanished and the model remained.

The imperialism tenet carries the doctrine from organisation to world economy. Written in Zurich in 1916 and published in the revolutionary year, the study of imperialism as the highest stage of capitalism identified five features, the concentration of production into monopolies, the fusion of bank and industrial capital, the export of capital, the division of the world among international combines and the completed territorial partition of the globe among the great powers. A doctrine answer that recites the features and stops has performed the recital fault in its purest form, because the book was written as an argument with a strategic conclusion: uneven development under imperialism made a victory of socialism possible first in one country or a few, breaking the expectation that the most advanced economies would move first. That conclusion is the tenet's payload, and it is also the point where evaluation properly begins, since Karl Kautsky's rival account of ultra imperialism, and the later record of a capitalism that survived its supposed final stage, both press on exactly the joint where the theory meets its forecast.

The state tenet is the hinge tenet, the one whose two sides differ most dramatically. Before the revolutionary year, the doctrine's state thinking ran inside the inherited stage scheme, concerned with the democratic republic and the revolutionary government that would complete the bourgeois transformation. In the summer of 1917, in hiding and in argument with his own tradition, its author produced in The State and Revolution the most uncompromising statement of the Marxist theory of the state ever written by a man about to hold power: the bureaucratic and military machine of the old order was not to be laid hold of but smashed, and replaced by a Commune type order of elected and recallable officials paid workmen's wages, an armed people, and a state already beginning to wither because its functions were being absorbed by the population itself. A doctrine answer that quotes the summer book against the practice of the following winter without marking the collision has failed the dating discipline at its most consequential point. A doctrine answer that marks the collision, and then asks whether war, collapse and invasion explain it or merely excuse it, is doing the work the top band describes.

Three further tenets complete the standard answer, each with its own dating trap. The peasant tenet moves from the alliance formula of the earlier period, in which the peasantry figures as the proletariat's partner in completing the bourgeois revolution, to the revolutionary year's demand for the land to pass to those who worked it, to the governing years' oscillation between requisition and tax in kind, a trajectory that no single sentence about the peasantry can honestly summarise. The national tenet concedes the right of nations to self determination up to secession, a position defended against Luxemburg's famous dissent and against the centralisers of the author's own movement, and later tested against the federal structure actually built. The soviet tenet is the hinge's institutional residue: organs discovered by the workers in 1905, theorised in the revolutionary spring as the state form, and progressively subordinated in the governing years to the party that had championed them. A candidate who can date all three trajectories, and say plainly where each ended relative to where it began, possesses the doctrinal skeleton entire.

One correction belongs in every doctrine answer, because examiners watch for it as a marker of whether the candidate has read the texts or merely the summaries. The ringing formula of a party of a new type, which generations of textbooks place at the centre of the doctrine, belongs to the later codification of Leninism rather than to the pamphlets themselves, whose language is of organisation, centralism and consciousness argued in the heat of specific polemics. Similarly the very word Leninism, as the name of a finished system, was assembled after the founder's death, in the succession struggle, by men who needed a system to inherit. None of this diminishes the doctrine's coherence as an object of study. It reminds the candidate that the object has edges in time, and that an answer respecting those edges will always outscore an answer that treats the system as eternal.

## The record questions

Three episodes from the governing years carry a charge in examinations that no other episodes match, and candidates sense the charge and mishandle it in one of two directions. Some reach for the language of the prosecution, writing of the terror, the dispersal of the Constituent Assembly and the ban on factions in verdicts borrowed from the regime's enemies. Others reach for the language of the defence, explaining each episode as necessity and moving on before the evidence has been heard. Both directions fail for the same reason: an examination is not a tribunal and not a rally, and the marker is assessing the handling of a contested record, not recruiting the candidate to a side. The discipline taught in this section is therefore uniform across the three episodes. State the record as the scholarship presents it, attribute every contested element to the historians who contest it, steelman the strongest reading on each side, and let any judgement be the last thing the answer does rather than the first.

The terror first, because it is the gravest matter in the cluster and the one where slogans do the most damage. The institutional record is not in dispute: a political police, the Cheka, was founded in December 1917 under Felix Dzerzhinsky, weeks after the seizure of power and before the civil war had properly begun; hostage taking and summary punishment appeared in its practice during the following year; and in September 1918, after the assassination of the Petrograd Cheka chief Uritsky and the wounding of the head of government in the Kaplan attempt, a Red Terror was formally decreed. What the scholarship disputes is scale, systematisation and weight in the regime's character. George Leggett's study of the Cheka documented an apparatus that grew with frightening speed while remaining chaotic, under centralised in some respects and wildly local in others. Other historians have emphasised the reactive spiral of a government facing assassination, invasion and collapse; others again, the ideological readiness to classify whole categories of people as enemies. Estimates of the terror's victims produced by serious scholars diverge so widely that the divergence itself is the examinable fact, and the candidate's duty is to report the range as a disagreement among named historians, never to select the figure that suits a verdict and present it as the count.

### What belongs in an answer about the terror?

The record as the scholarship presents it, with attribution: the decrees, the founding of the Cheka, the escalation after the attempts on the leadership, and the disagreement among historians about scale and intention. A candidate reports who argues what, distinguishes decree from practice and capital from provinces, and keeps unsourced numbers out of the answer entirely.

The dispersal of the Constituent Assembly tests a different skill, because the episode is short, symbolically enormous and documented in unusual detail. The elections of November 1917, held under arrangements the Bolsheviks had inherited and honoured, returned a Socialist Revolutionary majority, with the Bolsheviks taking roughly a quarter of the vote. The assembly met in January 1918, refused to endorse the soviet government's declaration, and was closed after a single sitting, the sailor commanding the guard announcing that the guard was tired, an anecdote recorded by participants and repeated by every historian of the episode since. It never met again. The two honest framings are these. On one reading, the dispersal buried the one all Russian democratic mandate of the revolutionary period, and the road from that night to one party rule is the central story of the regime. On another reading, pressed by the government at the time and by some historians since, the assembly's composition lagged a revolution that had moved past it, the soviets embodied a more direct democracy of workers and peasants, and the assembly's majority party had split in ways that made its mandate ambiguous. A strong answer sets both framings out with their evidence, notes what each fails to explain, and observes that the episode's examinable core is not which framing a candidate prefers but whether the candidate knows both are held by serious scholars and why.

### How should the dispersal of the Constituent Assembly be handled in an essay?

As a dated event with two honest framings placed side by side. The assembly, elected after the seizure of power, met for a single day and was closed by the guard; one framing reads the act as the burial of a democratic mandate, the other as the replacement of a parliament by soviet power claiming a deeper one.

The ban on factions is the quietest of the three episodes and in some ways the most consequential for the doctrine's later history, which is why politics papers love it. At the Tenth Party Congress in March 1921, meeting as the Kronstadt rising was being suppressed across the ice, the leadership carried a resolution on party unity that ordered the dissolution of organised factions inside the party, with a clause, initially unpublished, permitting the expulsion even of central committee members who defied it. The resolution was presented as an emergency discipline for a party governing a ruined and hostile country, and its movers spoke the language of temporary necessity. It was never lifted. In the succession struggle that followed the founder's withdrawal, the ban became the instrument by which each successive opposition was defined as illegitimate in advance, and historians of the doctrine have argued ever since over the weight the episode bears: a wartime expedient whose permanence was the successor's choice, or the logical terminus of a model that had always treated organised dissent as a security problem. The full prosecution and defence of the doctrine, across all three episodes and beyond them, are assembled in the companion study of [the case against Leninism](/2014/06/15/case-against-leninism/), which candidates should raid for evaluation material while keeping the present discipline of attribution firmly in place.

### What does the ban on factions actually test in an essay?

It tests whether a candidate can hold emergency and permanence apart. The ban was carried at the congress that coincided with Kronstadt, presented by its movers as a temporary discipline for a besieged party, and then retained as a permanent instrument of rule. An answer that dates both moments, and names the gap between them, passes the test.

A closing word on tone, because the record questions provoke it. Candidates sometimes write these passages in a register of outrage or of reverence, apparently believing that the strength of a feeling certifies the strength of an answer. Markers are unmoved by both registers, and for a professional reason: feeling is unmarkable, while attribution, dating and the fair statement of an opposing case are markable in every sentence. The candidate who writes about the terror with the same disciplined calm used for the land decree is not displaying indifference. That candidate is displaying the one quality the examination can actually reward, which is command of the record. Command, in these questions, includes the candour to say where the record is worst, where it is contested, and where the sources themselves were produced by the victors. Nothing in the discipline taught here requires a candidate to soften a single documented fact. It requires the candidate to know, and to show, whose fact it is.

## The architecture of a model answer

Method becomes marks only when it hardens into architecture, a repeatable shape the candidate can assemble under time pressure for any question in the field. The shape taught here has four storeys. The opening fixes the discipline and the date, in two or three sentences that tell the marker which room the answer is written for and on which side of the hinge its material stands. The body runs the content in dated sequence, doctrine and evidence on their separate tracks, with junction sentences where the tracks meet. The evaluation introduces a named opponent and gives that opponent the strongest form of the objection before replying to it. The judgement closes the answer, and it is the only storey that cannot be moved: a judgement delivered in the opening is a hostage, defended for three pages at the cost of every analytical move the question was designed to elicit.

The opening deserves a worked example because openings are where the two-discipline answer announces itself. Compare two beginnings to a question on the party. The first begins with a definition of the vanguard party as a disciplined organisation of professional revolutionaries that leads the working class to power, and proceeds as though the definition settled something. The second begins by observing that the party model was stated in a pamphlet of the exile years for an illegal movement, was transformed in the revolutionary year when the party grew into a mass organisation competing for soviet majorities, and was transformed again when it became the sole party of a state, and that the question must therefore be answered for a dated party or not answered at all. The second opening is not longer by much. It is better by bands, because it has already performed the analysis the question exists to reward, and the marker knows by the third sentence that the answer understands its subject as a development rather than a monument.

The evaluation storey has an anatomy of its own, and its most reliable muscle is the named critic. An evaluation that reports that some critics object, or that the doctrine has been criticised, earns the mark reserved for gesture. An evaluation that names Rosa Luxemburg and states her objection, that the centralism of the exile model substituted the party for the class and would end by substituting the central committee for the party, has given the marker something assessable: a real argument, attributable, dated, and open to reply. The founder's own reply exists, his counter charge that his critic confused the discipline of a besieged underground with the discipline of a mass movement, and a candidate who stages the exchange has built an evaluation out of the period's own materials. The same anatomy serves every question in the field. Kautsky on democracy and dictatorship, Martov on the party and the class, Plekhanov on the prematurity of the whole Russian enterprise, Trotsky, who managed to be first a devastating critic of the organisational model and then its most gifted defender, each supplies an evaluation with a name, a date and a text.

### Why do contemporaneous critics earn more than later ones?

Because a contemporary critic argues with the doctrine inside its own moment, before the outcomes are known, and so tests the ideas rather than the legend. Luxemburg on organisation and Kautsky on democracy wrote without knowing how the story ended, which makes their objections evidence about the doctrine itself, not commentary on its reputation.

Beneath the architecture sits the question candidates actually ask in corridors, and this guide answers it head on: whether taking a critical line risks marks with a sympathetic marker, or a favourable line with a hostile one. Both fears misunderstand how marking works. Scripts are assessed against published criteria that reward evidence, dating, attribution and argued judgement, and markers are moderated precisely so that agreement with a candidate's conclusion cannot be the currency. A candidate concluding against the founder, with the record handled as this guide teaches, will outscore a candidate concluding for him with the record handled loosely, and the reverse holds with the valences exchanged. The model answers implied by this guide are therefore constructed so that a conclusion on either side can earn full marks, and that construction is deliberate: the method is the position. What a candidate must never do is hide the position being taken, because an answer whose verdict has to be excavated reads as an answer that has not reached one.

### When does a question want a survey rather than a judgement?

The tell sits in the command words and the marking tradition behind them. Discuss, examine and outline the interpretations invite a mapped survey with the schools named and compared. Assess, evaluate and how far demand a weighed judgement by the end. Candidates who read the verb before the topic choose the right architecture before writing a line.

Two practical corollaries complete the architecture. The first concerns length allocation inside the answer: content should occupy the body in proportion to its dated importance, not in proportion to the order in which it was revised or how vividly it is remembered, and the evaluation storey should never be shorter than a fifth of the whole, since a fifth is roughly the share of the marks it carries. The second concerns the final paragraph, which weak candidates spend on summary. Summary earns nothing that the answer has not already earned. The final paragraph is for the judgement, defended in two sentences, qualified in one, and left standing. A candidate who ends an answer still introducing material has announced that the plan failed, and markers, who are readers before they are judges, believe such announcements.

## The coup question and the historians

Of all the history questions in the field, the one that asks whether October was a coup or a revolution carries the richest historiography and the cruellest traps, and it receives a section to itself. The full scholarly treatment of the seizure of power and its Marxist readings is reserved to the companion analysis of [the October Revolution in Marxist analysis](/2014/05/01/october-revolution-marxist-analysis/), and the candidate's task here is narrower: to learn how the debate is structured, which names anchor which camp, and what evidence each camp must answer. A candidate who can reproduce that structure can walk into any variant of the question, however it is phrased, and begin writing within a minute.

The coup reading, in its strongest scholarly form, is associated above all with Richard Pipes, who presented the October seizure as a coup d'etat executed by a disciplined minority exploiting the collapse of authority, and who read the regime that followed as the working out of that original illegitimacy. The reading rests on evidence no answer may dodge: the seizure was planned by a party committee and executed by the Military Revolutionary Committee of the Petrograd Soviet in the name of a congress that had not yet met; the capital's transfer of power preceded any national ratification; and the government's fall was accomplished in the capital with a small number of armed actions against a ministry that had already lost its garrison. The social revolution reading, associated with Sheila Fitzpatrick and with the revisionist generation that followed the opening of the archives, replies that a coup analysis mistakes the mechanism of the seizure for the character of the process: through the autumn, factory committees, soldiers committees and soviets across the country were moving towards the slogans of the party that seized power, the Second Congress of Soviets ratified the transfer with a majority, and the subsequent spread of soviet power through the provinces, piecemeal and often locally driven, is not the footprint of a mere putsch. Alexander Rabinowitch, whose study of the seizure remains the most densely evidenced in any camp, complicates both readings usefully, portraying a party far less monolithic than the coup school requires and a capital far more radicalised than the conspiracy model allows.

### How should an answer treat the word coup in its opening paragraph?

Treat it as a claim to be tested, not a label to be accepted or refused. The opening should register what the word asserts, a seizure by a minority without a mandate, and what its rival asserts, a transfer of power ratified by soviet majorities, then promise the evidence by which the answer will decide between them.

The candidate's handling of the camps should observe three rules. First, name historians with their works and their evidence, not as decorative authorities: Pipes argues from the intentions and organisation of the party leadership, Fitzpatrick from social processes at the base of society, Rabinowitch from the day by day record of the capital, and the older documentary labours of E. H. Carr underpin the narrative scaffolding on which all three camps build. Second, give each camp the evidence it is strongest on before testing it with the evidence it prefers to omit: the coup reading must meet the soviet majorities and the provincial spread, the revolution reading must meet the committee planning and the dispersal that followed within months. Third, remember that the question is a judgement question in most of its phrasings, so the survey of camps is the middle of the answer and not its end. The candidate's own weighing, conducted on the evidence rehearsed, is where the band is decided.

The same three rules serve the other historiographical questions in the field, on the civil war's outcome, on the New Economic Policy and on the succession, because the camps recur with variations. The older totalitarian model read the regime backward from its later form and found the whole story present in embryo in the doctrine and the seizure. The revisionists read it forward from the society, finding improvisation, chaos and social forces that no leadership controlled. A post revisionist generation has worked to hold intention and circumstance in one account, restoring ideology and leadership choice to the story without surrendering the social depths the revisionists mapped. A candidate need not adopt the vocabulary of schools to profit from the map. What the marker rewards is the visible act of placing an interpretation, saying whose it is, and testing it against a rival, and that act can be performed in plain sentences by any candidate who has learned the names in this section and the evidence each name carries.

## The texts to cite and the quotations that work

A doctrine answer cites texts the way a history answer cites documents, as dated evidence with a provenance, and the candidate's working library for this subject is smaller than the Collected Works suggest. Eight texts carry nearly every examination use. The pamphlet of 1902 carries the party model and the consciousness argument. The Two Tactics of 1905 carries the stage scheme and the alliance formula in their classical form. The wartime study of imperialism carries the world economy and the strategic conclusion drawn from it. The April Theses carry the hinge itself, ten points that can be summarised in a sentence each. The State and Revolution carries the Commune model and the theory of the state's withering. The polemic against Kautsky of 1918 carries the defence of the dictatorship of the proletariat as the regime understood it. The pamphlet on left wing communism of 1920 carries the mature tactical teaching, compromise, manoeuvre and the critique of revolutionary impatience, which candidates underuse because it complicates the caricature of its author as a mere maximalist. The dictated letters of the final winter carry the Testament and the succession question. A candidate who can attach one precise claim to each of these eight, with its date, owns the citation apparatus of the whole topic.

Citation technique matters as much as selection. The strong form names the text, gives its date or its moment, and states the claim in the candidate's own words before any quotation is risked, so that the quotation confirms an argument already made instead of substituting for one. The weak form strings quotations together as a garland, each expected to glow on its own, and markers read garlands the way jewellers read paste. One quotation per text in a single answer is a sound ration, and the quotation should be the sentence in which the text's distinctive move is concentrated. For the pamphlet of 1902, that sentence is the claim that class political consciousness can be brought to the workers 'only from without' the economic struggle, the hinge of the whole organisational argument. For the summer book on the state, the concentrated sentence is the bleak chiasmus of its first chapter, 'So long as the state exists there is no freedom', which a candidate can then set against the apparatus the following years constructed. For the April moment, the slogan itself, 'All power to the soviets', carries the reversal in five words. For the evaluation storey, Luxemburg's sentence from her manuscript on the revolution, that freedom is 'always the freedom of the one who thinks differently', gives the critique of the regime's political order its most quoted and most durable form.

Quotation carries risks that this guide states plainly, because they are the risks candidates actually run. A quotation remembered wrongly is worse than a paraphrase remembered rightly, since the paraphrase risks only vagueness while the misquotation advertises carelessness in the candidate's strongest suit. Quotations migrate in textbooks and on posters into forms their authors never wrote, and the famous lines attached to this subject have suffered that migration heavily; a candidate who is not certain of a wording should paraphrase and attribute the thought, which no marker penalises. Quotations in translation add a further caution, since renderings differ between editions, and the safe practice is to quote the shortest form that carries the point. Above all, a quotation is evidence about what a text says, not evidence about what happened, and the candidate who deploys a soaring sentence from the summer of 1917 as proof of the character of the government of 1921 has committed the timelessness error in its most seductive costume.

## Figures without figures

This guide now gives the instruction that its readers most often write to query, and it gives it without qualification: no model answer in this field should contain an unsourced number for the dead, the executed, the starved or the imprisoned. The instruction is not squeamishness. It is a description of how history marking works. A marker who encounters a confident figure for the victims of the terror, or of the famine of the early nineteen twenties, or of the civil war as a whole, and who knows the scholarly literature, knows that the figure has a provenance, that the provenance is a particular historian working from particular records with particular definitions, and that the candidate has almost certainly lifted the number without the apparatus that makes it meaningful. The figure then damages the whole answer, because it certifies that its author's quantities are folklore. In a subject where the numbers are genuinely and deeply contested, where estimates by serious scholars can differ by multiples, the sourced range is the only honest unit, and examiners have arranged their criteria to make it the rewarded one as well.

Three techniques carry the load that bare figures cannot. The first is the attributed range, in which the candidate states that estimates of the terror's executions produced by historians working from the archives differ widely, names the direction of the disagreement, and declines to pretend a precision the scholarship does not possess. The second is qualitative scale done rigorously, in which words such as mass, systematic and by category are used as the precise terms they are, each tied to a documented practice, the hostage decrees, the category based arrests, the camp system whose existence and growth are recorded even where its toll is argued over. The third is the pivot to significance, in which the candidate observes that the historiographical argument turns less on the disputed totals than on questions the evidence can settle: whether repression was central or local in initiative, whether it preceded or followed the regime's emergencies, whether its categories widened over time. Those questions are arguable from documents, and an answer that argues them reads as more serious, because it is more serious, than an answer trading in magnitudes nobody can verify.

The protocol extends beyond the terror to every quantity in the field. Crowd sizes at demonstrations, party membership figures, the vote shares of the assembly elections, the territorial losses of the Brest Litovsk treaty and the harvest figures of the famine years all have provenances, and the candidate's reflex should be uniform: attribute, or describe, or omit. There is one honourable exception, which is the figure whose provenance is the point, as when an answer discusses how the regime's own statistical apparatus, or the succession struggle's polemics, manufactured numbers for use as weapons. There the number is evidence about its manufacturer, and it should be quoted as such, in the way a forged document is quoted, with its character stated. Even in that case the candidate's own voice asserts no total. The habit will feel like a restraint for exactly one practice essay. After that it feels like what it is, which is the professional standard of the discipline the candidate is hoping to join.

## Five positions across the hinge

The dating discipline becomes real when it is applied position by position, and this section performs the application for the five positions that examinations return to most often. In each case the pattern is the same: the position on the near side of the hinge, the position on the far side, and the specific error a candidate commits by confusing them. A candidate who masters these five crossings possesses the hinge not as a slogan but as an instrument, and will find that most questions in the field are one of the five crossings wearing a question's clothing.

The party position crosses first and most famously. On the near side stands the organisation of the exile years: small, centralised, built around professional revolutionaries, designed to survive the police and to carry consciousness into a movement that could not yet generate the finished doctrine from its own struggles. On the far side stands the party of the revolutionary year: swollen by an enormous influx of workers and soldiers, arguing with itself in public, its leader obliged to fight his own central committee over the April line and again over the timing of the insurrection, and winning majorities in the soviets by persuasion because no other instrument was available. The error is to judge the far side party by the near side blueprint, or to excuse the later apparatus by the necessities of the underground. Each configuration answered the conditions of its moment, and the examination rewards the candidate who names the conditions before judging the configuration.

The soviet position crosses in the opposite emotional direction, from theory to elegy. On the near side, the soviets are an inherited memory of 1905, organs the workers invented without permission, valued by the doctrine's author well below the party in his scheme of instruments. On the far side of the April turn, the soviets become the state form itself, the Commune's Russian descendant, the bodies through which the dictatorship of the proletariat was to be exercised and the state was to begin its withering. Then the governing years bend the position a third time, as the soviets hollow into transmission belts of a power located elsewhere, a transformation the doctrine's author registered in his final writings with visible alarm about the apparatus he had built. The error is to quote the middle configuration, the soviets of the revolutionary spring, as the description of the regime at any other date, whether to credit the regime with a democracy it had ceased to practise or to convict the doctrine of a hypocrisy that the timeline refines into tragedy.

The war position crosses with the fate of an empire. On the near side stands revolutionary defeatism: the argument, maintained through the world war against the patriotic majorities of European socialism, that the defeat of one's own government was the lesser evil and that the imperialist war must be turned into civil war. On the far side stand the decrees of a government: peace proclaimed, then the treaty of Brest Litovsk signed at a price in land, people and resources that horrified the signer's own party, then, after the treaty's collapse, a revolutionary state defending itself with a regular army, conscription and officers of the old regime. The error is to treat the slogan of the exile years and the practice of the governing years as a single position called his view of war, when the distance between converting a war and surviving one is precisely the distance the examination wants measured.

The peasant position crosses on the question of land. On the near side, the doctrine schedules the peasantry as the ally of the first stage, the partner in a democratic dictatorship that would clear the countryside of feudal remnants while leaving the socialist transformation of agriculture to a distant future. On the far side, the revolutionary year shortens the schedule drastically: the land decree of the first days adopted, nearly whole, the programme of the peasant mandates compiled by a rival party, putting confiscation and local redistribution into law at a stroke. The governing years then oscillate, requisition squads in the famine of the civil war, a fixed tax in kind under the retreat of the New Economic Policy, each swing argued by its author as the price of the alliance on which the regime's survival depended. The error is to write of his peasant policy in the singular. There were three, and their sequence is the biography of the alliance itself.

The state position crosses furthest of all, and it is the crossing the succession question is built from. On the near side stands the most libertarian text in the Marxist canon by a major leader: the machine smashed, the officials elected and recallable, the standing army replaced by the armed people, the state withering from its first day because administration was becoming everyone's business. On the far side stands the practice of the governing years and, beside it, its author's own late reckoning: the letters of the final winter complaining that the apparatus had been borrowed from the old regime and only lightly repainted, that the machinery of state answered to its own habits, that the party's general secretary had accumulated an authority the founder now feared. The error is double headed. One head quotes the summer book as the regime's description and convicts its author of simple fraud. The other head treats the book as a youthful fancy its author discarded, when the late letters show him still measuring the regime against its standard and finding the distance alarming. An answer that holds the book, the practice and the late measurement in one frame has understood the subject as its examiners understand it.

## The questions setters set, worked

General method earns its keep on particular questions, and this section walks through the five question shapes that recur across the history and politics papers, showing for each one where the two-discipline answer takes hold. The treatments are deliberately given as architecture and material rather than as model prose, because an answer memorised is an answer wasted: setters rotate their phrasings precisely to defeat the memorised essay, while the architecture transfers to every phrasing unchanged.

The continuity question asks, in politics papers, whether the doctrine continued Marx or broke with him, and it is the purest theory question in the field. Its architecture is point by point comparison, never global assertion. The candidate selects four grounds of comparison, the party, the state, the peasantry and the timetable of revolution, and on each ground performs the same operation: state Marx's position from its text, state the doctrine's position from its dated text, and characterise the relation in a precise verb, continued, extended, revised or reversed. The four verdicts will rarely agree, and their disagreement is the answer's distinction, since a doctrine that reversed Marx on the timetable while extending him on the state and revising him on the peasantry cannot be captured by a single adjective, and the candidate who demonstrates that incapacity has answered at the level the question secretly demands. Evaluation enters through the contemporaries who pressed each ground at the time: Plekhanov on the timetable, Luxemburg on organisation and spontaneity, Kautsky on the state. The judgement then weighs the four grounds by their importance to the whole, and defends the weighing, which is a harder and better exercise than defending a label.

The imperialism question asks the candidate to assess a theory, and its trap is the recital of features described in the matrix section. Its architecture is argument, evidence, forecast. The candidate first states the theory as an argument with a function: an explanation of the world war that also repositioned the revolutionary timetable. The evidence storey then tests the theory's machinery against the record its author used and the record since, the concentration of capital, the role of finance, the partition and repartition of territory, noting where the description still bites and where the twentieth century refused it, in the survival and expansion of capitalism after its supposed highest stage and in the decolonisation that proceeded by national rather than socialist revolution. The forecast storey judges the strategic conclusion separately from the descriptive machinery, because a theory can describe well and predict badly, and the candidate who separates the two judgements earns the distinction that the single verdict answer forfeits. Kautsky's alternative, an ultra imperialism of jointly exploited peace, belongs in the answer not as a footnote but as the standing rival the theory was written to destroy, and the evaluation closes by asking which of the two rivals better survives the century each claimed to foresee.

The peasantry question appears in both rooms wearing different clothes, and it is the finest training ground for the two-discipline answer. In the history paper it asks why the regime survived the countryside or how the land settlement shaped the civil war, and its material is evidence: the land decree's adoption of the rival party's mandates, the committees of poor peasants, the requisitioning and the revolts it fed, the tax in kind that conceded the argument. In the politics paper it asks about the alliance as a theoretical problem, and its material is the tension inside Marxism itself, a doctrine designed for industrial workers obliged to make its revolution in a country of peasants, with the alliance formula, the two stage scheme and the revolutionary year's improvisation as successive solutions. The candidate who can feel the costume change, and who writes evidence in the first room and concepts in the second while letting each strengthen the other at the junctions, has understood the whole method of this guide in a single question.

The dictatorship question asks what the dictatorship of the proletariat meant, and it punishes the candidate who answers from the phrase's later career. Its architecture is excavation. The candidate recovers the phrase's meaning in Marx, a class rule exercised through the Commune's forms, rare in his writing and tied to a transition; follows it into the revolutionary year, where it is identified with soviet power and explicitly contrasted with rule by a party or a man; follows it into the governing years, where its author defends it in the polemic against Kautsky as rule resting directly on force and unrestricted by law, a formulation whose candour the answer must neither hide nor sensationalise; and closes by noting the distance between the phrase's first and last stations as the precise measure of the regime's transformation. Evaluation here belongs to the exchange of the period itself, Kautsky's book against the reply it provoked, two old comrades arguing over the inheritance in the middle of the event, and the candidate who stages that exchange writes history and theory in the same paragraph without confusion.

The retreat question, on the New Economic Policy, is the connoisseur's favourite because it tests whether a candidate can think about doctrine under the pressure of failure. The material is stark: by the spring of 1921 the regime faced peasant revolt, urban collapse and the Kronstadt rising, and its response was to legalise the market it had tried to abolish, restore private trade, replace requisition with a tax and invite foreign concessions, while its author described the manoeuvre openly as a retreat and a defeat for the direct transition attempted during the war. The theory problem is what a retreat does to a doctrine: whether the admission revises the theory of transition itself, whether the mixed economy was a stage or a surrender, and how the party's internal argument, between those who canonised the retreat and those who endured it, prefigured the succession struggle over the doctrine's meaning. The history problem is what the retreat reveals about the regime's sources of survival, its willingness to trade ideological ground for grain and quiet. Both problems reward the dating discipline intensely, because the entire question is a comparison between the positions of 1918 and 1921, and the candidate who holds those dates steady will find the answer assembling itself.

## The succession question as an answer

No question in the field carries more marks per paragraph or ruins more good candidates than the one that asks whether the founder led to the successor, and it deserves a full treatment because it is the question on which the two-discipline answer proves itself. The ruin usually begins in the first line, where the candidate announces that the road from the one to the other was straight, or that it was broken, and then spends the answer as an advocate. The method replaces advocacy with assembly: continuities in one column, ruptures in the other, each entry dated and sourced, the judgement reserved until both columns stand complete. What follows rehearses the assembly at the strength an examiner hopes to meet.

The continuity column is formidable and must be given its full weight. A single party held power from the seizure onwards, and the rival parties of the revolution were suppressed, exiled or absorbed within the founder's lifetime and under his authority. The political police founded in his first weeks survived every change of initials to become the successor's instrument. The ban on factions of 1921 supplied the constitutional fiction by which later oppositions were criminalised. The doctrine of the party as the conscious vanguard, entitled to lead a class that could not yet speak its own interests, offered a standing warrant for substitution. The practice of the civil war, command, requisition, hostage and terror, normalised instruments that a peacetime despotism could inherit oiled and ready. Historians of the continuity school, from the totalitarian model of the cold war years to Pipes in his later work, have built the case that the successor's system was the founder's logic unfolded under a ruder hand, and a candidate must be able to state that case at its strongest, because evaluation marks are only ever awarded for defeating strong versions.

The rupture column is equally formidable, and it begins with a difference of expectation. The founder's doctrine, on both sides of the hinge, treated the Russian revolution as the opening of a European one, and the regime's survival strategy assumed rescue by revolution abroad; the successor's doctrine of socialism in one country inverted the assumption and made a programme of the isolation the founder had treated as a death sentence postponed. The economic argument cuts both ways and must be handled entry by entry: the founder's final economic writings imagined a long mixed economy, a cooperative path and a cultural revolution measured in decades, while the successor's great break of the collectivisation drive abolished that timetable by force, at a cost in lives that returns the candidate to the figures protocol. The social character of the ruling stratum changed as the old guard was destroyed in the purges, an event with no precedent in the founder's practice, whose terror had been aimed outward at class enemies and rival parties rather than inward at the party's own cadres. The historians of rupture, among them Stephen Cohen in his rethinking of the succession and Moshe Lewin in his studies of the founder's last struggle and of the society the successor inherited, argue that alternatives existed inside the tradition and were defeated politically, not refuted logically. Trotsky, who had every polemical reason to maximise continuity in order to indict the usurper as a betrayer of a healthy doctrine, and Isaac Deutscher, his biographer, who treated the succession as a tragedy of isolation and backwardness, supply the answer's most interesting tension: the two men closest to the events disagreed about whether the doctrine or the circumstances carried the guilt.

The judgement storey, when both columns stand, usually turns on a distinction the candidate should make explicit: between what the founder's regime made possible, what it made easy, and what it made necessary. Possible is a weak claim and nearly always true; palaces of single party rule and political police plainly made a later despotism possible. Easy is a stronger claim, about instruments inherited and habits formed. Necessary is the strongest claim, that no other outcome was available given the doctrine and the seizure, and it is the claim the evidence supports least comfortably, because the founder's own last writings show him struggling against the apparatus, because the succession was contested by real alternatives with real support, and because a decade separates the death from the despotism in which choices were made by identifiable men. A candidate who grades the three claims separately has written an answer of genuine analytical power, and has done so without a single slogan in either direction, which is the entire discipline of this guide displayed on its hardest question.

## One topic, four kinds of assessment

The two disciplines are examined under several assessment regimes, and while the method of this guide holds in all of them, its proportions change with the format. Candidates waste real marks by preparing a single answer shape and deploying it everywhere, so this section adjusts the method to the four settings in which the topic most often appears. No syllabus or paper is named, deliberately: assessment regimes rename their instruments constantly, while the types endure, and a guide that teaches the types remains useful after every renaming.

The school and early undergraduate history paper examines the revolutions as a narrative with an argument inside it. Its questions are generous with events and strict about evidence, and its marking rewards the candidate who knows the sequence cold, the February collapse, the dual power, the July Days, the Kornilov affair, the October seizure, the peace, the civil war, in their order and with their causal joints exposed. In this setting the biography discipline carries the largest share of the load, the historiography enters in its simplest two camp form, and the figures protocol earns its keep immediately, since school marking is where unsourced numbers are punished with the most cheerful consistency. The candidate's revision should privilege the timeline and the camps, and the answer's architecture should let the narrative carry the argument visibly rather than burying it in a theoretical preamble the marker did not ask for.

The undergraduate politics and political theory paper examines the doctrine as a position among positions. Its questions are comparative by instinct, Marx against his Russian heir, the heir against his critics, the model of the party against democratic theory's objections, and its marking rewards definition, distinction and the staged exchange between named thinkers. In this setting the doctrine discipline dominates, the biography enters only as the dating apparatus, and the greatest risk is the narrative sprawl described in the opening section, the candidate recounting October when the question asked about the state. Revision should privilege the text cards and the critic cards, and the answer's openings should define before they narrate, because a theory marker reads an undefined first page the way a history marker reads an undated one, as a promise the script will not keep.

The civil service optional paper in political science examines the topic as part of a canon of political thought, and its distinguishing feature is the premium it places on structured, balanced, examinable prose under severe time discipline. Questions in this setting ask for the doctrine's place in the Marxist tradition and its assessment as political theory, and they reward the answer that subdivides cleanly, states the steelmanned case on each side, and reaches a measured judgement without either devotional or prosecutorial colouring. The two-discipline answer serves this setting particularly well, because the balanced architecture it teaches is precisely the architecture the setting rewards, and the figures protocol and the attribution habit display the temperament such examinations exist to select. Revision should privilege the worked question shapes of this guide and the practice of writing conclusions that weigh rather than declare.

The eligibility and entrance test setting examines the topic in fragments: short answers, structured responses and objective items in which the doctrine's terms, texts and dates are tested at speed. Candidates often condescend to this setting and are punished for it, because fragmentary formats punish vagueness more efficiently than essays do. A multiple choice item on the April Theses cannot be answered with a paragraph's worth of atmosphere; it demands the date, the demand and the distinction from the older line, held separately and exactly. In this setting the dating discipline becomes the whole game, the text cards become flashcards, and the misreadings corrected in this guide become the distractors the items are built from, since test setters construct their wrong options from the common errors of the field. Revision should therefore treat every correction in the following section as a predicted wrong answer, and should drill the pairs, pamphlet against programme, coup against revolution, founder against successor, until the distinctions fire faster than the confusion can.

## The vocabulary of the answer

Markers award a quiet but steady stream of marks for vocabulary handled as an instrument rather than as decoration, and this field's vocabulary is unusually treacherous because its commonest terms changed meaning inside the period itself. A candidate who defines six terms with precision, and then uses them consistently, will outwrite a candidate with twice the facts and a loose lexicon. The definitions that follow are the working ones for examination purposes, each given with the distinction it exists to protect.

A soviet was a council of workers, soldiers or peasants, elected from workplaces and units and recallable by them, arising first in the revolution of 1905 as an organ of strike coordination and returning in the revolutionary year as a claimant to state power. The distinction the definition protects is between the soviet as an institution with a social base and the soviet as a word in a regime's title: an answer that can say when the soviets were the first and when they became chiefly the second has located one of the field's central transformations in a single term. Dual power names the condition of the revolutionary spring and summer, in which the Provisional Government held formal authority while the Petrograd Soviet held the allegiance of the garrison and the factories, and neither could govern without the other's toleration. The term matters because it converts a narrative of drift into an analysis of a structure, and because the April Theses are unintelligible except as a proposal to end the duality in the soviets favour.

Democratic centralism names the party's organisational settlement, freedom of discussion before a decision, unity of action after it, and the candidate should define it with both halves sounded, since answers that stress only the centralism have accepted the caricature and answers that stress only the democracy have accepted the apologetic. The dictatorship of the proletariat, excavated in an earlier section, requires its definition to be dated every time it is used, the Commune form of the summer book and the force resting formulation of the following year's polemic being different instruments sharing a name. War communism is the retrospective label for the governing system of the civil war years, requisitioning, nationalisation, the suppression of private trade and the militarisation of labour, and the label's retrospective character is itself worth a sentence, since its author alternated between presenting the system as emergency necessity and as a mistaken attempt at a direct transition, and the alternation is evidence in the debate about his intentions. Vanguard, finally, is a functional word before it is an honorific one: it denotes the part of a class or movement that, by consciousness and organisation, marches ahead of the rest, and the examination question is always whether the function described a relationship of leadership earned in struggle or a licence for substitution claimed in advance. A candidate who keeps that question attached to the word will find the evaluation storeys of many answers half built before they begin.

Two further terms guard the borders of the subject and are worth the candidate's care precisely because they are usually handled as slogans. Revisionism was the movement's internal insult for the amendment of doctrine in the direction of gradualism, and its presence in an answer should always be dated and attributed, since the charge tells the reader more about the charger than about the charged. Opportunism performed similar border work on the other flank. An answer that uses either word in its own voice, unattributed, has imported the period's polemical weapons into the analysis, and markers notice the smuggling. The discipline is the same one that governed the record questions: the period's language is evidence, to be quoted and examined, while the candidate's language is analysis, to be kept clean of borrowed verdicts. Clean language is not neutral in the sense of empty. It is the precision that lets a strong verdict, when it finally arrives, land with the weight of everything that was fairly stated before it.

## Handling sources in the history answer

The history paper's distinctive craft is the handling of sources, and on this subject the craft has local rules that general examination advice does not supply. The sources of the revolution divide into families with different diseases, and the candidate who can name a source's family and its disease in a single sentence earns the provenance marks that separate the top band from the one below. This section walks the families in the order an answer meets them.

The decree family comes first: the decrees on peace and on land issued in the seizure's first days, the declaration of rights, the decrees dissolving and founding institutions. A decree is evidence of intention and of claimed authority, and of nothing else directly. It does not prove its own enforcement, its reception in the provinces or its survival past the month of its issue, and the classic candidate error is to cite the land decree as proof that the land question was settled, when the decree's career in the countryside, uneven, locally reinterpreted and entangled with the peasants' own seizures that preceded it, is the real examinable story. The strong habit is to pair every decree with a reception: a provincial report, a historian's finding, a subsequent decree amending the first, so that the paper trail shows a government discovering the distance between proclamation and power.

The memoir family is the field's glory and its minefield. The revolution was recorded by participants of genius: Nikolai Sukhanov, the Menshevik internationalist whose notes on the revolution remain the great eyewitness account of the Petrograd months, hostile to the Bolsheviks and indispensable about them; Trotsky, whose history of the revolution is a participant's epic that doubles as a succession struggle document; Kerensky, justifying a premiership that collapsed in his hands; and a shelf of lesser witnesses, diplomats, journalists and soldiers, each with a vantage and a wound. The candidate's discipline with memoirs is triangulation: a memoir claim enters an answer as one witness's testimony, gains strength when an unlike witness or a document corroborates it, and must carry its author's interest visibly, as when Trotsky's account of October is used for the mechanics of the seizure, where his knowledge was unrivalled, with more caution on the motives of his rivals, where his knowledge was a weapon. Markers reward the candidate who writes that a source is a participant's account written in exile during a struggle for the succession, because the sentence demonstrates that provenance is a habit rather than a garnish.

The statistical family is the figures protocol in its working clothes. The revolution's quantities, votes, memberships, harvests, prices, casualties, were produced by institutions in collapse or in construction, and many of the most repeated numbers descend from polemics rather than from counts. The assembly election figures are among the most solid in the field, being the product of an empire wide count conducted before the counting state collapsed, and even they are argued over at the margins of turnout and classification. Party membership figures swell and shrink with the purposes of the teller and the moment of the telling. A candidate need not become a statistician of the revolution. The examinable skill is the uniform reflex already taught: attribute the quantity to its producer, prefer the range the scholarship argues within, and where the number's provenance is a weapon, say so and use it as evidence about the weapon's maker.

The speech and article family closes the survey, and it carries a warning specific to this subject. The leader's own words survive in overwhelming abundance, edited, collected and canonised by the state he founded, which means the archive itself has an editor with an interest. Speeches were revised for publication, articles were written for factional moments that later editors preferred to forget, and the final dictated letters were suppressed outright for decades because their contents threatened the heirs. A candidate who registers that the Collected Works are a monument as well as a record, and who therefore prizes the texts that embarrassed the monument, the suppressed letters, the pamphlet its author later qualified, the theses his own committee voted down, displays the deepest form of the provenance habit: suspicion applied to the abundance itself. That suspicion, fairly distributed across every camp's sources, is the closest thing the history discipline has to a definition of rigour, and it is entirely learnable.

## The comparative frame

No answer on this subject reaches the top band in isolation, because the doctrine was formed in argument and can only be measured against the positions it argued with. The comparative frame supplies the standing comparisons that examiners expect a strong candidate to command, arranged not as a gallery of rivals but as a set of instruments, each comparison calibrated to expose a different feature of the doctrine. A candidate who holds the four comparisons in this section can generate evaluation material for almost any politics question in the field without inventing an opinion on the spot.

The comparison with Plekhanov exposes the timetable. The father of Russian Marxism had built the stage scheme the doctrine inherited: a backward country must pass through its bourgeois revolution, develop its capitalism and its working class, and approach socialism only as those maturities allowed. The heir's career can be read as the progressive demolition of that inheritance from inside its own logic, the imperialism study dissolving the assumption that development proceeds country by country in sequence, the April Theses discarding the stage at the moment it was supposedly being enacted. Plekhanov's response to October, horror at a seizure he judged premature and doomed, completes the comparison with a symmetry examiners relish: the teacher lived to denounce the pupil's success as the refutation of the teaching, while the pupil's defenders replied that the teaching had been outgrown by the world it described. An answer that stages the exchange has located the continuity question's deepest stratum, the point where fidelity and development become indistinguishable.

The comparison with Martov exposes the party. The two men had founded a newspaper together and split a congress over a clause, Martov's wider definition of membership against the narrower one, and the split hardened into two theories of what a workers party is: a broad movement within which consciousness grows, or a disciplined detachment that carries consciousness to the movement. The revolutionary year tested both theories in the same streets, and Martov's subsequent fate, leading a Menshevik party that opposed the seizure, defended the soviets against the regime that claimed them, and was eventually hounded into exile, gives the comparison its moral weight. A candidate should resist the temptation to treat Martov as a footnote of defeat. His critique of the regime from inside the socialist camp, that a minority dictatorship in a backward country would be driven to substitute terror for the consent it lacked, is one of the period's most consequential arguments, and it was made early, in writing, by a man who shared the doctrine's premises and rejected its conclusions.

The comparison with Luxemburg exposes spontaneity and the national question together, and it has the rare virtue of running in both directions. Her critique of the organisational model, written when the model was new, warned that ultra centralism would strangle the movement's life; her manuscript on the revolution, written in prison and published after her death, praised the Bolsheviks' audacity while indicting their suppression of democracy and their land and national settlements as errors that would deform the revolution's future. The doctrine's author, for his part, held her in a regard he extended to almost no other critic, defending her stature in print in his final active years even while he fought her on self determination and she fought him on the party. The comparison's examinable gift is that neither figure is the other's refutation: each saw a danger the other underpriced, she the danger of the apparatus, he the danger of the unorganised moment, and a candidate who distributes the insight fairly writes the kind of evaluation that moderation meetings remember.

The comparison with Kautsky exposes democracy. The pope of Marxism, as the movement half affectionately called its leading theorist, became in the revolutionary year the renegade of the founder's furious pamphlet, and the exchange between them, Kautsky's book on the dictatorship of the proletariat against the reply it provoked, is the period's central debate about whether the new state was democracy's extension or its abolition. The comparison disciplines the candidate's vocabulary: Kautsky argues from universal suffrage and the maturity of the class, the reply argues from the class content of institutions and the fraudulence of formal equality amid material domination, and the two arguments pass each other at a height that an answer must map rather than merely referee. Staging the exchange also protects the candidate from the anachronism of judging the debate by its later echoes. In the year of the exchange, both men were arguing about a revolution in progress whose outcome neither could know, and the answer that preserves that uncertainty understands the period better than the answer that reads the verdict of the following decades back into the pamphlets.

## A model answer, taken apart

Guides that preach architecture owe their readers one sight of the building, so this section presents the skeleton of a strong answer to a straddle question of the kind setters favour, with the commentary an examiner might write in its margin. The question is a familiar one: how far did the theory of the party determine the character of the state that emerged from the revolution? The extracts are shortened to their load bearing sentences, and the commentary between them performs the only service that matters, which is to show the marker's marks being earned one by one.

The opening runs as follows. The question joins a doctrine to an outcome, and the answer must therefore date both. The doctrine of the party was stated in the exile years for an illegal movement, practised in the revolutionary year by a mass party competing for majorities in elected councils, and inherited after the civil war by the sole legal organisation of a one party state; the state whose character is in question is likewise three states, the Commune imagined in the summer of the revolutionary year, the besieged apparatus of the war years, and the settled bureaucracy its founder criticised in his final letters. An answer that keeps those dates separate can ask how far the first explains the last; an answer that merges them has decided the question before examining it. The margin commentary is brief: discipline identified, periods fixed, the verdict visibly postponed, and the question's own verb, determine, already under quiet pressure.

The body's first movement then states the doctrine with its conditions clause and its author's own qualification of 1907, and notes what the doctrine did not contain: no prescription for a one party state, no theory of a ruling apparatus, an organisational answer to the problem of illegality rather than a constitutional design. The second movement turns to evidence, and it is here that the answer earns its history marks inside a theory question: the party of the revolutionary year won its majorities in the soviets by argument, the coalition with the Left Socialist Revolutionaries governed jointly until the peace treaty split it, and the single party monopoly arrived by stages through the civil war's emergencies rather than by the blueprint of any pamphlet. The third movement introduces the pressure of circumstance at full strength, invasion, blockade, famine, the collapse of the European revolutions on which the doctrine had counted, and asks what organisational inheritance was available to a government under such pressure except the habits of the underground: secrecy, command, the treatment of dissent as danger. The margin notes record the pattern a top band answer always shows, each movement ending with a junction sentence that states what the movement has done to the question, so the argument accumulates rather than merely proceeds.

The evaluation movement stages the exchange the period itself supplies. Martov's charge, that a party organised to substitute itself for a class in opposition would substitute itself for the class in power, is stated in its strongest form and dated to the years when it was a warning rather than a verdict. Against it is set the defence the record supports: that the same party repeatedly submitted itself to the verdict of the soviets it could lose, that its leader fought his own committee in the open in the revolutionary year, and that the distance between the conspiratorial detachment of the exile blueprint and the arguing mass party of the revolutionary year is the distance the doctrine travelled when conditions changed. Then the evaluation turns, because an evaluation that turns only once is advocacy: the habits that circumstance excused in the besieged years were retained and codified in the quiet years, the faction ban outliving its emergency, the apparatus outgrowing its masters, until the founder's own last letters were describing a state he recognised as a deformation of his design. The margin here notes the steelmanning in both directions and the absence, conspicuous to a marker, of any sentence that could have been written before the evidence was examined.

The close delivers the judgement the opening postponed, graded by the three strengths distinguished in this guide's treatment of the succession. The doctrine made the later state possible, by supplying a legitimating theory of tutelage and an organisation built for command; it made that state easier, by bequeathing instruments and habits that emergency had normalised; it did not make that state necessary, since the doctrine's other half, the soviet democracy of the hinge year and the withering state of the summer book, stood in the same inheritance accusing the outcome, and since men chose, at identifiable moments, which half to keep. The commentary closes the script with the observation markers make about such endings: the verdict could have fallen the other way on the same evidence, differently weighed, and would have earned the same band, because what was assessed was never the verdict. It was the road.

## The late writings and the codified doctrine

Two bodies of material sit at the end of the subject's timeline, and examinations reach them from opposite directions. The founder's last writings belong to the biography discipline and to the doctrine discipline at once, since they are simultaneously the acts of a dying leader and a revision of the doctrine by its author. The codification that followed his death belongs chiefly to the doctrine discipline, since it is the process by which a development became a system with his name on it. A candidate who can handle both has closed the timeline, and closure of the timeline is what distinguishes a candidate who knows the subject's whole shape from one who knows its famous middle.

The late writings, dictated between the winter of 1922 and the spring of 1923 as strokes progressively removed their author from the government he had founded, form a connected reckoning. The letter to the congress, the Testament of the succession debate, assessed the leading figures of the party with a candour that guaranteed its suppression, warned of a split along the fault line between the general secretary and Trotsky, and characterised the general secretary's accumulation of authority as a danger the party should answer by removing him from that post. The companion notes addressed the apparatus itself: the workers and peasants inspection that had become a byword for the bureaucracy it was meant to police, the state machine described as borrowed from the old regime and barely anointed with a soviet polish, the nationalities policy accused of Great Russian chauvinism in the Georgian affair, in terms that indicted the founder's own lieutenants. The articles of the same months sketched the positive programme the reckoning implied: cooperation as the road by which a peasant country might grow toward socialism over decades, a cultural revolution of literacy and elementary administration as the precondition of everything else, patience elevated into doctrine. For examination purposes the late writings do triple work. They are evidence for the biography answer, the founder as his own regime's critic. They are doctrine, a final revision in which the timescale lengthens and the state shrinks in favour of the schoolroom. And they are the hinge's last gift to the succession question, since both camps in that argument claim them, the rupture school reading a repudiation of the emerging order, the continuity school reading a manager's complaints inside a system he never questioned. The candidate's task is to present the texts with dates and addressees, and to refuse both camps the ownership of documents that belong to the record.

The codification proceeded by other hands. Within months of the founder's death, the successor's lectures on the foundations of the doctrine bearing the founder's name offered the first systematic statement of Leninism as a complete system, defining it as the Marxism of the era of imperialism and proletarian revolution, a definition whose elegance concealed an act of authorship: the living development, with its reversals, qualifications and final revisions, was being frozen at the points most useful to the heirs. The short course historiography of the following decade completed the freezing for the party's official memory. None of this is an argument that the system so codified was a fabrication; its elements are all present in the texts, selected and arranged. It is an argument about selection, and it gives the doctrine candidate a final refinement of the dating discipline: when an answer cites Leninism, it should know whether it is citing the development or the codification, the river or the canal. Setters know the difference and reward the candidate who shows it, because the confusion of the two is the quiet form of the costliest error with which this guide began. The founder and the system share a name, and the whole art of the subject is knowing when the name denotes the man, when the doctrine he left unfinished, and when the edifice his heirs built upon it.

## Misreadings corrected

Certain errors recur in scripts on this subject with the regularity of seasons, and examiners could set their watches by them. This section corrects the six that cost the most, in the form a candidate can use: the error stated, its origin explained, and the correction given as a habit. None of the six is a failure of intelligence. All six are failures of the disciplines this guide teaches, which is the hopeful fact about them, since disciplines can be learned in a way that intelligence, whatever that is, cannot be assigned as homework.

The first misreading treats the seizure of power and the revolution as synonyms, so that a question about the one is answered with material about the other. The seizure was an event of days in one capital; the revolution was a process of years across an empire, of which the seizure was the hinge rather than the whole. The correction is the scale habit: before answering, state the scale of the question, event, process or era, and match the material to it. The second misreading makes the founder the author of every act of his government and every idea of his movement, a biographical short circuit that dissolves committees, congresses, rivals and circumstances into a single will. Its origin is the textbook chapter organised around a name. The correction is the agency habit: for every claim about what he did, ask what the record shows about who else decided, resisted, amended or executed, and write the answer with those agents present. Scripts that restore the other agents immediately read as more historical, because they are.

The third misreading reverses the direction of the April Theses, presenting them as the party's programme imposed on its leader by the pressure of the streets, when the documentary record shows a leader returning from exile to fight his own party's settled line, losing the first votes, and winning the argument in the party's forums over the following weeks. The correction matters beyond the episode, because the misreading dissolves the single most examinable fact about how doctrine changed in this tradition: by argument, in institutions, against resistance, in writing. The fourth misreading describes the New Economic Policy as the founder's conversion to the market, or, in the mirror version, as a trick concealing an unchanged intention to abolish it. The texts support neither simplification: the retreat was described by its author as a retreat, seriously and indefinitely, and the same author's late writings extended its logic while the party argued about its soul. The correction is the quotation discipline applied to self description: when a historical actor names his own act with unusual candour, the answer should begin from the name he gave it and argue from there.

The fifth misreading locates the origin of the terror wholly in ideology or wholly in circumstance, according to the candidate's prior allegiance, when the scholarship's actual argument is about the interaction, an ideology that classified enemies by category meeting a circumstance that supplied emergencies in unbroken succession. The correction is the interaction habit: for every record question, the answer should be required to name one ideological and one circumstantial factor and to state how they acted on each other, which dissolves the false choice the misreading feeds on. The sixth misreading is the gentlest and the most pervasive: the belief that the subject's story ends with the founder's death, so that answers close in the winter of 1924 as though the doctrine's career were complete. The codification, the succession struggle and the system's long afterlife as the official doctrine of states and parties across the century are part of the examinable subject wherever the doctrine is examined as a doctrine, and the correction is the timeline habit in its simplest form: the subject ends where the question says it ends, and the candidate should check before closing the book at the funeral.

## Reading the question before answering it

Between the revision and the answer sits an act of reading that decides more marks than any other ninety seconds of the examination, and it can be taught as a routine. The candidate reads the question three times with three different questions in mind. The first reading asks for the room: history or politics, event or doctrine, and the telltales rehearsed earlier, dates and battles against concepts and texts. The second reading asks for the verb: the command word that fixes the architecture, survey against judgement, explanation against assessment, and with it the marking tradition that stands behind the verb in that assessment type. The third reading asks for the trap: the assumption the question is carrying that the answer is meant to refuse, the settled verdict smuggled into its phrasing, the undated term, the conflated pair. Three readings, three decisions, and the plan that follows from them writes itself in the matrix's terms: discipline, content to lead with, evaluation to stage, trap to avoid.

The routine has a corollary about choice, since most papers offer more questions than any candidate must answer, and choice is where strong candidates quietly bank their advantage. The rule is to choose by trap rather than by topic. A question on a beloved topic carrying a trap the candidate has not rehearsed is a worse bet than a question on a colder topic whose trap is one of the six misreadings already corrected in the notes, because content can be assembled in the hall from dated cards while an unspotted trap corrupts every paragraph it touches. Candidates should also honour the straddle questions when their preparation is genuinely two disciplined, since setters calibrate such questions to reward the rare candidate who can work both rooms, and the calibration means the same quality of answer earns more there than on a single room question. Choice, in short, is a marks decision disguised as a preference, and it deserves the same dating and attribution discipline as everything else in this guide: choose in writing, in the plan, with the reasons stated, and do not revisit the decision once the pen has committed.

## Three exchanges that carry the evaluation

Evaluation paragraphs fail most often for a simple reason: the candidate possesses opinions about the doctrine but no arguments with names on them, and opinions cannot be marked. This section therefore stages the three exchanges from which the field's evaluation material is drawn, each presented as it should appear inside an answer, with the objection at full strength, the reply at full strength, and the residue that a judgement can honestly claim. A candidate who can reproduce these three exchanges, in miniature, owns the evaluation storey of nearly every question the setters set.

The first exchange concerns organisation, and it opened in 1904, when the organisational model of the exile pamphlet was attacked from inside the movement by two of its most gifted contemporaries. Trotsky, then aligned against the model's author, warned in a pamphlet of that year that the logic of substitution ran downhill: the party organisation substituting itself for the class, the central committee for the organisation, and finally a single leader for the committee. Luxemburg, writing in the same season on the Russian party's disputes, argued that the ultra centralism proposed would transfer the initiative of a living movement to a committee of night watchmen, and that the errors of a genuine movement were more fruitful than the infallibility of any central committee. The replies available to the doctrine are real and should be given: that both critics wrote about a party of exiles under a police regime, where looseness was not democracy but exposure; that the model's author spent the revolutionary year fighting his own committee in open forums, a strange career for the supposed dictator of the design; and that the mass party of that year admitted, argued and expelled on a scale neither critic had imagined when they described a sect. The residue is genuinely double. The critics described a mechanism that the later apparatus exhibited with terrible fidelity, and they described it before the apparatus existed, which makes their texts the strongest evaluation evidence in the field; and the defence's evidence, that the mechanism slept while the movement lived and woke as the movement died, is itself an argument about conditions that the critics' framework cannot easily absorb. An answer that leaves the residue double has evaluated. An answer that resolves it by loyalty has merely voted.

The second exchange concerns democracy, and it opened in the first year of the regime, when Kautsky published his indictment of the dictatorship and the founder replied with a fury that still scorches the page. Kautsky's case: that democracy was not a bourgeois ornament but the necessary medium of a class learning to rule, that a minority dictatorship in a backward country would be driven from expedient to expedient until terror became its system, and that the dispersal of the assembly announced the destination in advance. The reply: that the democracy being mourned had always excluded the exploited in substance while including them in form, that soviet power enfranchised the producers as producers and disenfranchised only the exploiters, and that a revolution besieged by the old world's armies could not be judged by the constitutional standards of the powers besieging it. The residue here is asymmetrical in an instructive way. On the institutional prediction, the years that followed treated Kautsky's warning with a respect its author's reputation did not survive to enjoy. On the theoretical charge, that formal democracy conceals a class content, the reply permanently altered how political theory discusses constitutions, including among the reply's enemies. A judgement that grades the two halves separately, prediction to the critic, theory in part to the founder, models the exact operation the succession section taught with its three strengths of claim.

The third exchange is the strangest, because it is an argument the founder conducted with himself, in the open, across the hinge. On one side stand the texts of the revolutionary spring and summer: the soviets as the state, the machine smashed, the officials recallable, the withering begun. On the other side stand the writings of the governing years in their unguarded moments: the admissions that the apparatus had been inherited rather than created, that the drive toward a direct transition had been a mistake, that the party's own machine required watching by the very classes it ruled. Between the two sides sits the practice, and the examination question is which side the practice obeyed. Candidates sometimes treat the self argument as an embarrassment to be managed. It is the opposite: it is the strongest evidence that the doctrine contained its own standard of judgement, and an answer that measures the regime by the founder's stated standard, rather than by the candidate's imported one, performs the most defensible evaluation in the whole field, immanent critique, the doctrine judged by its own lights. That is why this exchange closes the section. The first exchange supplies the critics, the second supplies the rival theory, and the third supplies the standard, and an answer furnished with all three need never borrow a verdict from anyone.

## Revising the revolution and Leninism together

Revision for this subject fails in a characteristic way: the candidate revises the revolution in one week and the doctrine in another, filing the events in a history folder and the texts in a politics folder, and then cannot understand why the answers will not assemble. The two were never separate in reality, and the revision method of this guide, which generalises the approach of the wider [Marxism revision guide](/2022/01/15/marxism-revision-guide/) to this topic's special structure, is to revise them as one subject on one timeline with two kinds of entries interleaved. The instrument is a set of cards, physical or digital, built in four families, and the families matter more than the stationery.

The first family is the date cards, one for each load bearing episode and text, carrying on one side the date and the name and on the other the three facts an answer can use and the single inference each fact supports. The second family is the text cards, one for each of the eight working texts, carrying its date, its occasion, its concentrated claim and its author's own later qualification where one exists, since the qualification is what the dating discipline is made of. The third family is the critic cards, one for each named opponent, carrying the objection in the opponent's strongest sentence, the reply the doctrine can honestly make, and the residue the exchange leaves, in the pattern of the previous section. The fourth family is the camp cards, one for each historiographical position, carrying its historian, its evidence base and the fact it prefers to omit. A candidate who has built the four families possesses the subject in answer form, because every question in the field is a request to combine cards across families in a particular order, and the combinations can be practised like scales.

The practice method that binds the cards together is the timed plan. Working from past questions, the candidate spends the planning minutes the examination allows, and no more, producing the matrix row for the question set: discipline, content to lead with in dated order, evaluation staged from the critic cards, trap named. Only when a dozen such plans can be produced fluently should full answers be written, and even then the ratio should stay at three plans to one full answer, because examinations are lost in the planning far more often than in the prose. A weekly rhythm serves the method: one session for building cards from the week's reading, one for timed plans against questions drawn blind, one for a single full answer written to the clock and then audited against this guide's disciplines, dates checked, figures hunted down and attributed or removed, verdict located and confirmed to be standing at the end where it belongs.

Two companion tools support this workflow, and they divide the labour between them exactly as the disciplines divide. The structured study notes at [VaultBook's Marxism study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) suit the card families: a candidate keeping dated text cards, critic cards and camp cards in a private notes vault can revise them anywhere, add the qualification column as it grows, and keep the figures protocol as a standing checklist at the head of the file where it will be seen before every practice answer. The second tool addresses the other half of preparation, the questions themselves. The [ReportMedic UPSC previous year question explorer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/upsc-pyq-explorer.html) lets a candidate work through previous years questions as a training set, harvesting real phrasings for the timed plan drill, sorting them by the room they belong to, and testing the three readings routine against setters' actual verbs rather than against imagined ones. Used together, the two tools enact the guide's whole thesis: the notes keep the disciplines apart and dated, and the question bank keeps the candidate honest about which discipline any given question is examining.

The final stage of revision is the fusion pass, in which the two folders of the failing method are deliberately merged. The candidate takes the timeline and walks it once as pure narrative, aloud if the room allows, from the Simbirsk schoolboy to the suppressed letters, checking that every event can be placed and every gap named. Then the same timeline is walked as doctrine, text by text, each claim attached to its year and its occasion, each reversal marked at the hinge and after it. Then, and this is the pass that pays, the two walks are alternated episode by episode, event, text, event, text, until the joins are fluent: the exile produces the pamphlet, the war produces the imperialism study and the notebooks, the return produces the theses, the hiding produces the book on the state, the government produces the decrees, the terror, the retreat and the reckoning. A subject revised to that fluency no longer lives in two folders. It lives where the examinations will look for it, in one mind that can change rooms without changing the subject.

## Time, stamina and the shape of the sitting

Examinations are athletic events conducted in prose, and the candidate's method must survive the body's part in them. The disciplines of this guide have time signatures, and rehearsing them at the wrong tempo is the commonest reason a well revised candidate underperforms a worse revised one who has practised the sitting itself. This section assigns the times. The planning allocation has already been fixed by the matrix: the opening minutes of each answer belong to the three readings and the row they produce, and the candidate who begrudges those minutes will repay them, with interest, in the third paragraph, where unplanned answers discover their confusion. The writing allocation follows the architecture's proportions: content in dated sequence taking the body's largest share, evaluation never falling below its fifth, and the final minutes defended, against every temptation to add one more fact, for the judgement and its qualification. A candidate should decide, in practice and not in the hall, the physical signal for moving to the evaluation: a proportion of the answer's pages, a position of the clock, a number of paragraphs, any signal will do provided it is mechanical, because under pressure judgement abdicates and only mechanisms remain.

Stamina has a doctrinal dimension on this subject that candidates rarely anticipate. The topic's emotional weather, admiration in some candidates, revulsion in others, fatigues the hand and the judgement together, and the third hour's answer on the terror is written by a different moral creature than the first hour's answer on the pamphlet. The record discipline is the protection: the candidate who has automated attribution and dating does not need to decide, at the point of exhaustion, how to feel about the Cheka, because the method has already decided how to write about it, and the conservation of moral energy for the judgement storeys, where feeling disciplined into argument actually earns marks, is a real if unglamorous advantage. Practical corollaries follow. Practise the record questions in the tired position, at the end of full practice sittings, not fresh at the start. Practise the openings until they are handwriting deep, since the opening is written when nerves are loudest. And practise leaving an answer: the disciplined abandonment of a paragraph that has gone wrong, struck through and replaced by the plan's next movement, is a skill that separates candidates who serve their plan from candidates who serve their sunk costs.

## The answer as a whole

Step back from the machinery and the guide's teaching reduces to a single picture, which a candidate can carry into the hall when the cards and matrices have been left at the door. The subject is one man and one doctrine, held apart so that each can be seen clearly, and joined at a hinge where the man reversed the doctrine in the middle of the event that made both immortal. Every question is an invitation to stand somewhere in relation to that picture and argue. The history questions invite the candidate to stand inside the events and weigh what can be known about them. The politics questions invite the candidate to stand inside the arguments and weigh what can be claimed for them. The great questions, the succession above all, invite the candidate to stand at the hinge itself and measure what crossed it and what did not. A candidate who knows where each question is standing them, and who has the dates in the bones and the critics by name and the figures under discipline, will find that the answers are not retrieved but composed, there in the hall, out of materials that fit together because they were revised together. That composure, rather than any verdict about its subject, is what this guide has been for.

## The doctrine abroad

Politics papers regularly ask about the doctrine as an international project, and history papers ask about the regime's foreign conduct, and the two questions share a body of material that candidates often omit from revision because it fits neither the pamphlet folder nor the Petrograd folder. The material begins from a premise already met in the hinge section: the doctrine of the revolutionary year expected the Russian revolution to be the first act of a European one, and the regime's survival strategy, its party's strategy and its theory of itself were all built on that expectation. The Communist International, founded in Moscow in 1919, was the expectation institutionalised: a general staff of world revolution, gathering the parties that split from the old socialist internationals over the war and the Russian example, and binding them by conditions of admission, twenty one in the famous enumeration, that exported the organisational model of the founding party as the price of membership. The congresses of the International's first years read the European situation through Russian categories, and the failures accumulated in the same rhythm as the hopes: the Hungarian soviet republic of 1919 rose and fell within months, the German October of 1923 was prepared and then called off in confusion, and the doctrine's author, in his last active interventions, was already counselling the patience, the united front and the retreat on the international front that his pamphlet on left wing communism had theorised against the impatience of the young parties.

For the doctrine answer, the examinable core is the tension the International embodied. A movement claiming to embody the self emancipation of the working class organised itself on the model of its most clandestine and centralised section, and required national parties to subordinate their judgements to a centre seated in the capital of the one successful revolution. Candidates should be able to state the defence as its makers stated it: that the old internationals had died of their looseness in August 1914, voting war credits and national defence, and that discipline was the lesson drawn from a documented catastrophe. They should equally be able to state the critique as the period's dissenters stated it, at the congresses and after them: that the conditions exported a Russian solution to parties facing parliaments, legal unions and mass electorates, and that the splits the conditions imposed, at Tours, at Halle, at Livorno, divided movements whose unity the moment required. The evaluation then has real material on both sides and a datable sequence to judge, which is all an evaluation ever needs.

For the history answer, the same years supply the regime's double foreign policy, conducted simultaneously by the foreign ministry and by the International, diplomacy and revolution pursued as two instruments of one survival. The Treaty of Rapallo with Germany in 1922, by which the two outcast states recognised each other and renounced claims, is the emblematic document of the diplomatic track, and candidates mishandle it when they treat it as an ideological event rather than as the statecraft of an isolated government buying room to breathe. The analytical point the marker rewards is the recognition that the two tracks were not hypocrisy but strategy under a single premise, that time was the regime's scarcest resource and both tracks purchased it, until the European revolution's failure to arrive converted the temporary duality into the permanent condition the successor's doctrine would rename. An answer that can narrate Rapallo and the twenty one conditions in adjacent paragraphs, and say what each was buying, has achieved the two discipline fusion on foreign ground, and will be remembered when the scripts are ranked.

## The biography's hard cases

Every famous life accumulates a sediment of episodes that examinations test precisely because they are contested, and the candidate's handling of them is read as a sample of the whole evidential character. Three such episodes in this life recur in questions and in markers' reports, and this section takes them in turn, not to settle them but to display the handling, which is the examinable property. The general rule precedes the cases: where the evidence is contested, the answer's subject is the contest, and the candidate writes about the claims, their makers and their documents with the same care the uncontested record receives.

The German gold allegation is the senior case. That the German government facilitated the sealed train is documented and undisputed; that German money reached the Bolsheviks, and in particular that it purchased the party's policy, has been alleged since the revolutionary year itself, when the Provisional Government's investigation publicised materials against the party's intermediaries in the summer of 1917, and the allegation has been renewed by historians and journalists ever since, with archival fragments on subsidies to revolutionary propaganda in Russia established for some channels and the decisive link to the party's central funds remaining, in the judgement of most specialists who have worked the archives, unproven. The companion case, the Sisson documents, is the cautionary tale inside the case: a dossier purchased and published by an American official in 1918 purporting to prove that the Bolshevik leaders were German agents, and demonstrated within a generation, most famously by the diplomat and historian George Kennan, to be substantially forged. A strong answer uses the pair exactly so: the facilitation is fact, the subsidy allegation is a live question with evidence graded by channel, the agency claim rests on a forged dossier and a logic that confuses an enemy's hope with a client's obedience, and the candidate who writes those three sentences in that order has displayed more historical intelligence than three pages of denunciation or vindication could carry. Markers look for the grading instinct, since it is the instinct the discipline exists to teach.

The illness and succession case is quieter and sadder, and it tests a different virtue, restraint. The strokes of 1922 and 1923, the aphasia, the removal from the capital to the estate at Gorki, the dictated letters smuggled into the party's deliberations by his wife and secretaries, the leadership's decision about which of his texts the congress would be permitted to hear: these are documented in the record of the institution he founded turning, before his death, into the arena of his heirs. Around the medical facts a literature of speculation has grown, diagnoses argued from symptoms across a century, and the candidate's duty is to notice the speculation's character, interesting, unverifiable and irrelevant to most questions, and to keep the answer on the institutional ground where the evidence lives: what was written, what was suppressed, who decided, and with what consequences for the doctrine's custody. Restraint here is not squeamishness but aim, since the succession question's marks lie in the custody of the texts, not in the pathology of the author.

The third case is the legacy's physical form, the embalmed body displayed in the mausoleum on Red Square against the expressed wishes the widow placed on record, and the candidate may wonder what an examination could want with it. The answer is that the mausoleum is the codification argument made visible: a doctrine that had theorised the withering of authority embalmed its founder into an object of permanent veneration, and the party that inherited him governed for decades in the presence of the relic, quoting the texts in editions it controlled beneath the portrait it had standardised. History questions about the regime's character and politics questions about the doctrine's transformation both open onto this symbol, and the candidate who can use it, briefly, attributed, as the material form of the distinction between the development and the codification, has found the rare illustration that is also an argument. The discipline, as always, is proportion: a sentence of symbol inside an answer of evidence, never the reverse.

## From the marker's desk

Candidates revise against an imagined examiner who is usually a fiction, half schoolmaster and half hanging judge, and the fiction costs marks because it misdirects preparation toward appeasement and away from evidence. This section replaces the fiction with the working reality of marking, in the general terms that apply across the assessment types this guide serves. Scripts are read against criteria by markers who are themselves moderated, sampled and standardised, working at speed through piles in which the distinguishing features of strong answers are, by professional necessity, the features that can be seen quickly. Everything in this guide has been arranged to make the right features visible at a marker's reading speed, and it may help the candidate to know exactly which features those are.

The first is the dated sentence. A marker reading the fortieth script of a sitting develops an involuntary flinch at the undated generalisation, the doctrine described in the present tense, the government act floating in an unlocated past, and a corresponding lift at the sentence that begins with a year or a text and stays located. The lift is not pedantry. Dating is the fastest visible proxy for the whole complex of skills the criteria describe as analysis, because a candidate who dates has already distinguished periods, and a candidate who distinguishes periods can be trusted with evaluation. The second feature is the named opponent. Evaluation criteria speak of counter argument and balance, and the marker sees those abstractions made flesh in a proper noun: Luxemburg objects, Kautsky replies, Pipes contends, Fitzpatrick counters. A script in which the critics are a crowd called some historians reads as a script whose evaluation was assembled from posture rather than from reading, and no quantity of balanced adjectives repairs the impression.

The third feature is provenance attached to quantities, the attributed range and the sourced figure, which the marker reads as a certificate of the script's whole evidential character, unfairly but reliably: a candidate careful with the terror's numbers is presumed careful with the treaty's terms. The fourth is the junction sentence, the explicit statement of what the evidence has just done to the doctrine, which markers prize because it is the point at which description becomes argument and because it cannot be faked by a candidate who does not understand the relation being stated. The fifth feature is the verdict's address. Markers are readers, and a verdict announced in the first paragraph and defended thereafter flattens their reading of everything that follows, since each new paragraph is experienced as advocacy; the same verdict arriving after the assembly is experienced as a conclusion, and conclusions are what the criteria pay for. Candidates who understand that marking is a reading experience before it is a measurement will grasp why this guide has spent so much of its length on arrangement. Content is the candidate's knowledge. Arrangement is the candidate's knowledge made visible at the speed at which it will actually be judged.

One more reality deserves stating, because it consoles and disciplines in equal measure. Markers reward the candidate whose conclusion they disagree with, provided the road was honestly built, and they do so not from saintliness but because the criteria leave them no choice: the marks are attached to operations, dating, attribution, steelmanning, weighing, and not to destinations. The moderation system exists to catch the marker who pays for agreement. A candidate may therefore write, in perfect safety, the most unfashionable verdict in the field, and the most fashionable, and receive for each exactly what the operations earned. The candidate's true opponent in the hall is never the marker's politics. It is the candidate's own undated, unattributed, unopposed draft, the one that has not yet been through the disciplines, and that opponent can be beaten in revision, which is the most hopeful sentence in this entire guide.

## Drills that build the answer

Method is retained through the fingers or not at all, and this guide closes its teaching with the drills by which the two-discipline answer is converted from something a candidate understands into something a candidate does under pressure. The drills are short by design. Each isolates one operation of the method and repeats it until the operation becomes reflex, and a fortnight of daily practice with them will do more for a script than a month of additional reading, because the scripts this subject produces are rarely short of knowledge. They are short of operations performed on the knowledge at speed.

The dating drill takes any ten claims about the doctrine, drawn at random from the candidate's notes, and requires each to be rewritten with its date, its text and its occasion attached, in a single sentence each, against a clock that allows a minute a claim. A claim that cannot survive the drill, that dissolves when its date is demanded, is exposed as exactly the kind of furniture that fills weak scripts, and the candidate learns to feel the dissolution physically, as a pause of the pen, long before an examiner can feel it. The junction drill takes a paragraph of pure narrative, a storming, a congress, a decree, and requires three junction sentences to be written for it, each stating a different relation between the event and the doctrine: confirmation, embarrassment, improvisation. Writing all three for the same paragraph teaches the hand that junctions are choices, and that the honest choice is the one the evidence in the paragraph actually supports. The steelman drill takes the critic cards and reverses them: for each opponent, the candidate writes the doctrine's reply at full strength, then the opponent's counter reply, so that the exchange can be run in either direction and the evaluation storey never depends on the question arriving from a rehearsed side.

The verdict drill addresses the close. The candidate takes a question already planned and writes three final paragraphs for it, one concluding each way and one concluding at the graded middle, possible, easy, not necessary, or its mirror. The exercise is the practical proof of this guide's central promise, that the method is conclusion neutral, and candidates who have performed it stop fearing the marker's sympathies because they have held all the verdicts in their own hand and found the road to each equally buildable. The figures drill is the briefest and the most mechanical: a page of the candidate's own earlier practice answers is audited for quantities, and every number is either given its provenance in the margin or struck. Two passes of this drill on old work permanently changes the drafting hand, which begins to attribute in the act of writing, and that change alone is worth a band on any question where the record is contested.

The last drill is the room drill, and it binds the set. The candidate takes a question and writes its first paragraph twice, once for each discipline, the history version opening on evidence and the politics version opening on a definition, using the same material. The exercise makes the two rooms palpable as styles of attention, and after a week of it the candidate reads a new question and feels the room announce itself, which is the sensation this entire guide has been constructing, one drill, one date, one attributed figure at a time.

## Narrating the revolutionary year

The history answer's basic unit is the narrated year, and the revolutionary year is the most demanding narrative in the field because so much of it is turning point and so little of it is background. Weak scripts narrate it as a list of crises survived by the eventual winners. Strong scripts narrate it as a sequence of transfers, of authority, of allegiance, of initiative, in which each month changes what the next month can contain. This section walks the year once at answer speed, exposing the joints a candidate should articulate, because a narrative whose joints are visible is already an argument.

The February collapse is the joint on which everything else turns, and its examinable essence is that nobody made it. Bread queues and strikes in the capital drew the garrison into mutiny, the tsar abdicated with no successor able or willing to hold the throne, and authority fell into the street, where two institutions picked it up: the Duma committee that formed the Provisional Government, holding the forms of the state, and the Petrograd Soviet, holding the workers and soldiers whose obedience any government would need. The candidate's sentence for February should therefore be about the duality bequeathed to every later month: from the first week, Russia had a government that could decree and a power that could prevent, and the year's story is the story of that arrangement's unbearable prolongation. Dual power is not a label for the period. It is the mechanism of the period, and an answer that states the mechanism can explain the year's rhythm, in which the government repeatedly announced what the soviet's constituency repeatedly vetoed, above all the continuation of the war.

The April return inserted into that mechanism a will determined to break it. The theses demanded the duality be ended in the soviets favour at the moment when the government still believed the duality was a partnership, and the party's own resistance to its leader's line is the month's most useful evidence, since it proves the line was an argument and not an inevitability. June and July then tested the mechanism to destruction in public. The government's offensive at the front collapsed and took the army's remaining discipline with it; the July Days, an armed movement of soldiers and workers that the Bolsheviks first tried to restrain and then to lead, were suppressed with the party's leaders arrested or fled and its press smashed, and the summer's obituaries for the party were written in every chancellery and newspaper office in the capital. The candidate who narrates July as a defeat within a mechanism rather than as a misfortune recovers its analytical value: the duality survived the days, the government that emerged was too weak to disarm the soviet's constituency, and the party's organisation, precisely because it had been built for illegality in another century's argument, survived its own proscription with an ease that astonished its enemies.

August supplied the year's great reversal in the Kornilov affair, when the government's commander in chief moved troops on the capital, in circumstances still argued over, and the government found itself defended by the very forces it had spent July prosecuting. The soviet organisations, the railwaymen, the armed workers and the Bolsheviks among them stopped the advance, and the party emerged from proscription as the capital's effective defender, its prestige and its rifles restored together. September translated the prestige into institutions: Bolshevik majorities in the Petrograd and Moscow soviets, the chairmanship of the Petrograd Soviet passing to Trotsky, and the demand for a congress of soviets to take power acquiring a constituency. October then reads, in a properly jointed narrative, not as a thunderbolt but as the last transfer in the sequence, executed by the soviet's own Military Revolutionary Committee under cover of defending the congress, against a government whose authority had already departed in the earlier transfers and whose fall the congress ratified before dispersing its rivals. The candidate who has narrated the year this way can meet the coup question on its strongest ground, because the narrative itself has become the evidence about what kind of act October was, an insurrection prepared by a committee inside a transfer of allegiance that a year of duality had made available.

The months after the seizure belong to the same narrative discipline. The November elections and the January dispersal are the transfer of legitimacy refused; the December founding of the Cheka is the besieged state's answer to its own insecurity; the peace negotiations at Brest Litovsk are the duality's foreign form, a government purchasing survival with territory while its own coalition broke over the price. Each event should enter an answer attached to the transfer it performed, of power, of legitimacy, of security, of time. Narration of that kind is the history discipline's version of the dating habit, and it is learnable in exactly the same way, by walking the year aloud until the joints click in order and the candidate discovers that the argument was inside the story all along, waiting for the telling to expose it.

## For students and exam candidates

Your preparation divides by room, so divide your week by room as well. For the history paper, hold the year in your head as transfers of authority, know the camps and their historians by name, and never let a number into an answer without its provenance attached. For the politics paper, hold the eight texts with their dates, define the six treacherous terms before you use them, and keep a critic ready for every tenet, Luxemburg for the party, Kautsky for democracy, Martov for substitution, Plekhanov for the timetable. Across both rooms, the drills govern: date every claim you practise, write junction sentences until they come unbidden, and end every practice answer with a verdict that the answer has earned rather than announced. When the question frightens you, return to the matrix row, discipline, content, evaluation, trap, and write the row before you write the essay. The topic rewards the prepared out of proportion to its difficulty, because its traps are known, its critics are named and its hinge is a single date. Prepare the operations and the marks follow the operations.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

Teach the topic as two disciplines from the first lesson, and resist the timetable's pressure to merge them into a single narrative of the man and his movement. In history teaching, the transferable gains are provenance and scale: students who learn to grade the German gold allegation, fact, allegation, forgery, have learned a skill that serves every subsequent topic, and students who can distinguish the seizure from the revolution can distinguish any event from its process. In politics teaching, the gains are dating and definition: a cohort that attaches a year to every quotation as a reflex will write about every thinker better, and the controversy over the pamphlet's status is the finest classroom demonstration available of how a text's meaning depends on its occasion. Use the exchanges as seminar architecture, one camp's text against the reply each week, and set the record questions as exercises in attribution rather than as debates to be won. Assessment literacy should be taught openly: students who have seen the matrix, the drills and the marker's desk view stop writing for an imagined hanging judge and start writing for criteria, which is the moment their marks begin to move.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The examination treatment offered here simplifies a scholarly field whose frontiers remain active, and the simplifications are signposted rather than hidden. The archival opening transformed the evidentiary base for the terror, the party's internal life and the succession, and the arguments among Pipes, Fitzpatrick, Rabinowitch and the post revisionist generation continue to turn on documents as much as on disposition. Researchers will note the points where this guide's teaching rules flatten live disputes: the scale and systematisation of repression, the weight of ideology against circumstance in the regime's radicalisation, the reading of the late writings as repudiation or as management. Journalists covering the afterlife of the doctrine, its codified forms, its mausoleum politics and its contested anniversaries, will find the distinction between the development and the codification the most portable tool in the guide, since most public confusion on the subject is a confusion of those two objects. The guide's disciplines, date, attribute, steelman, are offered as the scholarly conscience in examination dress.

## Why the Reds won: the civil war as an answer

One history question stands beside the coup question in frequency and in the marks it can carry, and this guide would be incomplete without working it: why did the Bolsheviks win the civil war? The question looks like a request for a list, and weak scripts answer it with one, geography, terror, White disunity, foreign half heartedness, Trotsky's train, ticked off in descending order of the candidate's confidence. The strong answer treats the list as raw material for an argument about a system under stress, because the examinable truth is that the causes interacted, and the interactions are where the marks are. This section builds the answer at the strength the question rewards, and it doubles as a final exercise in every discipline the guide has taught, dating, attribution, the interaction of ideology and circumstance, and the refusal of the single cause.

Begin with the ground itself, since geography in this answer is analysis and not scenery. The regime held the centre: the two capitals, the industrial heartland, the hub of the railway web, the arsenals and the population mass, while its enemies held the rim, Siberia, the south, the north west, separated from each other by the very distances the centre's railways abolished for its owner. Interior lines meant the Red Army could shift forces between fronts that the Whites could not coordinate across, and the point deserves its full weight in an answer because it explains how a regime repeatedly near collapse on one front survived to fight the next. Add the demographic and industrial asymmetry and the ground has done half the argument's work before a single decision is introduced, which is exactly why the strong answer states it first and then refuses to let it decide the matter alone.

The White failure is the second storey, and it must be built as the Whites' contemporaries and historians have established it, not as the victors' caricature. The White movement never became a government: its leaders, Kolchak in Siberia, Denikin and then Wrangel in the south, Yudenich before Petrograd, held incompatible politics, restored landlords in their rear where they ruled at all, alienating the peasantry whose land settlement was the revolution's most popular act, and offered the border nations a slogan of Russia one and indivisible that drove Poles, Finns, Ukrainians and Cossacks toward neutrality or toward Moscow. Their foreign patrons supplied materiel unevenly and withdrew in stages as war weariness consumed the intervening powers, a pattern the candidate should describe with its chronology rather than with a verdict on allied sincerity. The analytical point is comparative: the Reds offered the countryside a land settlement already enacted and an enemy that could be described as restoring the landlords, while the Whites offered order at a price the villages had already refused to pay, and civil wars are decided in villages more often than in capitals.

The Red construction is the third storey, and it is where biography and doctrine re enter the history answer at full strength. The Red Army was built, against the doctrine's own earlier instincts, as a regular army of conscription, tens of thousands of former tsarist officers employed under the supervision of political commissars, its discipline enforced with a severity its commander, Trotsky, applied to his own side's failures as publicly as to the enemy's. War communism, whatever its later doctrinal career, functioned during the fighting as an extraction machine that fed the army and the cities at the countryside's expense, and the terror functioned in part as the regime's answer to its own administrative weakness, a shortcut where institutions were absent. The candidate should state these functions without converting them into justifications, and should set beside them the costs that the same record documents: the peasant revolts that multiplied as the Whites receded, Tambov and a hundred lesser risings, and the Kronstadt explosion that announced, at the moment of victory, that the extraction machine had consumed the alliance on which the regime stood. The New Economic Policy then closes the answer's arc, since a victory followed immediately by a strategic retreat is itself the strongest evidence about what the victory had cost and what it had not secured.

The judgement storey weighs the storeys against each other, and it has a characteristic strong form: the war was won by the interaction of a centre that could be held, an enemy that could not unite its rear, and a regime willing to build an army and an extraction state with a ruthlessness its opponents matched in cruelty but not in organisation. Candidates should notice what that form excludes. It excludes the verdict that the outcome was inevitable, which the year's repeated near collapses refute, and it excludes the verdict that terror alone explains it, which the Whites' own terror and the peasants' discrimination between the two sides refute together. It also excludes the sentimental verdict that the better cause prevailed, a judgement the record of the aftermath makes difficult to sign. What remains is an explanation with weights attached, each weight defended in a sentence, which is the most an examination has ever asked of a candidate and the least this question, properly read, will accept.

## The doctrine among the theorists of organisation

Undergraduate politics papers love a placement question, in which the doctrine must be set among the general theorists of power and organisation and judged by their standards as well as by its own. Candidates often treat such questions as an invitation to stray from the topic, and wander gratefully into an essay about bureaucracy in general. The disciplined answer does the opposite: it uses the general theorists as instruments for measuring the doctrine, three comparisons in particular, and returns from each with a sharper account of the thing being examined. This section supplies the three, with the use of each stated as plainly as the fact.

Robert Michels is the first instrument, and the sharpest. Writing before the world war from inside the German socialist movement he had studied at close quarters, Michels announced an iron law of oligarchy: organisation, however democratic its purpose, generates a leadership stratum with interests of its own, and the organised party becomes an end in itself for the men who staff it. The comparison writes its own evaluation. The doctrine of the party was formulated in the same decades, against the same movement's drift, by a man who believed discipline could be kept democratic by vigilance, recall and the pressure of a revolutionary class in motion. The governing years then staged the experiment Michels had theorised: a party of hundreds of thousands acquiring a paid apparatus, an apparatus acquiring a general secretary, and the founder, in his final letters, describing the concentration of authority at the apparatus's summit as the regime's mortal danger. A candidate does not need to award Michels the victory outright; the strongest answers note that the founder saw the law operating and fought it with the instruments his doctrine possessed, congresses, factions, publicity, and that the instruments failed after his withdrawal for reasons the succession section has already weighed. The comparison's gift is that it converts the evaluation of the party model from an exchange of slogans into a test against a named, dated, falsifiable claim by a theorist with no stake in the Russian outcome.

Max Weber is the second instrument, measuring the regime rather than the doctrine. Weber, who followed the Russian revolutions with an expert's attention, left political theory the vocabulary of authority's kinds and of bureaucracy as the fate of every large organisation, together with the distinction between the ethic of conviction and the ethic of responsibility in political action. Set against the governing years, the vocabulary performs immediate work: a movement legitimised by charisma and by a doctrine of history's direction was obliged, within months of taking power, to routinise itself into offices, files and ranks, and its founder's late complaints about the apparatus are, in Weber's terms, the charisma's founder discovering routinisation from inside. The ethic distinction frames the record questions with a precision candidates can borrow: the decrees of the terror and the treaty of Brest Litovsk can each be argued, and were argued by their makers and their critics, as collisions between the ethic of ultimate ends and the ethic of consequences, and an answer that names the collision has given its evaluation a structure older and stronger than the polemics of either camp. Weber also supplies the succession question with its coldest formulation, since the problem of who inherits charisma, and by what procedure, is the problem the regime never solved and the doctrine never theorised.

The third instrument is the comparison the setters reach for most and candidates handle worst, the placing of the doctrine beside Marx himself on the question of the state, which this guide has treated in the worked questions and returns to here only to mark its difference from the other two comparisons. Michels and Weber are external measures, theorists of organisation and authority against whom the doctrine is tested from outside. The Marx comparison is an internal measure, a question of inheritance, and it must be answered from the texts in the point by point manner already taught, not from impressions of tone. Confusing the two kinds of comparison is a recognised script fault: candidates write about Marx as though he were a rival commentator on the doctrine rather than its source, or about Weber as though the doctrine were obliged to agree with him. An answer that announces which kind of measure it is applying, inheritance or external test, and applies it consistently for a paragraph, earns the marker's confidence in a way that survives even disagreement with the measurements themselves. That confidence, accumulated across a script, is finally what the top band is made of.

## Names, calendars and small accuracies

A short section on the small accuracies closes the teaching, because markers form their impression of a script's reliability from its smallest furniture long before its argument has unfolded. Three clusters of detail recur in this field, and each is cheap to master and expensive to neglect. The first is the calendar. Russia kept the Julian calendar until the new government adopted the Gregorian in the winter after the seizure, which is why the February revolution is February in the Russian reckoning and March in the western one, and why the October seizure is October in its own name and the seventh of November in the modern date. An answer that gives both styles once, correctly, and then proceeds consistently in one of them, advertises a candidate who knows that the sources themselves disagree about what day it is and has decided how to handle the disagreement. An answer that mixes styles silently manufactures errors no marker can distinguish from ignorance.

The second cluster is the names of parties and factions, which encode arguments. Bolshevik and Menshevik descend from the majority and minority of a single vote at the congress of 1903, a division over an agenda item that named two wings for a generation, and the candidate who knows the origin can explain the otherwise baffling fact that the supposed minority was often the larger faction in the years that followed. The Socialist Revolutionaries divided into a right that dominated the assembly elections and a left that entered the revolutionary government and left it over the peace, and an answer that writes of the SRs without the distinction will mishandle both the elections and the coalition. Transliteration is the third small matter: the sources spell the protagonists several ways across editions and languages, and the candidate's duty is consistency within the answer rather than allegiance to one system, since a script that argues with itself about a spelling invites the marker to wonder what else it argues about. Small accuracies are not pedantry in a subject whose big accuracies, dates, figures, attributions, are the whole discipline. They are the discipline at its most visible, worn on the script's sleeve.

## The one-page plan

Every discipline in this guide can be folded into a single page, and the candidate should perform the folding in the final week, because the act of compression is itself the last and best revision. The page carries the timeline in two interleaved columns, events on the left and texts on the right, joined by a visible line at the hinge. Down its middle run the five crossings, party, soviets, war, peasants, state, each with its near side and far side positions in a phrase apiece. Along its foot stand the names that must never be missing: the critics with their objections in five words each, the historians with their camps in three. In its corner, in the candidate's own hand, the three standing prohibitions: no undated quotation, no unattributed figure, no verdict before its evidence. A page so built is not a crib, since nothing on it can be copied into an answer directly. It is the subject's skeleton, and the candidate who can redraw it from memory on the morning of the paper, in five minutes, without a hesitation at the hinge, owns the topic in the only sense examinations recognise: as a structure that survives nerves, fatigue and an unfamiliar phrasing, and that will assemble itself into an answer around whatever question the setters have chosen to ask.

The folding has one further use that candidates discover too late when they skip it. Material that will not compress is material that has not been understood, only stored, and the page exposes the stored patches mercilessly: the text whose claim cannot be phrased, the episode whose inference cannot be named, the critic whose objection blurs into a mood. Each failure on the page is a precise diagnosis, pointing at the exact card that needs rebuilding while rebuilding is still possible. Candidates should therefore fold early enough to act on the diagnosis, treating the first attempt at the page as a survey of their own gaps and the second as the revision those gaps demanded. The finished page, carried in the head rather than the bag, is the guide's whole method at pocket size, and it is enough.

A word about what the page deliberately leaves off, since omission is half its design. It carries no casualty figure, because figures travel with their attributions or not at all, and attributions live on the camp cards where the argument about them can be rehearsed. It carries no verdict on the succession, because a verdict memorised is a verdict that will be announced too early, in defiance of everything the page exists to protect. It carries no quotation longer than a line, because the quotations that matter are the ones the candidate can locate in a dated text and defend word by word. What remains when the omissions have done their work is a page of pure structure, dates, names, crossings and prohibitions, and structure is the one cargo that never burdens the candidate carrying it into the hall. Candidates who have built the page report the same small transformation, which teachers of the topic will recognise: the subject stops feeling like a crowd of facts competing for allegiance and starts feeling like a building whose rooms they can walk through in the dark, finding each argument in its place, each date on its shelf, each critic waiting in the doorway where the evaluation will need a voice.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you write an exam answer on Lenin?

Begin by deciding which discipline set the question, because history and politics reward different material under the same name. Date every position you use, on whichever side of the revolutionary spring it falls. Build the answer in storeys: a short opening that fixes the period, a body that keeps the life and the doctrine on separate tracks, an evaluation that stages a named critic, and a verdict placed at the end where the evidence can pay for it. Attribute every figure, quote sparingly and only from texts you can locate, and let the plan be written before the prose.

### Q: What is the biggest mistake in exam answers on Lenin?

Candidates lose the most ground by collapsing the founder and his successor into one undifferentiated figure, writing as if the system of the nineteen thirties were simply the doctrine of the revolutionary years unfolded. Examiners treat the link between the two men as a problem to be argued, with continuities and ruptures weighed on evidence, so a script that presupposes the link in either direction surrenders the analytical marks before the argument starts. A companion fault is leaning on the exile pamphlet about party organisation as though it were a constitution for the whole career, ignoring its author's later qualifications.

### Q: Which Lenin texts should you cite in an exam?

Eight texts carry nearly every use. The pamphlet of the exile years for the party model and the argument about consciousness, the tactics pamphlet of the first revolution for the alliance formula and the stage scheme, the wartime study of imperialism for the world economy and its strategic conclusion, the April Theses for the great reversal, the book on the state written in hiding for the Commune model, the polemic against Kautsky for the defence of proletarian dictatorship, the pamphlet against left wing impatience for the mature tactics, and the dictated letters of the final winter for the succession. Attach a date and one precise claim to each.

### Q: How do you handle the coup question in an essay?

Refuse the word as a verdict and accept it as a hypothesis. Set out what the description claims, a seizure of power by an organised minority without a popular mandate, and what the rival description claims, a transfer ratified by elected councils whose majorities had swung behind the insurrection's slogans. Then test both against the autumn evidence: the committee planning and the capital's mechanics on one side, the garrison politics, the provincial spread and the congress vote on the other. Name the historians who hold each reading, and reserve your own judgement for a close that the rehearsed evidence has visibly earned.

### Q: Is it safe to criticise Lenin in an exam?

Yes, and it is equally safe to defend him, because marking rewards operations rather than conclusions. Criteria pay for evidence handled with provenance, positions fixed in time, opposing cases stated fairly and judgements that arrive after the weighing, and moderation exists to catch any marker who pays for agreement instead. What is unsafe is the unargued verdict in either direction, the denunciation that substitutes for the record or the reverence that declines to meet it. State the position you are taking, name the evidence carrying it, give the strongest counter case its full force, and the conclusion can fall where it honestly falls.

### Q: Which historians should you name on the Russian Revolution?

Anchor each camp with a name and a work. Richard Pipes carries the reading of October as a coup executed by a determined minority amid collapsing authority. Sheila Fitzpatrick carries the social history reading, in which the revolution overflows every party's intentions. Alexander Rabinowitch supplies the most densely documented account of the seizure itself, awkward for both camps in useful ways. Orlando Figes narrates the tragedy through the old order's loss of legitimacy, Edward Hallett Carr built the documentary scaffolding, and Stephen Cohen with Moshe Lewin serve the succession debate. Name the historian with the evidence each argues from, never as decoration.

### Q: How do you use the April Theses in an answer?

Use them as the hinge made visible. Summarise the demands briefly, no support for the Provisional Government, power to the soviets, the land and the banks under new control, then spend your words on what the document did: it discarded a stage theory the movement had held for decades, it was resisted and initially voted down inside the author's own party, and it reset the party's tasks for the rest of the year. The theses earn their place in an answer as evidence that doctrine in this tradition changed by argument at identifiable moments, which is the premise of every dating move the strong answer makes.

### Q: Which quotations work for a Lenin answer?

Short ones, certainly worded, one to a text, each attached to the claim it concentrates. The assertion that class political consciousness reaches workers only from outside their immediate economic struggle carries the whole organisational argument of the exile pamphlet. The bleak sentence from the book on the state, that while the state exists freedom does not, sets the doctrine's promise against the apparatus that followed. The slogan demanding all power for the soviets compresses the April reversal into five words. For evaluation, Luxemburg's defence of the freedom of the one who thinks differently has outlasted every rival formulation. When wording is uncertain, paraphrase and attribute instead.

### Q: How do you write about casualty figures safely?

Never assert a total in your own voice. The scholarly estimates for the terror, the civil war and the famine diverge by multiples, and examiners treat a confident unsourced figure as a certificate that a script's quantities are folklore. Work instead with attributed ranges, naming the historians whose estimates you report and the direction of their disagreement, or describe scale qualitatively with precise terms tied to documented practices, decrees, categories of arrest, the growth of the camp system. Then pivot to the questions evidence can settle: whether repression was driven from the centre or the localities, and whether it preceded or followed the emergencies invoked to explain it.

### Q: How do you revise the revolution and Leninism together?

Build one timeline with two kinds of entries interleaved, events and texts sharing a single sequence, and revise from card families rather than from folders: date cards for the episodes, text cards carrying a claim and its author's later qualification, critic cards with the objection and the honest residue, camp cards with a historian and the evidence each prefers. Practise with timed plans drawn from past questions, three plans for every full answer, and audit each finished answer for the standing faults: undated positions, unattributed numbers, an evaluation without a named opponent, a verdict that arrived before its evidence.

### Q: How do you structure an essay on whether Leninism continued Marx?

Compare point by point and refuse the single adjective. Choose four grounds, the party, the state, the peasantry and the revolutionary timetable. On each ground state Marx's position from its text, then the doctrine's position from its dated text, then choose the precise verb the relation deserves: continued, extended, revised or reversed. The four verdicts will seldom agree, and their disagreement is the essay's distinction. Bring the contemporaries in as evaluation, Plekhanov guarding the timetable, Luxemburg pressing organisation, Kautsky defending democracy, and close by weighing the grounds against each other and defending the weighing itself.

### Q: What should a teacher mark first in a Lenin essay?

Mark the disciplines before the details. Check first whether the script knows which room it is in, whether the material matches the question's discipline or drifts into the other one. Check second for dating, whether positions and quotations carry their years or float in a timeless present, since that single habit predicts the script's ceiling. Check third for attribution, of figures to their historians and of objections to their named critics. Only then mark content errors, because a dated, attributed, room aware script with a factual slip is a strong script under repair, while a fluent timeless one is a weak script in disguise.

### Q: How do you use the April Theses without overstating them?

Remember what the document was and what it was not. It was a set of ten theses delivered to party audiences and printed in the party paper, a fighting programme for a factional argument its author initially lost, not a government decree and not the party's settled line until the conference adopted its substance weeks later. It did not nationalise industry outright, it proposed control and staged measures, and it did not predict the civil war's regime. Credit the theses with the reversal they performed, the abandonment of the old stage formula, and resist inflating them into a blueprint for everything that followed, which the timeline contradicts.

### Q: Why does the two-discipline answer earn marks in an evaluation paragraph?

Because it hands the evaluation two independent standards instead of one borrowed opinion. The doctrinal track supplies the internal standard, what the texts promised at their dates, most sharply the Commune model of the revolutionary summer. The biographical and historical track supplies the external record, what the government did and what its author later said about the apparatus he had built. An evaluation paragraph that measures the record against the doctrine's own dated promises performs immanent critique, the most defensible operation in the field, and markers can reward every sentence of it because each claim is attached either to a text or to a documented act.

### Q: How do you plan revision across Lenin's life, writings and the revolution as one topic?

Walk one timeline three ways. Walk it first as narrative, the schoolboy of Simbirsk becoming the exile, the exile becoming the head of a besieged government, the strokes and the suppressed letters closing the story, until every episode can be placed without hesitation. Walk it again as doctrine, pamphlet, tactics, imperialism study, theses, the book on the state, the polemics, the retreat and the late reckoning, each claim fixed to its year. Walk it a third time alternating, event, text, event, text, until the joins are fluent and the life explains the writings while the writings interrogate the life. Then practise plans against past questions until the rooms announce themselves on first reading.
