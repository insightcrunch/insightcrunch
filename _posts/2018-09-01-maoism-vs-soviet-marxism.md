Maoism and Soviet Marxism began as two branches of one inheritance, and for roughly two decades they presented themselves to the world as a single camp with a single doctrine. The quarrel that divided them is often retold as a contest between two states, two leaders and two national interests, and that telling is not false so much as radically incomplete. Beneath the diplomatic rupture sat a disagreement about the theory itself, stated in documents on both sides, argued in the vocabulary both parties shared, and consequential enough that each side accused the other of abandoning Marxism altogether. The disagreement can be named in one sentence. Soviet doctrine held that antagonistic classes had been abolished inside socialist society, so internal class struggle had ended; Chinese doctrine held that class struggle continues under socialism and that a new bourgeoisie could be generated within the ruling party itself. Almost everything else in the split either fed into that divergence or flowed out of it.

![A reading desk with Soviet and Chinese party documents, annotated maps and a comparison notebook, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-94.webp)

This article is a comparison built around that centre. It sets out seven differences between the two doctrines, gives each position in the form its own documents gave it, and then shows why one of the seven behaves differently from the rest. The agent of revolution, the strategy for taking power, the method of party leadership, the assessment of Stalin, and the strategy toward war between the systems were all real divergences, argued with passion and with consequences. Yet the dispute over continuing revolution was generative in a way the others were not. Affirm it, and a ruling party acquires a theoretical reason to turn revolution inward against its own apparatus, which is the road that led to the Cultural Revolution. Deny it, and a ruling party receives a theoretical guarantee that its own order cannot be challenged from within in class terms, the road to a bureaucratic stability no internal critic could legitimately disturb. The namable claim of this article, the continuing-revolution generator, is that two of the gravest outcomes of twentieth-century communism follow from opposite answers to a single question.

What belongs here, and only here, is the comparison itself.

### Why did two allied parties come to denounce each other as traitors to a shared doctrine?

Each side held a theory of what socialism does to class relations, and each theory made the other's practice illegitimate by definition. If antagonistic classes had vanished, then a campaign against a party apparatus could only be factional vandalism. If a bourgeoisie could regenerate inside the apparatus, then declaring class struggle finished could only be a screen for privilege.

A word about method before the detail begins. Doctrinal disputes inside this tradition were never merely semantic, because the parties involved treated theory as a licence for action. A formulation adopted at a congress could redirect the lives of millions, which is why the polemics were conducted with a ferocity that puzzles readers who expect philosophy to be quieter. When the Chinese side published its nine comments on the Soviet open letter during 1963 and 1964, and when the Soviet side answered through its own press and party channels, both were doing something more than scoring points. Each was attempting to fix the meaning of Marxism for the world movement, and each understood that the loser of the argument would lose authority over parties on several continents. The stakes explain the tone, and the tone should not be mistaken for evidence that nothing intellectual was at stake.

It also matters that the two doctrines were not symmetrical products. Soviet Marxism by the nineteen fifties was a codified orthodoxy, taught through standard textbooks, anchored in a state that had existed for four decades, and presented as the settled science of society. The Chinese doctrine was younger, formed in a revolution completed only in 1949, carried by a party that had spent most of its life in rural warfare rather than in government, and still being written by a leader who treated theoretical essays as instruments of ongoing struggle. One side therefore defended a finished system while the other advanced a developing one, and part of the bitterness came from that asymmetry. The custodians of the finished system heard improvisation as heresy. The improvisers heard custodianship as embalming.

## One disagreement that generated the rest

Strip the split to its skeleton and a single bone carries the weight. In the Soviet account, the expropriation of the old possessing classes removed the material foundation of antagonism between classes. What remained after the nineteen thirties were two friendly classes, workers and peasants, together with a stratum of intellectuals, and their relations might involve friction, criticism and even sharp administrative conflict, but not class struggle in the strict sense. The state built on that foundation could therefore be redescribed, in the party programme adopted in 1961, as a state belonging to the whole people rather than an instrument of one class against another. Internal politics became a matter of administration, education and vigilance against external enemies and survivals, not a continuation of revolution by other means.

The Chinese account denied the premise at its root. Ownership, on this reading, settles less than the Soviet textbooks claimed. A society that has abolished private ownership of the principal means of production still distributes income, authority, education and access unequally, still carries the habits and ideas of the old order, and still staffs its commanding institutions with people whose position can harden into privilege. Under those conditions, the Chinese documents argued, bourgeois influence does not merely survive as a remnant. It is continually produced, and its most dangerous site of production is inside the communist party and the state apparatus themselves, where control over allocation can substitute for legal title. Hence the conclusion that gave the doctrine its name: revolution cannot be declared complete at the moment of expropriation, because the struggle between the socialist road and the capitalist road continues throughout the entire period of socialism.

Notice how much follows once that premise is granted or refused. Grant it, and a campaign against party officials accused of taking the capitalist road becomes, in theory, a continuation of the revolution rather than an attack on the revolution's own government. Refuse it, and the same campaign becomes unintelligible except as a power struggle dressed in borrowed vocabulary. Grant it, and the Soviet Union itself can be redescribed as a society where a privileged stratum has restored capitalist relations behind socialist forms. Refuse it, and that redescription collapses into slander against a fraternal state. The premise is a hinge. Swing it one way and the Cultural Revolution acquires a doctrinal charter; swing it the other and the Soviet order acquires a doctrinal shield. This is why the present article treats continuing revolution as the generator and the other six differences as its companions rather than its equals.

The hinge also explains a feature of the polemics that otherwise looks like pedantry. Both sides quoted Lenin obsessively, because both needed the founder's authority for a question Lenin had not settled in their terms. The Soviet side leaned on the proposition that classes are defined by their relationship to the means of production, from which it followed that a stratum without property could not be a bourgeoisie. The Chinese side leaned on Lenin's warnings that the small producer engenders capitalism daily and hourly, and on his insistence that the transition period is an entire epoch of struggle. Each canon was genuine. The dispute was over which strand of the inheritance governed a situation neither founder had lived to see: a communist party in power for decades, administering a society it had already expropriated.

### Which difference did the participants themselves treat as deepest?

Their own polemical choices give the answer. The public exchanges of the nineteen sixties returned again and again to whether classes and class struggle persist after expropriation, because both sides understood the other quarrels could be compromised while this one admitted no midpoint. A doctrine either licenses revolution against the apparatus or it forbids the very concept.

There is a further reason to place this disagreement at the centre, and it concerns explanation rather than fidelity to the participants. A good comparison should account for outcomes, not merely catalogue opinions. The agent and strategy differences explain how the Chinese party won power in 1949; they are differences about the road to a revolution. The continuing-revolution difference explains what each regime did with power once the revolution was won, and it is the only one of the seven that predicts the characteristic pathology of each system. Where the doctrine denied internal antagonism, the pathology was immobility protected by theory. Where the doctrine affirmed internal antagonism without limit, the pathology was convulsion authorised by theory. Two pathologies, one question: that symmetry is the strongest evidence that the comparison has found its centre.


## Maoism as a name: three things the word can mean

Comparison requires clean labels, and the labels in this dispute are unusually treacherous. Within Chinese party usage, the authorised term was Mao Zedong Thought, denoting the body of ideas attributed to the Chinese leader and, in the party's own formula, to the collective wisdom of the Chinese party as he expressed it. That body of doctrine was presented as the application of universal Marxist truth to Chinese conditions, not as a new stage of the theory itself. The word Maoism, by contrast, was largely a term used by outsiders, by opponents, and later by movements abroad that wished to claim the Chinese experience as a general model. When, in the late nineteen eighties, a Peruvian party codified Marxism-Leninism-Maoism as a third and higher stage of the doctrine, it was making a claim the Chinese party itself had never made in that form. Keeping these usages apart prevents a common error: attributing to Beijing a universal codification that was in fact produced elsewhere, after the period of the split itself.

Soviet Marxism needs the same care. The label here denotes the doctrine as codified in the Soviet Union from the nineteen thirties onward: dialectical and historical materialism in textbook form, a theory of the state and of classes derived from the experience of the first socialist country, and a set of strategic propositions about war, coexistence and the world movement that hardened into orthodoxy. It was not identical with everything Soviet thinkers wrote, since scholarship inside that world contained variety and occasional quiet dissent. But as an official doctrine, taught to cadres and exported to fraternal parties, it had a definite shape, and it is that shape which entered into collision with the Chinese alternative. The comparison in this article is between two official doctrines and the movements that carried them, not between two civilisations and not between two national characters.

A third label deserves a sentence. Marxism-Leninism was the inheritance both sides claimed, and the ferocity of the dispute came partly from the fact that neither would concede the inheritance to the other. Each presented itself as the faithful continuation and the other as the deviation: revisionism in the Soviet case as the Chinese charged it, dogmatism and adventurism in the Chinese case as the Soviet side replied. A reader who keeps in mind that both parties were arguing over a will, each convinced it was the legitimate heir, will understand why compromise formulations satisfied nobody. The argument was never only about policy. It was about custody of a tradition that both regarded as the science of revolution.

## The seven differences grid

The findable artifact of this article is the seven differences grid, which compresses the comparison into a single instrument. Read down the rows for the range of the dispute; read across each row for its internal logic, since every row pairs a Soviet formulation with a Chinese counter-formulation, names a document where the position was stated, and records what followed when the position was acted upon. The final row is the generator discussed throughout the article, and its consequences column is correspondingly the longest shadow.

A grid flattens what narrative complicates, and the flattening should be acknowledged rather than disguised. The rows were not independent in life. The Chinese confidence about peasant war grew out of the same decades that produced the mass line, and the Soviet confidence about classes grew out of the same experience that produced the textbook system. Nor did every participant weigh the rows equally: Soviet polemics often treated the Chinese positions on war as the most dangerous, because they touched the survival of states, while Chinese polemics treated the class question as the essence, because it touched the legitimacy of regimes. The grid is a map for study. The sections that follow restore the terrain, one difference at a time, before the verdict weighs them against each other.

### What does a grid reveal that a narrative of quarrels hides?

Sequence can make a dispute look like a chain of accidents, each clash provoking the next. A grid shows structure instead: the same premises recurring inside different controversies, so that an argument about agricultural policy and an argument about foreign policy turn out to be one argument wearing two coats. Structure lets a reader predict the next clash.

## The revolutionary agent: peasants and workers

Classical doctrine assigned the leading role in revolution to the industrial working class, and the Soviet experience appeared to confirm the assignment. The October insurrection had been made in a capital city, by workers and soldiers organised through soviets, in a country where the proletariat was a minority but a concentrated and strategically placed one. Soviet strategy generalised that experience. In the textbooks exported to fraternal parties, the working class was the hegemonic force of any revolution worthy of the name, the peasantry its principal ally, valuable and necessary yet incapable by itself of generating a socialist project. A revolution led in the countryside, by an army recruited from peasants, fighting for demands that were at first agrarian and national, sat awkwardly inside that schema, and Soviet advisers said so, repeatedly, during the decades when the Chinese party was building exactly such a revolution.

The Chinese answer grew from investigation rather than from embarrassment. The report on the peasant movement in Hunan, written in 1927 after weeks of travelling through counties in revolt, argued that the poor peasantry was the most revolutionary stratum in Chinese society, the force whose energy would decide whether the revolution meant anything beyond a change of flags in the cities. Later formulations systematised the insight. In a semi-colonial and semi-feudal country, the argument ran, the principal contradiction was not between a mature proletariat and a mature bourgeoisie but between the nation and imperialism, and between the peasantry and the feudal order that weighed on it. The party of the proletariat would lead, but it would lead a war whose soldiers, supplies, territory and immediate demands were overwhelmingly peasant. Leadership and social composition were prised apart: a party could be proletarian in programme while its army was peasant in composition, provided its ideology and discipline held.

Soviet critics found the prising apart suspect, and their suspicion had a theoretical address. If the class character of a movement is read off from its programme and its leading party rather than from the social weight of its participants, then the criterion of class has quietly migrated from sociology to self-description. The Chinese side answered that the October precedent itself involved a party leading classes beyond its own, and that the alternative reading would disqualify most actual revolutions in the colonial world in advance. The argument matters beyond its period because it set a pattern. Wherever later movements in Asia, Africa or Latin America claimed Marxist leadership for peasant wars, they were working, knowingly or not, inside the space this Chinese answer had opened, and their Soviet-aligned critics were working inside the objection.

### Why did the Chinese party make the peasantry its principal force?

Because the society it confronted left no other mass force available at the required scale. The industrial working class was small, concentrated in treaty ports exposed to foreign power, and repeatedly crushed when it rose. The peasantry was the overwhelming majority, burdened by rent, debt and taxation, and already in motion. Doctrine followed the investigation.

There is a subtler layer to the agent difference, concerning time. Soviet strategy imagined the peasantry as a class of the past, destined to be collectivised and gradually dissolved into modern agriculture once power was won. The Chinese writings treated the peasantry as a class with a present and a long future, whose habits of small production would shape socialism itself for generations. That difference in temporal imagination helps explain the later divergence over communes, over the pace of collectivisation, and over whether small production was a residue to be administered or a force to be mobilised. It also explains why the Chinese side heard Soviet advice about agriculture as the voice of a country that had already forgotten its own countryside.


## Strategy for power: protracted war against the October model

If the agent differed, the road to power had to differ with it. The Soviet model, drawn from October, centred on the insurrection: a crisis of the old regime, a disciplined party seizing the nodal points of the capital, and a new state proclaimed from the centre outward. Exported through the Communist International, that model carried an implicit timetable. Revolution was an event, prepared by organisation and propaganda, executed when the moment ripened. The Chinese experience refused the timetable at every point. Urban risings in 1927 were annihilated. The party that survived did so by retreating into rural base areas, building soviets in the countryside, and waging a war whose unit of time was not the week but the decade.

The writings on protracted war, composed in 1938 while the Japanese invasion was transforming the war's character, converted that experience into doctrine. A weaker revolutionary force, they argued, should trade space for time, preserve itself through strategic retreat, build political power in base areas behind enemy lines, and pass through stages of equilibrium and counter-offensive before any final confrontation. The cities, far from being seized at the outset, would be encircled by a countryside already organised under revolutionary government, and would fall at the end of the process rather than the beginning. Strategy here is not a detail of military technique. It encodes a theory of where power in such a society actually sits: not in the ministries of a capital that foreign gunboats could reach, but in the villages where rent was collected, grain was taxed and the state's writ thinned into the authority of landlords and magistrates.

Soviet commentary on this doctrine was ambivalent in a revealing way. The victory of 1949 could hardly be denied, and the Chinese road was duly praised as a creative application of Marxism to national conditions. Yet the praise usually carried a qualification: the Chinese case was an exception licensed by semi-colonial peculiarity, not a rival model for general use. Chinese theorists resisted the qualification. If the majority of humanity lived in countries more like China than like Russia, then the exception was the rule for most of the world, and the October model was the special case. That inversion, politely stated in the nineteen fifties and aggressively stated in the nineteen sixties, threatened the Soviet claim to be the universal centre of revolutionary experience, which is why arguments about military strategy so often sounded, in the polemics, like arguments about rank.

### What did encircling the cities require an army to become?

A government in embryo. An army that holds territory for years must tax, judge, educate, organise production and protect harvests, or lose the population. Protracted war therefore fused military and political construction into a single activity, and the party's habits of administration, propaganda and land policy were formed inside the war rather than after it.

Protracted people's war also travelled. As a phrase and as a method it became the most exportable element of the Chinese doctrine, taken up by movements that had little interest in the finer points of the class debate. Its export history is instructive for the comparison, because it shows which parts of a doctrine move easily and which do not. A strategy for fighting from the countryside can be detached from its home context and imitated wherever terrain and state weakness permit. A theory of class regeneration inside a ruling party cannot be detached in the same way, since it presupposes that the movement has already won. The international Maoism of later decades was therefore disproportionately a Maoism of the first rows of the grid, of agent and strategy, while the generative row, the one this article treats as decisive for the two regimes themselves, mattered most where parties already held power or hoped to hold it soon.

There is an irony in that distribution worth pausing over. The doctrine's most famous export was its method of taking power; its most consequential element was its theory of what power does to the takers. Movements abroad could adopt the method without the theory, and frequently did, which is one reason the label Maoism came to mean, in much journalistic and even scholarly usage, little more than rural guerrilla war under a communist flag. The comparison offered here restores the balance. For the two states whose quarrel defines the subject, the argument about classes under socialism outweighed the argument about base areas, because by the time the polemics opened, both parties were already in power and the urgent question was what their power was doing to their societies and to themselves.

## The mass line and the professional vanguard

Every Leninist party faces a question about the direction in which correct ideas travel. The Soviet answer, codified in party manuals, ran from the top downward in form even when it claimed the opposite in spirit. The vanguard, armed with theory, brings consciousness to the working class from outside its spontaneous experience, organises that consciousness into programme and discipline, and leads. Consultation with the masses was affirmed, but the epistemology was clear: truth resides in the theory and in the centre that masters it, and the task of lower levels is application. The Chinese doctrine of the mass line, stated in resolutions on leadership methods during the nineteen forties, described a different circuit. Correct ideas, on this account, originate in the scattered and unsystematic views of the masses themselves. The party's task is to gather those views, concentrate and systematise them through study, return them to the masses as policy, and test them in action, repeating the circuit without end.

The contrast should not be sentimentalised. The mass line remained a doctrine of party leadership; the concentrating was done by the party, which decided which scattered views counted as the voice of the masses and which counted as backwardness to be corrected. Chinese critics of the doctrine, and later historians, have noted how readily the circuit could become a ratification machine, in which the centre heard what it wished to hear and returned it with the authority of the people attached. Soviet polemics made a parallel point from the other direction, treating mass-line language as a populist solvent of discipline. Yet the doctrinal difference was genuine, and it had operational consequences. A party formed by the mass line was trained to investigate before prescribing, to send cadres into villages and factories as students before treating them as instructors, and to regard policy failure as evidence that the circuit had been short-circuited by commandism. A party formed by the textbook model was trained to read policy failure as evidence of poor transmission.

### How did the mass line differ from consultation as usually practised?

Consultation asks for opinion about a decision already framed elsewhere. The mass line, as its texts state it, claims that the raw material of policy, knowledge of conditions and grievances, exists only among the people who live them, and that the party's theoretical work begins after the gathering, not before it.

The mass line also connects the comparison to the generative disagreement, in a way that is easy to miss. A party that believes correct ideas circulate between leaders and led has a standing reason to fear the hardening of its own apparatus into a separate caste: the circuit breaks when the concentrators stop listening. A party that believes truth descends from a mastered theory has a different standing fear, that of dilution and indiscipline. When the Chinese doctrine later identified the party apparatus itself as the site where a new bourgeoisie could form, it was extending the mass line's suspicion of the centre into a theory of class. When Soviet doctrine replied that such suspicion dissolved the party's authority, it was defending the epistemology of the textbook. The rows of the grid lean on one another, and the leaning is part of what the grid is for.


## Stalin assessed twice

The first open crack in the camp ran through the reputation of a dead man. In February 1956 the Soviet leader addressed a closed session of his party's twentieth congress and delivered a devastating account of Stalin's cult of personality and the crimes committed under it. The speech was intended as an internal reckoning; its effects were international and uncontrollable. Communist parties everywhere faced members who had defended for decades what their own leadership now described as criminal, and the authority of the Soviet centre, which rested partly on its claim to have embodied the doctrine without deviation, was damaged by its own hand.

The Chinese response, published in editorials during 1956 on the dictatorship's historical experience, refused the wholesale character of the repudiation while accepting much of the criticism. Stalin, on the Chinese assessment, had been a great Marxist whose achievements predominated over his errors by a wide proportion, whose mistakes were real and instructive, and whose record could not be negated in its entirety without negating the Soviet experience itself. The difference was not chiefly about the facts of the terror, on which the Chinese editorials were not soft. It was about the method of judgement. Total negation, the Chinese argued, was itself a political act with consequences: it disarmed the movement before its enemies, demoralised its ranks, and handed hostile propaganda a gift it could never have manufactured alone. A proportional judgement, by contrast, allowed criticism to proceed without liquidation of the inheritance.

Beneath the methodological argument sat a self-interested one, and candour requires naming it. The Chinese leader's own position inside his party bore a family resemblance to the position Stalin had occupied inside his, and a doctrine that permitted the total posthumous negation of a founding leader was a doctrine that could one day be applied in Beijing. Soviet commentators understood this perfectly and said so in private long before they implied it in print. Yet reducing the Chinese assessment to self-protection misses its intellectual content, which later events inside China gave a bitter twist. The proportional method was also turned, in time, upon its own author: the resolution adopted by the Chinese party in 1981, assessing its leader's record after his death, applied a recognisably similar structure of predominating merit and serious error. The document is treated in this series as a document, with its date and status stated, and its verdict belongs to the biography article rather than to this comparison. What matters here is that the method of assessment became itself one of the durable differences between the two traditions: negation as rupture against judgement as proportion.

### Why did the assessment of Stalin become a test of loyalty across the world movement?

Because every party had built its authority partly on the claim that the Soviet road, Stalin included, was the proven road. A verdict on Stalin was therefore a verdict on each party's own past propaganda, its own internal regime, and its own leader's standing. Parties split over the speech not because they knew Russia well but because they knew themselves.

The Stalin row of the grid also demonstrates how a difference can be both genuine and instrumental at once. The Chinese position expressed a real methodological conviction about how revolutions should judge their dead, and it simultaneously served the Chinese party's claim to independent standing within the movement. The Soviet position expressed a real need to break with a terror that had consumed the party itself, and it simultaneously served a new leadership's need to consolidate. Doctrinal history written as if ideas float free of such uses would be naive; doctrinal history written as if the uses exhaust the ideas would be unable to explain why the arguments still repay reading after the leaders who deployed them are gone. This article aims at the harder middle course throughout: state the idea at its full strength, note the use it served, and refuse to let either cancel the other.

## Peaceful coexistence and the nature of imperialism

The second great public difference concerned the character of the age. The Soviet congress of 1956 had drawn from the existence of nuclear weapons and the changed balance of forces a strategic conclusion: war between the two systems was no longer fatally inevitable, and peaceful coexistence between states of different social systems could serve as a general line for the movement as a whole. From that conclusion flowed others. The transition to socialism might in some countries take a parliamentary road. The principal task of communist parties in the developed world became the defence of peace, around which the broadest alliances could be built. Soviet doctrine presented the package as the creative development of Leninism for the nuclear age.

Chinese polemics rejected the elevation of coexistence into a general line while accepting coexistence as a diplomatic practice between states. The distinction, which the polemics insisted upon with wearisome repetition, was between what a socialist state might prudently do in its foreign relations and what the world movement should proclaim as its strategy. Imperialism, on the Chinese account, retained its aggressive nature; paper tigers remained tigers in their effects on the peoples they bit; and a doctrine that taught the oppressed to wait upon the reasonableness of their oppressors disarmed precisely the struggles, in Asia, Africa and Latin America, where the movement's future was being decided. Behind the argument about war stood an argument about the centre of gravity of world revolution. Soviet strategy located the decisive arena in the relations between the great powers. Chinese strategy located it in the countryside of the world, the vast zones where national liberation movements were fighting, and where, in the Chinese metaphor, the imperialist cities would eventually be encircled as the Chinese cities had been.

### What did peaceful coexistence claim about the postwar world?

That its central fact had changed. Where earlier doctrine treated war between the camps as a recurring necessity produced by imperialism itself, the Soviet formulation of the nineteen fifties held that a world war could be prevented by the strength of the socialist camp and the peace movement, and that competition would be decided increasingly by economic and political means.

Historians continue to argue over how far this difference was doctrinal and how far it was the theoretical dress of state interest. A Soviet Union that had achieved nuclear parity had reasons of state to avoid a doctrine of inevitable war; a China still excluded from the United Nations and facing a hostile alliance on its coasts had reasons of state to preach the imminence of struggle. The reasons are real, and this article has no interest in denying them. But the doctrinal formulations outlived the circumstances that prompted them, were taught to two generations of cadres as the meaning of Marxism, and structured how parties on every continent chose their strategies. Ideas in this tradition were load-bearing even when they were also convenient. That double character, sincere and serviceable at once, is the normal condition of the material this series examines, and the reader who demands that a position be one or the other will misunderstand every article in the cluster.

The coexistence dispute also carried the first unmistakable signs of the split inside the realm of protocol. A Chinese attempt to state its position at a gathering of parties in 1957 produced a compromise formula about the road of October that satisfied neither capital. A meeting of eighty-one parties in 1960 papered the cracks with a statement both sides signed and both sides immediately glossed in opposite senses. By the time the polemics became open, veterans of those conferences could recognise the whole later argument in embryo: each side reading the shared texts as warrants for its own line, each accusing the other of bad faith in the reading. The episode teaches a general lesson about doctrinal movements. Shared scriptures do not prevent schism; they determine its language.


## The Soviet doctrine: antagonism abolished

To understand the generator, each side must first be built at full strength, beginning with the Soviet construction in its own terms. The construction rested on a report delivered in the nineteen thirties on the draft constitution, in which Stalin announced that the exploiting classes in the Soviet Union had been eliminated. Land, factories, banks and trade had passed into social ownership; the landlords, capitalists and kulaks as classes no longer existed; and the society that remained consisted of workers, peasants and intellectuals whose interests, whatever their frictions, were fundamentally harmonious. The report drew the theoretical conclusion explicitly. A society without antagonistic classes was a society in which the classic engine of internal upheaval had been switched off. Subsequent Soviet doctrine built the edifice out: the dictatorship of the proletariat, having completed its task, gave way in the programme of 1961 to the state of the whole people, and the party of the working class restyled itself the party of the entire people.

The construction was not stupid, and treating it as mere complacency fails the steelman standard this series applies. It had a rigorous core, which the objection section below states in its strongest form. Classes, in the canonical definition the Soviet side inherited, are groups distinguished by their relation toward the means of production. Where the means of production are socially owned, no group stands to another as owner to non-owner in production, and therefore no group can be a bourgeoisie in the strict sense, whatever its privileges, income or manners. Privilege without property might be corruption, bureaucratism or degeneration, all of which Soviet discourse could name and punish administratively. What it could not be, on the canonical definition, was a class, and a politics organised against it could not be class struggle. The conclusion followed from the definition with real logical force, and the Soviet theorists who pressed it against the Chinese position knew exactly how much force it had.

The construction also performed work inside Soviet life that its authors valued and that a fair account should record. It underwrote a promise: that the emergency was over. A state founded in revolution, hardened by collectivisation and terror, could announce to its citizens that the age of internal enemies had closed, that the remaining tasks were construction, education and the raising of living standards, and that the party's monopoly rested on accomplishment rather than on siege. For a population that had lived through decades of mobilisation, that promise was not nothing. The doctrine of abolished antagonism was, among other things, an offer of normality, and its appeal inside the Soviet Union and among war-weary parties abroad should be weighed alongside its convenience for the apparatus that proclaimed it.

### When did Soviet doctrine declare antagonistic classes abolished?

The declaration came in stages rather than at a single moment. The constitutional report of the nineteen thirties announced the elimination of the exploiting classes as an accomplished fact. The programme adopted a generation later completed the logic by redescribing the state as belonging to the whole people and the party as the entire people's party.

Against this construction the Chinese side pressed a question that the canonical definition had difficulty absorbing. If class is exhausted by legal relation to productive property, what accounts for the persistence of sharply unequal control over those means in societies where ownership is formally social? Who decides investment, allocation, appointment and reward, and in whose interest are those decisions predictably made? The Soviet framework answered with the vocabulary of administration: such decisions are made by organs of the whole people, staffed by a stratum that serves rather than owns. The Chinese framework replied with the vocabulary of power: control exercised continuously and hereditarily in effect, defended by ideology and insulated from recall, is the substance of which class domination is made, whatever the property register says. Between those two vocabularies the entire later argument was fought, and it is worth noticing that the argument is not antiquarian. Any theory of post-capitalist society, including ones being written far from this dispute, must still decide whether class follows the title deed or the hand on the lever.

## Continuing revolution: the Chinese answer

The Chinese construction began from the opposite end of the same inheritance. Where Soviet doctrine stressed the abolition of classes through expropriation, Chinese texts stressed the longevity of everything expropriation does not touch. The superstructure, in the Chinese essays of the nineteen sixties, does not politely retire when the economic base is transformed. Ideas, habits, cultural hierarchies and institutional routines persist, and within the economy itself the persistence of commodity production, wage grades and distribution according to work preserves what the Chinese texts, following a thread in Marx's critique of the Gotha programme, called bourgeois right: formal equality that reproduces real inequality. A society carrying that inheritance, administered by a party whose officials enjoy differential access to goods, information and deference, contains on the Chinese account all the ingredients from which a new exploiting stratum can crystallise, needing only time and ideological permission.

The theory of continuing revolution under the dictatorship of the proletariat, formulated in the documents surrounding the Cultural Revolution and summarised in the party constitution adopted in 1969, converted that analysis into a programme. Class struggle between the proletariat and the bourgeoisie, and between the two roads, continues throughout the historical period of socialism. The principal site of that struggle is the party itself, where persons in authority taking the capitalist road constitute the bourgeoisie inside the citadel. Revolution must therefore be continued, repeatedly, against those persons and the relations that produce them, or the transition to communism will be reversed into its opposite. The Soviet Union was named in the polemics as the proof of the danger: a society where, in the Chinese phrase, a privileged stratum had usurped leadership and restored capitalism behind socialist phraseology.

### What is continuing revolution under socialism?

the Chinese doctrine that class struggle does not end when capitalist property is abolished, because a new bourgeoisie can arise within the party and state apparatus itself, so revolution must be continued against it. The Soviet position held that antagonistic classes had already been abolished.

Stand back from the polemical heat and the theoretical move becomes visible. The Chinese doctrine relocated class from the register of property to the field of power, distribution and ideology, and then read the party-state as a terrain on which class formation could recur. That relocation is the doctrine's most original and most dangerous element, and the two adjectives belong together. Original, because it identified a real phenomenon that the canonical definition rendered invisible: the stratification of post-revolutionary societies and the conversion of office into durable advantage. Dangerous, because a class defined by political position and ideological tendency can be found anywhere the finder needs to find it, and the history of the doctrine's application is inseparable from that elasticity. The objection section that follows gives the Soviet critique of exactly this point at full strength, as the brief for this article requires and as fairness in any case demands.

The Chinese texts were not unaware of the difficulty, and their attempts to discipline the concept deserve recording. The bourgeoisie inside the party was supposed to be identifiable by its line: by policies that widened differentials, restored material incentives as the principal motor, subordinated politics to expertise and output, and reopened the door to the market and to foreign capital. A line, unlike a bloodline, could in principle be debated on evidence. In practice the identification of a line with a person, and of a person with a class, collapsed distinctions that the theory needed in order to remain a theory, and the collapse was not an accident of implementation alone. A doctrine that locates the enemy within the leadership, in a party that permits no organised opposition, supplies no institutional procedure by which the accused can be distinguished from the merely defeated. That structural gap, visible in the texts to a careful reader, is where the strongest Soviet objection inserts its lever.


## The strongest Soviet objection, stated at full strength

The objection must now be given the floor without interruption, in the strongest form its own formulations support. It runs as follows. Marxism is a materialist theory of class, and its criterion of class is where a group stands toward the means of production: ownership or non-ownership, control of the labour of others through that ownership, appropriation of surplus product on the basis of title. That criterion is what distinguishes a science of society from a moral vocabulary of praise and blame. Once the means of production stand in social ownership, the material basis of antagonistic class division is removed, because no group can live by appropriating the labour of another through property. Inequalities of income, education and authority remain, and they may be criticised, corrected and punished, but they are differences within a people, not antagonisms between classes, and the state that administers them is a state of the whole people rather than the dictatorship of one class over another.

From that foundation the objection advances to its verdict on the Chinese doctrine. A theory that discovers a bourgeoisie inside a party where no corresponding property relation exists has abandoned the materialist criterion in favour of a political test. The new bourgeoisie is identified not by what its members own but by what they think, advocate or are accused of representing. Class thereby becomes a label that the holder of power can attach to any opponent, and the history of the doctrine's use confirms the logic: the political test was precisely the instrument by which opponents were purged, in campaigns whose targets were selected by factional alignment and then redescribed in class language. A criterion that cannot acquit is not a criterion. The Soviet conclusion followed: continuing revolution is not a development of Marxism but its replacement by a voluntarist politics in which the leader's judgement, dressed as class analysis, stands above the society it claims to describe.

The objection gains further strength from a direction its authors did not always emphasise. The Chinese doctrine, they could note, proves too much. If control over allocation constitutes class domination regardless of ownership, then the distinction between socialism and capitalism loses its anchor in production altogether, and any administered society, including the one governed by the doctrine's own authors, stands condemned by the same test whenever its officials enjoy privilege. The doctrine thus supplies no stable ground from which its own regime can be distinguished from the restoration it denounces elsewhere, except the asserted correctness of its current line, which is again a political test. A theory whose only exemption is itself has, on the Soviet reading, confessed the objection in the act of answering it.

### Why did the Soviet side call the Chinese criterion political rather than material?

Because the identification of the alleged class proceeded by positions taken in policy disputes, not by any relation to property that could be independently verified. Two officials in the same factory could be classed oppositely by their line. That assignment inverted the method: consciousness was being used to determine class, rather than class position to explain consciousness.

Set against the polemical literature, which often caricatured the Soviet position as a bureaucrats' excuse for a quiet life, the objection in this form deserves its reputation as the hardest problem the Chinese doctrine faced. It does not deny that privilege existed in socialist societies. It does not deny that officials could degenerate, conspire or restore. It denies that the vocabulary of class, as Marxism had defined it, can be stretched over those phenomena without breaking, and it predicts exactly the practical consequence that followed: where the stretched vocabulary was used to settle struggles at the summit of a ruling party, the struggles were settled by power and the vocabulary arrived afterwards. Any honest comparison must let that prediction stand in the record, because the record, in the Chinese case, gave it substantial confirmation.

## Control without ownership: the Chinese reply and its cost

The Chinese reply began by contesting the premise that formal ownership exhausts the relation to the means of production. Ownership in law, the reply argued, is a shell that can be filled by different contents. The decisive questions are who controls the use of the productive forces, who appropriates and directs the surplus, whose children inherit advantage, and which stratum's interests the pattern of decisions systematically serves. A factory directorate and ministerial apparatus that monopolise those decisions, defend their differentials as natural, and transmit their position through education and placement have reproduced the substance of a possessing class under a form that the property register cannot see. On this account the Soviet criterion is not wrong but incomplete: it photographs the title deed and misses the hand on the lever, and a materialism that cannot see power over production as a material relation has mistaken a legal category for the whole of reality.

The reply drew support from within the shared canon. Marx's own discussion of the joint-stock company had treated the separation of ownership from control as a real transformation inside capitalism itself, and his remarks on bourgeois right in the transition period conceded that distribution according to work preserves an unequal standard behind an equal one. Lenin's late writings worried publicly about a bureaucratic apparatus that the workers' state had inherited and barely mastered. The Chinese theorists assembled these threads into a claim that their doctrine extended, rather than abandoned, the materialist tradition: class analysis must follow effective control and the reproduction of advantage, or it will certify as classless any society disciplined enough to abolish the share certificate while keeping the hierarchy.

Then the concession, which this article is required to state plainly and does not soften. The Chinese position supplies no property-based criterion by which its alleged bourgeoisie can be identified independently of political judgement. Its tests are tests of line, tendency and association, applied by the victors of internal struggles, and in its great historical application the identification of capitalist roaders was made by political decision and ratified by campaign rather than established by any procedure the accused could contest. Whatever the force of the reply about control, the doctrine in practice operated as the Soviet objection said it would operate: as a political test. A comparison that concealed that outcome would fail its subject. The two positions thus end in a symmetry of failure that the verdict section will weigh: the Soviet criterion could not see a real stratification in front of it, and the Chinese criterion could not protect its own application from the power it was meant to police.

### What could control mean where formal ownership had already changed hands?

It meant the durable capacity to decide. Officials who allocate investment, set the terms on which enterprises keep or surrender their surplus, appoint and promote, and distribute scarce goods exercise, in aggregate, the functions that ownership performs elsewhere. The Chinese argument was that a class analysis stopping at legal title would miss those functions entirely.

There is a coda to the exchange that belongs to intellectual history rather than to either party. Later social theory, much of it written by scholars with no allegiance to either capital, returned repeatedly to the terrain this dispute had opened: the analysis of bureaucratic stratification, of cultural and educational capital as vehicles of inherited advantage, of managerial control in economies of every formal type. The questions the Chinese doctrine raised in a polemical and finally destructive form turned out to be genuine questions, which outlived the answers both sides gave. That afterlife does not vindicate the doctrine's application. It does complicate any verdict that the doctrine contained nothing but the rationalisation of a purge, just as the Soviet doctrine's genuine logical core complicates any verdict that it held nothing but the rationalisation of an apparatus. The comparison is between two serious and two damaged things.


## How the split unfolded: criticism, withdrawal, polemics

Doctrines collided in public only after a sequence of deteriorations that historians can date with unusual precision, and the sequence matters because it shows the quarrel changing its nature as it descended from the summit to the frontier. The opening event was the secret speech of 1956 and the Chinese editorials that answered it with qualified dissent. Through the later nineteen fifties the disagreement remained, in form, a family matter: sharp exchanges at conferences, rival glosses on shared statements, a Chinese leader telling a Soviet one, in remarks later published by the Chinese side, that Stalin's errors should not be used to negate the sword of his achievement. Each capital still assumed the alliance would hold, because each still needed it.

The deterioration accelerated around 1960. At a conference of parties in Bucharest, the Soviet leadership organised a collective denunciation of the Chinese delegation, converting a bilateral dispute into a trial before the movement. Weeks later, Moscow withdrew its advisers and technical assistance from China and cancelled the contracts under which Soviet specialists had been building Chinese industry, an act of economic coercion whose figures belong to the protocol of other articles and whose meaning here is doctrinal as much as material. A state that believed the alliance rested on shared theory had reached for the lever of dependence, and the Chinese side drew the conclusion it would repeat for a decade: that the Soviet leadership treated fraternal parties as subordinates and socialism as a hierarchy of states. The withdrawal also freed the Chinese party from the practical need for deference. A movement that must complete its industrialisation alone owes nobody doctrinal obedience, and the polemical temperature rose accordingly.

Open exchange began in 1963. A Chinese letter of June that year proposed points for a general line of the international movement; a Soviet open letter of July replied; and from September the Chinese side published its celebrated series of comments, nine in all, dissecting the Soviet positions on Stalin, on war and peace, on the roads to socialism, and on the nature of the Soviet system itself. The final comments crossed the threshold from dispute to anathema by describing the Soviet leadership as a revisionist clique that had usurped power and restored capitalism. Soviet replies, in the press and through allied parties, returned the compliment in the vocabulary of dogmatism, nationalism and adventurism. By the middle of the decade the two parties were excommunicating each other in the shared language of a faith both claimed to guard, and fraternal parties everywhere were being required to choose.

### Which came first in the quarrel, doctrine or state interest?

The record refuses the answer both polemics preferred. Doctrinal formulations from the nineteen fifties, on coexistence and on Stalin, preceded the worst clashes of state interest, yet each doctrinal sharpening tracked a deterioration in relations between the two governments. The summary is reciprocal causation: theory gave the quarrel its language and its stakes, while state conflict gave it urgency.

Inside China, the external quarrel fused with an internal one, and the fusion is indispensable to understanding why the class doctrine radicalised when it did. The failures of the Great Leap Forward and the famine that accompanied it, treated under protocol in the biography article in this series, had damaged the authority of the Chinese leader within his own party and elevated colleagues whose instincts ran to administration, incentives and recovery. The theory of continuing revolution supplied a language in which that internal distribution of power could be redescribed as a struggle between two lines and ultimately two classes. Soviet observers noted the convenience and built it into their objection. Chinese defenders replied that a theory is not refuted by being useful. Both observations are true, and the tension between them is the permanent difficulty of judging doctrines that were also weapons. The comparison can only hold both truths in view at once, which is what the generator framework attempts: the doctrine explains the form the internal struggle took, even where interest explains its timing.

## Border clashes and the end of fraternity

In 1969 the dispute reached the frontier. Along the Ussuri River in the northeast, where the boundary between the two states ran through territory whose demarcation both sides contested, armed clashes occurred between Soviet and Chinese forces, beginning in March and recurring later in the year, with further tension along the western frontier in Xinjiang. This article reports those events factually and without detail, as its brief requires: two states that had signed a treaty of alliance two decades earlier exchanged fire, each accused the other of provocation, and each placed its forces on a footing that led sober observers in many capitals to discuss the possibility of a wider war, including the use of the ultimate weapons both now possessed or approached. No casualty figures are given here, and none are needed for the comparison, because the significance of the clashes for the doctrinal story is not numerical. It is that the vocabulary of fraternal socialism had exhausted its restraining power.

The clashes transformed the split's meaning for everyone outside it. While the dispute remained polemical, third parties could treat it as a quarrel within a camp that might still close ranks against a common enemy. Soldiers dying on a frozen river ended that comfort. Communist parties that had postponed choosing were forced into clarity; governments that had built strategies on the assumption of a monolith rebuilt them; and the Chinese leadership itself began the reorientation that would produce, within a few years, an opening to the United States, a development whose diplomatic history belongs elsewhere but whose doctrinal premise was stated plainly enough in Beijing: the principal danger had been redefined, and the country that official Chinese theory described as social-imperialist now occupied the place once reserved for the imperialism across the Pacific. A doctrine of principal contradictions, of the kind examined in the companion article on contradiction and practice, had been applied to the world, and the world had been rearranged by it.

### Why did the polemics become public when they did?

Privacy protected the alliance while it was valued above victory. Publication became rational for each side once it concluded the other could not be corrected internally and that the audience was the world movement, whose parties were the prize. The letters and comments of the early nineteen sixties were addressed, over the opponent's head, to every party headquarters.

For the doctrinal comparison, the frontier fighting also performed an unintended experiment. Soviet theory had held that relations between socialist states belonged to a new type, free of the antagonisms that property and competition impose on capitalist states. Chinese theory, by 1969, held that a restored capitalism in the Soviet Union explained its conduct as that of an ordinary expansionist power. The clashes did not adjudicate between those theories, since a single case cannot bear that weight and since border disputes have causes older than either doctrine, reaching back to the treaties of the tsarist century. What the clashes did was destroy the plausibility of the claim that shared doctrine by itself pacifies relations between states. Both theories survived the destruction, as theories do, by absorbing the event into their own categories. The reader is left with the soberer conclusion that doctrinal kinship is a weak restraint when territory, security and prestige are engaged, a conclusion neither side's textbooks were built to teach.


## The world movement divides

A schism between two centres becomes a schism inside every party that acknowledges either, and the nineteen sixties duly produced splits across the communist world. In country after country, minorities broke from parties aligned with Moscow to form organisations that took the Chinese side of the argument, adopting its vocabulary of revisionism and its strategy of protracted war where conditions seemed to permit. In Western Europe the new formations were mostly small, though their influence on the student generation of 1968 ran far beyond their membership, as Chinese texts, posters and slogans circulated through movements that often knew the doctrine in a simplified and romantic form. In parts of Asia the alignment had heavier consequences, since parties facing actual insurgent possibilities had to decide whether the Chinese road was a model or a metaphor. In Latin America the division intersected with an existing argument about armed struggle, and organisations siding with Beijing competed with both the older parties and the admirers of the Cuban example.

Describing that afterlife structurally, as this article's brief requires, means attending to what the aligned currents shared rather than to the activity of any organisation in the present. They shared a canon: the Chinese polemics of the nineteen sixties read alongside the earlier writings on peasant war and the mass line. They shared an enemy defined doctrinally rather than nationally, since their first polemical target was usually their own country's established communist party and the Soviet line behind it. They shared a strategic preference for the countryside and for wars of long duration over insurrectionary attempts in the capitals. And they shared a fate that the comparison should record without Schadenfreude: nearly all of them discovered that a doctrine formed for a vast peasant country at war travelled badly into societies with different class structures, and that the Chinese centre whose authority they had invoked was itself moving, by the nineteen seventies, toward positions, on alliances and on the world balance, that its admirers abroad struggled to reconcile with the canon they had memorised.

### How did parties far from Moscow and Beijing choose their sides?

Rarely by study alone. Local rivalries inside each movement, generational revolt against established leaderships, and judgements about which strategy fitted local conditions all pressed on the choice, and the doctrinal texts were often read afterwards as warrants for a decision already taken. Yet the texts mattered: they fixed what the new organisations believed they had chosen.

The Indian case, treated at length in the companion article on the Naxalism Maoist current, shows the pattern in its most consequential form. A current that broke from the established parties as the nineteen sixties closed took the Chinese side's characterisation of its own country's revolution, attempted the strategy of annihilation and rural base building in conditions radically unlike those of China before 1949, and was destroyed as an organised force within a few years, leaving a residue of organisations and ideas that later reassembled in altered forms. The episode is the strongest evidence for a general proposition about doctrinal transfer: strategies carry the imprint of the societies that produced them, and the imprint does not wash out in transit. The Chinese doctrine's own theory should have predicted the difficulty, since its founding gesture was the insistence that universal truth exists only in concrete application. Its exporters, Chinese and foreign alike, repeatedly forgot the gesture in the act of honouring it.

Albania occupies a singular place in the afterlife and deserves a paragraph of its own. The small Balkan state aligned itself with China against the Soviet Union at the start of the nineteen sixties, broke its ties with Moscow, and for a decade and a half served as the Chinese position's European voice, broadcasting its polemics and receiving its support. The alliance then dissolved in its turn, in the later nineteen seventies, over the Chinese opening to the United States and the theory that rationalised it, with the Albanian leadership denouncing its former patron in the same vocabulary of betrayal that both had once aimed at Moscow. The episode completes a pattern the comparison should not soften: in this tradition, alignments formed by doctrinal agreement proved exactly as perishable as alignments formed by interest, and each successive schism reproduced the structure of the one before it, a centre accused of revisionism by a smaller party claiming custody of the true line.

## Doctrine as state interest: the geopolitical reading tested

No question in the brief for this article recurs more persistently in the literature than whether the split was ideological or geopolitical, and the comparison is now in a position to give the question a disciplined answer. The geopolitical reading has formidable evidence. The two states disputed a long frontier inherited from an unequal century. The Soviet Union sought a condominium with the United States that a revolutionary China experienced as encirclement and betrayal. The Chinese leadership resented a relationship in which aid arrived with advisers, conditions and an expectation of deference, and in which the junior partner's security requests, including assistance toward an independent nuclear deterrent, were refused or hedged. A historian who explained the rupture entirely through those materials would not lack for documentation, and the polemics themselves can be read as the ideological superstructure of a quarrel that states would have had in any vocabulary.

The doctrinal reading has evidence of a different kind, and it is the kind this article has been assembling. The formulations at issue predated the worst state conflicts and survived them. The class question was argued in texts whose internal logic can be followed without reference to any frontier. Parties with no frontier, no nuclear programme and no alliance to escape split over the same propositions, which is difficult to explain if the propositions were mere flags for a quarrel between two governments. And the consequences of the doctrines inside the two societies, the convulsion in one and the immobility in the other, followed the logic of the premises with a fidelity that pure flag theories cannot reproduce. Flags do not generate campaigns against their own apparatus; premises do.

The disciplined answer is therefore layered rather than exclusive. State interest selected the timing, the intensity and the eventual military expression of the split. Doctrine selected its form, its language, its stakes and its domestic consequences, and doctrine is what made reconciliation impossible, because states can compromise over territory and trade but parties that have defined each other as class enemies in theory cannot easily sign a communique. Each reading alone explains the half of the record the other leaves dark. The reader who finishes this article should be able to deploy both, and to specify, for any episode in the sequence, which layer is doing the explanatory work: the secret speech is unintelligible without doctrine, the withdrawal of advisers unintelligible without statecraft, and the border clashes unintelligible without both.

### Why could the two parties not simply agree to differ?

Because each doctrine contained a theory of the other side's society. Soviet doctrine implied that the Chinese campaigns were the work of a leadership detached from a society without antagonistic classes left to fight. Chinese doctrine implied that the Soviet order was a restoration governed by a new exploiting stratum. A disagreement classifying the opponent's regime leaves no neutral ground.

An earlier schism had rehearsed the pattern on a smaller stage. The expulsion of Yugoslavia from the Soviet-led camp in 1948, after its leadership refused subordination, established that the movement's unity had always rested partly on hierarchy, and that a party which insisted on an independent road would be read as a heretic whatever its doctrine said. The Chinese leadership studied that episode closely and drew from it the determination never to occupy the Yugoslav position of isolated defiance without the means to sustain it, which is one reason the Chinese polemics aimed from the start at the allegiance of the whole movement rather than at bilateral redress. Where Belgrade had appealed to principle and been quarantined, Beijing appealed to the parties of the world and built, for a time, an alternative centre. The comparison with 1948 also disciplines the geopolitical reading in an interesting way: the Yugoslav quarrel involved almost no doctrinal innovation, while the Chinese quarrel produced a theory. States may fall out for reasons of state; only the Chinese case required its participants to rewrite the doctrine of class under socialism, and the rewriting is the part of the inheritance that still demands analysis.

There remains the question of sincerity, which popular treatments love and serious ones avoid. Did the leaders believe their doctrines, or merely wield them? The evidence assembled in this article points to a conclusion that dissolves the question's premise. Men who had made revolutions under the guidance of these texts, who had watched comrades die for formulations, and who reached for the same vocabulary in their private deliberations as in their public polemics, were not standing outside their doctrines choosing instruments. The doctrines were the medium in which their interests became visible to themselves. A Chinese leader confronting the recovery of his party by administrators reached for the theory of continuing revolution not as a disguise for a power struggle but because that theory was how a power struggle inside a communist party could be thought at all, by anyone formed in that tradition. The Soviet leaders who defended the abolition of antagonism were similarly formed. Sincerity, in such material, is not the alternative to interest. It is the shape interest takes in minds furnished by a doctrine.

## The party as terrain: two theories of the apparatus

Return now to the generative disagreement with the polemics behind us, and examine its institutional form, because the class argument was always also an argument about what a ruling party is. In the Soviet construction, the party after the abolition of antagonistic classes becomes the organised conscience of a united people. Its monopoly is justified by its programme and its record of construction; its internal life is governed by democratic centralism understood as the disciplined execution of decisions whose correctness the centre guarantees; and criticism is a tool for improving implementation, not a challenge to the order the party embodies. A member who described the party itself as the seat of a class enemy would not be wrong within the theory so much as unintelligible within it, like a move in a game the rules do not contain.

In the Chinese construction, the party is the principal terrain on which the class struggle of the socialist period is fought. That single re-description changes everything about internal life. If the bourgeoisie can regenerate inside the apparatus, then the apparatus cannot be the final judge of its own health; judgement must periodically arrive from outside its routines, through mass mobilisation directed at the apparatus itself. The Cultural Revolution was that proposition enacted, with consequences examined in the companion article and deliberately not re-litigated here. The theoretical point for the comparison is that the Chinese doctrine dissolved the boundary between the party as subject of revolution and the party as its object. The Soviet doctrine fortified that boundary. Every later difference in the two systems' behaviour under stress, in their handling of dissent, of succession, of economic failure, can be traced in part to which side of that boundary each regime's theory placed the party on.

Succession illustrates the trace cleanly. A doctrine in which the party embodies a united people has no legitimate mechanism for removing a leadership that has failed, since failure by the embodiment of the whole can only be conspiracy or illness; Soviet successions therefore occurred as palace events, justified retrospectively. A doctrine in which the party contains the enemy has a mechanism in surplus, since any succession can be theorised as the defeat of a capitalist-road headquarters; Chinese successions therefore repeatedly took the form of campaigns, justified in advance by theory. Neither mechanism is a procedure in the constitutional sense. Both are what a doctrine provides when it must explain how power changes hands inside a system that admits no opposition. The comparison does not flatter either, and was not designed to.

The distinction between the two criteria of class deserves a statement in plain prose, because students routinely blur it when they first meet the dispute. A property-based criterion can in principle be checked by an observer who shares none of the disputants' commitments. Registries, laws and accounts either show a group appropriating surplus through ownership or they do not, and a claim made in those terms can be false in a way the claimant must acknowledge. A political criterion depends on the interpretation of lines, statements and associations, and its application requires a judge whose own standing is part of what is disputed. The Soviet objection, stripped to this epistemological core, is that the Chinese doctrine moved class identification from the first kind of test to the second while retaining the authority that only the first kind could confer. The Chinese reply, stripped likewise, is that the first kind of test had been blinded by its own formalism, certifying the shell of social ownership while the substance of command migrated elsewhere. A student who can state that exchange without taking either side's vocabulary for granted has understood the deepest layer of the split, and the FAQ section returns to the point in examination form.


## Education, culture and the weight of the old world

A further difference, folded into several rows of the grid yet worth unfolding on its own, concerned the superstructure: the realm of education, art, habit and idea. Soviet doctrine after the abolition of antagonism treated the superstructure of socialism as fundamentally sound, a realm where the remaining tasks were cultivation and the patient elimination of survivals. Survivals was the operative word, and it carried a theory inside itself. A survival is a fragment of a dead order, shrinking with time, dangerous mainly through inertia. The appropriate politics toward survivals is education administered by a confident state, and the timescale is generational but untroubled.

Chinese doctrine treated the same realm as a battlefield on which the outcome of the whole transition remained undecided. The texts of the nineteen sixties returned obsessively to schools, universities, theatres and publishing houses, not from a leader's dilettantism but from the logic of the generator. If a new bourgeoisie forms within the apparatus, it forms first as a stratum of outlook: in admissions policies that favour the children of officials and experts, in a curriculum that sorts the population into mental and manual castes, in a culture that teaches deference to rank while reciting equality. The superstructure, on this account, is not where the old world survives. It is where the new class is manufactured. Hence the otherwise puzzling intensity with which the Chinese campaigns attacked educational selection, examination systems and the prestige of expertise, treating each as a mechanism of class reproduction rather than a technical arrangement.

### Why did the Chinese campaigns treat schools as class machinery?

Because selection decides who may rise, and a selection system built on inherited cultural advantage will restock the directing stratum from the same families under any property regime. Examinations, credentials and the division between mental and manual labour appeared, in the Chinese analysis, as the quiet machinery by which privilege reproduced itself while the property register recorded only equality. Attacking it followed from the theory.

Soviet commentary regarded the assault on educational selection as vandalism against civilisation itself, and the disagreement exposes the two doctrines' different relations to expertise. The Soviet order, having industrialised under party direction, had fused its legitimacy to the competence of its engineers, planners and academicians, and its theory honoured that fusion by treating the intelligentsia as a loyal stratum of the people. The Chinese order, born in guerrilla war, had fused its legitimacy to mobilisation and distrusted every credential that could not be re-earned in struggle. Each theory ratified the founding experience of its regime. The comparative point is that doctrines of class under socialism are also, inevitably, doctrines about which kind of knowledge rules, and the quarrel over schools was a quarrel over whether the diploma or the campaign would arbitrate the future composition of the elite.

There is an application of this difference that reaches beyond the two countries, into any movement that wins power and must then staff a state. The Chinese warning, abstracted from its campaign form, is that a revolution which changes property but leaves the machinery of selection untouched will be governed within a generation by the heirs of the old selection, wearing new titles. The Soviet warning, abstracted likewise, is that a revolution which attacks the machinery of selection in the name of equality will destroy competences it cannot replace and will govern through ignorance sanctified as virtue. Both warnings were issued from experience. Both were borne out, in different decades, in the country that issued the other one. The grid's rows, once again, turn out to be less a scorecard than a pair of mirrors.

## Mao Zedong Thought and the later codification

Terminology was flagged near the start of this article; it now deserves its full treatment, because the distinction between Mao Zedong Thought and Maoism is the point on which reference works most often mislead. Inside the Chinese party, the canonised body of doctrine bore the leader's name joined to the word Thought, and its status was carefully bounded. It represented, in the party's formula, the integration of the universal truth of Marxism-Leninism with the concrete practice of the Chinese revolution. It did not claim to supersede its parent doctrine, and its author was presented, at least in the formula, as the foremost voice of a collective rather than as a solitary lawgiver. That bounded status mattered to the party's self-understanding: the Chinese contribution was a national application of genius, not a third stage of the science.

Abroad, the bounded status did not survive export. Movements that adopted the Chinese line during the split needed a banner under which to distinguish themselves from the Soviet-aligned parties they had left, and Maoism, a word the Chinese party itself used sparingly, served the purpose. The term gathered to itself the whole Chinese experience: peasant war, the mass line, the polemics, the Cultural Revolution, and the sayings in the little red book that circulated globally in the late nineteen sixties. As a banner it was effective precisely because it was vague, allowing students in Paris, peasants in Andhra Pradesh and intellectuals in Lima to march under one word while meaning noticeably different things by it.

The codification arrived later and from an unexpected quarter. In the late nineteen eighties the Communist Party of Peru, whose insurgency had been launched at the start of that decade, systematised the inheritance as Marxism-Leninism-Maoism, proclaiming Maoism a third, higher stage of the doctrine, with its own canonical components: the theory of continuing revolution, the mass line as a theory of knowledge, protracted people's war as a universal military doctrine, and the analysis of bureaucratic capitalism in the oppressed countries. The codification was an act of genuine systematisation, whatever one's judgement of its content, and it fixed the meaning of Maoism for the currents that accepted it. It also completed an irony. The most rigorous definition of Maoism as a universal doctrine was produced not in Beijing but in the Andes, by a party whose founder's synthesis the Chinese party never endorsed in that form, after the Chinese party itself had begun dismantling parts of the legacy the codifiers were embalming.

### Who codified Marxism-Leninism-Maoism, and where?

A South American party, in Peru, during the late nineteen eighties, as it systematised the doctrine under which it was already fighting. The codifiers treated the Chinese experience as a fresh stage of the universal theory rather than as a national application, exceeding anything the Chinese party had claimed for Mao Zedong Thought.

For the comparison, the terminological history carries a methodological lesson that this series applies across its regional clusters. Doctrines acquire their names retrospectively and often externally, and the name then governs what readers look for in the texts. A student who approaches the Chinese writings of the nineteen sixties through the later codification will read them as the scripture of a finished system, which is how the codifiers read them, and will miss the extent to which they were interventions in an unfolding argument, improvised against opponents and circumstances. A student who approaches them through the Chinese party's own bounded formula will see the interventions and may miss the system's ambitions. The grid in this article is designed to hold both readings at once: seven differences stated as doctrine, each with the document and the consequence that keep it anchored in history.


## Two regimes, two pathologies

The generator claim can now be tested against the behaviour of the two systems in their later decades, which is where a merely polemical comparison would stop and an analytical one begins. The Soviet order, armed with a theory that denied internal antagonism, developed a characteristic way of not seeing its own problems. Economic stagnation could be registered as a difficulty of planning technique but not as a symptom of interests, because the theory contained no legitimate interests apart from the whole people's. Dissent could be processed as pathology, foreign agency or survivals, but not as the voice of a stratum with grievances the system itself produced. Privilege could be deplored as a moral failing of individuals but not analysed as the systematic product of a structure of control. The theory did not cause the stagnation; material causes did that. What the theory caused was the poverty of the regime's self-description, and a regime that cannot describe itself cannot reform itself except by accident or collapse.

The Chinese order, armed with the opposite theory, developed the opposite incapacity. A doctrine for which class enemies regenerate inside the apparatus can never declare its work finished, and therefore can never grant its society the stability in which institutions mature and predictability accumulates. Every policy failure could be, and repeatedly was, redescribed as the sabotage of hidden class forces, which relieved the doctrine of the need to examine its own economics. Every succession became an existential struggle, because the loser was not a defeated colleague but a representative of the bourgeoisie whose defeat the theory demanded and whose existence the theory guaranteed in advance. The campaign form, renewed across decades, consumed the administrative competence and the social trust on which any durable socialism would have had to rest. The theory did not cause every disaster of the period; ambition, error and circumstance did their share. What the theory caused was the conversion of each disaster into a reason for another campaign.

Set side by side, the two pathologies have the shape the namable claim predicts. Deny that class struggle persists under socialism, and the regime loses the concept it would need to criticise its own stratification, so stratification proceeds unnamed until it becomes unspeakable and then unbearable. Affirm that class struggle continues without supplying a criterion by which its targets can be identified independently of power, and the regime gains a weapon that any holder of power can aim, so the struggle proceeds until it has consumed the institutions that were supposed to conduct society beyond struggle. The catastrophes differ in tempo and texture, one slow and one convulsive. Their common root, on the argument of this article, is that each doctrine answered the question of class under socialism in a form that disabled self-correction: one by abolishing the question, the other by making its answer unfalsifiable.

### What remains usable in the Chinese critique of bureaucratic power?

Its central perception, separated from its campaign practice: that formal socialisation does not by itself dissolve hierarchy, that control over allocation is a material power requiring material checks, and that a directing stratum will reproduce its advantages through education and appointment unless institutions are built to prevent it. Later scholarship returned to each point by other roads.

A comparison must also register what the generator does not explain, lest the claim harden into the kind of total key this series distrusts. The generator does not explain the famine that followed the Great Leap, whose causes lie in procurement, information and utopian targets rather than in the class doctrine, which was radicalised partly in response to that catastrophe. It does not explain the Soviet victory in the war against Germany, the Chinese recovery after 1949, or the genuine achievements in literacy, health and industrial capacity that both regimes recorded and that their populations remembered even while enduring the pathologies. It does not explain why the Soviet order, when it finally ended, ended with a whimper arranged by its own elite, nor why the Chinese order survived its founder by transforming its economy under the management of his own party. A claim that explains the characteristic deformations of two systems, and the impossibility of their reconciliation, has earned its place without pretending to be a philosophy of their whole history.

## What survives of each doctrine as theory

Survival must be assessed separately for the two sides, because their afterlives took opposite forms. The Soviet doctrine survives chiefly as a negative inheritance and as a set of questions it forced others to answer. Its textbook system collapsed with the state that sponsored it, and few now defend the proposition that hostile classes had been abolished in the society that proclaimed it. Yet the objection it pressed against the Chinese position retains its force in any seminar where the dispute is reconstructed: the demand for a criterion of class that does not dissolve into the accuser's convenience remains the standing challenge to every theory of bureaucratic class, and the scholars who write about new classes and nomenklaturas still have to answer it, whether they cite its source or not. The Soviet doctrine also survives in the documentary record as the most complete attempt ever made to codify Marxism as a finished system, which gives it a permanent use for historians of ideas: it is the control case for what happens to a critical theory when it becomes the ideology of a state that can no longer afford criticism.

The Chinese doctrine survives in fragments that have had stranger journeys. Protracted people's war passed into the general theory of insurgency, studied in staff colleges by officers who have never opened the polemics. The mass line passed into the vocabulary of organisation and even of management, usually shorn of its class content. The analysis of bureaucratic regeneration passed, as noted earlier, into social theory's broader investigation of how advantage reproduces itself under formally equal rules. And the specific synthesis codified as Marxism-Leninism-Maoism survived as the doctrine of particular movements, described structurally in this article's account of the afterlife, whose fortunes belong to the regional articles of this series rather than to a comparison of the two great doctrines. What did not survive, on either side, is the claim that animated the polemics: that one centre possessed the finished truth of the doctrine and the other had betrayed it. The schism's most durable product may be the demonstration, conducted at enormous cost, that the inheritance could not be held in one piece.

### Why does the peasant strategy mislead readers about the deepest difference?

Because it is the most visible. Armies in the countryside, base areas and encircled cities fill the photographs and the memoirs, and readers naturally take the spectacle for the essence. Both parties compromised over strategy when circumstances required, whereas the class question admitted no compromise, because it defined what each regime was.

There is a final survival to record, quieter than the others. The dispute taught the wider world a vocabulary it still uses without attribution. Talk of a new class inside a revolutionary state, of restoration behind socialist forms, of the countryside of the world against its cities, of lines and headquarters within a single party, entered journalism, scholarship and the internal arguments of movements that would deny any debt to either capital. Doctrines that lose their states do not necessarily lose their words. The comparison this article has conducted is therefore not an autopsy of two dead systems only. It is also a genealogy of concepts still at work, in arguments whose participants sometimes do not know that the terms of their disagreement were fixed in the polemics of the nineteen sixties.


## The economics beneath the polemics

Doctrinal disputes in this tradition were never confined to philosophy, and the split had an economic substratum that the comparison should surface, because each side's theory of class was entangled with a rival model of how a socialist economy should be built. The Soviet model, formed in the nineteen thirties and exported as the template of socialist construction, rested on centralised planning, priority for heavy industry, large units of production in town and country, and the subordination of consumption and agriculture to the accumulation of industrial capacity. Its theory of incentives was material and differential: wage grades, bonuses and piece rates would harness self-interest to the plan until abundance made such harness unnecessary. Its theory of knowledge was hierarchical: the centre, aggregating the reports of ministries, knew the economy as no locality could.

The Chinese leadership, after an initial period of close imitation in the early nineteen fifties, diverged on nearly every element, and the divergences were argued in the same vocabulary as the class dispute. Against the priority of heavy industry stood the formula of walking on two legs, developing modern and indigenous methods, large and small enterprises, together. Against the ministerial hierarchy stood repeated campaigns of decentralisation, pushing authority toward provinces, communes and enterprises. Against material incentives as production's chief motor stood the slogan of politics in command, the claim that consciousness and collective identification could substitute, in part, for differential reward. Soviet economists read these departures as amateurism and said so in their journals with increasing openness as relations soured. Chinese economists and propagandists read the Soviet model itself as a mechanism of stratification: a system that concentrated decisions in ministries, rewarded a technical stratum with widening differentials, and measured success in tonnage was, on the Chinese account, building the material base of the new bourgeoisie its theory denied.

The economic argument matters to the comparison because it shows the generator at work outside the realm of pure doctrine. The Chinese charge that capitalism was being restored in the Soviet Union was never only about personnel or ideology; it named policies, the restoration of profit as an enterprise criterion in the Soviet reforms of the nineteen sixties, the widening of wage differentials, the toleration of market mechanisms at the margins, as the substance of restoration. The Soviet reply named Chinese policies in return: the communes as an attempt to leap over stages of development by administrative fiat, the backyard furnaces as the reductio of voluntarism, the disruption of planning by campaign as proof that politics in command meant economics in chaos. Each side's economics supplied the other's class theory with its evidence. A reader who follows only the philosophical texts will miss how much of the argument was conducted over the design of factories, farms and wage packets.

### Why did wage differentials become a doctrinal battlefield?

Because distribution is where a theory of class touches daily life. An order paying its directors many multiples of its cleaners must explain the multiple as necessity or as the reward of skill. The Chinese texts treated widening differentials as bourgeois right expanding into a class relation. The Soviet texts treated compressed differentials as an egalitarian prejudice that sabotaged production.

Agriculture concentrated the same argument in its sharpest form. The Soviet collective farm, whatever its inefficiencies, represented in Soviet theory the settled solution to the peasant question: land socialised, production collectivised, the peasantry converted into a class of collective farmers allied with the workers and slowly converging with them. The Chinese commune of 1958 attempted something the Soviet model had never risked, the fusion of production, administration, education, welfare and militia in a single rural unit, at a scale and speed that Soviet commentary regarded as madness and said so, at first in the fraternal code of concern and later without code. When the commune system retreated after the catastrophe of the Great Leap, Soviet writers treated the retreat as vindication. Chinese theorists treated the Soviet collective farm, in turn, as the institutional form in which a rural directing stratum had entrenched itself beyond recall. Beneath the technical argument about farm size lay the recurring question: which arrangements of rural life generate a new hierarchy, and which dissolve one? Neither side's farms answered the question well, and the peasantry of both countries paid for the experiments by which the answers were sought.

## Revisionism: the word that did the fighting

Every schism mints a word for its enemy, and the word this schism minted, or rather sharpened, was revisionism. In the Chinese polemics the term named the whole Soviet deviation: the revision of Marxism's revolutionary content under the pressure of governing, until nothing remained but the vocabulary. The charge had a genealogy the polemics made explicit, reaching back to the Bernstein controversy at the end of the nineteenth century, when a German socialist had proposed revising the doctrine's revolutionary conclusions in the light of capitalism's stabilisation. By casting the Soviet leadership as Bernstein's heirs, the Chinese texts placed the split inside a seventy-year drama of orthodoxy and betrayal, in which they themselves played the part once played by the defenders of the revolutionary core. The rhetorical gain was enormous, because it converted a dispute between two states into the culminating act of a sacred history every cadre knew.

The Soviet side answered with a matching pair of terms, dogmatism and sectarianism, supplemented as the quarrel deepened by charges of nationalism and petty-bourgeois revolutionism. Its genealogy reached back to the same founding decades: the dogmatist was the one who repeated the letter of the doctrine against its living development, who mistook the quotations of one period for the science of all periods. On that reading, the Chinese were not revolutionaries but prisoners of their own revolutionary past, applying the categories of a peasant war to a world of nuclear weapons and industrial states. Each side's insult thus contained a theory of history. Revisionism accused the other of changing with the times for corrupt reasons; dogmatism accused the other of refusing to change with the times for corrupt reasons. Between the two accusations, the possibility that a doctrine might change for good reasons, or hold fast for good ones, found little room, and the polemics' sterility at their worst moments came from exactly that exclusion.

### What work did the word revisionism do that an ordinary accusation could not?

It relocated the dispute from policy to custody. To call a rival's policy mistaken leaves the rival inside the tradition, entitled to argue again at the next congress. To call the rival a revisionist expels the rival from the tradition altogether, reclassifies the rival's state as an imposture, and licenses every fraternal party to treat obedience to the rival as betrayal. The word was therefore not decoration on the split. It was the split's legal instrument.

The afterlife of the word confirms its power. Long after the two capitals had ceased to compete for the movement's allegiance, organisations and tendencies continued to define themselves by the charge, discovering revisionism in each successive leadership that disappointed them, in a regress that the Indian and Peruvian and European histories in this series repeatedly display. A term forged to describe a specific doctrinal operation, the emptying of a theory's revolutionary content by its custodians, became a universal solvent of legitimacy. The comparison can end its account of the word with the observation its history forces: the charge of revisionism was sometimes true, in the limited sense that custodians do domesticate doctrines, and it was structurally incapable of being proven false by the accused, which is why it destroyed every movement that organised itself around the charge for long.


## The audience in between: smaller states and the Third World

Between the two giants stood the remainder of the socialist camp and the uncommitted world, and their responses complicate any picture of a dispute conducted between two doctrines in a vacuum. The states of Eastern Europe, whose security rested on the Soviet alliance, aligned publicly with Moscow while privately drawing varied conclusions. Some of their leaderships welcomed de-Stalinisation as liberation and resented the Chinese defence of Stalin's reputation as an attempt to re-freeze a thaw they needed. Others, watching the Chinese insistence that a party could defy the centre and survive, stored the lesson for their own future quarrels with Moscow, even while voting the correct way at conferences. Romania's leadership in particular learned to extract room for manoeuvre from the existence of an alternative centre, playing the schism as an instrument of national autonomy while adhering to neither doctrine in its heart. The schism thus weakened the camp's discipline even where it changed no one's allegiance, because a centre that can be defied by one giant can be bargained with by everyone.

For the newly independent states of Asia and Africa, the dispute arrived as an unexpected gift and an unwelcome demand. The gift was leverage: two competing socialist powers meant two sources of aid, recognition and rhetorical support, and skilled governments from Cairo to Jakarta extracted advantage from the rivalry. The demand was for a choice of models at exactly the moment when the choice of a development path was the central question of their politics. The Chinese model spoke directly to poor agrarian countries in a way the Soviet model, for all its industrial prestige, did not: here was a great revolution made by peasants, in a country that had been semi-colonial within living memory, preaching self-reliance and the suspicion of experts. The Soviet model spoke to states that wanted steel mills, dams and a patron's guarantee. Which model a liberation movement or a new government found persuasive tracked its circumstances with embarrassing fidelity, and the doctrinal argument about classes under socialism, so central in the two capitals, often mattered less to the audience than the argument about whose road a poor country could actually walk.

### How did the existence of two centres change the bargaining power of small parties and states?

A monopoly centre can discipline by excommunication, since the expelled have nowhere to go. A duopoly converts excommunication into recruitment, because the rival centre gains by welcoming the expelled. Every party and state in the system therefore acquired an implicit threat it had not possessed before, and the whole camp's internal politics, from conference votes to aid negotiations, was repriced by the existence of the alternative.

North Korea's conduct during the split offers the comparison a study in miniature, and it is handled here as history, in keeping with this article's constraints. Caught between its two giant neighbours and patrons, its leadership refused for years to commit irrevocably to either side, condemned the personality cult in terms borrowed from one capital while defending positions associated with the other, purged factions aligned with each patron in turn, and emerged with an autonomy neither patron had intended to grant. The episode belongs in this article because it demonstrates, at the level of a single state's survival strategy, the same proposition the Romanian case demonstrates in Europe: the schism's most universal product was room. Whatever the doctrines said, the existence of two of them enlarged the freedom of everyone else in the system, an outcome neither centre sought and neither could reverse without the reconciliation both had made impossible.

The Third World audience also returned something to the dispute, because the Chinese claim to lead a global countryside depended on the response of actual movements in that countryside. Victories and defeats abroad were read back into the polemics as evidence. The Vietnamese party, dependent on both patrons during its war, refused to choose and thereby delivered the most elegant rebuke available to it, accepting aid from each while endorsing neither's claim to custody of the doctrine. The Indonesian party, by contrast, aligned closely with Beijing and perished in the catastrophe of 1965 for reasons overwhelmingly domestic, a sequence the Soviet press nonetheless mined for warnings about adventurism. Doctrine, in these exchanges, behaved like a currency whose value was set by events its issuers did not control, and both centres learned that a claim to universal leadership exposes the claimant to judgement by every local failure committed in its name.

## A shared grammar: why the quarrel could not be translated away

Outsiders sometimes ask why the dispute could not be settled by the ordinary diplomatic solvent of ambiguous wording, and the answer lies in the grammar the two sides shared. Both parties inhabited a universe in which texts were authoritative, in which the correct formulation was a political fact, and in which a single adjective, inserted or withheld, could mark the boundary between a fraternal party and a renegade one. The conferences of the period turned on such adjectives. A statement describing the Chinese party's road, a formula about the dictatorship of the proletariat, a clause about the possibility of peaceful transition: each was fought over for days because each side's cadres would read the final text as law. Ambiguity, which diplomacy uses to let opponents retreat, was unavailable to parties for whom the text was the territory.

The shared grammar also explains the peculiar intimacy of the hatred. Heresy is a category that exists only inside a faith, and the two parties were heretics to each other in the precise sense: each had arrived at its deviation by reasoning from the common premises with materials the other recognised. A Soviet theorist reading the Chinese comments encountered not nonsense but an alternative construction of his own canon, textually learned and internally consistent, which is a far more disturbing experience than encountering an enemy's propaganda. The Chinese theorists reading the Soviet programme encountered the same. Polemics between strangers are propaganda; polemics between heirs are excommunication, and the documentary record of the split has the temperature of excommunication throughout.

### Why did both sides quote the same founders to opposite conclusions?

Because the inheritance genuinely contained both conclusions in embryo. The founders had written about classes defined by property and about the withering of the state, about the persistence of bourgeois right and about the dictatorship that would abolish classes, without ever governing a post-revolutionary state long enough to be forced into a single doctrine of its internal life. The heirs were not distorting a settled teaching. They were completing an unsettled one, in opposite directions, under opposite pressures.

That incompleteness is the deepest answer the comparison can give to the question of why the split took a doctrinal form at all. The theory the two parties shared had been built to analyse capitalism and to guide its overthrow. For the society that came after the overthrow, it supplied principles, warnings and fragments, but no finished constitution of ideas. Two revolutionary states, differing in their founding experiences, their levels of development and their positions in the world, completed the theory as their circumstances pressed them to complete it, and then discovered that the completions contradicted each other at the foundation. The tragedy of the polemics, if the word is permitted in an analytical article, is that each completion fastened on something real: the Soviet one on the genuine logical structure of the class concept, the Chinese one on the genuine stratification of post-revolutionary power. A theory that had contained a procedure for deciding between them might have held the movement together. It contained none, because its author had expected the question to be settled by a history that instead produced two answers at once.


## Reading the polemics: the Nine Comments as texts

The documentary core of the split rewards reading as literature as well as evidence, because the form of the polemics shaped what could be said inside them. The Chinese comments answering the Soviet open letter, published between September 1963 and July 1964, were composed as a systematic treatise distributed across nine instalments: the dictatorship's historical experience restated, on Stalin, on Yugoslavia as the alleged preview of restoration, on war and peace, and on the unity of the movement, and finally on the Soviet leadership itself as a revisionist clique. The architecture was deliberate. By the time the reader reached the anathema of the final comment, the verdict had been prepared by what presented itself as a patient review of every disputed question, each documented with quotations from the founders and from the opponents' own statements. The form taught a generation of readers, far beyond China, how a doctrinal execution was performed: begin with history, proceed through principle, end with the unmasking of persons.

The Soviet replies adopted the opposite architecture, dispersing their case across editorials, congress speeches and the statements of allied parties, which gave the Soviet position the appearance of a chorus and the Chinese position the appearance of a single commanding voice. Historians of the dispute have noted the asymmetry's effect on posterity. The Chinese case survives as a canon, bound and quotable, that a student can still read in sequence and feel the argument accumulate. The Soviet case survives as a climate, reconstructible from many documents but rarely encountered whole, which is one reason later sympathisers with the Soviet objection often present it less vividly than its logic deserves. This article has attempted to correct that imbalance by stating the objection in its strongest reconstructed form, and the reader who tests that reconstruction against the Soviet documents will find its elements all present, though nowhere assembled with the polemical elegance of the other side.

### How were the Nine Comments meant to be read by their first audience?

As a curriculum. The comments were studied in organised sessions across China and circulated to fraternal parties as the authorised account of the dispute, so their first readers encountered them not as journalism but as instruction, with the expectation that the arguments would be reproduced in the reader's own setting. Their systematic order, question by question toward the final verdict, reflects that pedagogical purpose.

Beneath the architecture sat a theory of the audience that both sides shared and that explains the polemics' peculiar mixture of scholasticism and menace. The immediate addressee was never the opposing leadership, which neither side expected to persuade. The addressee was the cadre of the world movement, imagined as a reader who would weigh quotations, check references and render a verdict on custody of the doctrine. That imagined reader explains the footnotes, the care with dates, the insistence on quoting the opponent accurately enough to convict him from his own mouth. It also explains the disappointment that saturates the later documents of both sides, as it became clear that the actual audience was deciding on other grounds: on loyalty, on interest, on the local balance inside each party. The polemics were written for a republic of letters that the movement did not, in the event, contain.

## The dictatorship of the proletariat: a phrase fought over

No phrase carried more freight in the dispute than the dictatorship of the proletariat, because each side's theory of class under socialism was encoded in its handling of the term. In the Soviet programme adopted early in the nineteen sixties, the dictatorship had completed its historical task with the abolition of the exploiting classes and had grown over, in the official phrase, into the whole people's state. The transformation was presented as the doctrine's own prediction fulfilled: an instrument of class suppression withers as the classes to be suppressed disappear. Chinese polemics treated the announcement as the theoretical heart of the betrayal. To dissolve the dictatorship while bourgeois right, commodity production and the danger of restoration persisted was, on their account, to disarm the proletariat in the middle of the transition and to license the privileged stratum to govern without even the formal acknowledgment that a class question remained open.

The Chinese counter-formulation, the continuing dictatorship exercised against a bourgeoisie that included elements inside the party, raised the question its opponents pressed relentlessly: against whom, concretely, is the dictatorship to be exercised when the old owners are gone and the new enemy is defined by line? The texts answered with the category of capitalist roaders, persons in authority whose policies restored capitalist relations. The category's career in practice, from the identification of the head of state as the leading capitalist roader downward, is the empirical core of the Soviet objection and has been treated as such in this article. For the comparison, the point to fix is the symmetry of the two moves. The Soviet side dissolved a class instrument on the ground that its object had vanished; the Chinese side retained the instrument and supplied it with a new object that its own theory generated. Each move was internally motivated. Each created the characteristic blindness of its regime: the one could no longer name domination inside its own order, the other could name nothing else.

### Why did the whole-people formula provoke such fury in Beijing?

Because it announced, in a single formula, that the class question inside socialism was closed. If the state belongs to the whole people, then no stratum within it can be a class enemy, no campaign against the apparatus can be a class struggle, and the Chinese leader's domestic opponents could quote Soviet authority for the proposition that his theory was an anachronism. Formulas in this tradition are weapons with serial numbers, and this one's target was legible.

A coda to the phrase's history belongs to the record. The Chinese party's own constitution, revised after its leader's death and the arrest of his closest associates, quietly retired the continuing-revolution formulation as the guiding line, and the resolution of 1981 reassessed the theory as a grave error of the leader's later years while preserving his standing as the revolution's principal founder. The Soviet formula of the whole people's state, meanwhile, survived in its own constitution until the state itself expired. Doctrinal formulas, the comparison suggests, die in two ways: by repudiation, which leaves the argument available for future use, and by the death of the state that carried them, which leaves only the archive. The Chinese repudiation, precisely because it was argued and documented, is why the Chinese side of this dispute remains a living subject of study in a way the Soviet side, for all the force of its central objection, often is not.


## How each side wrote the other's history

A doctrinal schism eventually rewrites the past it shares, and the split produced two incompatible histories of the same movement. In the Chinese telling that hardened during the nineteen sixties, the Russian Revolution remained a founding glory, Lenin remained beyond reproach, and the fall began with the successors: a line of degeneration running from the secret speech through the programme of the whole people's state to the alleged restoration, with Stalin repositioned as the last defender of the correct road despite his errors. The telling required delicate surgery, since the same Stalin had been the architect of the system the Chinese were describing as restored, but the surgery was performed by periodisation: the restoration was dated not from the revolution's victory but from the succession, so that the Soviet Union of the nineteen thirties could be honoured and the Soviet Union of the nineteen sixties condemned as its negation.

In the Soviet telling, the mirror operation was performed on China. The Chinese Revolution remained a great event, the Chinese party's victory a triumph of the world movement, and the fall began when a leadership formed by peasant war mistook its national experience for universal law and raised a personality cult of its own while denouncing another's. Soviet historiography of the dispute, such as it was, tended to dissolve the doctrine into the biography, treating the theory of continuing revolution as the rationalisation of one man's refusal to retire into the honourable elder statesmanship the Soviet system prescribed for its founders. The comparison has resisted that dissolution throughout, on the ground that the doctrine's logic can be stated, tested and found consequential independently of its author's motives. But the Soviet historiographical instinct points at something the Chinese texts preferred to hide: how much of the doctrine's career depended on the unique position of a founder who remained, simultaneously, the party's supreme theorist, its military legend and its head of state, a combination no successor could inherit and no institution could survive unchanged.

### What did the two rival histories agree on, beneath their quarrels?

On the greatness of the shared beginning. Both narratives preserved October and the Chinese victory as sacred events, both treated Lenin as the uncontaminated source, and both located the fall in a succession. The agreement is diagnostic: heirs who dispute an inheritance do not usually dispute that the estate was once magnificent. The shared reverence is what made each side's charge against the other a charge of betrayal rather than of error.

The rival histories also agreed, without ever saying so, on a method: the explanation of doctrinal change by the moral quality of leaders. Revisionism in the Chinese telling was something men did when they grew comfortable; dogmatism in the Soviet telling was a failing of men grown vain. Later scholarship, working after the passions cooled, has generally preferred the structural explanations this article has leaned on: the different founding experiences of the two parties, the different levels of development of the two societies, the different positions each state occupied in the world. Yet the older moralised histories retain a documentary value beyond their reliability. They show how the participants experienced the dispute, as a drama of fidelity and apostasy, and any account that corrects their method while ignoring their experience will fail to explain why the polemics burned as they did. The historian's task with such material is double, and the comparison has attempted both halves: to explain the dispute better than the participants could, and to remember that the participants' worse explanation was itself one of the dispute's causes.

## The Western reception: students, translators and the little red book

The split's strangest theatre was the capitalist West, where the Chinese doctrine arrived in the late nineteen sixties as a commodity of the book trade and a banner of generational revolt. The little red book of quotations, compiled for the Chinese army and then exported in translation by the hundreds of thousands, became for a season the most visible political text on earth, carried by students who in many cases knew the doctrine it excerpted only through the excerpts. Western Maoism, as the phenomenon is loosely called, was a genuine intellectual current in a few places, notably France, where philosophers and former students of philosophy subjected the Chinese texts to serious reading and drew from them a critique of the Soviet-aligned party at home. In most places it was closer to a style of refusal, in which China figured as the proof that the Soviet model was not the only socialism and that revolution remained possible in a world the older parties had declared settled.

Soviet-aligned parties in the West experienced the current as an affliction from an unexpected direction. Their authority over the radical young had rested on being the established opposition; the Chinese alternative allowed the young to be more orthodox than the orthodox, to outbid the party in revolutionary purity while accusing it of the precise crime, revisionism, that the party's own tradition had taught them to dread. The polemics thus reproduced themselves at the level of the campus and the factory youth section, in miniature and often in parody, yet with real organisational consequences, as splits carried away minorities who in some countries built durable small organisations and in others vanished within a decade. This article describes that afterlife structurally, as its brief requires, and names no organisation of the present; the comparative point is that the export version of the Chinese doctrine was selected, by its recipients, for its usefulness against the Soviet-aligned parties of their own countries. What travelled was the charge. What often stayed behind was the theory of class regeneration that had generated the charge, which required a ruling party to apply to and therefore had no use in opposition except as rhetoric.

### Why did the Chinese doctrine appeal to young radicals who knew little of China?

Because it solved their local problem. It allowed a break with the established communist parties that presented itself not as a departure from the tradition but as its recovery, complete with founders' texts, a martyrology of betrayal and a living state as proof of possibility. Doctrines travel when they arm a quarrel already waiting for them, and the quarrel between generations inside the Western left was waiting.

The Western reception also fed back into the self-understanding of the two centres in ways the comparison should note. For Beijing, the spectacle of European students waving the little red book confirmed the claim that the centre of revolutionary gravity had shifted away from Moscow, and Chinese propaganda dwelt on it accordingly. For Moscow, the same spectacle confirmed that the Chinese doctrine in export was an adolescent solvent, dissolving discipline wherever it landed, and Soviet commentary treated Western Maoism as evidence for the prosecution in the case against its authors. Both readings were self-serving and both contained a fragment of the truth, which is the usual condition of the evidence in this dispute and the reason the article has insisted on stating each side's case in the form its ablest defenders would recognise.


## Dialectics as a battlefield

Even the philosophy of the doctrine became contested territory, and the contest illuminates the others because it shows the dispute operating at the level of method. The companion article devoted to contradiction and practice treats the Chinese essays in their own right; what belongs here is the use made of them in the quarrel. Soviet philosophy, as codified in the textbooks, presented dialectics as a settled system of laws and categories, teachable by exposition and defensible by citation. The Chinese essays on contradiction, written two decades earlier in a guerrilla base area, presented dialectics as an instrument of investigation: the universal law was the unity and struggle of opposites, and its entire content lay in the concrete analysis of the particular contradiction, its principal aspect, and the conditions under which aspects transform into one another. When the polemics opened, each side's philosophy indicted the other's politics. Soviet philosophers found in the Chinese essays a subjectivism that dissolved objective law into the leader's situational judgement. Chinese philosophers found in the Soviet textbooks a metaphysics in dialectical dress, a catechism that had forgotten that the doctrine's soul was the concrete analysis of concrete conditions.

The quarrel over method had a practical edge that pure philosophy rarely carries. A dialectics of concrete analysis licenses the investigator to find that the principal contradiction has changed, that yesterday's ally is the present danger, that the party itself has become the terrain of struggle. A dialectics of settled laws licenses the custodian to reply that the science has already determined the character of the epoch and the society, and that situational improvisation is empiricism in uniform. The theory of continuing revolution presupposed the first method; the doctrine of abolished antagonism presupposed the second. Readers who wonder how a dispute about classes could also be a dispute about textbooks have the answer here: in this tradition the textbook is a constitution, and a dispute about its interpretation is a dispute about who may legitimately act.

### What did the Chinese side mean by calling the Soviet textbooks metaphysical?

That the textbooks had converted a method of inquiry into a list of conclusions. Where the Chinese essays treated every category as a direction for investigating a specific situation, the codified system treated its categories as descriptions already known to fit every situation, so that investigation became the filing of new facts under old headings. The charge was unfair to the best Soviet scholarship and accurate about the catechism most cadres actually studied.

It is worth pausing over the human setting of the Chinese essays, because the comparison's discipline of treating texts as documents includes their circumstances. The lectures on contradiction and practice were delivered in 1937 at the party's university in a loess cave settlement, to cadres of an army encircled and outnumbered, as part of a campaign to raise the theoretical level of a movement whose leaders feared it would be destroyed by its own empiricism and dogmatism in equal measure. The dogmatists of that internal argument were the returned students who quoted the Soviet textbooks against the experience of the Chinese war, and the essays were weapons in that fight before they were classics. Two decades later their author deployed the same weapons against the textbooks' homeland. The continuity is not a scandal and not a secret; it is the normal biography of a fighting doctrine, and it explains why the Chinese side experienced the Soviet codification not as a neutral science but as the ideology of the very factional style it had defeated at home.

## Under the successors: developed socialism and the reassessment

The doctrines outlived their principal authors in instructively different ways, and a brief historical treatment of their careers under the successors completes the comparison. In the Soviet Union, the generation that followed the secret speech's author consolidated the doctrine of abolished antagonism into the theory of developed socialism, proclaimed as the society's achieved stage in the late nineteen sixties and written into the constitution of 1977. Developed socialism was that same state furnished with an economics: a mature, stable, essentially completed socialism whose remaining tasks were intensification, discipline and the gradual approach of communism across a timescale that receded as it was approached. The doctrine's function was frankly conservative in the literal sense. It conserved. It told the apparatus that history had vindicated it, told the citizen that the present order was the destination his parents had been promised, and told the theorist that no new categories would be required. When a later Soviet leadership attempted, in the nineteen eighties, to reform the order the doctrine described, it discovered that the doctrine had left the society without a legitimate language for its own transformation, and the reformers' improvised vocabulary of acceleration and restructuring slid within a few years into a repudiation of the inheritance itself. The whole-people state ended not with a defence of its theory but with its theory's quiet abandonment by its own custodians.

In China, the succession took the opposite form. Within two years of the founder's death, the party had arrested his closest associates; within five, it had adopted the resolution of 1981 that reassessed his record, preserved his standing as principal founder of the revolution, and condemned the theory of continuing revolution as a grave error that had led to catastrophe. The economic course that followed, of market reform under party leadership, was never reconciled with the doctrine this article has examined; it was simply pursued alongside a formula that attributed the founder's Thought to his correct early period and quarantined the later theory as aberration. The asymmetry with the Soviet case is exact and worth the comparison's final emphasis. The Soviet doctrine died unrenounced, by the expiry of its state. The Chinese doctrine was renounced, in part, by its own party, in a document that performed on its author the proportional assessment he had once performed on Stalin. One tradition embalmed its founder's system and perished with it. The other divided its founder against himself and survived, transformed almost beyond recognition, under the management of the division.

### Why did the Chinese party preserve the founder while condemning his theory?

Because its own legitimacy was mortgaged to his revolution. A total negation on the Soviet model of the nineteen fifties would have indicted the founding of the state, the war that made it and the party that he had led for four decades. The proportional method, merit predominating over error, allowed the party to amputate the doctrine while keeping the pedigree, at the price of a permanent ambiguity at the centre of its official history.

Neither succession should be read into the polemics afterwards as their refutation, tempting as that reading is. That the Soviet order collapsed does not prove the Chinese criterion of class correct, since the order that collapsed was brought down by failures its own theory could not name but also by economic and national pressures no criterion of class, by itself, explains. That the Chinese party condemned continuing revolution does not prove the Soviet objection correct in its entirety, since the condemnation was issued by the very apparatus the doctrine had accused, sitting in judgement on the accusation, a circumstance the doctrine's ghost is entitled to note. The comparison ends its historical work with the same discipline it began: the outcomes are evidence about the doctrines, weighty evidence, and they are not verdicts that relieve the reader of the argument.


## The national question in the shadows of both doctrines

Each party accused the other of the sin its own tradition most feared after heresy: nationalism. Soviet polemics charged that the Chinese doctrine was the ideology of a great-power chauvinism in Marxist dress, pointing to the territorial claims implicit in Chinese maps of the lost territories, to the treatment of the dispute as a matter of national honour, and to a style of leadership that placed one country's experience at the centre of the world's future. Chinese polemics returned the charge with interest, describing the Soviet Union as a social-imperialist power whose domination of its allies, whose interventions in Eastern Europe, and whose bargaining with the United States over the heads of smaller nations reproduced the conduct of the empires Marxism existed to oppose. The exchange is worth the comparison's attention because it reveals a limit both doctrines shared. A theory built to subordinate national interest to class analysis had no comfortable vocabulary for a world in which its two greatest states pursued national interests in its own name, and the accusation of nationalism functioned, for each side, as the available translation of that discomfort.

The historical record gives each accusation material to work with, which is why neither can be dismissed as mere propaganda. The Soviet Union did intervene in Hungary in 1956 and in Czechoslovakia in 1968 to preserve its alliance system, and did develop, in the doctrine associated with its later leadership, a theory of limited sovereignty for the states of its camp that sat poorly beside the vocabulary of proletarian internationalism. China did press historical grievances about the tsarist-era treaties in its polemics, did treat the border question as a test of national dignity, and did, after the split's military climax, construct an international alignment whose logic was the balance of power rather than the world revolution its texts continued to invoke. A comparison that acquitted either side of the national charge would be failing the evidence. The more precise conclusion is that the doctrines were universalist theories carried by nation-states, and that the friction between the theory and the carrier is not an accident of these two cases but a structural feature of the whole tradition, visible wherever a revolutionary doctrine has held a state.

### Did either doctrine have a theory of its own state's national interest?

Not an honest one. Both traditions treated national interest as a category applicable to other states, while their own state's conduct was described in the vocabulary of international duty, historical mission or defence of the camp. The absence is itself a finding of the comparison: the dispute's most violent moments occurred exactly where the missing theory should have been, in the space between two states' security claims that neither doctrine could acknowledge as legitimate on both sides.

There is a smaller, sharper point to extract from the national exchange before leaving it. The Chinese accusation of social-imperialism, socialism in words and imperialism in deeds, was the theory of continuing revolution applied to foreign policy: a state whose internal order had been restored to capitalism would behave abroad as capitalist states behave, whatever its flags proclaimed. The accusation therefore stands or falls with the internal analysis, and its career illustrates how completely the generator organised the Chinese worldview by the nineteen sixties' end. Every phenomenon, from wage policy in a Soviet factory to tanks in a European capital, was processed through the single premise about class regeneration. A theory that explains everything its holders encounter has achieved a kind of completeness that should make its holders suspicious, and the Soviet objection about unfalsifiability, first met in this article as an argument about class criteria, returns at the level of the whole system. The comparison notes the return without needing to decide it, because the verdict section must now weigh the whole.

## How to read the two doctrines as documents

A short discipline for readers who go from this comparison to the sources will repay the space it takes. Read the polemics in pairs. A Chinese comment read alone persuades by accumulation; read against the Soviet text it answers, it becomes an argument, with selections, emphases and silences that the pairing exposes. Read the programmatic documents for what they assume rather than only for what they assert. The Soviet programme from the opening years of the nineteen sixties is most revealing where it is most serene, in its confidence that the remaining problems of its society are problems of growth and education. The Chinese comments are most revealing where they are most repetitive, since the repetitions mark the points the authors believed the audience was resisting. Read the silences as evidence. Neither side's canon contains an honest account of the national interest of its own state, as the previous section argued; neither contains a procedure for the peaceful correction of a supreme leader; neither contains a theory of how the argument between them could legitimately be settled. The silences are the doctrines' shared confession.

Read, finally, with the dates attached. These were not treatises composed in a library's calm. The secret speech was a weapon in a succession; the editorials of 1956 were a shield raised in a storm; the comments of 1963 and 1964 were a declaration of war issued in instalments; the whole-people programme was a promise made to a tired population by a leadership that needed one. Dating a text does not reduce it to its occasion, and this article has resisted that reduction throughout. But a reader who knows the occasion can see which parts of a text are load-bearing argument and which are the scaffolding of the moment, and can therefore carry away the argument without swallowing the scaffolding. That skill, applied to the whole of this series' material, is the nearest thing the cluster offers to a method, and the persona sections below translate it for the three audiences the series serves.

### What is the single most common error made by readers of the polemics?

Treating the winner of each exchange as the side whose quotations are longer. Both sides quoted the founders copiously and accurately enough for their purposes, because the canon genuinely supported both constructions. The discriminating reader watches instead for the premise each side declines to state, since the unstated premises, about property, about the party, about the state, are where the actual disagreement lives and where the quotations stop helping.


## Verdict: weighing the seven differences

The comparison can now render its judgement, and the judgement has three parts: a ranking of the differences, an assessment of the two central doctrines, and a statement of what the split, considered whole, demonstrates about the tradition that produced it.

The ranking first. Of the seven differences, four belong to the period of revolution rather than to the period of rule, and their importance, though real, is bounded by that fact. The agent and the strategy decided how the Chinese party conquered power, and they retain permanent interest for the theory of revolution in agrarian societies; the mass line codified a style of leadership whose influence has outlasted its context; the assessment of Stalin decided the moral bookkeeping of the movement's past and rehearsed the method by which the Chinese party would one day judge its own founder. The difference over war and coexistence belonged to the period of rule but faced outward, toward the relations between systems, and it faded as the nuclear stalemate taught both capitals the prudence one of them had preached. The remaining two differences, over classes under socialism and over continuing revolution, are one difference stated at two depths, and they outweigh the rest combined, because they concerned what a socialist society is, and because their consequences were visited not on battlefields or conference floors but on the daily life of two great populations across decades. A student who remembers only one sentence from this article should remember that the split was deepest where it was most theoretical, and that the theory in question was the theory of the post-revolutionary order itself.

The assessment of the two doctrines must be as even-handed as the evidence and as unsparing as the record, and it can be given in two sentences each. The Soviet doctrine possessed the stronger criterion and the weaker eyesight. Its insistence that class be anchored in production relations protected the concept from dissolution into rhetoric, and its logic, pressed home in the objection this article stated at full strength, has never been answered on its own ground; yet the doctrine built upon that criterion certified as classless a society visibly stratified by control, and in doing so it disabled the regime's capacity to describe, and therefore to correct, the order it governed. The Chinese doctrine possessed the sharper eyesight and the ruinous criterion. It saw, earlier and more clearly than its rival, that expropriation does not abolish domination and that a ruling apparatus can consolidate into a self-reproducing stratum; yet the test it proposed for that stratum's presence dissolved, in application, into the political judgement of whoever held power, and the campaigns it licensed inflicted on its own society a share of the very arbitrariness it claimed to be abolishing. Each doctrine is thus the other's best critic and its own worst advertisement, which is the deepest reason the comparison resists a verdict for either side.

What the split demonstrates about the tradition is the third and largest judgement. Marxism entered the twentieth century as a theory of capitalism and of the revolution against it. The split was the moment when the tradition's two greatest states discovered, in public and at each other's expense, that the theory contained no settled account of the society the revolution was supposed to found: no agreed criterion for when classes have ended, no procedure for correcting a ruling party from within its own premises, no theory of relations between socialist states, no honest vocabulary for the national interests of revolutionary governments. Each side improvised its completion under pressure, and the improvisations contradicted. Later movements inherited the improvisations as doctrines and fought over them in turn, in the regional theatres this series examines. The split is therefore not an episode in the history of Marxism to be filed between the revolution and the collapse. It is the central event of the tradition's career in power, the point at which its unexamined premises presented their bill, and it will repay study for exactly as long as human beings continue to ask what happens to a revolution's promises when the revolution wins.

### Why does the verdict refuse to award the dispute to either side?

Because the record convicts each doctrine at the point of the other's accusation. The Soviet charge that the Chinese criterion would become a purge instrument was borne out by the campaigns. The Chinese charge that the Soviet criterion would blind a regime to its own stratification was borne out by the stagnation and the silent expiry that followed. A comparison that crowned either side would have to forget half of its own evidence.

One further discipline belongs to the verdict, concerning the temptation of hindsight. It is easy, from the far side of the Soviet collapse and the Chinese transformation, to read the polemics as the thrashings of doctrines already doomed, and to treat the participants' passion as a failure to see what the reader sees. The discipline this series applies to all its material forbids the ease. The participants were deciding, with the information and the formation they had, questions that remain genuinely difficult: how to define class where property has been socialised, how to prevent a revolutionary government from hardening into a caste, how two states sharing a doctrine should behave when their interests collide. That their answers failed in opposite directions is the finding. That the questions were real is the finding's necessary companion, and the reader who holds both has understood the split better than either of its protagonists could afford to.


## The geography of world revolution: two maps

By the end of the nineteen sixties the dispute had produced rival maps of the world, and the maps deserve a section because they show doctrine operating as grand strategy. The Soviet map was organised around the confrontation of two systems and the avoidance of their direct collision. Its decisive terrain was Europe and the strategic balance; its decisive process was the shifting correlation of forces, measured in industry, armaments and the allegiance of states; its expectation was that time, and the demonstration effect of a prosperous socialism, would do the work that insurrection had once been expected to do. The Chinese map was organised around a different centre of gravity entirely. Its decisive terrain was the vast intermediate zone of Asia, Africa and Latin America, where the contradictions of imperialism were sharpest and its rule thinnest; its decisive process was the accumulation of national liberation struggles, the countryside of the world encircling its cities as the Chinese countryside had encircled its own; its expectation was that the storm centres of revolution had moved, permanently, away from the continent where Marx had expected them.

In the nineteen seventies the Chinese map was redrawn in the theory of the three worlds, which sorted states into a first world of the two superpowers, a second world of the lesser developed powers, and a third world comprising the great majority of humanity, whose struggles the theory cast as the principal force of the age. The redrawing had a doctrinal audacity the comparison should register: it classified the Soviet Union with the United States in a single category of hegemonic powers, completing in strategic theory what the charge of social-imperialism had asserted in polemic, and it licensed alliances, including with the lesser imperialisms and even with the principal enemy of the previous map, against the superpower now designated the most dangerous. Soviet commentary treated the theory as the final abandonment of class analysis for geopolitics, and for once the two sides agreed on a fact: the map had indeed subordinated every other distinction to the identity of the principal enemy. They disagreed only on whether that subordination was the essence of dialectics or its betrayal.

### What did the two maps disagree about, beneath the cartography?

About where history was being made. The Soviet map located the motor of change in the competition of systems at the summit of the world order, which made the preservation and prosperity of the leading socialist state the movement's first duty. The Chinese map located the motor in the revolts of the oppressed nations at the order's base, which made solidarity with those revolts, whatever their leadership, the first duty. Each map demoted the other's motor to a sideshow.

The maps also determined what each capital could see. A strategy organised around the correlation of forces at the summit rendered the Soviet leadership attentive to stability in its own sphere and correspondingly hostile to adventures, including the adventures of its own allies, that might disturb the balance. A strategy organised around the storm centres rendered the Chinese leadership attentive to every insurgency's propaganda value and correspondingly indulgent toward movements whose local prospects were hopeless but whose existence confirmed the map. The comparison's earlier account of doctrinal transfer, of strategies carrying the imprint of their birthplace, applies here at the scale of the planet: each side had universalised its own road, the Soviet road of state construction defended by deterrence, the Chinese road of encirclement waged by the rural majority of mankind. The maps were the two revolutions' autobiographies, projected onto the globe.

## Party schools and the manufacture of orthodoxy

Doctrines of the kind compared in this article do not float from text to text. They are manufactured, in identifiable institutions, by identifiable labour, and a section on the machinery of manufacture belongs in any full comparison. The Soviet system perfected the machinery first: a hierarchy of party schools rising from the district to the central institute, a standard curriculum anchored in the textbook of dialectical and historical materialism and the history of the party, examinations that certified a cadre's reliability along with his knowledge, and a publishing apparatus that could put an identical formulation into the hands of a student in Vladivostok and a fraternal delegate in Montevideo within the same season. The product was a genuinely international doctrinal language, in which a Romanian economist and a Mongolian propagandist could recognise each other's categories at sight. Whatever the system's intellectual costs, and this article has not concealed them, the achievement of scale was real, and no rival centre ever matched it.

The Chinese machinery was built for a different purpose and bore the marks of its guerrilla origin. Its characteristic institution was not the school with its syllabus but the study campaign, in which a designated text was read collectively, discussed against the reader's own conduct, and applied in criticism and self-criticism sessions that made doctrine a discipline of the self. Rectification, the name the party gave the method during the nineteen forties, aimed to remake the cadre's style of work and thought, not merely to inform him. When the split came, the two machineries produced two kinds of adherent abroad. The Soviet-trained cadre carried away a system, portable and complete, whose weakness was that its completeness discouraged the investigation the Chinese texts demanded. The adherent formed by the Chinese method carried away a practice, investigation, the mass line, the campaign, whose weakness was that a practice without a settled system could be redirected by whoever defined the campaign's target. The split inside so many third parties was, among other things, a collision between men formed by the school and men formed by the campaign, each experiencing the other's formation as a kind of illiteracy.

### Why does the machinery of doctrinal education matter to a comparison of doctrines?

Because a doctrine's content in the world is the content its machinery can transmit. The Soviet system's genius for codification explains why its positions arrived abroad as finished formulae, and the Chinese system's genius for mobilisation explains why its positions arrived as methods and slogans detached from their texts. Judging either doctrine only by its best books mistakes the part for the transmitted whole.

There is a postscript to the machinery that the comparison should not omit, because it concerns the fate of the texts themselves. The Soviet textbook system, having certified generations, was dismantled with the state, and its books survive as the archaeology of an orthodoxy. The Chinese texts of the dispute fared better as literature and worse as law: the essays of the nineteen thirties retained their place in the canon of the party that condemned their author's later theory, the quotations book passed into the global history of political publishing, and the nineteen sixties polemics became the scripture of movements their authors never met. A doctrine's afterlife is decided less by its truth than by the portability of its artefacts, and on that measure the guerrilla's pamphlets outlasted the ministry's textbooks, which is perhaps the final irony the comparison has to offer on the difference between the two traditions.


## The mirror of earlier schisms

The tradition had schismed before, and the comparison gains depth by holding the split against its predecessors. The expulsion of the anarchists from the First International established the pattern of a founder's authority used to define a deviation out of the movement. The collapse of the Second International in 1914, when its parties voted war credits for their own governments, taught the founders of the Third that a schism could be an act of fidelity, the refusal to remain inside a body that had betrayed the doctrine's core. The struggle against Trotsky in the nineteen twenties added the pattern that most directly anticipates the Sino-Soviet case: a dispute inside a ruling party, conducted in the vocabulary of doctrine, whose loser was reclassified from comrade to class enemy and whose stakes were the character of the order that followed revolution. The Chinese theorists knew that history intimately and cited it; their charge that a new Trotsky question had arisen, with the roles reassigned, was among the polemics' most wounding strokes, because it implied that the Soviet leadership had inherited not Lenin's mantle but the position of those Lenin's heirs had once condemned.

Set against that lineage, the Sino-Soviet split is distinguished by one feature that none of its predecessors possessed: both parties to it held state power over great populations, commanded armies and nuclear programmes, and could therefore conduct a schism at a scale, and with consequences, that the earlier disputes never approached. The First International's quarrels moved pamphlets. This quarrel moved divisions to a frontier. Yet the structure of legitimation remained eerily constant across the century: a canon, a custody dispute, a deviation named and anatomised, an expulsion, and a rival centre claiming to embody the continuity the expeller had broken. A student who grasps that structure possesses a key that opens schisms throughout the tradition's history, including several examined elsewhere in this series, and can see the Sino-Soviet case as at once the largest instance of the pattern and the instance that broke it, since after two states had excommunicated each other, no subsequent centre could plausibly claim the universal custody both had discredited.

### What distinguishes a schism from an ordinary doctrinal disagreement?

The claim of custody. Disagreement argues about what the doctrine means; schism argues about who is entitled to mean it, and settles the question by separation. The test in any dispute inside this tradition is therefore institutional rather than intellectual: the moment each side begins organising the other's exclusion, rather than the other's persuasion, a disagreement has become a schism whatever its subject.

## The cost inside third parties

The bill for the schism was not presented only in the two capitals. Inside the parties that split, the doctrinal war was waged by men and women who had joined a single movement and now discovered that their comrades of decades were agents of a foreign centre. The expulsions and counter-expulsions of the nineteen sixties were conducted, in many countries, with a bitterness that the original dispute's scale cannot explain but its structure can: each faction held the theory that the other side's position was not an error but a class allegiance, and class allegiances are not compromised with. Friendships, marriages, trade union fractions and publishing houses divided along a line drawn by an argument about the class nature of societies most of the disputants had never seen. The comparison records the pattern without adjudicating its local instances, several of which are handled in this series' regional articles, because the pattern is itself the evidence for one of this article's recurring claims: doctrines that classify their opponents cease, at a certain intensity, to describe the world and begin to manufacture it.

There were also parties that refused the choice, and their experience deserves the record's respect. The Vietnamese communists, fighting a war in which both patrons' aid was indispensable, maintained a formal neutrality that amounted to a quiet declaration of independence from the entire custody dispute. The Japanese party quarrelled with both centres in turn and emerged with its autonomy enlarged. The Romanian leadership, as noted earlier, converted the schism into national room for manoeuvre. Each refusal demonstrated the same proposition from a different angle: the custody claim depended on the parties' willingness to be judged, and a party that declined the tribunal could not be excommunicated, only denounced, which is a weaker thing. The centres' authority, so formidable in the polemics' imagination, rested on consent more than either centre could admit, and the split's long-term effect was to teach the whole movement that the consent could be withdrawn. In that sense the schism never ended. It generalised.

### Why did some parties refuse to choose between the two centres?

Because the choice, as the centres framed it, required subordinating a national movement's judgement to a foreign tribunal's verdict on questions those movements had not asked. Parties with their own wars to fight, their own states to bargain with or their own publics to persuade discovered that neutrality in the custody dispute was the price of autonomy in everything else, and several paid it deliberately and said so.


## The material sinews: aid, trade and the price of fraternity

Beneath the polemics ran a material relationship whose terms shaped the dispute's emotional register, and the comparison must describe it, under the figures protocol that governs this article, in qualitative terms. Through the nineteen fifties the Soviet Union was China's industrial tutor and principal supplier of complete plants, designs, credits and specialists, and the Chinese industrial base of the period was built substantially on that transfer. The relationship was never experienced in Beijing as the disinterested fraternity of the communiques. It arrived with advisers whose authority grated, with joint arrangements whose terms recalled older patterns of foreign tutelage, with refusals and delays on the most sensitive technologies, and with a hierarchy of knowledge in which the Chinese partner was cast permanently as the pupil. Chinese polemics later described the entire transfer as a transaction conducted at a price and repaid with interest, which was ungenerous and not baseless; Soviet accounts described a generosity exploited and then denounced, which was self-serving and not baseless either. The withdrawal of the specialists in 1960 converted the grievance into a founding myth of self-reliance, and the myth did real work in the decade that followed, underwriting the Chinese claim that a poor country could industrialise by mobilisation where the Soviet model required a tutor.

Trade after the split contracted to a fraction of its former place in both economies, and each side built its external economic relations around the other's absence: the Soviet Union deeper into the division of labour of its own bloc, China toward a posture of self-reliance supplemented, as the nineteen seventies opened, by a turn toward the very Western markets and technologies its polemics had anathematised. The doctrinal significance of the material story is the one this article has drawn repeatedly. Interests did not invent the doctrines, but interests selected which doctrines would be sharpened, when, and against whom; and the memory of dependence, of advisers withdrawn mid-project and blueprints taken home, gave the Chinese theory of a restored and arrogant Soviet bureaucracy a plausibility to its first audience that no purely textual argument could have manufactured. Ideas in this tradition were believed, when they were believed, because experience had prepared their hearers.

### Why did the withdrawal of the advisers matter beyond its economic cost?

Because it was legible as a revelation of the relationship's true structure. A fraternity that can be suspended by telegram, the Chinese argument ran, was a hierarchy wearing fraternity's clothes, and the lesson was applied far beyond economics: if dependence in industry meant subordination, then dependence in doctrine, on the centre that supplied the textbooks, meant the same. Self-reliance became a theory of knowledge as well as of production.

## The bomb and the doctrine of war

One further difference, entangled with coexistence yet distinct from it, concerned nuclear weapons and the meaning of their existence. The Soviet conclusion from the bomb, drawn by the leadership that succeeded Stalin, was that the weapon had transformed the character of war between the systems: a general war would destroy the civilisation socialism existed to build, and therefore its prevention became the premise of all other strategy. The Chinese statements of the period took a publicly different line, treating the weapon as a paper tiger in the hands of a power whose real strength lay elsewhere, insisting that the outcome of wars is decided by men rather than by arms, and refusing to grant the bomb the authority to veto the struggles of the oppressed. Soviet commentary professed horror at the alleged Chinese insouciance about casualties, and quoted, endlessly, remarks attributed to the Chinese leader about the survivability of his country in a nuclear exchange; the quotations' provenance and context were contested then and remain a lesson in the polemics' evidentiary standards, which this article notes without reproducing the exchange's figures, in keeping with its protocol.

Beneath the rhetoric lay a genuine strategic asymmetry that the doctrinal language partly concealed and partly revealed. The Soviet Union, possessing a growing arsenal and a European empire to defend, had become a status quo power in the military sense, whatever its ideology said; its doctrine of war's avoidability was the theory of that condition. China, possessing no arsenal until the middle nineteen sixties and facing a hostile superpower on its coasts, had reasons to deny that the adversary's weapons settled the political question; its rhetoric of the paper tiger supplied the theory of that condition. Each accused the other of tailoring Marxism to its arsenal, and each accusation landed. Yet the doctrinal residue outlasted the arsenals' asymmetry. The proposition that political will and mass mobilisation decide outcomes, against the proposition that the correlation of material forces decides them, is a very old argument inside the tradition, older than either state, and the nuclear quarrel was its most consequential modern staging. The comparison leaves the argument where the tradition left it: unsettled, and liable to reappear wherever a weaker revolutionary force confronts a stronger material power.

### What did the paper tiger formula claim, in its own terms?

That the weapon's possessor was strategically weak where it appeared strong, because its power rested on the exploitation that generated resistance everywhere, while the forces opposing it were strategically strong where they appeared weak, because they represented the direction of history. The formula was a morale doctrine and a theory of the long term compressed into an image, and its critics, in Moscow and elsewhere, replied that images do not intercept missiles.


## The scholarly literature: how historians have apportioned the explanation

A comparison owes its readers a map of the scholarship as well as of the doctrines, since the two are easily confused. The literature on the split falls, with the usual untidiness, into four broad schools. The first, written largely during the Cold War by analysts in the West, treated the split as a conflict of national interests conducted in ideological code, and read the polemics the way one reads diplomatic correspondence, for the interests beneath the formulae. The second, associated with historians of the Chinese revolution, insisted on the autonomy of the doctrinal dispute and reconstructed the Chinese positions from the texts with a seriousness the first school rarely matched. The third, emerging as archives opened after the Soviet collapse, worked the documentary record of the alliance's internal history, the summits, the advisers, the quarrels over nuclear assistance and the frontier, and produced a granular account in which doctrine and interest interpenetrate at every level. The fourth, written from within the broader Marxist tradition by its historians and critics, treated the split as the tradition's own reckoning with power, and asked what the dispute reveals about the theory both sides shared.

This article has drawn on all four schools while belonging, in its method, to none exclusively, and its namable claim can be located against them precisely. Against the first school, the generator claim holds that the doctrines had causal force of their own, since premises about class under socialism generated campaigns and immobilities that interest alone does not predict. Against the second, it holds that the doctrines were carried by states whose interests selected their timing and their targets, so that textual reconstruction without statecraft explains the argument's content but not its career. Against the third, it adds only that the archival grain, however fine, still requires a framework to say which documents matter most, and offers the class question as that framework. Against the fourth, it enters the caution that the tradition's self-reckoning was conducted by parties with armies, and that its conclusions were enforced as well as argued, a circumstance no purely internal history of ideas should be allowed to forget.

### How should a student weigh a doctrinal text against an archival document?

As answers to different questions. The text states what its authors wished to be believed, by their cadres and by posterity, and is therefore the primary evidence for the doctrine. The archive records what was said and decided where belief was not the audience, and so stands as the primary evidence for the decision. The split is unusually instructive because both bodies of evidence are rich, and because they so often point in different directions at once.

The literature also preserves a warning about sources that this series has applied throughout its regional clusters. Each side's documentary output was produced by institutions with an interest in the record, and the memoirs that supplement it were written, in many cases, by participants settling accounts. The Chinese publication of conversations and internal remarks was selective in ways that served the publisher's line at the moment of publication; the Soviet record was guarded by an apparatus that treated the dispute as a state secret in its operational parts. A researcher entering the field should therefore triangulate relentlessly: the polemic against the archive, the memoir against the minutes, the claim about the other side's motives against the other side's paper trail. The comparison's own practice, of stating each doctrine from its documents and testing it against the outcomes it licensed, is offered as the portable version of that discipline.

## Questions the comparison leaves open

An honest comparison ends by inventorying its own limits, and four questions survive everything assembled here. The first is counterfactual: whether a Soviet leadership that had handled the succession of 1956 with the proportional method, or a Chinese leadership that had accepted a property-based criterion with institutional safeguards, could have held the camp together. The evidence assembled suggests the doctrines were load-bearing enough that different handling might have delayed the rupture without dissolving its grounds, since the grounds lay in the two revolutions' different completions of an unfinished theory. The second question is comparative in a wider frame: whether the pattern identified here, a generative disagreement about the post-revolutionary order, recurs wherever a revolutionary doctrine holds two states at once, or whether the Sino-Soviet case is singular in the completeness of its documentation. The series' other regional clusters supply partial parallels and no exact repetition.

The third open question concerns the criterion of class itself, which the verdict declined to award. Social theory since the split has not settled whether control without ownership constitutes class in a sense strong enough to bear the weight the Chinese doctrine loaded onto it, and the question has migrated into debates about managerial strata, platform owners who own little of the infrastructure they command, and officials whose power is entirely positional. The polemics are not the place those debates will be settled, but they remain the most dramatic historical laboratory in which the criterion was tested at the scale of nations. The fourth question is about the reader. Every generation that has taken up these texts has found its own quarrel prefigured in them, the quarrel between the custodians of an inheritance and its renovators, between the fear of betrayal and the fear of embalming. The comparison cannot prevent that use and would not if it could. It can only insist, as it has throughout, that the texts be read whole, in pairs, with their dates attached and their silences audible, before they are conscripted.

### Why end a comparison with open questions rather than a settled doctrine?

Because the subject itself is a demonstration of what settled doctrines cost. The two parties compared in this article each possessed a finished answer to the class question under socialism, and the finishes were the problem. A comparison that closed its own questions by decree would have learned the wrong lesson from its material, however satisfying the symmetry might feel.


## Stages of revolution: New Democracy against the two-stage orthodoxy

One doctrinal difference has been present throughout this article without receiving its own treatment, because it hides inside the rows on agent and strategy. It concerns the stages through which a revolution in a colonial or semi-colonial country must pass. The orthodoxy inherited from the Communist International prescribed two stages: a bourgeois-democratic revolution, completing the tasks of national independence and agrarian reform under a broad alliance, followed, after an interval of development, by the socialist revolution proper. The Chinese doctrine of New Democracy, formulated in the writings of 1939 and 1940, revised the schema at its hinge. The democratic stage in a semi-colonial country, it argued, would not be a bourgeois dictatorship of the old type but a joint dictatorship of the revolutionary classes under proletarian leadership, a new-democratic republic that was neither capitalist in the classical sense nor yet socialist, and that could pass into the socialist stage without a second insurrection because the party of the proletariat already held the leadership of the state.

The revision sounds scholastic and was anything but. It solved the Chinese party's practical problem, which was how to justify holding power at the head of an alliance whose programme was national and agrarian rather than socialist, without either abandoning the alliance's limits or pretending the limits did not exist. It also created the theoretical space in which the later doctrine of continuing revolution would grow, because a transition conceived as a long period under proletarian leadership, with the class composition of society still mixed and the outcome still contested, is precisely the terrain on which class struggle can be said to continue. Soviet theorists, whose own schema assigned the post-revolutionary state a clearer class identity at each stage, regarded the Chinese construction with a suspicion that grew as the Chinese side generalised it. A stage that is neither one thing nor the other, governed by a party that decides when it has ended, looked from Moscow like another instance of the Chinese habit of dissolving fixed categories into the leader's situational judgement. From Beijing, the Soviet stages looked like a timetable drawn from European history and imposed on societies whose development refused to consult it.

### What did New Democracy claim that the two-stage orthodoxy did not?

That the democratic revolution in a semi-colonial country could be led, from its first day, by the party of the proletariat, and could therefore grow over into the socialist stage as a continuation of the same leadership rather than as a new revolution against new rulers. The claim relocated the decisive question from the calendar of stages to the identity of the leadership, which is where the Chinese doctrine always preferred to locate it.

The stages argument also governed the two centres' advice to the rest of the colonial world, and therefore much of their competition within it. Parties in Asia, Africa and the Middle East were pressed to choose not only between patrons but between calendars. The Soviet-aligned calendar counselled broad national fronts, patience with bourgeois allies, and the postponement of socialist demands until the national stage had matured, counsel that its critics in the Chinese orbit denounced as the subordination of revolution to the foreign policy of a state that feared disturbance. The Chinese calendar counselled the construction of an independent armed force and a party leadership from the outset, on the ground that an alliance in which the communists dissolved their independence would end, as the Chinese party's own catastrophe of 1927 had taught it, in the ally's massacre of the dissolved. Both calendars carried the authority of a successful revolution behind them, which is why the argument between them could never be settled by citation. It had to be settled, country by country, by events, and the events of the postwar decades in Asia supplied each side with its vindications and its catastrophes in roughly the proportions the comparison has led the reader to expect.

## Vietnam: the party that would not choose

The Vietnamese communists occupy a place in this history that no summary of the split should skip, because their conduct tested both doctrines against the hardest case the period offered. A party fighting a war for national reunification, dependent for weapons and supplies on both socialist giants, courted and pressured by each to endorse its line, the Vietnamese leadership declined for the entire decade of the open polemics to anathematise either patron. The refusal was not indecision. It was a doctrine of its own, never fully theorised by its authors, to the effect that a movement engaged in an actual war of liberation could not afford the luxury of the custody dispute, and that the unity of the socialist camp, however fictitious, remained an asset worth more than either centre's approval. Both capitals found the position exasperating and both, in the end, respected it enough to continue supplying the war, which is the measure of how far a determined party could bend the schism's logic when its need was undeniable.

The Vietnamese case also illuminates the stages dispute from the receiving end. The party in Hanoi had built its strategy on a synthesis that owed elements to both calendars: a national front of the broadest composition, a party that never dissolved its independence or its army, a southern insurgency conducted as protracted war, and a northern state constructing socialism while the war continued. Doctrinally promiscuous by the standards of either centre, the synthesis was vindicated in 1975 in the only currency the period fully respected. The comparison draws the moral its method requires. Doctrines in this tradition were judged by their holders as universal sciences, but their actual career was that of repertoires, from which movements under pressure selected, combined and discarded under the discipline of survival. The two centres' inability to see their own doctrines as repertoires, their insistence that the inheritance was a system to be accepted or betrayed entire, was itself one of the split's causes, and the parties that treated the inheritance as a repertoire were, not coincidentally, the ones that used the split rather than being used by it.

### What does the Vietnamese refusal prove about the custody dispute?

That custody was a claim, not a fact. A party with its own army, its own war and its own legitimacy could decline both tribunals and suffer no excommunication that mattered, which demonstrates that the centres' authority over the movement rested on the parties' need for it. Where the need was absent or outweighed, the anathemas of both capitals arrived as literature rather than as law.


## Translation and the travelling text

The polemics were fought in Russian and Chinese and decided, in much of the world, in translation, and the machinery of translation deserves the same attention the article gave the machinery of education. Each centre maintained foreign-language publishing houses whose output flooded the bookshops of the movement: the Soviet side through its long-established apparatus for the classics and the congress documents, the Chinese side through a press in Beijing that issued the selected works, the quotations and the polemical comments in the major languages of every continent, in editions priced for students and soldiers rather than for libraries. Translation choices inside that flood were doctrinal acts. The rendering of a single term, the word for the stratum inside the party, the phrase for the whole-people state, could tilt a foreign reader's understanding of where the argument's weight lay, and the translators of both houses worked, as translators of scripture have always worked, in the knowledge that their choices would be quoted as the text itself.

The travelling text also changed in the travelling, in ways neither centre controlled. The Chinese writings arrived in countries whose readers lacked the context of the Chinese revolution and supplied their own, reading the mass line through their parish, their campus or their guerrilla column. The Soviet texts arrived freighted with the authority of the first revolution and were read, by the nineteen sixties, through a growing scepticism about the state that issued them. A comparison of doctrines that confined itself to the texts as written would therefore miss the doctrines as received, and the received versions, simplified, sloganised and weaponised for local quarrels, are the ones that did most of the historical work in the third parties whose splits this article has described. The scholarly moral is uncomfortable for a series built on close reading, and it should be stated anyway: the history of a doctrine is only partly the history of what its authors meant. The rest is the history of what its carriers needed, and the carriers are legion.

### Why did the quotations book outsell the treatises in the doctrine's export?

Because it was built for the purpose the treatises were not. A systematic work demands a reader with leisure, context and a teacher. A pocket book of short quotations, organised by theme, demands only a pocket and a quarrel, and it allows every reader to become a quoter, armed for the meeting and the demonstration. The form was the mass line applied to publishing: doctrine concentrated, returned to the masses, and tested in action.

## The new man and the remoulding of human nature

A final doctrinal strand, quieter than the others and in some ways the most revealing, concerned human nature and the timescale of its transformation. Soviet doctrine, in its mature form, was a doctrine of patience about people. The new man of communism would emerge as abundance grew and as the material conditions of selfishness withered; until then, the existing human material, with its acquisitiveness intact, was to be harnessed by incentives, educated by example and disciplined by law. The Chinese doctrine of the period was a doctrine of impatience. Human nature, on its account, is remade in struggle and can be remade now, by campaigns that attack selfishness directly, by the subordination of material incentive to moral example, by the deliberate levelling of the distinctions, between town and country, mental and manual labour, leaders and led, in which the old nature was housed. The model soldier Lei Feng, promoted in the nineteen sixties as the exemplar of selfless devotion, and the model brigade of Dazhai, promoted as proof that a village could transform its circumstances by unaided effort, were the doctrine's saints and its evidence.

Soviet commentary treated the remoulding project as the purest voluntarism, an attempt to abolish by exhortation the material roots of behaviour that only abundance could dissolve, and predicted its collapse into hypocrisy and coercion. Chinese commentary treated the Soviet patience as the ideology of a stratum that had made its peace with privilege and required a theory in which privilege's abolition could be postponed forever. The comparison notes, once more, the symmetry of the accusations and the partial truth of each. The remoulding campaigns did produce coercion and a theatre of virtue in which denunciation substituted for transformation. The patient doctrine did furnish its apparatus with a permanent alibi. And beneath the symmetry sits the question the whole dispute keeps returning to in new dress: whether a post-revolutionary order should trust the transformation of circumstances or the transformation of people to carry it toward its stated goal, when the evidence of the century suggests that each, attempted alone, corrupts into its opposite.

### What separated the two doctrines on the question of incentives?

A judgement about what incentives do to the society that uses them. For the Soviet side, differential reward was a neutral instrument, regrettable and temporary, whose effects on distribution could be managed by policy. For the Chinese side, the instrument was itself a teacher, instructing every citizen daily in the priority of private gain, and therefore a mechanism by which bourgeois relations reproduced themselves inside socialism's own wage packet. The argument was about pedagogy disguised as economics.


## A note on evidence: the numbers this article does not give

Readers of the earlier clusters in this series will recognise a discipline at work in these pages, and it should be made explicit once, because the split is a subject on which numbers circulate with unusual confidence and unusual contamination. This article gives no casualty figures for the border clashes of 1969, no membership figures for any party, and no figures for the aid extended or the advisers withdrawn in the rupture of 1960. The abstention is not squeamishness. Each of those numbers, in the literature, arrives carrying the colours of the institution that compiled it: frontier casualties reported by two general staffs with opposite incentives, party membership as claimed by parties for whom the claim was an argument, aid totals assembled by the giver to prove generosity or by the receiver to prove exploitation. Where a figure's provenance is the story, quoting the figure without the provenance misleads, and quoting both, for every number the narrative touches, would convert a comparison of doctrines into an audit of claims. The article has therefore described scale qualitatively, an army's growth by an order of magnitude, a trade reduced to a fraction, a current present on several continents, and has reserved its precision for the terrain where precision can be honest: the content of the doctrines, the sequence of the documents, and the logic by which each position generated its consequences.

The discipline has a positive side that should not be missed. A comparison forbidden to lean on contested magnitudes is forced back onto the material that actually distinguishes the two doctrines, which is argument. Nobody needs a casualty figure to state the Soviet criterion of class or to test the Chinese reply about control, and the polemics themselves, for all their fury, were conducted at the level of proposition and document rather than of statistics. The one great exception proves the rule by its fate. The single most consequential number in the whole dispute, the proportional assessment of Stalin's record offered by the Chinese side in the nineteen fifties, was not a measurement at all but a judgement wearing a number's clothes, and it functioned, as this article showed, as a method that would one day be turned on its own author. Numbers in this subject are safest when they confess what they are.

### Why do estimates connected with this dispute vary so widely between sources?

Because the counters were combatants. States compiled the figures that served their case, parties published the membership that flattered their claim, and later scholars inherit archives assembled under those incentives, with definitions, of a casualty, of a member, of a unit of aid, that shift between sources. The variation is not a defect of the scholarship alone. It is a property of the evidence, and honest writing reports the variation instead of selecting from it.

## The split in the wider map of this series

The comparison belongs to a series, and its place in the architecture deserves a closing word before the persona sections translate it for their audiences. The regional clusters that precede and follow this one examine what happened to the doctrine when it was carried into India, into Latin America, into the varied terrains of Asia and Europe. The Sino-Soviet dispute is the hinge on which several of those examinations turn. The Indian Maoist current treated in this series took its charter from the Chinese side of the case and its fate from the refusal of Indian conditions to resemble Chinese ones. The Peruvian codification that settled the meaning of Maoism for a generation of movements was an attempt to settle, from the Andes, the custody dispute the two capitals had left open. The Soviet doctrine against which the Chinese case was built receives its own full treatment later in the series, and the reader who arrives there from this comparison will find the other side of the correspondence, the doctrine as its custodians understood it from inside, stated without the polemical frame its enemies built for it.

The series' method, visible across its clusters, is the single-difference pattern this article shares with its treatments of Gramsci and Althusser and of Marx and Keynes: find the disagreement from which the others can be generated, state both sides from their own documents, and let the outcomes arbitrate. Applied to a schism between states, the pattern yields the continuing-revolution generator. Applied elsewhere it yields different engines, and the differences among the engines are themselves instructive. Gramsci's dispute with the orthodoxy of his day turned on the nature of consent in a stable society; the Chinese dispute with the Soviet orthodoxy turned on the nature of class in a revolutionary one. A tradition is healthiest, the pattern suggests, when its generative disagreements are argued in the open by parties who share a state of peace. The catastrophe recorded in this article is what happens when the argument is conducted instead by parties who share nothing but a canon, and who command, between them, the means to make their exegesis a matter of frontiers.


## The length of the transition: two imaginations of time

Underneath every difference catalogued in this article sits a disagreement about time, and drawing it out gives the comparison its most abstract and perhaps its most durable formulation. Soviet doctrine, in its mature form, imagined the transition from capitalism to communism as a road with surveyed stages and a destination that recedes politely: socialism built, developed socialism achieved, communism approached across a historical period whose length the programme declined to specify but whose direction was never in doubt. Time in that imagination is cumulative and reassuring. Each plan fulfilled adds its storey to an edifice that does not shake. The political emotion the imagination cultivates is patience, and its characteristic vice is the indefinite postponement of every promise the edifice was built to redeem.

Chinese doctrine, in the form this article has examined, imagined the transition as a contested passage without a surveyed route, in which the traveller can be turned back at any point and in which the destination's name is claimed by caravans travelling in opposite directions. Time in that imagination is not cumulative but precarious: gains are reversible, restoration is a standing possibility, and the only security lies in repeated renewal of the struggle that made the gains. The political emotion it cultivates is vigilance, and its characteristic vice is an inability ever to stand down, to let a society consolidate what it has built without suspecting the consolidation of being the counter-revolution in disguise. Set the two imaginations side by side and the whole dispute rearranges itself as their corollary. A party that believes time is on its side can afford the whole people's state. A party that experiences time as a slope down which societies slide back into class rule must keep the revolution in session permanently, and must find, in each generation, the class enemy whose existence the slope requires.

### Which imagination of time proved more hospitable to ordinary life?

The patient one, on the evidence of the decades when each governed, and the comparison should record the point without flinching, since it is the strongest human argument the Soviet doctrine possesses. Societies need intervals in which the future is not an emergency, and the doctrine that abolished antagonism, whatever its theoretical evasions, underwrote such an interval for its citizens. The vigilant imagination purchased its insights into power at the price of the interval, repeatedly, across a generation.

Yet the patient imagination carried a deferred cost that its own theory could not process, and the comparison's balance requires the cost to be stated with equal plainness. A doctrine that teaches a society its contradictions are finished leaves that society's members without a legitimate language for the contradictions that persist, and grievances denied a language do not disappear; they accumulate outside the official vocabulary, in jokes, in queues, in the private cynicism that travellers to the late Soviet world reported as its most striking atmosphere. When the language of reform finally arrived, it arrived from the summit, improvised, and the society discovered that it had no practised grammar for arguing about itself. The vigilant society, by contrast, had grammar in surplus and peace in deficit. Between a people forbidden to name its divisions and a people forbidden to rest from naming them, the comparison is not required to choose a preference, and declines. It is required to show that the choice was forced by premises about time that both parties mistook for laws of history.

## What the split felt like: the popular experience

Doctrinal history written from documents can forget that the dispute was lived by hundreds of millions of people who never read a polemic, and a section of this comparison belongs to them. In the Soviet Union, the Chinese ally of the nineteen fifties had been a fixture of popular culture: friendship societies, exhibitions, films, a vast younger brother whose revolution Soviet citizens had been taught to regard as their own achievement's confirmation. The reversal, when it came, was administered through the press in the vocabulary of injured fraternity, and it asked ordinary people to reclassify as a threat the nation they had been taught to embrace, on the ground of doctrines few could have stated. In China the reversal was administered more violently and more intimately, since the campaign against the internal enemy that the doctrine licensed reached into every work unit and school, and the external quarrel with Moscow supplied the atmosphere of encirclement in which the internal campaign's excesses were rationalised to its participants. The border clashes of 1969 were felt, in both countries, as a genuine war scare, with civil defence measures and a popular anxiety that the documents of either party, read alone, would never convey.

The popular experience also included a quieter register that the comparison should honour: bewilderment at the language itself. Citizens of both states had been educated in a vocabulary whose terms were now being turned, by their own teachers, against the teachers' former allies, and the spectacle of identical words carrying opposite verdicts was itself a political education of a kind neither regime intended. It taught a generation, in both countries, the lesson that official truth is revisable by authority, and the lesson outlasted the doctrines it was learned from. Historians of the late Soviet period trace part of that society's celebrated cynicism to exactly such reversals, administered across decades; historians of China trace part of the post-1976 generation's pragmatism to the same school. The split's deepest legacy may be neither a doctrine nor a frontier but a habit of hearing, acquired by hundreds of millions, in which the pronouncements of any centre are received with an inner reservation the pronouncers can sense but never quite abolish.

### Why does the popular experience belong in a comparison of doctrines?

Because doctrines of this kind claimed to be the consciousness of whole peoples, and the claim is part of their content. A theory that announces the abolition of antagonism while its citizens queue and grumble, or that announces a permanent revolution its citizens experience as permanent insecurity, is being tested by the experience whether the theorists attend or not. The comparison attends, briefly, as a matter of method.


## The new class: the Yugoslav anticipation

The Chinese doctrine of a bourgeoisie regenerated inside the party did not arrive from nowhere, and its most important anticipation came from inside the communist world itself, a decade before the open polemics. Milovan Djilas, once among the highest leaders of the Yugoslav party, published in exile in 1957 the argument that the communist bureaucracy in power constituted a new class: a stratum that owned nothing in law yet monopolised the administration of nationalised property, drew its privileges from that monopoly, and reproduced itself through control of appointment and education. The book was treated by every ruling party of the period as treason in its purest form, and its author was imprisoned by his own former comrades. Soviet doctrine, in particular, could not admit the argument's premise without dissolving its own, since a new class inside a society of social ownership was exactly what the canonical criterion forbade.

The Chinese polemics never rested their case on Djilas, whose anti-communism made him unusable as an authority, and the Chinese theory differed from his in a decisive respect: where Djilas treated the new class as the inevitable product of the party's monopoly itself, the Chinese doctrine treated the bourgeoisie in the party as a contingent product of wrong lines and surviving bourgeois right, corrigible by continuing revolution under the correct leadership. The difference is the difference between a critique of the system and a weapon for use inside it. Yet the structural perception is the same, and the comparison gains by naming the kinship, because it shows the perception arising independently wherever a revolutionary party settled into administration, in Belgrade as in Beijing, and being answered in Moscow with the same definitional refusal. A perception that recurs across regimes, languages and intentions is unlikely to be a mere artefact of any one polemic, which is the strongest external corroboration the Chinese side of the class argument possesses, and it costs the comparison nothing to record it, since the Soviet objection, that the perception still lacks a criterion of class in the strict sense, stands exactly where it stood.

### Why could neither capital afford to cite the Yugoslav anticipation?

Because its author had drawn the conclusion both capitals needed to avoid: that the phenomenon belonged to the system of party monopoly itself rather than to the errors of a rival leadership. Moscow could not cite a proof that its own order generated a class. Beijing could not cite a proof that the generation would continue under any leadership, including the one conducting the campaigns against it. The anticipation was therefore left to the exiles and the scholars.

## Socialist legality against campaign justice

A last doctrinal contrast, implicit in much of the foregoing, concerns law. The Soviet order of the period prided itself on socialist legality: the restoration, after the terror, of stable codes, professional courts and the principle that the state's own organs act within promulgated rules. The pride was partly justified by comparison with what had preceded it, and the doctrine of abolished antagonism supported it theoretically, since a society without class enemies has no need of revolutionary tribunals and can afford the luxury of procedure. Law, in that construction, is the form politics takes when politics has been settled.

The Chinese campaigns of the period embodied the opposite theory in practice: that law in a society still divided by class struggle is a terrain of that struggle, that codes and courts staffed by the old world's personnel would protect the old world's interests, and that justice must periodically be administered by the mobilised masses outside the professional apparatus if it is not to become the apparatus's property. The mass trial and the struggle session were that theory's institutions, and their record, of verdicts decided by acclamation and penalties answerable to no code, is the practical ground on which the Soviet objection about the political test drew much of its force from observers who cared nothing for the class criterion. A doctrine that cannot tell a citizen, in advance and by rule, what conduct will be punished and how, has given up one of the few unambiguous gains the Soviet order could claim over its own past, and the comparison records the loss as part of the Chinese doctrine's price, alongside the insight into bureaucratic power that was its purchase.

### What did the two theories of law reveal about the two theories of class?

Each was the other's corollary in institutional dress. Where antagonism has been abolished, law can be a settled code administered by professionals, because no class enemy remains for emergency to hunt. Where antagonism regenerates inside the state itself, a settled code looks like the enemy's fortification, and justice must keep bursting its banks. The courtroom and the campaign are the class theories of the two capitals, enacted.


## Imperialism in the two theories

The founders' text on imperialism was common property, and the dispute over its meaning in the nuclear age structured much of the strategic quarrel this article has already examined from the side of coexistence. The Soviet development of the theory ran through the analysis of state-monopoly capitalism: imperialism in its late phase fuses the monopolies with the state, plans after its fashion, and becomes capable, under the pressure of the socialist camp and its own working class, of restraining its aggressive impulses for long periods. From that analysis the strategy of peaceful competition followed as a theorem rather than a preference. The Chinese development ran the other way. Imperialism remained, in the Chinese formula, moribund and decaying exactly as the founder had described, its aggression rooted in the monopoly structure itself rather than in the policy of particular governments, and therefore incapable of the long self-restraint the Soviet theorem required. Paper tiger and state-monopoly partner were not insults exchanged across a table. They were rival readings of a shared scripture, each with a strategy folded inside it.

The readings diverged most consequentially over the national liberation movements, which the Chinese theory placed at the centre of the epoch and the Soviet theory, in its developed form, increasingly treated as a factor to be managed within the competition of systems. A liberation movement deciding whose aid to accept in the nineteen sixties was therefore also deciding, whether it knew it or not, between two theories of its own significance: protagonist of the age, in one; auxiliary of a balance centred elsewhere, in the other. The flattery in the Chinese construction was real and was felt, and it accounts for a portion of that construction's influence in the colonial world that its doctrinal content alone cannot explain. Movements prefer theories in which they are the heroes. The Soviet theory's relative neglect of that preference was a political cost its authors, confident that material aid outweighs doctrinal flattery, systematically underestimated, which is itself a datum for the comparison: the side with the subtler theory of class at home had the cruder theory of motivation abroad, and the side with the cruder criterion had the finer ear for the psychology of the oppressed.

### Why did the two readings of imperialism matter more to third parties than the class dispute did?

Because the class dispute concerned societies already socialist, a condition no third party yet inhabited, while the reading of imperialism told every liberation movement what its enemy was, what its war meant and whose friendship it could trust. Doctrines are adopted at the point where they answer the adopter's question, and for most of the world in that decade the question was not the nature of the post-revolutionary order but the road out of the colonial one.

## The treaty and the machinery of alliance

The alliance whose collapse this article has traced had a legal and institutional skeleton, and its career deserves a compact record. The treaty of friendship, alliance and mutual assistance signed in 1950 bound the two states for a generation, provided for consultation and joint action against the power it named as the common threat, and underwrote the decade of transfer and tutelage described earlier. Around it grew the ordinary machinery of socialist fraternity: joint commissions, party delegations at every level, student exchanges that sent a generation of Chinese engineers through Soviet institutes and a smaller flow in the other direction, friendship societies with memberships in the millions on paper, and a calendar of anniversaries at which the unity of the camp was ritually re-performed. The machinery matters to the comparison because it explains the split's peculiar pain. States that merely trade do not excommunicate. States bound by a machinery of fraternity, staffed by men whose careers were the alliance, experienced its failure as a kind of amputation, and the violence of the polemics is partly the sound of institutions discovering that their founding purpose had been withdrawn.

The treaty itself outlived the fraternity by two decades, lapsing in 1980 when the Chinese side declined the renewal its terms required, a formality that surprised nobody and closed the legal history the polemics had closed in fact long before. The student exchanges left the alliance's most durable human residue: a generation of specialists in each country whose formation had occurred in the other, and whose later careers, as interpreters, negotiators and scholars of the former ally, embodied the dispute's strange intimacy. Enemies who have never been friends do not produce such people. The comparison ends its institutional account with them because they personify its central finding, that the split was a quarrel inside a single world, conducted in a single language of doctrine, between parties who understood each other far too well to forgive the understanding.

### What did the alliance's machinery contribute to the split's bitterness?

Density of connection. Every institution of fraternity became, after the rupture, an inventory of betrayals: the adviser a spy in retrospect, the exchange student a hostage of fortune, the joint commission a record of condescension. A thinner relationship would have failed more quietly. The bitterness was proportional to the intimacy, which is why the polemics read less like diplomacy than like a divorce conducted in theological Latin.


## What each doctrine owed to its enemy

Schisms are usually narrated as pure losses, of unity, of strength, of lives spent on internal war. The comparison should close its analytical work by recording the stranger truth that each doctrine was improved, in identifiable ways, by the existence of its enemy, and that the improvement is part of the historical record alongside the damage. The Soviet doctrine, before the split, had been drifting toward a complacency in which its categories were recited rather than used. The Chinese challenge forced its theorists to argue: to reconstruct the criterion of class from the founders' texts, to defend the whole people's state programme against a learned opponent, to specify what peaceful coexistence claimed and what it did not. The objection this article stated at full strength is a better piece of theory than anything the Soviet textbooks had produced in the quiet decades, and it exists because the challenge required it. Custodians argue well only when the custody is contested.

The Chinese doctrine owed its enemy a corresponding debt. Before the polemics, the Chinese positions existed as a practice, a set of texts from the war years, and a style of leadership, formidable but unsystematised. The need to answer Moscow, document by document, forced the systematisation: the nine comments are, among their other functions, the moment at which the Chinese experience was compelled into the form of a general theory, with its premises stated and its conclusions drawn in a sequence a student can follow. The theory of continuing revolution itself was formulated under the polemics' pressure, as the Chinese side worked out, against the Soviet objection, what its practice implied about class under socialism. Whether the resulting theory is judged a discovery or a catastrophe, its existence as a theory, rather than as a leader's instinct, is the polemics' product. Enemies in this tradition were also editors, and the comparison that forgets the editing will misunderstand the texts, which everywhere bear the marks of the opponent they were written to defeat.

### Why did the polemics produce better theory than the quiet decades had?

Because quiet is the natural state of an orthodoxy, and an orthodoxy at peace optimises for recitation. A challenge from inside the tradition, armed with the same canon, cannot be answered by recitation; it must be answered by reconstruction, in which the defenders discover what their positions actually commit them to. Both sides emerged from the exchange knowing their own doctrines more precisely, at the price of being unable any longer to share them.

The debt extends to the movements that watched. Parties that never took either side were educated by the spectacle into a permanent scepticism about custody claims, and the habit of reading a centre's formulations against its interests, which the polemics taught by example, became part of the political literacy of a generation far beyond the communist world. Even the doctrines' failures instructed. The Soviet failure taught that a theory which cannot name its society's stratification will be discarded with the society. The Chinese failure taught that a theory which names stratification without a test that can acquit will consume the society it names. Those two lessons, bought at the price the record shows, are the split's bequest to any future movement that proposes to hold power in the name of equality, and they are the reason this comparison has treated both doctrines with a seriousness their custodians' conduct does not always invite.

## The documentary afterlife: memoir, film and monument

A dispute conducted in texts acquires, with time, a second life in the artefacts a culture makes from it, and the afterlife is worth a brief survey because it governs what later publics think the split was. The memoir literature came first and remains the most treacherous stratum: secretaries, interpreters, advisers and defectors from both systems published accounts in which the summits and the quarrels are rendered as scenes, with dialogue no stenographer preserved. The comparison has used such material only where the documentary record corroborates it, and the researcher persona section below repeats the caution, because the memoir of a schism is itself a continuation of the schism by other means, each author still pleading a custody case before the tribunal of posterity.

Film and monument divided along the frontier the polemics had drawn. Soviet culture, after an initial season of fraternal epics celebrating the alliance, withdrew the Chinese theme into silence and then into the guarded hostility of the border years, a silence that is itself evidence of the doctrine of abolished antagonism applied to memory: a friendship that could not be remembered honestly was not remembered at all. Chinese culture inverted the treatment, converting the quarrel into a staple of political education, in which the Soviet revisionist became a stock figure of the era's theatre and song, intelligible to audiences who had never seen a Russian. The stock figure outlived the policy that created him, and its persistence in the period's art is one reason the Chinese public's image of the northern neighbour changed more slowly than the diplomacy of the following decades might suggest. Monuments, in both countries, kept the older faith: the friendship palaces and exhibition halls built in the alliance's decade stood on, repurposed and unexplained, the architecture of a fraternity neither state could afford to demolish and neither could afford to describe. A comparison of doctrines that ignored these deposits would miss the medium in which most citizens of both states actually encountered the dispute, not as argument but as atmosphere, and atmosphere, in the long run, is what a doctrine becomes.

### What can the artefacts tell a researcher that the documents cannot?

How the dispute felt at street level, and therefore what it did to the political instincts of the populations who lived inside it. Documents record what the centres decided to say. A withdrawn film, a renamed hall, a stock villain in a provincial opera record what the saying did to the sayable, and the history of the sayable is where doctrines go when they become common sense or its opposite.

## The conferences: three attempts to hold the camp together

The split's institutional history was written at three conferences, and their sequence shows the custody dispute consuming the machinery built to contain it. The first met in Moscow in 1957, when the parties gathered for the fortieth anniversary of the October Revolution and produced a declaration that both giants signed. The Chinese delegation had arrived with its own draft and its own candidate for the declaration's central formula, and the negotiation over whether the world's transition would be described in terms that privileged the Soviet experience or the Chinese one consumed the conference's real hours. The compromise text named the Soviet road as the pattern's source while admitting variety in the roads of others, a formula each side read as its victory. The Chinese leader's presence at the anniversary, and his public statements there about the east wind prevailing over the west wind, gave the gathering an appearance of unity that the delegations' private bargaining had already falsified. The declaration survives as the last document of the camp that both centres could sign without lying about what they meant, though each was already glossing it in opposite senses on the journey home.

The second attempt, the 1960 meeting of eighty-one parties, was convened to repair a rupture that had by then acquired the Bucharest ambush and the withdrawn advisers among its facts. Its statement was longer, more heavily negotiated and less believed than its predecessor, a catalogue of formulas under which each delegation sheltered its own line. The Chinese side signed while publishing its reservations in the form of its own authoritative commentaries; the Soviet side accepted the signature as the submission it was not. Historians of the dispute treat the 1960 statement as the camp's last common text and its first common fiction, and the comparison can only endorse the judgement, since a unity that exists exclusively inside a document neither party will gloss the same way has already ceased to exist in the world the document describes.

The third conference, in 1969, completed the sequence by the eloquence of an absence. Convened in Moscow after the frontier clashes of that spring, it assembled the parties of the Soviet alignment to condemn, in carefully anonymised language, the deviations of an unnamed great party that declined to attend. The Chinese absence was the conference's true agenda and its true result: the camp whose unity the earlier conferences had strained to preserve now met as one of the schism's two parties, under the forms of the whole. A Romanian delegation's refusal to endorse the condemnation, and a handful of other abstentions, marked the limits of even that partial unity. For the comparison, the three conferences furnish the institutional proof of the proposition argued throughout: that the dispute was about custody, since what failed, conference by conference, was not agreement on policy, which might have been patched, but the existence of a tribunal both sides recognised as entitled to judge between them.

### Why did the compromise formulas of the conferences satisfy nobody for long?

Because each formula was a treaty between two readings, and the readings were the dispute. A sentence that Beijing glossed as licensing the Chinese road and Moscow glossed as confirming the Soviet one did not reconcile the parties; it merely relocated their quarrel into the exegesis of the sentence, where it resumed at the next conference with the added grievance that the other side had broken its word. Ambiguity, the diplomat's solvent, is an irritant to parties for whom the text is law.

The conferences also left a procedural legacy that outlasted the camp. Later movements that attempted international coordination without a centre, and there were several in the decades after the split, designed their gatherings in explicit reaction against the Moscow model: no authoritative declarations, no custody of a doctrine to award, no excommunications to pronounce. The reaction is intelligible and its costs were the mirror of the original's. A movement that cannot excommunicate also cannot define itself, and the loose internationals of the later period dissolved for want of the discipline whose abuse had destroyed the disciplined ones. The comparison draws no moral for organisers, which is not its business. It records the oscillation because the oscillation is the split's longest shadow in organisational history: between a centre that strangled and a dispersion that evaporated, the tradition never found the stable form its theory of the party had promised, and the conferences of the schism are where the search's failure is documented in triplicate.

## The vocabulary the split left behind

Schisms deposit words the way rivers deposit silt, and the Sino-Soviet dispute left a vocabulary that long outlasted the alignment which minted it. Capitalist roader, the term for the person in authority whose line allegedly restored capitalism, passed from the Chinese campaigns into the general lexicon of the world's left, available thereafter for any leader accused of betraying a movement from its summit. Two-line struggle normalised the idea that a single party contains an organised internal enemy with a headquarters, a programme and a class nature, and the idea proved exportable to movements that had never read the polemics, because it answered a universal need of factional life: the need to describe one's opponents as a thing rather than as a tendency. Paper tiger survived as the world's shorthand for a power whose menace exceeds its substance, quoted by speakers who could not name its author. Social-imperialism enjoyed a briefer and fiercer career as the charge that completed the Chinese case, and its construction, an adjective of allegiance fused to a noun of condemnation, remains the template for a family of later coinages by which movements accuse their former selves.

The Soviet side's deposits were quieter and correspondingly easier to miss. Developed socialism entered the vocabulary of an entire bloc as the name of a condition that required no further definition and permitted no further questions, and its career demonstrates that a term can govern by vagueness as effectively as another governs by menace. The whole-people formula performed its work and vanished with its state, leaving almost no trace in the speech of the movements that survived it, a disappearance the comparison finds diagnostic. The Chinese terms travelled because they were weapons, and weapons are carried. The Soviet terms administered, and administration is not carried anywhere; it is left behind in the offices where it was used. A doctrine's linguistic afterlife is therefore a rough index of the use its inheritors found for it in opposition, which is the condition in which most inheritors of both doctrines have lived since the states that authored them passed or transformed.

### Why do the Chinese terms survive in movements that reject the Chinese doctrine's conclusions?

Because the terms name experiences that recur wherever movements hold or seek power: the leader who betrays from the summit, the faction that organises as a state within the party, the enemy whose strength is theatrical. A vocabulary survives by fitting new quarrels, and the split's coinages were minted, by both sides, in the most quarrelsome decade of the tradition's history, which fitted them for export to every quarrel that followed.

There is a discipline the vocabulary imposes on its users, and the comparison should end its survey by noting it. To call a leader a capitalist roader is to have already decided the class question this article spent its length examining; to call a society developed socialism is to have already decided that its questions are closed. The words are verdicts in disguise, and the first service a student of the split can perform, in any later dispute where the old coinages circulate, is to exchange them back into the arguments they abbreviate and examine those. That exchange, performed patiently, is the whole method of this article in miniature, and it is fitting that the vocabulary section, of all sections, should state it before the persona pages translate the article for its three audiences.

## Two countrysides: the Virgin Lands and the communes

The two regimes' rural campaigns of the nineteen fifties make a compact parable of the whole comparison, and it is worth telling before the article closes its analytical work. The Soviet Virgin Lands campaign ploughed the steppes of Kazakhstan and Siberia into grain production by mobilising hundreds of thousands of young volunteers, machinery and administrative command, on the theory that the socialist state's problem in agriculture was one of scale, inputs and organisation, soluble by the same methods that had built its industry. The Chinese communes, a few years later, reorganised the entire rural population into vast multifunctional units, on the theory that the countryside's problem was one of social relations, soluble by transforming the relations themselves and releasing, in the transformation, energies that no quantity of inputs could purchase. Each campaign was the other's doctrine enacted on a landscape. The Soviet campaign trusted the plan, the machine and the specialist, and distrusted only insufficient scale. The Chinese campaign trusted mobilisation, politics and the remoulding of relations, and distrusted the specialist as a stratum in embryo.

Both campaigns harvested an initial triumph and a long bill. The steppes yielded enormous harvests in their first seasons and then the familiar reckoning of soil exhaustion, erosion and yields that would not repeat, a reckoning the Soviet system absorbed in its characteristic way, by silence and by imports. The communes yielded their triumph of mobilisation in waterworks and terraces that endure in the landscape, and then the catastrophe this series treats under its figures protocol in the biography article, a reckoning the Chinese system absorbed after its characteristic fashion, by retreat administered as adjustment and never as confession. The parable's point for the comparison is not that both failed, since both also built. It is that each regime, confronting the same recalcitrant subject, the peasantry and its land, reached for the instrument its theory of class and knowledge prescribed, and that the instrument, in each case, was the regime's founding experience generalised into a universal key: the war economy's mobilisation in one, the industrial plan's command in the other. Doctrines decide what their holders can see as a solution, and the countrysides of two continents bear the marks of what these two could see.

### What do the two rural campaigns reveal that the polemics conceal?

That the doctrines were not only argued but enacted, daily, by millions who never heard them stated, and that their truth was therefore tested in a currency the polemics never mention: harvests, soil, hunger and endurance. The texts of the split are the comparison's evidence for what the parties thought. The countrysides are its evidence for what the thinking cost and what it built, and a verdict that weighed only the texts would be a verdict on half the record.

## Custody of the canon: who edits the founders

One machinery of the dispute has gone unmentioned, and it is the most literal form the custody question took: the editing of the founders' texts. Both capitals maintained institutes charged with the Marxist archive, and both published the collected works in editions whose selections, orderings and annotations taught the reader what the inheritance contained. Control of the canon was power in its purest doctrinal form, since a party that decides which of the founder's sentences circulate, and with what commentary, has gone far toward deciding what the founder can be quoted as saying. The Chinese side's complaint that the Soviet institutes had become the tradition's Vatican, licensing interpretations from a closed archive, and the Soviet side's complaint that the Chinese quotations book had replaced the works with a catechism of excerpts, were the same complaint in mirror image: each accused the other of interposing an apparatus between the reader and the source. The accusation's symmetry did not make it false in either direction. It made it structural, a permanent temptation of any tradition that venerates texts, and the researcher's defence against it is the one this series has practised throughout: read the founders in editions whose editors' interests are known, read the polemics against the archive, and treat every canon, including the one implicit in this article's own selections, as an argument rather than as a shelf.

### Why does control of an archive matter to a dispute about class?

Because the criterion of class, the assessment of Stalin, the meaning of coexistence and every other question in the grid were argued from the founders' sentences, and the sentences available for quotation were an editorial product before they were an argument. Whoever edits the inheritance edits the range of the arguable, which is why two parties disputing the inheritance fought, among their other battles, over paper, print and the custody of the dead.

A parallel custody was exercised over the living. Each centre maintained its recognised interpreters, the theorists whose bylines on the polemical articles told the initiated whose hand held the pen, and each centre's interpreters were read abroad the way court pronouncements are read, for the shifts in formula that signalled a shift in line. A changed adjective in a People's Daily editorial or a Pravda commentary could move parties on other continents to emergency sessions, which is a measure of how completely the movement's intellectual life had been centralised in the two capitals whose quarrel was, at that very moment, destroying the centre as such. The interpreters' authority evaporated with the alignment that sustained it, and their texts survive as the purest specimens of a genre the tradition no longer produces: the anonymous editorial as an act of world-historical legislation. Later editors in both capitals inherited the machinery without the congregation, issuing formulations to a movement that had quietly stopped gathering to hear them, which is why the last documents of the dispute read, to a unsympathetic eye, like laws promulgated for a country that had emigrated.

One further habit separates profitable reading from mere accumulation. Keep a private register of predictions. Each doctrine, taken seriously, forecasts the other's future: the Soviet texts forecast that campaign politics would exhaust the Chinese economy and end in a reckoning administered by the party itself, and the Chinese texts forecast that the Soviet order would harden into a privileged caste's quiet property. A reader who writes the forecasts down, dated, and then checks them against the successors' record owns a measuring instrument no commentary can supply, because the doctrines are made to sit their own examination, in public, with the marks published.

The point has a practical consequence for anyone who studies the dispute now. The documentary record of the split is not a neutral lake from which arguments are fished; it is a pair of reservoirs, each stocked by an institution with a case to prove, and the fish that surface most readily in each are the ones its keepers fed. The Soviet reservoir yields the programme, the congress report and the diplomatic note in exemplary order. The Chinese reservoir yields the polemic, the editorial and the leader's annotated remark, in editions whose apparatus of commentary grew more reverent as the succession receded. A researcher who drinks from one reservoir only will reproduce its keeper's case with scholarly apparatus, a fate that has befallen distinguished work on both sides of the literature. The comparison's defence, stated once more because it is the article's whole method, is to drink from both, to set the reservoirs against each other, and to treat the places where their accounts cannot be reconciled as the most valuable evidence of all, since a contradiction between two interested records marks the spot where something real resisted both.

## For students and exam candidates

Build your command of this topic around the generator rather than around the chronology. Examiners reward the candidate who can show that one disagreement, over whether class struggle continues under socialism, organises the other six, and who can state each side's position in the shape its own texts gave it before venturing a judgement. Learn the seven differences grid as a planning instrument: agent, strategy, party method, Stalin, war and coexistence, classes under socialism, continuing revolution. For each row you should be able to name a document, a date in prose where the question permits it, and a consequence. The questions that discriminate between candidates are the criterion questions. What makes a class, once property is socialised? Who may identify the enemy inside a ruling party, and by what test? Practise answering those in both directions, Soviet objection first and Chinese reply second, or the reverse, conceding what each side must concede. Carry the distinction between Mao Zedong Thought and the later codification of Marxism-Leninism-Maoism as a free-standing paragraph, since it corrects the commonest error in the field. Observe the figures discipline this article observes: no casualty totals for the frontier clashes, no membership claims, no aid totals. An answer that argues from the documents will outscore an answer that decorates itself with numbers it cannot defend, and the comparison's verdict, that each doctrine is convicted where the other's accusation lands, is a conclusion you may borrow provided you can rebuild the evidence beneath it. ## For teachers and seminar leaders

The split is the finest teaching case in this series for the proposition that ideas have consequences when parties hold power, and the seminar should be built to let students discover the generator themselves. Begin with the two programmatic texts, the Soviet statement that antagonistic classes have been abolished and the Chinese formulation of continuing revolution, and require each student to derive, from one premise alone, the institutional behaviour it licenses: the treatment of dissent, of succession, of privilege, of law. Only then introduce the sequence of the quarrel, and let the class test how much of the chronology the premises predict. The strongest classroom exercise is the objection exchange. Assign the Soviet objection, that a bourgeoisie without a property relation dissolves the materialist criterion into a political test, to the students most sympathetic to the Chinese position, and the Chinese reply, that control without ownership is the substance of class, to the students who lean toward the Soviet one. Each side must concede, in writing, the point its assigned position cannot answer. Close with the terminology problem, Mao Zedong Thought against the Peruvian codification, as a lesson in how doctrines are named by their exporters. The [VaultBook study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) give students the grid in portable form for revision, with the seven rows set out as a card that can be carried into the examination hall's last hour.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Treat the polemics as a primary source of a peculiar kind: documents written to be believed by a third audience, whose selections and silences are therefore evidence of what each centre thought the world movement needed to hear. Triangulate them relentlessly against the archival record of the alliance, the summit transcripts, the advisers' reports and the frontier documentation, much of which has become available since the Soviet collapse transformed the evidentiary base of the field. The research frontier lies less in the doctrines' content, which this comparison states, than in their reception: how fraternal parties, liberation movements and student generations selected from the two canons, and how the selections fed back into the centres' self-understanding. Journalists covering the afterlife of these ideas should observe the discipline this article observes toward surviving currents, describing them structurally and historically rather than through the claims of any present organisation, and should resist the two standing temptations of the subject, reducing the split to a border quarrel with footnotes, or reducing it to a seminar with armies. It was a dispute about the nature of post-revolutionary society, conducted by the only two states then positioned to test their answers, and the test results, convulsion in one case and immobility in the other, remain the largest body of evidence the twentieth century produced on the question both sides were asking.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the difference between Maoism and Soviet Marxism?

Seven divergences make up the comparison, and one of them organises the rest. The two sides parted over who makes a revolution, peasants in the Chinese account and industrial workers in the Soviet one; over how power is seized, by long rural warfare or by insurrection in the capitals; over how a party should lead, through the circuit of the mass line or through a disciplined hierarchy armed with textbooks; over how Stalin should be judged; and over whether war between the camps could be avoided. Underneath sat the fundamental parting: Moscow taught that hostile classes no longer existed inside a socialist society, while Beijing taught that struggle between classes persisted there and could breed an exploiting layer within the governing party itself.

### Q: What is protracted people's war?

It is the military doctrine the Chinese party distilled from its own survival. A revolutionary force too weak to win quickly refuses decisive engagement, withdraws into countryside it can govern, and builds there the institutions of an alternative power: taxation, justice, schooling, recruitment. Strength accumulates through phases the doctrine names as strategic defence, stalemate and counter-offensive, until the correlation of forces has inverted and the cities, ringed by organised territory, can be taken at the close rather than stormed at the start. The doctrine presumes an agrarian country, a state whose writ fades beyond the provincial towns, and a party prepared to fight for a generation. Exported widely afterwards, it became the best-known single element of the Chinese inheritance.

### Q: What is continuing revolution under socialism?

The phrase names Beijing's answer to the question of what follows expropriation. Abolishing private ownership, the doctrine argues, leaves untouched a whole inheritance of unequal distribution, inherited ideas and institutional routine, and it leaves the governing apparatus in command of allocation. From those materials a fresh exploiting stratum can crystallise inside the party that made the revolution, so the revolutionary process has to be renewed against that stratum or the transition will run backwards toward capitalism. Moscow's answer inverted every term: hostile classes had disappeared with the old owners, the Soviet state spoke for the entire people, and campaigning against the apparatus in the name of class war could only be factional destruction wearing theoretical dress.

### Q: What caused the Sino-Soviet split?

Two causal layers interlock, and assigning the rupture to either alone distorts the record. One layer is governmental: a disputed frontier inherited from the tsarist century, resentment at tutelage inside the alliance, requests for sensitive military help refused or hedged, advisers sent home in 1960 as an instrument of pressure. The other layer is doctrinal: rival completions of an unfinished theory, beginning with the reckoning over Stalin in 1956, hardening through rival accounts of war and coexistence, and reaching open anathema in the published polemics of 1963 and 1964, before soldiers exchanged fire along the Ussuri in 1969. Governments supplied the occasions and the bitterness; doctrine supplied the conviction that compromise would be betrayal.

### Q: Is Maoism the same as Mao Zedong Thought?

The two labels have different birthplaces and different claims. Inside China the canonised doctrine carried its leader's name with the word Thought attached, and the party defined it as Marxism-Leninism applied to Chinese conditions, a national achievement that stopped short of proclaiming a fresh stage of the universal theory. Maoism was coined mostly abroad, by admirers who wanted a banner and by opponents who wanted a target, and it gathered meanings the home formula never carried. A Peruvian party's codification in the late nineteen eighties then gave the word a precise and ambitious sense, as the name of a third stage. Collapsing the labels into one another erases the difference between what Beijing claimed and what its foreign heirs claimed on its behalf.

### Q: What is Marxism-Leninism-Maoism?

The term denotes a systematisation completed in Peru in the late nineteen eighties, when the party fighting there under that banner arranged the Chinese inheritance into a declared third stage of the doctrine, after Marxism and after Leninism. Its building blocks were the insistence that class war persists throughout socialism, the mass line understood as a way of producing knowledge, the universality claimed for protracted war as a military method, and a reading of dependent economies as bureaucratic capitalism. Whatever judgement a reader forms of the construction, its provenance is the point students most often miss: the neatest definition of Maoism as a world system was written in the Andes, not in Beijing, and decades after the polemics it purported to summarise.

### Q: Where did Maoism spread internationally?

Parties and factions aligned with Beijing emerged on most continents once the quarrel became public, and their common marks were a shared set of texts, hostility to their own countries' established communist leaderships, and a preference for rural strategy. South Asia produced the most consequential insurgent current, examined in this series through the Indian experience. Southeast Asian parties divided over the alignment amid wars of their own. Latin American organisations absorbed the Chinese line into a pre-existing argument about guerrilla war, and the region later produced the doctrine's most rigorous codifiers. Western Europe's aligned groups remained small in number yet outsized in visibility during the student revolts, where Chinese symbols circulated well beyond any organisation's control.

### Q: How did Mao assess Stalin?

He judged him by proportion rather than by repudiation. The Chinese editorials that followed the secret speech credited the dead Soviet leader with a record in which great achievement outweighed grave error by a broad margin, treated the terror and the cult as real and instructive failures, and warned that negating the whole record would negate the revolution he had led and demoralise every party that had defended it. The method was itself a position in the dispute, since wholesale condemnation, in Beijing's reading, served the new Soviet leadership's convenience. Proportionate judgement later became the template its author's own party applied to him, in the resolution that closed the era after his death.

### Q: Was the split ideological or geopolitical?

The evidence supports a division of labour between the two explanations. Questions of territory, security and rank between two great states explain when the quarrel sharpened, why advisers were withdrawn as a sanction, and why the argument ended with troops facing one another across a frozen river. Questions of doctrine explain what the quarrel was about, why parties with no frontier at stake divided over the same propositions, and why the two regimes behaved so differently toward their own populations. Above all, doctrine explains the impossibility of settlement: borders can be negotiated by governments that still recognise one another, whereas parties that have classified each other's regimes as class impostures have abolished the ground on which a settlement could be signed.

### Q: What survives of Maoism as a theory?

The unified system perished as a governing doctrine when the founder's own party formally condemned its central theory in 1981, and no state has since governed by it. Its components enjoyed separate afterlives. Generals and insurgents alike study the writings on protracted war. Organisers far from any communist allegiance borrow the mass line's circuit of gathering, concentrating and returning ideas. Theorists of bureaucracy keep rediscovering the warning that advantage can reproduce itself through office and schooling after property has changed hands. The late Peruvian codification persists as the charter of particular currents, treated in this article as a historical formation. The casualty of the story is the polemical premise itself, that a single centre could hold the tradition's finished truth.

### Q: How does the continuing-revolution generator order the seven differences?

The grid's seven rows are not presented as equals, and the generator is the name for their ordering principle. One row, the dispute over whether antagonism survives expropriation, behaves as the source from which the domestic consequences of both regimes flowed. Answer yes, and a ruling party holds a warrant to make revolution against its own summit; answer no, and it holds a certificate that no challenge from within can be legitimate. The remaining rows, about who fights, how power is taken, how leaders are judged and how war is regarded, explain the making of the two revolutions and the conduct of their rivalry. The generator explains the regimes themselves, which is why the comparison ranks it first.

### Q: What should a student separate between formal ownership and control of the means of production?

Two questions that the dispute taught students to ask separately. The first concerns title: in whose name do the factories, land and banks stand, and by what law are they held? In both societies examined here the answer was social, and on that ground Moscow denied that any bourgeoisie could exist. The second concerns command: who settles the investment plan, staffs the posts, distributes the scarce goods and passes advantage to their children? Beijing argued that a stratum monopolising those decisions performs the functions ownership performs elsewhere, whatever the register says. Examiners reward candidates who keep the questions apart, because conflating them reproduces, in miniature, the exact confusion over which the two parties excommunicated each other.

### Q: Why did the two parties' doctrines diverge after their shared victory period?

Victory confronted both parties with a society their shared theory had never described: one in which the old owners were gone and the revolution's own apparatus disposed of everything. The founders had left principles for that condition, warnings about survivals and about bureaucracy, but no agreed doctrine of its class structure, so each party manufactured the missing doctrine from its own biography. A party hardened by administering an industrial state for decades manufactured reassurance, the teaching that antagonism had ended. A party hardened by guerrilla war and suspicious of its own new officials manufactured vigilance, the teaching that the enemy now grew inside the citadel. The divergence records less a betrayal of the inheritance than the inheritance's silence at the decisive point.

### Q: How does the seven differences grid order the dispute?

Use the grid as a reading instrument rather than as a summary. Its rows move from the circumstances of revolution toward the nature of the order revolution creates: the agent, the strategy, the party's method, the judgement of Stalin, the prospects of war, the classes remaining under socialism, and the revolution's continuation. Each row sets a Soviet formulation beside a Chinese one, attaches a document where a student can verify the attribution, and records a consequence so the positions are weighed by what they did as well as by what they said. The ordering teaches the article's central lesson without further argument: rows near the top were negotiable in principle, while the rows near the bottom defined what each regime was, and therefore admitted no compromise.

### Q: What separates a property-based criterion of class from a political one?

The distinction turns on how a class is identified and who is entitled to identify it. Under the property test, membership follows from a group's place in production, established by whether it lives through owning productive assets and appropriating the labour of others; the evidence is legal and economic, and an outside observer can check it. Under the political test, membership follows from the line a person or group pursues, the policies they advocate, the company they keep, the tendencies detected in them; the evidence is interpretive, and the verdict depends on the authority of whoever interprets. Moscow's objection was that Beijing had substituted the second test for the first while borrowing the first test's certainty. Beijing's answer was that the first test, alone, had certified a hierarchy as harmony.
