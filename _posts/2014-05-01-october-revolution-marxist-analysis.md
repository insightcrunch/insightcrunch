The October Revolution is one of those rare events that cannot even be named without taking a side. Call it a revolution and you have already granted it a social depth, a mass of people in motion, a transformation that reached beyond the rooms where orders were signed. Call it a coup and you have already reduced it to a technique, a handful of determined men seizing the levers of a state whose guards had gone home. Both words are used by serious historians, about the same night, the same city, the same telegrams. The disagreement is not mainly about what happened between the evening of 25 October 1917 by the old calendar and the morning that followed. The chronology of those hours is tolerably well established. The disagreement is about what kind of thing the happening was, and that question cannot be answered until it has been divided into the two questions it secretly contains.

![The October Revolution, soviets, the insurrection and the coup or revolution question - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

Before the division is made, one set of facts deserves to stand in the open, because a reading of October that hides them has failed before it begins. Within weeks of the seizure of power, elections were held across Russia for a Constituent Assembly, the body that most political camps had treated for months as the legitimate arbiter of the country's future. The Socialist Revolutionaries emerged as the largest party in those elections and the Bolsheviks came second, strong in the great cities, the industrial districts and the armed forces, weak across the peasant countryside that held most of the population. When the assembly finally met in January 1918, it sat for a single session and was then dispersed by armed force, never to meet again. Any account of the October Revolution that cannot look directly at that sequence is not an account but a commemoration or an indictment. This reading looks at it directly, places it in the ledger where it belongs, and refuses to let either the seizure's undoubted popular roots or the assembly's undoubted dispersal be argued away.

## Two questions wearing one name

The organising discipline of this article is a distinction worth naming outright, because a reader who carries it away can use it on every other disputed seizure of power in the historical record. Call it the two-question separation. The first question is a question about mechanism. How was power taken, by how many people, through what organisation, against what resistance, with how much fighting? Questions of mechanism have small answers. They are answered by counting committees, battalions, railway stations and telegraph offices, and the answers are often unflattering to large words. The second question is a question about social process. What grievances, institutions and mass movements made the taking possible, sustained it through its first dangerous weeks, and prevented the old order from simply returning the following month? Questions of process have large answers, spread across factories, villages, barracks and bread queues, and they cannot be settled by pointing at the small group that drafted the orders.

A coup, in ordinary usage, is an event that answers the first question in a particular way: power taken by a small organised minority, swiftly, from inside or alongside the state, with the wider population as spectator. A revolution, in ordinary usage, answers the second question in a particular way: a transformation driven by mass participation that remakes the social order and not merely its personnel. Nothing in logic prevents both descriptions fitting one episode at once, because they describe different strata of it. A seizure can be executed as a minority operation by a party that has spent the preceding months winning real majorities in the institutions where the crisis was being decided. That combination is not a paradox to be dissolved. It is, on the evidence assembled below, close to a description of what occurred in Petrograd in the autumn of 1917, and the work of this article is to test it stratum by stratum.

### Was the October Revolution a coup?

The seizure itself was a planned minority operation with limited fighting, which fits the description. It was carried out by a party that had won majorities in the Petrograd and Moscow soviets that autumn. Elections to the constituent assembly weeks later returned a different party as the largest.

The ledger method follows from the separation. Rather than narrating the year as a single rising action and inviting the reader to feel its verdict, this article sets out the contested claims in rows: what was claimed for popular support, for the soviet majorities, for the scale of the fighting, for the assembly elections and for the role of the party apparatus. Against each claim it places the traditional account, the revisionist account, and the narrower ground that the surviving evidence establishes regardless of camp. The narrative then does the same work in prose, because a table can discipline a dispute but cannot carry one. Three bodies of material do the carrying. The first is the sequence of the year itself, from the fall of the monarchy to the night of the insurrection. The second is the historiography, the named camps of historians who have fought over the episode for a century and whose quarrel is itself the most useful map a reader can own. The third is the theoretical problem the event posed for the Marxism in whose name it was made, a problem the participants understood at the time and argued about in print while the guns were still warm.

A note on what this article will not do, stated once and then honoured. It will not narrate the civil war that followed the seizure, nor the terror that accompanied it, beyond recording that both followed; those subjects belong to the treatments of the Soviet regime and of the case against Leninism elsewhere in this series, under their own protocols. It will not use the vocabulary of celebration or of condemnation. And it will not pronounce, in an editorial voice, a single-word verdict on the event as a whole, because the whole argument of the two-question separation is that a single word is precisely what the evidence does not license.

## The empire at war and the fall of the monarchy

Russia entered the crisis of 1917 carrying burdens that no government of the period could have lifted comfortably, and the sequence matters because the October seizure is unintelligible without the February collapse that preceded it. The war against Germany and Austria-Hungary had consumed men on a scale that strained the social fabric of a largely peasant empire: millions conscripted, casualties in the millions, railway networks buckling under military traffic, cities short of grain while the armies at the front consumed the surplus. Inflation hollowed out wages. The tsarist regime, having taken personal command of the armies, had attached its own prestige to a front that produced defeats, retreats and vast prisoner columns. Petrograd, the capital, sat close to the front, swollen with war industry, crowded with garrison troops who had no wish to be sent forward, and dependent on food deliveries that arrived late or not at all.

The monarchy fell in days, and it fell from the street upward rather than from a conspiracy downward. Demonstrations that began in early March by the modern calendar, around bread shortages and a lockout at the great Putilov works, drew in crowds whose demands escalated from food to the end of the war to the end of the autocracy itself. The decisive moment came when the garrison, ordered to fire, instead mutinied in regiment after regiment and went over to the crowds. A regime that has lost its capital's soldiers has lost the instrument by which regimes survive unpopularity, and Nicholas II, stranded at military headquarters, abdicated within the week. No party made February. The socialists of every shade were as surprised as the liberals, a fact worth remembering when February and October are carelessly fused into one process: the first was a collapse that nobody directed, the second a seizure that somebody planned.

What replaced the monarchy was a doubled authority whose instability shaped everything that followed. On one side stood the Provisional Government, drawn from the parliamentary opposition of the old regime, committed to continuing the war alongside Russia's allies and to postponing every fundamental question, land, peace, the shape of the state, until a Constituent Assembly could be elected to decide them. On the other side stood the Petrograd Soviet, a council of workers' and soldiers' deputies that had sprung up in the same days, dominated at first by moderate socialists who believed the revolution had to remain bourgeois and therefore declined to take power themselves. The soviet could stop the government from governing: its famous first order to the garrison had instructed soldiers to obey the government only where its commands did not contradict the soviet's. Historians call the arrangement dual power, and the phrase is exact. Two bodies claimed sovereignty, neither could exercise it fully, and between them stretched a year of drift in which the war continued, the land question festered, and the patience of soldiers and workers wore thin.

Into that drift the exiled revolutionary Vladimir Lenin returned in April, travelling across Germany in the sealed train that his enemies would use against him for the rest of his life, and detonated an argument inside his own party. The Bolsheviks, before his arrival, had been edging toward conditional support of the Provisional Government and cooperation with the other socialist parties. Lenin's April Theses demanded the opposite course in nearly every particular. Understanding that reversal is essential to the mechanism question, because the instrument that eventually took power in October was built, in the months between, by a party arguing with itself about whether taking power was even its aim. Lenin's life and intellectual formation have their own treatment in this series, [the intellectual biography of Lenin](/2014/03/01/lenin-intellectual-biography/), and the April turn is examined there from the inside; here it matters as the hinge of the year.

### What did the April Theses demand that the party had not demanded before?

They demanded an end to all support for the Provisional Government, a refusal to demand a return to the parliamentary republic the other socialists accepted, and the transfer of power to the soviets as the embryo of a different state form. They also demanded a break with the staged timetable the party had inherited.

The theses scandalised the Bolshevik leadership before they scandalised anyone else. Senior figures in the party regarded the programme as a fantasy disconnected from Russian conditions: the country was overwhelmingly peasant, the working class a minority, the socialist revolution, on the orthodox reading of Marxist theory, a task for a later stage after bourgeois democracy had matured. Lenin's answer, delivered in meeting after meeting through April, was that the war had compressed the stages, that the soviets offered a ready-made alternative form of power, and that a party which waited for permission from theory would be overtaken by a population already moving. He won the argument inside the organisation over the following weeks, not by seniority but by persuasion and by the accumulating evidence that the moderate course was failing in the country. The text in which he then developed the alternative state form, written in hiding during the summer and published as State and Revolution, is treated at length in [the reader's guide to State and Revolution](/2012/12/15/state-and-revolution-readers-guide/) elsewhere in this series, and its programme for a commune-style state shadows every claim the Bolsheviks later made about the legitimacy of October.

The spring also produced the first disillusionments that the ledger must register. In April the foreign minister's note assuring the allies that Russia would fight on to victory provoked armed demonstrations in the capital and the government's first reshuffle, bringing moderate socialists into a coalition. In June a great offensive against the Austro-German lines, undertaken to restore the army's honour and the allies' confidence, collapsed within days under desertion and refusal, and the front began to dissolve as soldiers voted with their feet in the literal sense, walking home to their villages in hundreds of thousands to be present when the land was divided. Each failure transferred credibility from the government to the soviets, and within the soviets from the moderate socialists, who owned the government's policies, toward the Bolsheviks, who owned nothing but opposition. That transfer, measurable in resolution after resolution passed by factories and regiments, is the social process the second question asks about, and it was well advanced before anyone planned an insurrection.

## The summer crises and the education of the garrison

July supplied the year's sharpest lesson in the difference between a crowd in motion and an organisation prepared to direct it. The triggers were characteristic of the drift: a Ukrainian autonomy dispute that cracked the coalition, cadet ministers resigning, rumours of further offensives, and a machine-gun regiment in the capital facing orders to the front that its men read as a death sentence for the revolution's most reliable defenders. Armed workers and soldiers poured into the streets across several days in early July, many of them demanding that the soviet take the power its leaders kept declining. The Bolsheviks, whose slogans the crowds carried, had not planned the rising and did not control it; Lenin, judging the moment unripe and the provinces unready, worked to convert an explosion into a demonstration and then to end it. The government, for once, acted with decision. Loyal troops were summoned from the front, the Bolshevik press was raided, arrest warrants described Lenin as a German agent, and the party's leaders were jailed or driven underground, Trotsky among the arrested and Lenin across the Finnish border in hiding.

### Why did the July rising collapse while the autumn seizure held?

In July the party had slogans in the streets but no majority in the soviets, no cover from any congress, and an opponent still able to summon loyal regiments from the front. By the autumn the majorities had been won, the government had lost its own defenders, and the seizure was timed to a congress that could ratify it.

The government's victory in July was real and brief, and its brevity is the point the social question fastens on. Repression could scatter a party's headquarters, but it could not restore grain deliveries, restart the offensive, unmake the land seizures spreading through the countryside, or persuade garrison soldiers that the front needed them more than the revolution did. The coalition re-formed under Alexander Kerensky, a socialist of theatrical energy who now embodied the revolution's official face, and within weeks it faced a challenge from the opposite direction that would undo everything July had won. The July episode also educated its victims. Bolshevik organisers drew a practical conclusion they never abandoned afterward: street explosions without institutional majorities were gifts to the government, while institutional majorities without a prepared moment were paper. The autumn strategy, patience in the soviets joined to a seizure timed against a congress, was July's tuition applied.

Underground and in hiding, the party's theorist produced the programme that the insurrection would later claim as its warrant. State and Revolution, drafted in a Finnish hut from notebooks of excerpts, argued that the bourgeois state machine had to be broken rather than inherited and replaced by a commune form on the model Marx had drawn from Paris in 1871: officials elected and recallable, paid workmen's wages, the standing army replaced by the armed people. Whatever the distance between that text and the state actually built after October, a distance examined under its own protocol elsewhere in this series, the text matters here because it shows the party's public theory at the moment of the seizure. The Bolsheviks did not present themselves as a committee of conspirators improving on the masses. They presented themselves as the instrument of a soviet majority enacting a theory of the state that had been published, argued over and printed for anyone to refute. Presentation is not proof, and the ledger will test the claims separately; but a reading of the mechanism that ignores the published programme mistakes the kind of operation October was.

## Kornilov: the failed move that armed the left

The year's second great transfer of credibility arrived wearing a general's uniform. Lavr Kornilov, appointed commander in chief in July, embodied the officer corps' conviction that the revolution had dissolved the army and that only a hard hand could restore the front. His programme, conveyed through intermediaries in the ambiguous negotiations of late August, mixed genuine military grievances with political demands: discipline restored by the death penalty behind the lines, the soviets and committees curbed, the capital's garrison subordinated. Whether Kornilov intended a dictatorship, and whether Kerensky first encouraged and then panicked at the movement of troops toward Petrograd, remain genuinely disputed among historians working from the same telegrams. What is not disputed is the sequence. Kerensky dismissed his commander in chief; Kornilov refused the dismissal and ordered cavalry and a division of Caucasian mountaineers toward the capital; and the defence of Petrograd fell, by default and by invitation, to the soviet organisations the government had spent July repressing.

The defence succeeded almost without a battle, and its methods prefigured October's. Railway workers diverted, delayed and misdirected the troop trains, using the network's own paperwork as a weapon. Soviet agitators met the advancing regiments and talked, fraternised and argued until units dissolved their orders by discussion, a tactic impossible to imagine against an enemy the soldiers regarded as foreign. The capital's workers were armed in the emergency, Red Guard detachments receiving rifles from stocks the government could not afford to refuse, and Bolshevik prisoners walked out of gaol into the defence committees as the men of the hour. Kornilov was arrested, his advance guard commander shot himself, and the affair guttered out in recrimination. But the distribution of power had altered in ways no proclamation recorded. The party that July had fled the police now stood as the defender of the revolution against military dictatorship, its cadres armed, its rivals in the soviet compromised by association with a government that had flirted with the general, and its central accusation, that the moderate course ended in counterrevolution, apparently confirmed by events.

### What changed hands during the defence of the capital?

Rifles changed hands, passing from state arsenals to worker detachments that were never afterward disarmed. Legitimacy changed hands too: the organisation lately hunted as a nest of foreign agents emerged as the shield of the revolution, while the government that had summoned the emergency was remembered for having made it necessary.

For the ledger, the Kornilov affair is the hinge between the two questions. Mechanically, it demonstrated that the state's coercive instruments in the capital were already answerable to soviet networks: trains obeyed railway committees, troops obeyed agitators, arsenals obeyed emergency committees on which Bolsheviks sat. Socially, it completed the education February had begun, teaching soldiers and workers to identify the soviet parties, and increasingly one party among them, as the custodians of the revolution's gains against a restoration. When historians of the traditional school describe October as a coup executed against a functioning state, the autumn evidence about how the state actually functioned is the revisionists' first exhibit. When revisionists describe October as the crest of a popular wave, the mechanism by which the wave was finally channelled, a party committee deciding a date, is the traditionalists' first exhibit in reply. The separation of the questions does not weaken either exhibit. It assigns each to the stratum where it is strong.

## The soviets in the autumn and the meaning of their majorities

Through September and into October the elected institutions of the revolution changed hands in full view, by procedures nobody alleged to be fraudulent. Resolutions demanding the transfer of power to the soviets, brought forward by Bolshevik delegates in factory after factory and regiment after regiment, began to pass where they had failed in the spring, because the men voting had changed their minds under the pressure of events rather than because the electorate had been replaced. The Petrograd Soviet adopted a Bolshevik resolution on power at the start of September and, after its moderate chairman resigned in protest, elected Trotsky to the chair. Moscow followed within weeks. A conference of the party's organisations registered the shift in the provinces as well, unevenly, more slowly, but in the same direction. These were not votes for a party programme in the modern sense. Delegates were mandated by workplaces and units, recallable by them, and their resolutions read as instructions about immediate questions: peace, land, bread, power to the institutions the voters themselves inhabited.

### How did the Bolsheviks win their soviet majorities?

By being the only major party whose answer to each accumulating grievance matched the grievance: immediate peace negotiations for the soldiers, the land to those working it for the peasant conscripts, control over production for the factory delegates, and power to the soviets rather than to a coalition that had spent the summer failing on all three.

The meaning of these majorities requires the care the ledger exists to enforce. A soviet majority was not a national majority, and the Bolsheviks knew it; the same weeks that brought them the two capitals left them a minority in the countryside's institutions and among the peasantry at large, whose political home remained the Socialist Revolutionary party, at least on paper. What the soviet majorities conferred was something different from a census victory: command of the institutions located where the crisis would be settled. The garrisons of the capital region, the railway junctions, the telegraph, the great factories of two industrial cities, the Baltic Fleet's committees within reach of Petrograd by sea and rail, these were the levers of any seizure and any defence, and by October the hands resting on them were, in the main, hands that voted for Bolshevik resolutions. The two-question separation gives this fact its precise weight. It does not convert a minority of the nation into a majority. It establishes that the seizure, when it came, was executed from inside institutions that had in those very weeks openly chosen the executors, which is a different thing from a band of outsiders storming a hostile citadel.

Opposition inside the party must be entered in the same ledger, because the mechanism question is falsified by any picture of a monolith moving as one mind. When the insurrection was debated in the Bolshevik central committee in mid October, the decision passed against the public dissent of two of its most senior members, Zinoviev and Kamenev, who argued that the party was overestimating its support and courting destruction, and who carried their objection so far as to signal it in a non-party newspaper. Lenin, still in hiding and furious at the disclosure, demanded their expulsion. The episode matters beyond its drama. A conspiracy does not usually publish its internal minority report in advance of the event, and a party confident of total control does not need its leader to argue, memo by memo, that the moment will not wait. The seizure was planned by an organisation with real factions, a real vote, and a leader whose authority had to be exercised as pressure rather than assumed as fact. All of that belongs to the mechanism, and all of it complicates the word coup in ways the ledger's rows will now make formal.

## The coup or revolution ledger

The table below is the article's instrument in its most compressed form. Each row states a contested claim. The second column gives the reading associated with the traditional historiography, in which October appears as a coup executed by a disciplined minority. The third gives the reading associated with the revisionist social historians, in which October appears as the political crest of a mass movement. The fourth column narrows the claim to what the surviving evidence, election returns, resolutions, memoirs from every camp and the documentary record of the institutions themselves, establishes without need of a camp's permission. Readers pressed for time can take the fourth column as the article in miniature, though the prose that surrounds it explains why arriving at that column cost several generations of scholarship.

| Contested claim | The coup account | The revisionist account | What the evidence establishes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Popular support | A small activist minority exploited chaos while the nation stood passive or hostile | The party rode a genuine popular movement for peace, land and worker control | Support was real, broad in the cities, garrisons and fleets, and growing through the autumn; it was not a majority of the whole population, and the countryside's allegiance lay elsewhere |
| Soviet majorities | Irrelevant credentials, since the soviets were unrepresentative bodies the party captured | Democratic proof, since delegates were mandated, recallable and repeatedly re-elected | The majorities were honestly won in the two capitals by open votes after months of argument; the institutions they commanded were local and functional rather than national |
| The scale of fighting | Absence of resistance proves the seizure was a walkover by plotters | Absence of resistance proves the old order had no defenders left | Fighting in the capital was limited because the government's authority had already dissolved; in Moscow resistance lasted days and cost lives in the hundreds by most estimates |
| The assembly elections | A national verdict against the Bolsheviks, annulled by force within weeks | An outdated snapshot, taken on party lists drawn up before the left split and before October realigned opinion | The Socialist Revolutionaries placed first and the Bolsheviks second on the returns historians cite; the assembly sat once in January and was dispersed by armed force |
| The role of the party apparatus | A disciplined machine substituted its will for the people's at every step | A responsive instrument that channelled demands arising from below | The party decided the timing and executed the seizure through a committee; it had reached that position by winning arguments in elected bodies whose mandates it then used and, in the assembly's case, overrode |

The ledger's discipline is that no column is permitted to borrow another column's evidence. The coup account is strongest on the mechanics of the final forty-eight hours and on the assembly, weakest on the preceding six months, where it must explain away an inconvenient quantity of voting. The revisionist account is strongest on the social history of the factories, garrisons and villages, weakest on the morning after, where it must explain how a movement so broadly mandated required the dispersal of the one institution elected by the entire nation. The fourth column's findings are less satisfying than either camp's story, being asymmetrical: a seizure minority in form and majoritarian in its immediate bases, a revolution in its social depth and a coup in its technique, an event whose two descriptions quarrel because each is answering only half of it. That asymmetry is not a compromise manufactured for politeness. It is what the rows, taken separately, severally report.

## The night of the insurrection: how power was actually taken

The instrument of the seizure was improvised from the defence machinery of the Kornilov days, and the improbability of that fact deserves emphasis. The Petrograd Soviet, in October, created a Military Revolutionary Committee to coordinate the garrison's defence against a feared counterrevolutionary stroke and against the government's plan to transfer revolutionary regiments to the front. In most accounts the committee was chaired on paper by a Left Socialist Revolutionary officer and directed in practice by Trotsky, and its ostensible business was protective: no unit to leave the capital without its countersignature, no order to be obeyed that it had not seen. In the space of a fortnight the countersignature became a parallel chain of command. Regiments that would not have moved for the party moved for the committee, because the committee spoke in the soviet's name and because its orders arrived dressed as defence of the revolution rather than as its next advance. When the confrontation came, the committee could therefore test its authority before staking it, issuing instructions about printing presses, bridges and railway stations and discovering that they were obeyed.

### What did the Military Revolutionary Committee hold before the rising began?

It held the obedience of the capital's garrison, secured by the countersignature rule; the allegiance of the fortress guarding the harbour; the railwaymen's and telegraph staff's willingness to route its messages first; and a mandate from an elected soviet that made its orders legible as defence rather than usurpation.

The government's own moves supplied the occasion and the timetable. Kerensky, learning of the preparations and pressed by his remaining allies, moved first: Bolshevik newspapers were ordered closed, arrests of committee members were authorised, military cadets were posted to raise the bridges over the Neva, and loyal forces were summoned from the front. The committee answered in kind and in daylight, presenting each of its seizures as the restoration of a right the government had attacked. Through 24 and 25 October its detachments took the bridges back down, occupied the telegraph agency, the telephone exchange, the railway stations, the state bank and the power stations, usually without a shot, because the men guarding them had instructions they no longer believed in or no instructions at all. By the evening of the 25th, when the Second All-Russian Congress of Soviets assembled in the Smolny Institute, the capital belonged in every operational sense to the committee, and the remaining business was a palace containing a rump of ministers guarded by cadets, a women's battalion and Cossack units who mostly declined to fight.

The taking of the Winter Palace has suffered the strangest afterlife of any episode in the year, because the version the world carries in its head was manufactured a decade later. The assault in the early hours of 26 October was, by the standards of the image, a shabby and protracted affair: a signal from the fortress guns, a cruiser firing blanks from the river, hours of negotiation, infiltration through unguarded doors and service entrances, the ministers found waiting in a dining room and arrested without massacre. Casualties in the capital across the whole operation were limited, a handful by the accounts most historians credit, against the thousands the propaganda image requires. In Moscow the same transfer cost far more: there the committee faced organised resistance around the Kremlin, fighting lasted the better part of a week, artillery was used inside the city, and the dead numbered in the hundreds by most estimates. The contrast between the two cities is itself a datum for the ledger. Where the old authority had dissolved completely, power changed hands almost by signature; where a remnant chose to fight, the seizure revealed the coercion normally hidden inside the word transfer.

### Where did the famous storming of the Winter Palace come from?

From a dramatised reconstruction staged for the revolution's tenth anniversary and fixed in the celebrated film of that commemoration, which employed more participants, more artillery and more choreography than the original night had contained. The hesitant, negotiated, half-empty original was replaced in memory by its own anniversary pageant.

The dramatisation point is not a curiosity for the footnotes. It demonstrates how the mechanism question gets corrupted in transmission: the side that won October had every incentive to paint the seizure as a mass storming, since a heroic crowd legitimises better than a countersignature, while the side that lost had every incentive to paint it as a sordid trick played in an empty city, since a trick requires no reckoning with why the city was empty. Between the pageant and the sneer, the documented texture of the night, patient, procedural, largely bloodless in the capital and bloody enough in Moscow, tends to disappear. A reader who wants the event has to want the texture, and the texture is recoverable: orders, logs, memoirs written by participants who detested one another and therefore corroborate the facts they agree on even while disputing every interpretation.

## The congress, the walkout and the ratification

The insurrection's timing against the congress was the party's central political decision, and it cuts both ways in the ledger. Lenin, from hiding, had demanded a seizure before the congress met, fearing delay and despising the gamble of asking permission. The course actually taken, associated with Trotsky's management of the committee, fused the rising to the congress's opening so that the transfer could be presented to the assembled delegates as a fact their own institution had authorised through its Petrograd organ. When the congress opened on the evening of the 25th, with the palace not yet taken, the Bolsheviks formed the largest delegation and, together with the Left Socialist Revolutionaries who supported the insurrection, commanded a majority of the hall. The moderate socialists, Mensheviks and right Socialist Revolutionaries, denounced the seizure as a military conspiracy against the revolution and, after an attempt by the veteran socialist Martov to broker a broad socialist government was overtaken by events, walked out in protest.

### Why did the moderate socialists walk out of the congress?

Because remaining would have seated them as witnesses to a transfer they regarded as a crime against the revolution's own legality, and because they calculated that the new power would collapse within days and return authority to sober hands. Their exit surrendered the hall, the ratification and the machinery of legitimacy to those who stayed.

The walkout is one of those decisions whose consequences inverted its intentions, and the congress that remained proceeded to enact the insurrection's public meaning: a decree on peace proposing an immediate armistice on all fronts, a decree on land abolishing landed property and handing the estates to peasant committees, and the formation of a new government, a Council of People's Commissars, responsible in the first instance to the congress and its executive. Martov's departing prophecy, that the victors would discover the whole country did not stand where the hall stood, was answered in the following weeks by risings, strikes of officials, sabotage in the ministries and a civil service that largely refused to serve. The ratification was genuine within its institution and partial within the nation, a sentence that sounds like evasion until the two-question separation gives it content. Institutionally, power had been transferred by the closest thing to a revolutionary parliament that existed, voting in the open after debate. Nationally, the congress represented the organised minority of the population, workers, soldiers and a slice of the peasantry, and its writ ran exactly as far as the railway lines and garrison towns that accepted it. Both clauses are true, and the civil war that followed was in part the violent adjudication between them, a subject noted here and treated elsewhere, as the introduction promised.

## The Constituent Assembly: the election, the sitting, the dispersal

No episode in the year carries more weight in the moral ledger of October than the fate of the Constituent Assembly, and it is placed here, in the middle of the narrative rather than in a concluding apology, because the brief of an honest case study is to give the hardest fact its full size at the point where the reader meets it. The assembly had been the common horizon of the entire revolution. Every party, the Bolsheviks prominently among them, had demanded its convocation since February, had accused the Provisional Government of delaying it, and had treated universal suffrage for a sovereign constituent body as the natural destination of the democratic revolution. Elections were held in November, under conditions of extraordinary difficulty, across an empire at war and in dissolution, and they remain, by the standards historians apply, a remarkably genuine expression of a vast electorate's wishes. The tabulations that scholarship has refined ever since, beginning with the pioneering reconstruction by the American historian Oliver Radkey, place the Socialist Revolutionaries first with roughly two fifths of the vote, the Bolsheviks second with roughly a quarter, and the liberal and national parties trailing. Regional texture mattered as much as totals: the Bolsheviks led in Petrograd and Moscow, in the industrial districts, in the Baltic Fleet and on the northern and western fronts, while the Socialist Revolutionary lists swept the agrarian provinces where most Russians lived.

One complication belongs in the record rather than in a footnote, because each camp wields it selectively. The Socialist Revolutionary party had split over the insurrection, its left wing supporting the soviet government while its right defended the assembly, but the electoral lists had been drawn up before the split crystallised, so the peasant vote for the party cannot be cleanly assigned between its two futures. Traditional historians treat the complication as marginal: the returns still show the nation declining to give the Bolsheviks a plurality. Revisionist historians treat it as substantial: the largest party on the ballot did not exist as a single party by the time the votes were counted. The evidence column of the ledger holds both halves. The lists were stale, and the staleness does not manufacture a Bolshevik majority out of a quarter of the vote. A party that had won the capitals and the fleets had not won the country, and it knew the figures as well as its critics did.

The assembly met on 5 January 1918 by the old calendar in the Tauride Palace, the same hall where the Provisional Government had sat, and its single sitting has become a set piece of the historiography. The Bolshevik and Left Socialist Revolutionary delegates demanded that the assembly endorse the soviet government's decrees and its declaration of the rights of the working and exploited people; the majority, led by the Socialist Revolutionary chairman Viktor Chernov, elected on the assembly's own authority, refused to subordinate the body to the soviets. The Bolshevik delegation withdrew after reading a statement of denunciation; the Left Socialist Revolutionaries followed later; and in the small hours the commander of the guard, a sailor, informed the remaining deputies that the guard was tired and the sitting must end. Deputies came back the following day to find the palace closed and guarded. A decree of the soviet executive formalised the dissolution. Demonstrations in support of the assembly in the capital were dispersed, with some bloodshed in the streets. No serious attempt was made anywhere to restore it by force, a silence that each camp reads to its own advantage: as proof that the nation acquiesced in soviet power, or as proof that a population exhausted by a year of revolution lacked the means to defend a parliament against armed men.

## Why almost nobody defended the Provisional Government

The mechanism question has a mirror image that is asked less often than it deserves. Coups are usually resisted by someone: a loyal garrison, a rival faction, a crowd defending the fallen. The Provisional Government, nominally the authority of a great empire, fell in the capital with fewer defenders than a provincial town hall might have mustered, and the near absence of resistance is among the strongest evidence that something more than a conspiracy had matured by the autumn. Kerensky's last days were a study in isolation. The officer corps despised him for the Kornilov affair, in which they believed he had first summoned and then betrayed their commander. The moderate socialists who staffed his coalition had spent their credibility underwriting his offensives and his delays. The garrison whose regiments he ordered to the front regarded the orders as a sentence of death for the capital's revolution and appealed over his head to the soviet, which rescinded them. Cossack units summoned to defend the government negotiated, delayed and in the end mostly stood aside. When the end came, the head of the government escaped the palace in disguise and drove out of the city to find troops, and the army he hoped to borrow declined, within days, to march.

### Why did the Provisional Government fall almost undefended?

Because every constituency that might have defended it had withdrawn its credit for a different reason: officers over Kornilov, socialists over the coalition's failures, soldiers over the front transfers, and the street over bread and peace. A government survives on layered loyalty; by October each layer had separately peeled away.

Set beside February, the symmetry is instructive and cuts against any single-word summary in either direction. The monarchy in February fell because its coercive instrument mutinied; the Provisional Government in October fell because its coercive instrument had already been reallocated, through the countersignature and the committees, to the institution that now displaced it. In neither case did the crowd in the street strike the decisive blow at the decisive hour; in both cases the street, the factories and the barracks had spent months deciding which orders they would obey, and the formal transfer merely registered the decision. That is the social process operating as a precondition of the mechanism, the exact relationship the two-question separation exists to describe. It does not follow that the transfer was democratic in the parliamentary sense, and the assembly's fate demonstrates the limits of that claim in the most concrete form available. It follows only that the seizure was the last act of a dissolution rather than the first act of a usurpation, and a reading that begins its story on the night of 25 October will mistake the signature for the document.

## The October Revolution in the traditional reading: a coup by disciplined men

The oldest sustained interpretation of October grew up in the shadow of the regime it produced, and its shape reflects the shadow. In the traditional account, whose classic statements were written by historians working in the West during the middle decades of the twentieth century, the revolution appears as a conspiracy executed by a small, disciplined, ideologically armoured party that exploited a temporary chaos to seize a state its population had never chosen to give it. Leonard Schapiro, in his studies of the party's origins and structure, traced the organisational lineage: a vanguard built for conspiracy under tsarism, schooled in factional warfare, carrying into power the habits of the underground, secrecy, centralism, the treatment of dissent as treason. Richard Pipes, writing the most uncompromising version of the argument at the century's end, presented October as a coup d'état in the strict sense, planned by a narrow leadership, executed by a few thousand activists, and unconnected to any authentic popular mandate, with the intellectuals' romantic indulgence toward it as one of the modern age's consequential errors. Between those poles stood a shelf of diplomatic and political histories in which the Bolsheviks' own retrospective mythology, the mass storming, the heroic epic, was dismissed as stagecraft laid over a squalidly efficient putsch.

The evidence the traditional reading marshals is not invented, and a case study does it no service by pretending otherwise. Its strongest exhibits are exactly the ones the ledger's fourth column confirms. The decision to rise was taken by a party committee, against internal dissent, on a timetable chosen for tactical advantage against a congress and an assembly election. The seizure in the capital was executed by a small number of organised detachments, and its bloodlessness there testifies at least as much to prior arrangement as to popular enthusiasm. The assembly, the only body in the entire crisis elected by something approaching the whole nation, was dispersed by armed men within ten weeks of the votes being cast. And the regime that followed, whose record belongs to another article's protocol, developed institutions of coercion whose apologists have never explained how they grew from a mandate of liberation. On the traditional reading these facts compose a single picture: the coup is the truth of October, the revolution its costume, and everything subsequent the unfolding of the first night's logic.

Where the traditional account is weakest, its critics have always known where to press. A conspiracy explains a weekend; it does not explain eight months. The reading struggles with the accumulation of contrary institutional evidence from the year itself: the resolutions passed by thousands of factory and regimental meetings, the re-elections that moved soviet majorities from party to party in the open, the garrison's refusal to defend two successive authorities, the railwaymen's independent obstruction of Kornilov, none of which the party commanded in any sense that a conspirator would recognise. Pipes and his predecessors answered such evidence by discounting the institutions: soviets were volatile, mandates were cheap, crowds were manipulated, soldiers were simply deserting under red slogans. Some of that discounting is reasonable in particular cases. As a general solvent it dissolves too much, leaving the coup itself unexplained, for a trick that requires the prior evaporation of an empire's entire coercive apparatus has already conceded the social transformation it was meant to deny. The traditional reading also carries the mark of its era: written when the Soviet state stood as the West's antagonist, its October is often the first chapter of an argument about the Cold War, and its authors would not all have denied the charge.

## The revisionist reading: a revolution made from below

The revision arrived, as revisions do, from the archives of ordinary institutions rather than from new manifestos. From the nineteen seventies a generation of social historians in Western universities turned from the party's central committee to the factory committees, the district soviets, the garrison units and the soldiers' wives, and reconstructed the year from those floors upward. Alexander Rabinowitch's study of the Bolsheviks' path to power, built on the party's own Petrograd records and a mass of local documentation, delivered the revision's most unsettling finding for both camps: the party of 1917 was not the disciplined monolith of legend but a sprawling, argumentative, often chaotic organisation, repeatedly outvoted by its own rank and file, whose influence grew precisely where it responded to demands arising independently in the workplaces and barracks. S. A. Smith's work on the capital's factories traced how workers' own conceptions of control, dignity and fairness radicalised through the year along tracks the party followed more often than it laid. Diane Koenker's studies of Moscow's workers and printers, and the joint work she pursued with William Rosenberg on strikes, supplied the same pattern in different keys: grievance first, organisation second, ideology arriving as a language for demands already formed.

On this reading the sequence of the year reverses its meaning. The party did not inject revolution into a passive population; a population battered by war, inflation and hunger generated, through its own elected institutions, a demand for a different power, and the Bolsheviks were the only major organisation willing to be identified, wholly and without coalitionist hedging, with that demand. The slogans of peace, land and soviet power succeeded, in Rabinowitch's account, because they named what soldiers, workers and peasants were already attempting to do: leaving the front, dividing the estates, ignoring the ministries. The soviet majorities of the autumn then become the revision's central exhibit, the documented moment at which the demand found its instrument, and the insurrection becomes almost an administrative consequence, the transfer of a power that had in substance already migrated. Sheila Fitzpatrick's compact synthesis of the revolution, written from within this scholarly generation, gave the reading its textbook form: October as the outcome of social forces that the war had unbound, with the party as catalyst and beneficiary rather than as puppet master.

The revision has its own ledger of difficulties, and its practitioners, to their credit, recorded several of them. Responsiveness is not the same as consent to everything done in responsiveness's name. The factory committees whose radicalism the revision documented were concentrated in a minority of the population; the peasantry, the nation's overwhelming mass, appears in the revisionist literature of the capitals mostly as an absence, a countryside seizing land on its own timetable and voting for a different party when asked. The revision also had to live with the morning after. A movement reading must explain why a power so rooted in elected institutions dispersed the most broadly elected institution of all within weeks, and the explanations offered, that the assembly was an anachronism, that its lists were stale, that soviet democracy was a higher form, are the Bolsheviks' own arguments returning in scholarly dress. Critics of the revision, including historians who shared its methods, noted a tendency to stop the film at the moment of seizure, where the social evidence is most favourable, and to let the coercive consolidation that followed be billed to a different account. The strongest version of the revision survives those criticisms, but it survives as a reading of how power was won, not as a certificate for how it was used.

## After the archives opened: complications for both camps

The collapse of the Soviet Union at the start of the nineteen nineties changed the evidentiary basis of the quarrel in ways neither camp had predicted. Archives that had been closed, purged or accessible only through official mythology became, for a period, remarkably open: party and state records, police files, the correspondence of leaders, the paperwork of the institutions the revisionists had studied from outside. The first harvest complicated everyone. Orlando Figes, in his vast narrative of the revolution and civil war, used the widened record to write a history that was social in method and severe in judgement, restoring individual experience, peasant politics and the sheer contingency of events to the centre while concluding that the tragedy lay in the revolution's own dynamics as much as in any conspiracy. Rex Wade's scholarly synthesis, which has served a generation of students, drew the balance with characteristic care: mass radicalisation and party organisation as interacting causes, neither sufficient alone. Mark Steinberg's collections of ordinary people's own words from 1917, letters, resolutions, poems scratched in notebooks, gave the social question a documentary voice that neither camp could wholly claim, since the voices demand peace and land in one line and curse the new authorities in the next.

### What did the opening of the archives change, and what did it leave unsettled?

It settled texture: the party's internal disorder, the soviets' genuine electoral life, the scale of the land seizures, the improvisation behind the legend of the plan. It left unsettled every question of weight, since the same files that document a popular movement also document the men who decided, in committee, when to spend it.

Post-archival work also returned attention to subjects both older camps had slighted. The national movements of the empire's borderlands, Ukrainian, Finnish, Baltic, Transcaucasian, appear in the widened literature not as scenery but as independent revolutions with their own timetables, for whom October in Petrograd was one signal among several and not always the decisive one. The peasantry's war against both city and landlord, the desertion crisis as a rural referendum, the soldiers' committees as a parliament of the trenches: each acquired monographs that made the two-capital story look like a synecdoche. Meanwhile a literature on the revolution's violence, written with access to records of the machinery of repression as it formed, made it harder for any reading to treat coercion as an afterthought, even within the limits this article observes about narrating the terror itself. And the electoral scholarship matured: Radkey's reconstruction of the assembly vote, refined by later Russian scholars working the provincial returns, firmed the figures into the ranges the ledger cites while also mapping, district by district, a country whose political geography no single camp's story fits.

Where does the quarrel stand after a century? A reader who surveys the field without allegiance will find the disagreement has migrated from the facts toward the weights. Few working historians now defend the pure conspiracy of the earliest traditional accounts, because the institutional record of 1917 will not carry it. Few defend the pure spontaneity of the earliest revisionist enthusiasm, because the committee minutes will not carry that either. The contested ground is the proportion and the judgement: how much of October was made by the men who planned its final night, how much by the millions who made its final night possible, and whether the sequel was inscribed in the seizure or chosen afterward by men who might have chosen otherwise. Those are better questions than the ones the camps started with, and they are the questions the two-question separation was built to hold open.

## The social question: peace, land, bread and the factory committees

Strip the year down to its material pressures and the social question answers itself in outline before any party is mentioned. Begin with the soldiers, because the war was the revolution's furnace. The Russian army of 1917 numbered in the millions, overwhelmingly peasant conscripts, officered by men whose authority rested on a deference the trenches had burned away. The soldiers' committees that sprang up after February converted the ranks into a deliberating body, and the deliberation ran in one direction: the war's purpose had evaporated with the dynasty that declared it, the land at home was being divided without the men in the trenches if they stayed, and every offensive was a tax on the survivors payable to a government that could not feed the capital. Desertion on the scale of the summer and autumn was not cowardice and not conspiracy. It was a plebiscite conducted with feet, and any power that wished to rule Russia had to be able to say, credibly, that the voting was over and the men could go home to the division of the land.

The land itself was the second pressure, older than the war and deeper than any slogan. Across the countryside the peasant communes had watched the gentry's estates through decades of hunger and land shortage, and from the summer of 1917 the watching turned to taking: manor houses burned in some districts, timber fenced in others, estate inventories seized by village committees, the whole movement proceeding on a village timetable that no party directed and several tried to ride. Peasant soldiers carried the expectation home in both directions, and the Socialist Revolutionaries, the party of the peasantry by inheritance, were consumed by the paradox of a movement whose ministry of agriculture endlessly drafted a land law while its own constituents enacted the law's substance with axes and account books. The Bolshevik land decree of the congress's second day, which abolished landed property and adopted wholesale a peasant programme compiled from local mandates, was an act of political judo whose honesty historians debate: it enacted, as the new state's first rural law, the platform of the party that had just beaten the decree's authors in the national election. Whatever its sincerity, its effect is recorded in the countryside's response to the coming civil war, when the villages defended the seizures against every landlord's return even while resisting the grain collectors of the state that had legalised them.

### What were soldiers, workers and peasants demanding by the autumn?

Soldiers demanded an end to the war and safe conduct home to the land division. Workers demanded bread at prices wages could reach, and control over factories whose owners were closing them or fleeing. Peasants demanded the estates, immediately and without compensation. Three demands, three constituencies, one common refusal of further postponement.

The workers' pressure had its own institutions and its own curriculum. The factory committees, elected on the shop floor from the first weeks of the revolution, began as watchdogs over hiring and supplies and were driven by the year's economics toward something larger: as owners locked out workforces, abandoned plants or starved them of orders in what workers experienced as an employers' offensive, the committees moved from supervision toward administration, demanding first the inspection of the accounts and then the direction of production itself. Historians of the shop floor have reconstructed this education in detail, and its political consequence was precise. Workers who had run a factory committee through a lockout did not need a pamphlet to explain the idea that the soviet, rather than a ministry of coalition, should hold power; they had rehearsed the transfer at the scale of their own benches. Add bread, whose absence had begun the year and whose queues in the capital lengthened again into the autumn as the transport system decayed, and the social question stands complete: a population in the decisive places had concluded, from experience rather than doctrine, that the existing authority could not deliver the three necessities of its life and should be replaced by the institutions it had built for itself.

Nothing in this account requires the population to have been Marxist, and the ledger should record that it mostly was not. The soldiers who voted for Bolshevik resolutions wanted peace and land, not the dictatorship of the proletariat as a theory of the state; the peasants who seized the estates wanted the land, and voted Socialist Revolutionary when asked to name a party; the workers who staffed the committees wanted bread and control, and argued about the rest. The social process supplied energy, direction and refusal; it did not supply a constitution. That distinction preserves the revisionist evidence at full strength while denying it the conclusion it sometimes reaches for. A revolution in the social sense had occurred in Russia through 1917, in the barracks and the villages and the factories, and it would have occurred under many managements of its final night. The question the mechanism asks is why the management turned out to be this one, and the answer to that returns the inquiry to the party that alone had organised itself to be standing at the switch when the current arrived.

## The mechanism question: how small the taking was

Against the immensity of the social process, set the smallness of the final operation, and hold both in view at once, because the two-question separation fails if either is allowed to eclipse the other. The seizure of power in the capital was accomplished by forces whose numbers look almost clerical beside the crowds of February: Red Guard detachments numbered in the thousands rather than the tens of thousands, sailors from the Baltic Fleet whose allegiance the committee could count, garrison units whose contribution was often simply to stay in barracks and refuse the other side. The orders fit on sheets of paper. The objectives were a list of buildings and junctions. The commander of the operation's political side worked from a former girls' school, the Smolny, amid telephones, couriers and canteen tea, in an atmosphere participants described as closer to an election night headquarters than to a storm. There was no moment at which the fate of the empire hung on a charge across a square. There was a sequence of signatures, arrivals, surrenders of keys, and dawn.

The traditional reading takes this smallness as the whole truth, and its logic deserves its strongest form rather than a caricature. States, on that logic, are not taken by the peoples in whose name they are claimed; they are taken by whoever organises the taking, and the credit for the taking belongs to the organisers and the blame for its methods likewise. A handful of men decided the date, overruled their own dissenters, disguised the offensive as a defence, struck at the moment of maximum institutional convenience, and presented the nation with a government before the nation had voted on the question in the only election that canvassed everyone. If the word coup has any use in political vocabulary, the traditionalists ask, what operation in the century fits it better? The revisionist reply, also at full strength, observes that the smallness is the signature not of conspiracy but of prior transfer: a lock turns easily when the building has already been emptied of everyone willing to defend the door. The committee's forces were few because few were needed; the ministers waited unguarded because the guard had been voted away from them over the preceding months; the telegraph operators routed the committee's messages first because the committee, and not the government, was the authority their own organisations recognised. On this reply the mechanism was small precisely because the social question had been answered at scale, and a coup that requires the prior dissolution of the state it seizes has absorbed so much revolution into its preconditions that the word strains.

A case study governed by the separation does not have to choose between those speeches, because they are about different columns of the ledger. The traditionalists are describing the fourth and fifth rows, the party apparatus and its deed against the assembly, and their description is accurate. The revisionists are describing the first and second rows, the support and the majorities, and theirs is accurate too. The error enters only when either speech claims to have described all five rows, when the mechanism's smallness is offered as proof that the support was counterfeit, or when the support's breadth is offered as proof that the mechanism was innocent. October's peculiarity, the thing that has kept a century of historians employed, is that it maximises both columns simultaneously: rarely has a seizure been at once so narrowly executed and so broadly prepared. Events that are merely coups do not require eight months of soviets changing their minds. Events that are merely revolutions do not require a committee choosing a night.

One more element of the mechanism deserves its place, because it explains the operation's surface legality, which participants on all sides remarked. The committee acted throughout under the colours of the soviet and of defence, and the congress ratified before the palace had even fallen, so the new power could date its birth from a vote rather than from an arrest. This was not hypocrisy in any simple sense; it was the revolution's own theory of legitimacy being applied by men who believed it, or at least believed that others believed it, and its practical effect was to give thousands of committee-men, delegates and clerks a formula under which they could serve the new authority without feeling themselves putschists. The Provisional Government's officials, by contrast, spent the first weeks refusing the new power's paperwork, and the resulting administrative strike, ministries empty, banks closed, files withheld, taught the victors a lesson about the distance between holding the telegraph office and holding a state. That distance, and the methods by which it was closed in the years that followed, belong to [the complete guide to Soviet Marxism](/2019/03/01/soviet-marxism-complete-guide/); [the case against Leninism](/2014/06/15/case-against-leninism/), argued in its own article in this series, takes up the consolidation directly. What belongs here is the narrow observation that the mechanism of October was designed to borrow legitimacy from the social process, and largely succeeded in borrowing it, which is why the two questions have been so hard to prise apart ever since.

## The theoretical problem: socialism in a peasant country

October posed a problem for Marxism that no amount of tactical brilliance could dissolve, and the participants knew it, because they had all been educated in the doctrine that made the event look impermissible. The Russia of 1917 was a peasant empire with islands of advanced industry. Its working class, though concentrated in giant factories that made it politically weighty beyond its numbers, was a small minority of the population. The orthodox reading of historical materialism, held by the Mensheviks and by the senior theorists of European social democracy, prescribed a sequence: first the bourgeois revolution, which would clear away the autocracy, develop capitalism and multiply the proletariat; then, after a long maturation, the socialist revolution on the agenda. On that timetable October was not a bold application of the theory. It was a violation of it, a seizure of power for socialism in a country that had not yet completed the capitalism socialism was supposed to succeed.

### What did orthodox Marxists insist had to happen first in Russia?

They insisted on the stage: a bourgeois democratic republic first, under which capitalism would develop the productive forces, expand the working class and create the material premises of socialism. A socialist seizure in a peasant country, on their account, must degenerate into coercion against the majority or collapse under the economy's immaturity.

The Mensheviks stated this objection at the time with a clarity their defeat has not dulled. Julius Martov and his colleagues argued that a minority proletarian party attempting socialist construction amid a peasant sea would be forced either to retreat or to rule by terror against the population in whose name it governed, and the party's oldest theoretician, Georgi Plekhanov, condemned the April turn as a delirium that ignored the alphabet of the doctrine. These were not liberals talking. They were Marxists citing Marx, and their argument from the stages was textually respectable, anchored in the master's insistence that no social order disappears before the productive forces it can contain have developed, and that new relations of production never appear before their material conditions have matured. If October is to be read as a Marxist event rather than merely an event carried out by men calling themselves Marxists, the stage objection has to be answered in theory and not merely outvoted in a committee.

Two answers were available in the tradition, and the year's originality lies in their convergence. The first was the theory of permanent revolution, developed a decade earlier by Trotsky from the experience of the revolution of 1905: in a backward country the bourgeoisie arrives too late and too frightened to complete its own revolution, so the tasks of the democratic stage, land, peace, national independence, fall to the proletariat, which, once in power, cannot stop at the bourgeois boundary but is driven to socialist measures and to dependence on revolution in the advanced West to sustain them. The second answer was Lenin's own trajectory in the spring of 1917, arriving by a separate road at a neighbouring position: the war had fused the stages, imperialism had made the chain break at its weakest link, and the soviets offered a form through which the democratic revolution could grow over, in his phrase's implication, into the socialist one without an interval of consolidated bourgeois rule. The intellectual biography of Lenin in this series follows the formation of the man who made that turn; the theory of permanent revolution receives its own extended treatment in the article on [permanent revolution explained](/2014/09/15/permanent-revolution-explained/); and the question whether the result should be called a development of Marxism or a departure from it is the explicit subject of the companion inquiry into [whether Leninism was a break with Marx](/2014/05/15/was-leninism-a-break-with-marx/). Here the point for the case study is narrower and can be stated flatly. October either vindicated a creative revision of the inherited theory, undertaken independently by two of its protagonists before the event, or it stands as the century's most consequential departure from the theory in whose name power was taken. It cannot be read as the routine execution of a doctrine everybody already held, because the doctrine everybody held is what the Mensheviks were quoting while they lost.

The theoretical dispute was not an ornament on the event; it governed the new state's first expectations and therefore its first crises. Both answers made the Russian seizure conditional on Europe. If the revolution remained isolated in a backward country, the permanent revolution argument itself predicted deformation, and Lenin said repeatedly in the first months that the Russian beginning would perish without the German continuation. The failure of revolution in the West, the German risings defeated, the Hungarian soviet republic crushed, is therefore not an external misfortune that befell an otherwise self-sufficient theory. It was the non-arrival of the theory's own stated precondition, and the adaptations of the regime that followed, the retreats and the coercions, were argued out among men who knew the precondition had failed. A Marxist reading of October that omits this conditional structure mistakes both the audacity and the trap of the original act: the seizure was made in the name of a world process, by a party whose theory told it that a national process alone would not be enough.

## The strongest objection, stated whole

Every case study in this cluster is required to give the case against its subject at full strength before any answer is attempted, and with October the objection is neither obscure nor weak. Stated whole, it runs as follows. A political party campaigned for months for the convocation of a Constituent Assembly, denounced its rivals for delaying the elections, and presented universal suffrage as the revolution's rightful arbiter. When the elections were finally held, freely and on a scale few European states of the era could match, that party received roughly a quarter of the vote and placed second to a rival it despised. It responded, within weeks, by dispersing the assembly at gunpoint after a single sitting and ruling thereafter through institutions whose electorates were narrower, whose mandates it controlled, and whose oppositions it progressively suppressed. On what principle, the objection asks, does such a party claim democratic legitimacy for its seizure of power? Soviet majorities in two cities, however honestly won, are majorities of the organised and the urban; they are not a national verdict, and the party knew the national verdict because it had demanded the election that delivered it. A doctrine that accepts an election's authority in advance and annuls it in arrears has not discovered a higher form of democracy. It has discovered that it prefers its own victories, and every subsequent claim to rule in the people's name inherits the discount applied on that January night.

The objection does not need the civil war, the terror or anything after to make its force; it is complete within the winter of the seizure itself, which is why it has outlasted every shift of historiographical fashion. Traditional historians make it the centre of their case. Honest revisionists concede its factual skeleton entire. And no answer that evades its central sequence, the demand, the defeat, the dispersal, deserves a hearing, because the sequence is documented in the party's own words at every step.

### Can soviet majorities answer the charge about the assembly?

They can answer a different charge: that the seizure lacked any mandate from the institutions where the crisis was decided. Against the assembly charge they function as a rival theory of representation, direct, mandated and recallable against the parliamentary form, and the rivalry was never submitted to the national test the assembly embodied.

The Bolshevik answer, which the separation requires to be stated as its makers stated it, was a theory of representation rather than a denial of the figures. Soviet democracy, on their account, was the higher form: delegates elected from workplaces and units rather than from territorial rolls, bound by mandates, recallable at any time, fusing executive and legislative in a body that worked rather than talked, and excluding from its franchise the employing classes whose votes a parliament counts as equal to their workers'. Against the assembly's arithmetic they set the soviets' sociology: an institution can be broader in territory and shallower in reality, representing a nation of isolated voters once, or narrower in territory and deeper, representing the organised producers continuously. The answer was not improvised for the occasion; it descends from Marx's reading of the Paris Commune and runs through the programme the party had published in the summer. It was, and remains, a coherent position that serious democratic theory has to engage rather than wave at.

Engagement, however, is where the concession this article is bound to record must be entered, in the terms the evidence compels. The argument from the higher form was made by a party that had spent the year campaigning for the assembly it dissolved, that had built its indictment of the Provisional Government substantially on the charge of delaying that assembly, and that discovered the parliamentary form's inadequacy at the precise moment the form returned an unfavourable result. Perhaps the discovery was sincere; the record shows the doctrine predated the defeat. Perhaps the doctrine was even right about the soviets' virtues in the institutions where they flourished. Neither possibility retires the chronological fact, and the chronological fact is the objection's core: the test was demanded, the test was held, the test was failed, and the tester was dispersed. A reading of October that separates the questions can accommodate everything the Bolsheviks claimed about soviet democracy while still entering that sequence, unqualified, in the ledger's fourth column. The two judgements do not cancel. They coexist, which is precisely the discomfort the event bequeaths to every tradition that claims it.

## The empire beyond the two capitals

Petrograd dominates the literature because the state's head was cut off there, but the empire the head governed was vast, and the revolution's character looks different from its other windows. In the industrial towns of the centre and the Urals, soviet power was often established in the weeks after the capital's seizure by local votes of the same kind, sometimes against resistance, sometimes by the simple departure of authority. Along the fronts, the armies disintegrated into the transfer: soldiers' committees endorsed the peace decree as a licence to go home, and whole units dissolved toward the railway stations in a migration no government could have ordered or stopped. In the countryside the land seizures, already far advanced, received the decree as a charter and accelerated, with the village communes completing a redistribution that constituted, for most of the population, the revolution's entire tangible content. The new state's writ in these months ran along the railway network and outward from the garrison towns; beyond the stations lay a countryside that had accepted the land and reserved its judgement on everything else.

The borderlands complicate every generalisation the capitals invite. Finland declared independence within weeks and descended into its own civil war between Reds and Whites, in which the Russian example served both as inspiration and as warning. Ukraine cycled through governments, Rada, Hetmanate, Directory, each claiming the revolution's mantle against the others while German occupation and peasant insurgency redrew the map beneath them. The Baltic provinces, Poland, the Caucasus and Central Asia each converted the collapse of the centre into national revolutions with social programmes of their own, sometimes allied with the soviet government, sometimes at war with it, always irreducible to it. A Marxist reading of October that stays inside Petrograd mistakes the seizure's address for the revolution's extent. The social process of 1917 was an imperial unravelling as much as a class rising, and the state that eventually re-collected most of the empire's territory did so by conquest and negotiation over years, a process belonging to the history of the civil war and noted here only to mark the boundary of what the October seizure itself accomplished. On the night itself, what had been accomplished was the transfer of the capital, the ministries and the claim. The empire took longer to persuade.

## The witnesses: how this episode is known at all

A case study in the historiography pattern owes the reader a word about the glass through which the episode is seen, because October is unusually dependent on a handful of famous witnesses whose positions stand between the reader and the record. The fullest single narrative by a participant hostile to the victors is the memoir of Nikolai Sukhanov, a Menshevik economist who sat through the Smolny nights with a notebook and produced an account whose detail even the Bolsheviks mined, and whose judgements, contemptuous of Lenin's gamble while conceding the moderates' bankruptcy, still set the terms of the traditional reading's better half. The fullest narrative by a partisan is John Reed's famous reportage of the ten days, written by an American journalist who marched with the victors, interviewed everyone, and produced the enduring heroic version; Reed is invaluable for atmosphere and unreliable for proportion, a combination his readers have not always kept in view. Trotsky's later history of the revolution, written in exile, is the seizure's official philosophy of itself, brilliant, documented and organised as a brief for its author's theory, to be used as one uses a great advocate's summation. Against these stand the dry materials the revisionists taught the field to prize: the resolutions and protocols of soviets and committees, the election returns reconstructed by Radkey, the factory archives, the soldiers' letters collected by later scholars. The camps of the historiography are, in part, constituencies of these sources: narrative historians drink from the memoirs, social historians from the protocols, and the quarrel of the interpretations is also a quarrel about which glass shows the truer colour. A reader equipped with the two-question separation can use all of them, because the memoirs are strongest exactly where the mechanism is concerned and the protocols strongest exactly where the social process is, and each source can be assigned to the question it is fitted to answer.

## What followed, and what this reading does not decide

The peace the soldiers demanded arrived at Brest-Litovsk in March 1918 at a price in territory and population that split the victors' own coalition and gave the new state's enemies their first recruiting ground. The civil war followed across the following years, with its fronts, its foreign interventions, its requisitions and its terror, White as well as Red, and the regime that emerged from it bore the war's marks in its constitution long after the guns stopped. All of that belongs, under the protocols of this series, to the treatments of the Soviet system and of the case against Leninism, and it is recorded here in one paragraph only so that the boundary is visible rather than evasive. The separation that governs this article applies to time as well as to concepts. Judgements about the regime of the nineteen twenties and thirties are not retroactive evidence about the resolutions passed in a Petrograd factory in September 1917, and the factory resolutions are not retroactive absolution for the regime. Each stratum answers its own question, and the reader who finishes this case study should be able to say, with the evidence assigned to its proper rows, what kind of event the seizure was, what kind of process carried it, and what kind of state followed, without allowing any of the three answers to do the others' work.

## Misreadings that survive every correction

Certain errors about the episode replicate faster than their corrections, and a reference article earns its keep by naming them in one place. The first is the storming: the mass assault on a defended palace, waves of attackers under fire, which belongs to the anniversary dramatisation discussed above and not to the negotiated, infiltrated, nearly empty original. The second is the empty opposite, the claim that the Bolsheviks enjoyed no popular support at all, which the autumn's electoral record inside the soviets refutes in document after document. The third is its mirror, the claim that the nation had chosen them, which the assembly returns refute with equal documentary force; the party held the decisive institutions and a quarter of the national vote, and the two facts must be carried together or not at all. The fourth is the fusion of February and October into a single revolution with a single character, when the first was an undirected collapse that surprised every party and the second a directed seizure that one party planned against the misgivings of its own senior members; the year contains both, and they are different species of event. The fifth is the attribution of the whole outcome to the party alone, a reading that erases the soviets that mandated it, the garrison that enforced it, the railwaymen who had rehearsed it against Kornilov and the factory committees that had educated its electorate; the party chose the night, but it did not manufacture the year. The sixth, finally, is the treatment of the assembly's dispersal as a detail, an epilogue unworthy of the main narrative's attention. In the ledger kept here it is a row of the first importance, the point where the revolution's two questions collide, and any summary of October that reaches its end without it has decided its verdict by omission.

A seventh error belongs with these because it flatters both camps at once: the belief that the outcome was planned from the start, that a design laid down in April or earlier unrolled through the year to its appointed night. The victors' mythology required the design, since prophecy honours the prophet, and the prosecution's case was simplified by it, since a conspiracy with a timetable is easier to indict than an improvisation. The record shows neither: a party that voted to burn its leader's insurrection letter in September, that discovered its instrument in a defence committee improvised against Kornilov, that settled its date a fortnight out against public internal dissent, and that governed its first winter by expedients none of its programmes had named. Contingency is not a minor correction to the legend. It is the medium in which the whole event, mechanism and process alike, actually occurred.

## The party as it actually was in the year of revolution

Both camps' arguments depend on a picture of the Bolshevik party, and the picture each camp needs is, in opposite ways, false to the organisation the documents show. The traditional reading requires a machine: disciplined, centralised, drilled in conspiracy, its ranks executing a leadership's design with the obedience of a general staff. The organisation that the party's own records reveal in 1917 was almost comically otherwise. It had entered the year as a small underground network of some tens of thousands, swollen within months by recruits who joined at factory gates and regimental meetings in numbers the central apparatus could barely register, reaching by its own counts several hundred thousand claimed members by the autumn, most of them new, many of them hazy about the programme, all of them voting in party meetings that repeatedly embarrassed the leadership. Local committees defied the centre; the party's Petersburg committee and its military organisation pulled against the central committee's caution in the summer; the July Days found the leadership chasing its own rank and file through the streets; and the insurrection itself, as recorded earlier in this article, was carried against the public dissent of two of the leadership's most senior names. Rabinowitch's archival verdict, that the party's openness and internal argument were conditions of its success rather than obstacles to it, has survived four decades of scrutiny because the documents keep confirming it.

The revisionist temptation is the mirror error, a party so porous and responsive that it dissolves into the movement and the mechanism question evaporates. The documents resist that too. For all its disorder, the organisation possessed assets no crowd could improvise: a network of experienced cadres hardened by exile and prison, a press that reached the barracks daily, a military organisation specialising in the garrison politics on which everything turned, and two leaders whose strategic judgements in the autumn, whatever their costs, were of a quality no rival party could match. When the central committee voted for insurrection it was not registering a plebiscite; it was deciding, in a room, against argument, and the decision bound the organisation's thousands of activists to a night's work they had not individually chosen. The truth in the mechanism column is a party loose enough to be genuinely rooted and organised enough to act as one body at the chosen moment, a combination rare in the history of revolutions and the real subject of most serious writing about it. Any single adjective, monolith or movement, amputates half the evidence.

## The coalition that nearly was: the railwaymen's ultimatum and the Left SRs

The fortnight after the seizure contained a possibility that the standard narratives, hurrying toward civil war, often skip, and the two-question method requires restoring it, because it tests what the new power's makers thought they were doing. The executive of the railwaymen's union, controlling the network on which the capital's food and the front's remnants depended, demanded a government of all the socialist parties and threatened to stop the trains of a Russia already starving if the parties refused to negotiate. Under that pressure, talks opened between the Bolsheviks and the moderate socialists on a coalition that would have widened the government, moderated its decrees and perhaps kept the revolution inside a soviet framework broader than one party. Senior Bolsheviks of the same tendency as the October dissenters favoured the course; Lenin opposed it with fury, reading the demand as the mechanism by which the seizure would be talked to death; the negotiations collapsed amid mutual ultimatums, troop movements against Moscow's resisting garrison, and the moderates' insistence that Lenin and Trotsky personally be excluded, a condition designed to be refused. The episode is the strongest documentary support for the view that one-party rule was not the seizure's blueprint but a sediment of its first weeks, deposited by refusals on several sides. It is equally strong support for the view that the blueprint's author knew exactly which deposits to refuse. Both supports belong in the ledger.

The Left Socialist Revolutionaries embody the same ambiguity in institutional form. The left wing of the peasant party had supported the insurrection, supplied the Military Revolutionary Committee with its formal chairman, voted the decrees at the congress, and after hesitation entered the government in December, taking portfolios that included agriculture and justice, the latter an uncomfortable seat from which to watch a security apparatus being born. For several months the soviet state was, formally, a coalition of the two parties that between them held the workers', soldiers' and much of the peasants' organised allegiance, a fact the pure coup narrative rarely accommodates. The coalition broke over the peace treaty of the following spring, which the Left SRs regarded as a betrayal worth resigning over, and their later descent into revolt against their former partners belongs to the civil war's history. What their participation establishes for this case study is narrower: the power made in October presented itself, and for a season functioned, as a soviet government resting on more than one party's mandate, and the road from that season to single-party rule was travelled through choices and ruptures that the record dates, names and argues about. October made the destination possible. Whether it made it necessary is the argument the adjacent articles in this cluster take up.

## Thirteen days: the calendar, the name and the branding of an event

A reference article should dispose of the calendar puzzle briskly, because it conceals a small lesson in how the event has been packaged. Russia in 1917 kept the Julian calendar, which by the twentieth century ran thirteen days behind the Gregorian calendar used in the West. The insurrection of 25 October in Petrograd's reckoning therefore fell on 7 November in London's or New York's, and the revolution carries its October name from the calendar its participants used, the new government itself switching the country to the Gregorian count the following February and thereby orphaning its own founding date. The packaging continued from there. The victors' official commemoration settled on the phrase Great October, with adjectives doing the work that argument declined to do; the exiled Trotsky insisted on the primacy of October as the proletarian answer to February's bourgeois act; Western textbooks that wished to file the event neutrally sometimes called it the November revolution, dating it by the reader's calendar and quietly voting, in the act of filing, for the view that the event belonged to world history rather than to its makers' mythology. Even the choice between Russian Revolution and October Revolution is a small historiographical act, the first dissolving the seizure into the year's whole upheaval, the second isolating the night the traditionalists indict and the revisionists contextualise. The reader who notices the packaging is less likely to be packed.

## The roads not taken: the counterfactuals historians argue

Serious writing about October keeps generating counterfactuals, and a case study can use them as instruments, provided each is labelled as an instrument and none is smuggled in as a finding. Four dominate the literature. The first asks whether a Kornilov dictatorship in the autumn, had the general's move succeeded, would have stabilised a military Russia or merely advanced the revolution by teaching it to shoot; the evidence of the defence's ease suggests the dictatorship would have faced the same dissolved instruments as the government it replaced. The second asks whether the moderate socialists, accepting power at the congress through Martov's formula of a broad socialist government, could have held the coalition against the pressures that tore every coalition of the year; their own walkout, and the summer's record, are the exhibits against, the railwaymen's negotiations the exhibits for. The third asks whether an assembly-centred republic, had the dispersal been resisted by a population willing to defend it, could have governed a country whose army had dissolved and whose villages were dividing the land by force; the silence that greeted the dispersal is the hardest datum for this road, and its defenders answer that exhaustion is not consent, which is true and also not a government. The fourth asks whether the Bolsheviks, had they waited for the assembly's verdict and accepted a minority's discipline, would have survived as a party at all; Lenin's whole autumn argument was that delay was death, and the fate of parties that waited, in that year, lends his premise a grim plausibility.

Counterfactuals cannot be entered in the ledger's evidence column, and this article enters none. Their function in the historiography is diagnostic: each camp's preferred road reveals what it thinks the year's real motor was. The traditionalist's assembly road presumes legitimacy was the scarce resource; the revisionist's coalition road presumes responsiveness was; the Kornilov road presumes coercion was; and the waiting road presumes timing was. The evidence assembled in the rows above suggests the scarcity that governed the year was none of these singly but the compound the two questions describe: authority that could simultaneously command the institutions of the crisis and survive contact with the nation's divisions. No claimant of 1917 possessed the compound entire. The Bolsheviks possessed more of it, in the places where the crisis was decided, than any rival, and the assembly's fate records the part they lacked.

## The first decrees and the first refusals

The new government's opening acts were chosen to ratify the social process before the mechanism's opponents could organise, and their selection tells the reader what the victors believed their mandate consisted of. The peace decree addressed the soldiers; the land decree addressed the villages; a decree on workers' control addressed the factory committees; the declaration of the rights of the peoples of Russia addressed the borderlands. Each act converted a movement already in motion into the state's own law, and the strategy's logic was openly avowed: the government would make itself the registrar of the revolution from below and dare its enemies to repeal the registrations. The resistance, when it came, arrived not first from generals but from clerks. Ministries declined to recognise the new commissars; the state bank refused to release funds to a government it regarded as usurpers, and money had to be extracted under guard; telegraphists and civil servants struck; the non-Bolshevik press campaigned against the seizure until decrees closed hostile newspapers, a measure defended as temporary and wartime and denounced, by socialists as well as liberals, as the revolution's first censorship. In December the government created an extraordinary commission for combating counterrevolution and sabotage, the organ whose initials would darken the century's vocabulary; its founding belongs to the record of the consolidation, whose narration this article has assigned elsewhere, but its date, within eight weeks of the seizure, belongs in any honest table of what the seizure set in motion.

The refusals matter to the two questions in opposite directions, and the separation sorts them cleanly. For the mechanism's critics, the officials' strike proved that the new power was a shell over a hostile or indifferent apparatus, a government of proclamations whose writ ran where armed men carried it. For the social reading, the same strike proved the opposite point about the old order: the state's own servants, given the choice between the fallen government and none, chose none, and served the new power or starved it without ever lifting a pen for the old one's restoration. Kerensky's parallel attempt to retake the capital with Cossack cavalry collapsed within days at the city's edge, defeated less by battle than by the same evaporation that had attended his defence: too few willing to ride, too many negotiating, the railwaymen stranding his transports as they had stranded Kornilov's. By the new year the contest for the capital region was over, and what remained, the periphery, the fronts, the assembly, would be settled by the mixture of vote, negotiation and force that the following sections of the year, and eventually the civil war, supplied.

## October measured against the tradition's own yardsticks

A Marxist reading owes the event one further test, internal to the tradition in whose name it was made: how does October score against the criteria Marx himself left for judging revolutions? The yardsticks are not obscure. From the Paris Commune he had drawn the positive model: the working class cannot simply lay hold of the ready-made state machinery and wield it for its own purposes; the replacement form is elected, mandated, recallable, paid at workmen's wages, its standing army abolished in favour of the armed population. From his historical writings he had left a method: men make their own history, but not in circumstances of their choosing, and the analyst's task is to trace the class forces whose collision the political drama expresses. And from his political economy he had left the materialist caution: institutions are to be judged by the social relations they organise, not by the banners over their doors.

Measured by the first yardstick, October's opening months present the strange double aspect that has structured this entire article. The seizure was executed in the Commune's name through the Commune's claimed heirs, the soviets, with recallable delegates and fused powers and the old ministries ostentatiously bypassed; and within the same months the new power closed newspapers, created a political police and dispersed a parliament, three acts no reading of the Commune text will generate. The tradition's defenders answer that the Commune itself, besieged, shot hostages and suppressed hostile papers, and that Marx praised its energy rather than deploring its severity; the tradition's critics answer that the Commune never claimed a monopoly of representation or outlawed the return of its electors' verdict, and that the distance between emergency measures in a besieged city and a system of rule is exactly the distance the yardstick measures. The dispute is genuine, textually grounded on both sides, and irresolvable by quotation, which is why this series assigns the state that resulted to its own full treatment rather than settling its character inside a case study of its birth. Measured by the second yardstick, the method, October has fared better in the scholarship: the social historians' demonstration that class forces, soldiers for peace, peasants for land, workers for control, drove the political sequence is historical materialism applied to its own founding event, and even the tradition's critics conduct their counterargument in its vocabulary, disputing the weights rather than the method. Measured by the third, the banners over the doors have never settled anything, which is the ledger's whole point.

## Keeping the two questions apart in practice

The separation is not only an interpretation of one Russian autumn; it is a portable instrument, and a short section on its use may be the most transferable thing in this article. Confronted with any disputed seizure of power, in any century, the reader's first operation is sorting: take each claim in the account being read and assign it to the mechanism column or the process column. Claims about who planned, who fired, how many stood in the square, which committee signed, belong to the first. Claims about who had ceased to obey, which institutions had changed their allegiance, what grievances had ripened past endurance, belong to the second. The second operation is audit: check whether the account's verdict is drawn from one column while its evidence sits in the other, the signature move of both the coup literature at its weakest and the revolution literature at its most lyrical. The third operation is the figures discipline used throughout this article: numbers that arrive with a camp's fingerprints on them, crowd sizes, casualty counts, percentages, are to be carried as attributed ranges or described qualitatively, never promoted to settled fact by repetition. Readers assembling a study file on this episode, whether for an examination, a seminar or a piece of writing, can keep the rows, camps and figures sorted with the series' companion, the [Marxism study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) tool, which exists for exactly this kind of layered material: claims on one side, evidence on the other, and the separation visible at a glance.

Applied to October, the instrument yields the formulation with which this article is content to close its argument. The seizure of power in Petrograd was executed as a planned minority operation, by a party committee's decision, through detachments numbered in thousands, against resistance so far dissolved that the capital changed hands with limited fighting while Moscow's resistance showed what the operation cost where dissolution had not preceded it. The process that made the operation possible and durable was a social revolution eight months deep, which had transferred the allegiance of the garrisons, the factories, the railways and much of the countryside's patience from the old authorities to new institutions, and which had given the executing party honest majorities in the bodies that commanded the crisis's decisive ground. The same party then dispersed the one institution elected by the whole nation, because its verdict contradicted the mandate it claimed, and that dispersal stands in the record unabsorbed by either of the preceding sentences. Coup in technique, revolution in preparation, and a founding contradiction in the settlement between them: the ledger's three clauses, each evidenced, none permitted to impersonate the whole.

## The geography of the mandate: fleets, fronts and second cities

One refinement of the support question remains, geographical, and it sharpens both camps' best points. Bolshevik strength in the autumn was not spread across Russia like a stain; it was concentrated in an archipelago of institutions whose common feature was organisation under pressure. The Baltic Fleet, based within reach of the capital and radicalised by a year of committees and by the fleet's own defence against the German advance, voted overwhelmingly for the party's resolutions and supplied the sailors who appear at every hinge of the seizure. The armies on the northern and western fronts, closest to the capital and to the land hunger of the peasant infantry, returned Bolshevik pluralities in the assembly elections even as the fronts farther south, and the Cossack hosts, voted otherwise. The Latvian rifle regiments, recruited from a nation ground between German occupation and Russian landlordism, became by common account among the most reliably Bolshevik units in the army, and their later employment as the new state's praetorians is a fact the consolidation's historians weigh heavily. Moscow, the second city, was more contested ground than Petrograd: its soviet had a Bolshevik majority, its garrison was divided, its district of merchants and officials resisted, and the week of fighting there, with artillery in the streets and the Kremlin changing hands, showed what the transfer looked like where the archipelago's control ran thin.

The geography matters because it disciplines the word support into something mappable. The party's mandate was real in the institutions that could act, thin in the territories that could only acquiesce, and the map of the first kind explains the seizure's success while the map of the second explains the civil war's necessity, from the victors' point of view, and its criminality, from the vanquished's. A national plebiscite would have drawn a third map, and did, in November, and the party's answer to that map has been recorded in its own section above. What the geography adds is the reason the November map did not settle the matter on the ground: the assembly's Russia was dispersed across a countryside of villages and a front of dissolving armies, while the seizure's Russia was concentrated at the nodes where coordination lives, the stations, the exchanges, the barracks, the printing houses. Revolutions are not referendums with better costumes; they are contests for the nodes, and October is the century's clearest demonstration that the nodes and the nation can return different verdicts in the same month. Whether that demonstration licenses the victors' theory of representation or indicts it is, once more, the question the reader is now equipped to argue rather than inherit.

## Two minority operations: the general's and the committee's

The year 1917 contains a controlled experiment that historians prize precisely because it is so rarely available: two attempted seizures of power by organised minorities, mounted within eight weeks of each other against the same government, in the same capital, drawing on the same railway network and the same pool of available troops. The general's operation had the forms of a classic coup. It disposed of cavalry divisions, the prestige of the commander in chief, the sympathy of much of the officer corps and propertied Russia, and a government so compromised by negotiation with it that its head had to change his story in public. It collapsed without reaching the city, undone by railwaymen who misrouted its trains, by telegraphists who delayed its orders, by agitators who talked its vanguard regiments out of their saddles, and by its intended victims arming themselves with rifles the government dared not withhold. The committee's operation, eight weeks later, disposed of no cavalry and little prestige beyond the soviet's. It disposed instead of the allegiance, or the neutrality, of every intermediary the general had lacked: the railwaymen were its friends, the telegraph was already countersigned, the garrison was its electorate, and the population of the decisive districts regarded its advance as the defence of their own institutions.

The comparison is the mechanism question's cleanest exhibit, because it isolates the variable. Organisation, daring and ruthlessness were present on both sides in quantities no honest ledger can rank far apart; Kornilov's staff work was not obviously worse than the committee's, and his troops were better soldiers. What differed was the social ground across which the two operations had to move. One moved against the current of every organised constituency in the capital and was dissolved by contact with them; the other moved with the current and could afford to be, in form, almost an administrative act. A coup is usually defined by its smallness, but the experiment of 1917 suggests the more exacting definition the ledger has been building toward: a seizure is a coup in the full sense when it must substitute the organisers' will for an absent or hostile social process, and it is something else, something for which political vocabulary is poorer, when the organisers' will arrives as the instrument of a process that has already decided the old power's fate and is choosing its successor. October belongs to the second category by the evidence of its own year's control experiment, which does not acquit it of the assembly, or of anything else in its rows; it locates, with unusual precision, what kind of act its defenders and its prosecutors are arguing about.

## The argument in Europe: the socialist world's divided verdict

The news from Petrograd split the international socialist movement within weeks, and the split is part of the event's Marxist reading because the judges were the doctrine's own senior custodians. Karl Kautsky, for a generation the nearest thing European Marxism possessed to a pope, condemned the seizure in print as a violation of the stages and of democracy alike, arguing that a minority dictatorship in a backward country would be forced into terror against the peasantry and would discredit the socialist idea for decades; his pamphlet on the dictatorship of the proletariat became the orthodox case against October from inside the faith. Lenin's reply, written in the midst of the civil war he was conducting, returned the compliment with interest, redefining democracy as a class institution and its parliamentary form as a fraud practised on the exploited, and the exchange fixed the poles between which Marxist argument about October has oscillated ever since. What gives the exchange its lasting value for a case study is that both men were applying the same inheritance to the same facts and reaching opposite verdicts by weighting the two questions differently: Kautsky weighted the assembly and the stages, Lenin the soviets and the seizure's success at holding power through its first winter.

Rosa Luxemburg's judgement, written in a German prison cell in the autumn of 1918 and published only after her murder, is the verdict historians return to most, because it refuses both poles with a precision neither matched. She hailed the Bolsheviks' audacity as the revolution's honour, the one act by which the Russian working class had saved the idea of socialism from the treason of the official parties, and in the same manuscript she dismantled their settlement: the dispersal of the assembly, the narrowing of the franchise, the suppression of a free press and of assembly itself, she argued, would not wither into democracy but would strangle the public life on which any soviet system depended, leaving rule by a clique where the dictatorship of a class had been intended, and she insisted that liberty granted only to the government's supporters is no liberty at all, since freedom is real only as the freedom of those who think otherwise. The manuscript's authority in the subsequent literature comes from its position: written by a revolutionary, in prison, without knowledge of the civil war's full course, it separates the seizure from the settlement as cleanly as this article's method separates the questions, and it does so from inside the tradition the seizure claimed. Maxim Gorky, from a different chair, the editor's desk of a Petrograd newspaper, reached a parallel division by a novelist's route, supporting the revolution's necessity while recording, week by week, his fear that an unready people was being subjected to an experiment whose price the experimenters would not be the ones to pay. The European argument matters to the ledger because it establishes that the two-question separation is not a referee's invention imposed a century later. It is the shape the argument took among the participants' own peers while the ink was wet.

## The women in the year of revolutions

The social process has a face that the committee minutes rarely record, and restoring it corrects both camps' iconography. The February revolution began, on the day the old calendar kept as International Women's Day, with women textile workers of the Vyborg district coming out on strike for bread and against the war, drawing the metalworkers after them and the city behind them; the autocracy's last week was opened by the constituency its historians were slowest to see. Through the year that followed, the soldiers' wives, the soldatki, formed a constituency with a politics of their own, organising for the allowances the state owed them, confronting officials over bread and fuel, and carrying into the queues and the district meetings a grievance that was simultaneously economic and a verdict on the war that had taken their households' earners. Women workers, whose share of the industrial workforce climbed as conscription emptied the benches, struck, organised and sat in the factory committees whose resolutions fill the revisionists' footnotes, though the delegations and the platforms remained disproportionately male. None of this appears in the mechanism's account of the seizure, whose committees were almost entirely men deciding in rooms; all of it appears in the social account, since the crowds that made February, the queues that unmade the government's credit and the households that judged every authority by bread were to a great extent women. A reading that assigns the process to the population must be able to say which population, and the honest answer for 1917 includes the half of it that the seizure's own iconography, sailors, soldiers, commissars, systematically cropped out of the frame.

## Petrograd's winter: what the seizure inherited

The morning after a revolution belongs neither to the coup narrative nor to the epic, and the winter that followed the seizure is the case study's final piece of social evidence, because it shows the process and the mechanism under load together. The capital the committee had taken was a city in material collapse: fuel stocks measured in days, flour in sacks rather than trains, a transport system shedding locomotives faster than workshops could return them, typhus in the tenements, and a population that had begun the long evacuation toward the villages and their potatoes. The new authority's first winter was spent on the unglamorous arithmetic of survival, requisitioning, rationing, bargaining with the railwaymen whose ultimatum it had outfaced, and watching the city's population drain away in a haemorrhage that would halve it within a few years. Historians who stress the seizure's ease sometimes write as though power, once taken in the capital, were a possession; the winter record shows it as a liability that had to be serviced daily in bread and coal, and the allegiance the new power retained through that winter, in the institutions that had made it, is among the social reading's harder data. The same record equally serves the critics: emergency bred emergency government, committees for supply acquired coercive habits, and the distance between the Commune programme of the summer's pamphlet and the practice of a besieged food dictatorship widened in the cold. Both data points sit in their proper columns. The winter proves the mandate had depth, and it proves the mandate's exercise was already costing the forms in whose name it had been claimed.

## The soldiers' parliament: how an army learned to deliberate

No institution of 1917 rewards study like the army, because the army was simultaneously the old regime's last instrument, the revolution's largest electorate and the social process's clearest measuring gauge. The Petrograd Soviet's first order, issued in the revolution's opening days and addressed to the garrison, had established the principle that remade military life: elected committees in every unit, control of weapons placed in the committees' hands, and obedience to the government's military orders conditional on their not contradicting the soviet's. What began as a safeguard against counterrevolution became, across eight months, a school of politics attended by millions. Regimental and divisional committees debated orders, audited officers, published newspapers, sent delegates to congresses and returned verdicts on the war that had the force of weather. Officers who retained their men's confidence survived by persuasion; those who relied on the old discipline found themselves isolated, occasionally arrested by their own soldiers, in the worst cases murdered in the settling of scores that every honest account of the year must register among its costs.

The front's political temperature can be read in the fate of the June offensive, the year's great experiment in whether exhortation could substitute for belief. The offensive was prepared with democratic ceremony, committees consulted, agitators deployed, the minister himself touring the trenches; and after initial advances it dissolved as units refused to relieve the first wave, reserves declined to move up, and whole regiments debated their orders while the enemy counterattacked. The lesson the army drew was not that it had been infiltrated, though agitators were present in plenty, but that the war's contract had expired: men who had enlisted, or been taken, to defend a tsar and a homeland could not be got to die for a coalition's diplomatic credit in Galicia. From the summer onward the committees' resolutions tracked the soviet pattern with a lag of weeks, peace and land and power to the soviets rising through the army's own representative machinery, and the assembly elections in November, where the northern and western fronts returned the pluralities recorded earlier, merely counted what the committees had already decided. For the two questions, the army is the bridge institution: it belongs to the mechanism because its obedience was the seizure's precondition and its countersignature the instrument, and it belongs to the process because its deliberation was the social revolution conducted in uniform, by the largest body of armed citizens the empire contained. A coup does not usually require the prior conversion of the army into a debating society that votes for the plotters. October did, and the conversion is documented in the army's own paper trail, resolution by resolution, in an archive no camp has succeeded in talking away.

## What counts as a revolution: the definitional scholarship

Part of the quarrel's longevity is a defect of vocabulary that comparative scholarship has tried, with partial success, to repair. Political vocabulary offers coup, putsch, rebellion, insurrection and revolution as though they were points on one scale of size, when the serious literature treats them as answers to different questions. Charles Tilly, the sociologist of collective action whose career was spent on exactly this taxonomy, taught his readers to separate the revolutionary situation, a condition of divided sovereignty in which rival blocs claim the same polity, from the revolutionary outcome, a durable transfer and transformation of power; by that grid Russia lived in a revolutionary situation from February onward, whatever one calls the October night, and the night's significance is that it decided which bloc would convert situation into outcome. Theda Skocpol, in the most influential comparative study of the century's second half, defined social revolutions as rapid, basic transformations of a society's state and class structures, carried through in part by class-based revolts from below, and built her account of France, Russia and China on the claim that such transformations are not made by vanguards executing designs but come through the collapse of old regimes under pressures their rulers cannot manage. On Skocpol's criteria, applied by a scholar with no brief for Bolshevism, the Russian sequence from 1917 through the civil war's settlement is a paradigmatic social revolution, and the October seizure figures in her analysis as an episode inside a structural crisis rather than as its author.

The definitional literature does not settle the argument of this article, and a case study should say plainly what it does instead. It relocates the argument. If revolution names the transformation of structures across years, then asking whether the night of 25 October was a revolution is a category error, and the traditionalists' coup and the revisionists' revolution pass each other without contact, one describing an episode, the other a process. If revolution names the seizure itself, then the inquiry collapses into the mechanism column and the social evidence becomes decoration. The two-question separation is, in effect, the definitional scholarship translated into a reading discipline, with the added clause the comparativists sometimes omit: the episode and the process are not merely different topics but morally and politically connected ones, since the episode's makers claimed the process's authority and spent it, among other places, at the Tauride Palace in January. Holding the categories apart is what allows the connection to be examined rather than assumed, by either side, in either direction.

## The memoir wars: how the victors rewrote the seizure

The last historiographical layer is the one the victors laid over the event themselves, and it belongs in a complete case study because so much of the received image of October, heroic and demonic alike, descends from it. The new state institutionalised its memory early, founding a commission for the history of the revolution and the party whose journals collected the participants' accounts while the participants were still arguing about them; the anniversary dramatisation that manufactured the storming belonged to the same project, memory as a state industry. Within a decade the succession struggle converted that industry into a weapon: as Trotsky was driven from power and then from the country, the official narrative of October was revised around him, his direction of the insurrection first minimised and then reassigned, until the standard history of the party published under Stalin's authority in the late nineteen thirties presented a seizure planned and led by a duo in which the exile's name could not appear as its organiser, and photographs and paintings were altered to match the text. Sukhanov's great hostile memoir, unanswerable in its detail, was withdrawn from Soviet circulation; Reed's partisan epic was canonised, its author conveniently dead and buried by the Kremlin wall before he could complicate it. Western scholarship of the traditional school grew up reading against this falsified record and sometimes overcorrecting toward the assumption that everything in it was false; the revisionists' archive work was, among other things, a recovery of the documents the memory industry had buried under its own commemorations. The reader of any dramatic account of October, including the famous ones quoted in schoolbooks, does well to ask the archival question first: through whose commemoration did this scene survive, and what did the commemoration need it to prove? The question does not discredit the scenes. It returns them to the evidence column, where the ledger can use them.

## The year in the villages: the peasant war beneath the politics

For four fifths of the population the revolution was not about soviets or assemblies at all, and a case study that confined itself to the capitals would misdescribe the social process at its widest. The Russian village entered 1917 with a land hunger generations old, temporarily schooled by the emancipation settlement, by the disturbances of 1905 and by wartime requisitioning into a single practical doctrine: the land of the gentry, the crown and the church was held unjustly, the community that worked it was its rightful owner, and the moment the state's police looked away, the doctrine would be enacted. February removed the police. Through the spring the villages tested the new authorities with petitions and committees; through the summer, as the government's land committees multiplied forms while the estates remained intact, the testing turned to direct action, timber cut, meadows mown, inventories seized, in the autumn whole estates taken over by commune decision, their owners expelled, their buildings occasionally burned, their land folded into the village's repartition. The movement had its own discipline and its own violence, proceeded district by district on news carried by returning soldiers, and answered to no party headquarters, though the Socialist Revolutionaries' local networks were its nearest political kin and the Bolsheviks its noisiest champions at a distance.

The villages' politics confound both camps' simpler stories, which is why the social historians who specialised in the countryside, Orlando Figes prominent among them, changed the field's centre of gravity. Against the traditional reading, the land seizures demonstrate that the revolution's social transformation was not a Petrograd performance: the most consequential redistribution of property in the country's history was executed by the peasantry itself, on its own authority, before the Bolsheviks had the power to order it and in defiance of every government that asked it to wait. Against any romantic revisionism, the same record shows a countryside whose revolution was local, patriarchal and profoundly suspicious of all cities and all parties, Bolshevik collectors included, a countryside that voted for its hereditary party in November, accepted the land decree as a receipt for what it had already taken, and would spend the civil war defending the receipt against whichever army, Red or White, threatened to annul it or to tax it beyond endurance. The peasants made their revolution and guarded it jealously against its supposed makers, a fact that explains, better than any ideological formulation, why the state built on October spent its first decade in an armed negotiation with its own rural majority. For the ledger the villages supply the social question's largest single mass of evidence and its sharpest limit at once: a process genuinely revolutionary in depth, whose beneficiaries declined to enlist under the banner of the party that claimed to have liberated them.

The soldier-peasant in the trench was the hinge between this world and the capital's, and his double identity explains the year's transmission mechanism. He received the village's news in letters read aloud in the dugouts, understood that the division of the estates would not wait for the war's end, and drew the conclusion the committees recorded: every month at the front was a hectare lost at home. Desertion, on this reading, was the land revolution recruiting its workforce, and the peace demand that carried the Bolshevik resolutions through the garrisons was, in the ranks' own understanding, a land demand wearing a uniform. Historians of the front have documented the traffic in both directions, the committees' mandates carrying village instructions into the army's politics and the returning deserters carrying the army's slogans into the village assemblies. By the autumn the two processes, rural seizure and military dissolution, were a single circulation, and the party that stood at its pumping stations, promising peace in the capital and land in the decree, was drawing on a pressure no proclamation had created. This is the deepest stratum the second question reaches, and any account of October that cannot accommodate a peasant with a rifle calculating his inheritance has not yet begun to explain why the old Russia could not be reassembled.

## The insurrection debate: how the decision was actually taken

The mechanism's inner history deserves a section of its own, because the picture of a leadership serenely executing a plan dissolves, at close range, into the most argumentative fortnight in the party's life. Lenin, in hiding outside the capital, opened the campaign with letters to the central committee in mid September arguing that the moment had matured: the soviets of the two capitals were won, the peasant movement was rising, the government was preparing, in his reading, to surrender Petrograd to the Germans and strangle the revolution piecemeal, and a party that declined power now would forfeit it for good. His formula for the operation, insurrection treated as an art rather than a conspiracy, insisted on the distinction this article has laboured: reliance on a rising class and a turning point in the revolution's history, mass support at the decisive points, a government already reeling, conditions enumerated like a staff officer's appreciation and wholly unlike a plotter's whisper. The central committee's first response was to vote, in effect, to burn the letter: a majority judged its circulation dangerous, and the party's leading bodies spent weeks doing everything except what it demanded, preparing a congress of soviets at which the question of power would be decided by politics rather than by arms.

Through early October the pressure from hiding intensified, memoranda arriving by courier, until Lenin returned in disguise to the capital and forced the question at a meeting of the central committee on 10 October by the old calendar, held in a sympathiser's flat with the hostess's name day as cover. The vote for insurrection as the order of the day passed by ten votes to two, the two being Zinoviev and Kamenev, whose objections were theoretical as much as tactical: the party lacked the technical apparatus to run the state, the country had not ripened, the assembly elections would expose the minority, and an isolated Russian revolution would be crushed or corrupted. A second, enlarged meeting a week later reaffirmed the decision against the same dissent, and the dissenters then committed the act that still astonishes students of party discipline, publishing their disagreement in a non-party newspaper days before the event, in the hope of mobilising the wider movement against the decision. Lenin demanded their expulsion as strikebreakers of the revolution. Nothing was expelled; the machine was too busy; and the seizure proceeded under the congress's colours rather than by the naked committee decision the vote had authorised, because the men running the Military Revolutionary Committee judged the soviet envelope indispensable, a judgement the dissenters' warning had, in its way, reinforced.

The debate matters to the ledger for three reasons, each assignable to its column. For the mechanism, it establishes the operation's true chain of command: not a plan unrolling from February but a decision contested to the last week, carried by one man's relentless advocacy against his own organisation's caution, and executed through an improvised detour, the defensive committee, that its executors preferred to the decision's literal form. For the social process, it establishes that the party's caution was not cowardice but an accurate reading of the same evidence this article has assembled: the dissenters' catalogue of weaknesses, the countryside, the apparatus, the isolation, was factually strong, and their error, if it was one, lay in weighting the columns differently, exactly the operation the reader has been practising throughout. For the theoretical question, the debate is the stage theory's last stand inside the party of power: the two men who quoted the timetable most faithfully lost the vote, and the man who had revised the timetable in April won it, a sequence that locates October's relation to orthodox Marxism more precisely than any later manifesto. The revolution's makers disagreed about whether they were permitted to make it. That disagreement, conducted in writing and preserved, is among the strongest reasons the event rewards study as a case rather than as a legend.

## The Provisional Government's chain of missed moments

A case study of a seizure owes its reader an account of the seized-from, and the Provisional Government's eight months repay the attention because its failure was not a single blunder but a chain of decisions each defensible in the week it was taken. The founding decision was the war: bound by alliance obligations, by loans, by the conviction that a separate peace would deliver Russia to Germany and disgrace the revolution, the government committed itself to the allied cause and, in June, to an offensive meant to prove the commitment. Each element of that policy was arguable; its compound was fatal, since the army's disintegration meant the policy's costs were collected from the very population whose patience the government needed for everything else. The second decision was postponement as a system: land reform, labour law, the national questions, the constitution itself, all deferred to the Constituent Assembly, and the assembly's elections themselves deferred, from the summer to September and then to November, on administrative grounds that were genuine and politically ruinous. A government that will decide nothing until a body meets, and then delays the body, teaches its citizens to decide for themselves, and the factories, regiments and villages took the lesson at the speed the record shows.

The third strand was the search for legitimacy through ever wider consultation that never reached decision: a Moscow state conference in August that heard every estate of the realm and settled nothing, a democratic conference in September that voted ambiguously on coalition and produced a preparliament whose authority nobody, including its members, could state. Kerensky's personal trajectory through these months, from the revolution's tribune to a premier governing by adjournment, has attracted biographers' severity, but the structural reading the social historians prefer is kinder and more damning at once: the coalition's formula, bourgeois ministers for competence, socialist ministers for legitimacy, all questions postponed, had exhausted its logic by the summer, and no occupant of the premier's chair could have made postponement govern a country whose army was deserting and whose villages were dividing the land. Into the vacuum stepped the one institution with a contrary formula, decide now and ratify after, and the two-question separation records the succession without needing to admire it: the government fell undefended in October because it had spent eight months declining to be defended on any ground its defenders could still believe.

## The moderate socialists' own case, stated as they made it

The defeated parties of 1917 are usually ventriloquised by the camps that outlived them, liberals adopting their electoral argument, revolutionaries adopting their obituary. Their own case, made in real time by men who knew the soviets from inside, deserves a section in its strongest form because it is the only critique of October that proceeds from within the revolution's own institutions. Irakli Tsereteli, the Menshevik strategist who dominated the soviet's first months, held that the revolution's survival depended on the alliance of the democracy with the propertied classes willing to accept it, since the alternative, a workers' government in a peasant country at war, must end in civil war, famine and a dictatorship forced to devour the democracy that bore it. The Mensheviks' economists added the materialist clause: Russia's productive forces could not sustain socialist construction, so a soviet government would distribute poverty, requisition the peasantry into hostility, and collapse into either reaction or a bureaucratic tyranny standing over a ruined economy. Martov, the conscience of the tendency, added the internationalist clause: sealed inside one backward country, the revolution would be deformed by its isolation into shapes its makers abhorred, and the duty of socialists was to refuse the gamble and preserve the movement for a European hour. The Socialist Revolutionaries, through Chernov's ministry and the party's endless internal arbitration between its wings, added the agrarian clause: the land socialisation the villages demanded required a constituent settlement to be lawful and durable, and a party of the capital's barracks that seized the question by decree would build the countryside's future on a foundation the countryside had not chosen in that form.

History's verdict on these arguments is the strangest entry in the entire ledger, because so much of their content was vindicated and their authors were destroyed anyway. Civil war came; famine came; the peasantry was requisitioned into hostility; isolation deformed the revolution's institutions in directions Martov's clause had specified with uncomfortable precision. And the men who had foreseen it lost everything, because foresight without an answer to the year's immediate questions, peace, land, bread, power, was a luxury the institutions of the crisis could not afford to reward. The moderates' tragedy, in the social historians' sympathetic reconstructions and in their own memoirs alike, is that their strategy required time the war had abolished: the coalition needed a quiet year to reach the assembly, and the year it was given contained a collapsing front, a Kornilov, and an opponent willing to decide. A reader applying the two questions will notice that the moderates' case is overwhelmingly a process argument, correct about the country's depths and the sequel's logic, joined to a mechanism they never possessed, since at no point after the spring could they have held the capital against a determined minority, their own included. October's winners held the reverse portfolio, a mechanism of genius joined to a process they had read correctly in the cities and misjudged, or accepted the misjudgement of, in the countryside and the long run. The ledger does not declare a winner between those portfolios. It exhibits them, costed, and leaves the reader with the year's hardest residue: the party that understood the situation's depths could not act, the party that could act had discounted the depths, and Russia paid the difference over the following generation, a payment whose narration belongs, as promised, to the articles that follow this one.

## The press and the word: how the argument travelled

Between the committee room and the regiment stood a medium both camps sometimes forget to count among the year's forces: print, and behind print, the spoken word of thousands of agitators. The revolution had unchained the press in February, and by the summer the capital supported a blizzard of newspapers, party sheets, soldiers' papers printed at the front, factory bulletins and wall posters, in a country where perhaps two adults in five could read comfortably and where reading was therefore often a collective act, one literate soldier with a newspaper holding a barracks the way a preacher holds a congregation. The Bolsheviks invested in this medium with a strategist's respect: their central paper, suppressed in July and resurrected under successive thin disguises, reached print runs that historians put in the high tens of thousands on ordinary days, and their military organisation's soldiers' paper spoke to the garrison in its own idiom. Rival parties had larger inheritances and famous names; what they increasingly lacked, as the autumn advanced, was a message that fit on the paper's front page without embarrassment.

The slogans deserve their reputation as the year's compressed theory. Peace, land and bread, in the form the crowds carried, was not a programme but a sorting device: it named the three grievances that the social process had made sovereign, and it allowed a soldier, a seamstress and a peasant delegate to discover, in three words, that they were voting about the same government. All power to the soviets performed the second operation, directing the sorted grievance at the institution the grievants themselves inhabited, and its genius, or its menace, depending on the column, was that it could be meant sincerely by a delegate who wanted his committee to rule and used instrumentally by a leadership that intended to rule through the committee's mandate. Historians of language in the revolution have traced how phrases migrated from leaflets into resolutions and from resolutions into the demands men shouted at ministers, a circulation in which the party was often the amplifier rather than the source. For the mechanism column, the press network was an instrument of coordination no rival matched. For the process column, the content the network carried was overwhelmingly generated below it. The separation holds even here, at the level of the sentence.

## A note on dates, names and numbers in this article

Reference discipline requires a brief accounting of the conventions used throughout, since the episode's facts arrive unusually entangled in its politics. Dates are given in the form the narrative requires, with the calendar named wherever confusion is possible; the seizure's night is 25 October by the Julian calendar then in force in Russia and 7 November by the Gregorian calendar used in the West, the assembly met in January of the following year on the same dual reckoning, and the new state's adoption of the Gregorian calendar weeks later does not retrospectively rename the events. Names follow the spellings most common in the English-language scholarship, with the understanding that every transliteration from the Cyrillic is a small compromise. Numbers are handled under the protocol the brief for this article imposed and the evidence justifies. Vote shares for the assembly elections are stated as approximate ranges of the kind the standard tabulations support, because the surviving returns are incomplete, the lists were stale in the manner described, and different scholarly reconstructions distribute the fractions differently at the margins. Crowds, memberships and print runs are attributed or described in orders of magnitude rather than false precision. Casualties of the seizure in the capital are described qualitatively as limited, because the sources' small numbers disagree and the honest statement is the qualitative one; the Moscow fighting is recorded as costing lives in the hundreds by most estimates, a range the literature sustains. Figures for the civil war and the terror that followed the seizure do not appear in this article at all, by design: they belong to the treatments of the regime, where the protocol for them is set by those articles' briefs, and introducing them here would smuggle a verdict about the sequel into a case study of the seizure. A reader who finds a number in these pages stated flatly, without a range, a source or a hedge, is invited to distrust it on the article's own principles and to check it against the camps' literatures, where it will certainly be found stated flatly, in opposite directions, by both.

## The paperwork of power: telephones, trains and the choreography of a transfer

The mechanism question rewards a descent to the level of wiring, because states in crisis are taken, in the first instance, through their communications. The Petrograd of 1917 ran on a nervous system of telegraph lines, telephone exchanges, railway signalling and printed proclamations, and the committee's operational art consisted of seizing the nodes of that system in an order that prevented the government from coordinating a response faster than the seizures accumulated. The telegraph agency went early, so that the news leaving the capital would be the committee's news; the telephone exchange followed, so that ministries could no longer speak to one another except through operators who had accepted the new authority; the railway stations and the bridges completed the circuit, controlling who could enter the city's conversation and who could bring force to it. Participants' memoirs return repeatedly to the eerie normality of the machinery: clerks reporting for duty, operators connecting calls, trains running to timetables drawn up by men who were, in the same hour, dismantling the state the timetables served. The revolution at the level of the node was a transfer of obedience among people who operated telephones for a living, and their compliance, unforced in most cases, is the social process visible at the resolution of a switchboard.

The operation against the Winter Palace, the transfer's ceremonial remainder, was directed on the ground by Vladimir Antonov-Ovseenko, a former tsarist officer turned revolutionary whose own later fate, arrest and execution in the purges of the nineteen thirties, belongs to the sequel's grim ledgers. His problem on the night was less military than theatrical: the palace had to fall visibly enough to close the question of who ruled, yet the government inside it was guarded by forces, cadets, a women's battalion, Cossack detachments that mostly melted away, whose destruction would have cost the victors the appearance of effortless succession on which their claim partly rested. The solution was the protracted, untidy performance the record shows: ultimatums delivered and expired, the fortress across the river firing a limited number of live rounds after the blank signal, small parties entering by doors the defenders had not thought to bar, the ministers discovered at their table and arrested in an atmosphere less of storm than of eviction. The congress, informed in the small hours that the palace had fallen, received the news as ratification's punctuation. The ministers were taken to the fortress and released in due course unharmed, a clemency the night permitted because its outcome was not in doubt, and the contrast with the treatment of prisoners in the years that followed has been noted by every camp that writes about the sequel.

Around these events ran the transfer's least visible and most consequential traffic: paper. The committee's mandates, printed and pasted across the city, derived their force from a formula, in the name of the soviet, that clerks and sentries had already learned to honour during the Kornilov defence; the government's counter-proclamations, printed in haste and distributed by cadets, arrived bearing the signature of an authority its own ministries were ceasing to obey. Printers' politics decided editions: the Bolshevik papers the government closed on the morning of the 24th were reopened by armed workers within the day, their presses guarded by the same detachments that held the bridges, and the issue that appeared carried the committee's proclamation of the transfer before the transfer was complete, a performative document whose truth its distribution helped to manufacture. The new government's first decrees were set in type the same night and read to the congress from proof sheets. A reader who wants to understand how a few thousand organised people take an empire's capital should study this traffic as attentively as any barricade: sovereignty in a modern state is substantially the expectation that certain signatures will be obeyed, and October was, among everything else it was, the week that expectation changed its address in Petrograd, one countersignature at a time.

The provinces received the transfer through the same wires, and their answers, arriving over the following days and weeks, drew the true map of the seizure's extent with a precision no proclamation could impose. City after city wired its soviet's recognition or its resistance; garrisons declared for the congress or for the assembly or for nobody; the railway network's nodes transmitted power outward from the capital at the speed of telegraphy and no faster, so that the revolution's geography for its first month was literally the map of its communications, islands of recognition in a countryside that the wires crossed without converting. Moscow's week of fighting was the largest of the exceptions; elsewhere the pattern repeated the capital's in miniature, committees and countersignatures, or dissolved into local arrangements that the centre would spend years renegotiating. The mechanism, studied at this resolution, looks less like a blow struck at a single point than like a signal propagated through a network whose relays had been prepared, node by node, by the year's institutional history. That is the most precise sentence the mechanism column affords, and it is also, read again, a sentence about the social process, about who had spent the year inside the relay stations learning which messages to pass. The two questions meet in the wiring, as they meet everywhere in this event once the reader looks closely enough, and the discipline of keeping them apart is what allows their meeting to be seen rather than merely asserted.

## What the victors expected: the seizure's own horizon

The last piece of the case the reader needs is the horizon inside the actors' own heads, because October looks different depending on how long its makers expected it to stand alone. The evidence of their speeches, letters and decrees agrees on the essential point: almost nobody in the Smolny that October expected, or planned for, a self-sufficient socialist state constructing itself in one backward country across decades. The expectation was of a beginning whose completion lay abroad. The Russian revolution was the breach; the German and European revolutions, confidently anticipated as the war ground its peoples down, would supply the advanced industry, the skilled proletariat and the world-market setting that Russian socialism lacked; and the new government's first foreign-policy acts, the peace decree addressed over the governments' heads to the peoples, the publication of the secret treaties, were calculated as detonators for that expected sequence. Lenin's own statements in the first months repeatedly set the Russian beginning's survival conditional on the European continuation, and the party's theorists argued in print about the forms a linked revolution would take with the unselfconsciousness of men discussing weather they could see approaching.

Read against that horizon, several features of the seizure that baffle later readers fall into place. The insouciance about the state apparatus, the willingness to proclaim a commune programme with no machinery for it, the land decree's borrowing of the rival party's platform, the peace at almost any price: each was the act of men who believed the interval before the European rescue would be short, and that the Russian government's task in the interval was to hold the breach and advertise it. When the rescue failed to arrive on schedule, when Germany's revolution came and was contained within a bourgeois republic, when the Hungarian and Bavarian soviet episodes were crushed, the interval stretched into a condition, and the government improvised its way from bridgehead to fortress, generating in the improvisation the institutions and doctrines, the one-party state, the theory of socialism in one country eventually formulated by Lenin's successors in their own succession struggle, that the founding horizon had never contained. The dispersal of the assembly sits inside this horizon too: men who expected a European soviet congress system within the year could treat a Russian parliament as a vestige of the interval's first week, and the casualness of that treatment is part of what the strongest objection prosecutes. None of this is offered as mitigation; the ledger's rows are unaffected by the actors' expectations, and a dispersal is a dispersal whatever the calendar in the dispersers' heads. It is offered because a Marxist reading that ignores the expected horizon cannot explain why intelligent men made the choices they did, and explanation, even of the indefensible, is the case study's first duty. The seizure was the opening move of a world revolution that did not take place. Everything about its sequel, including the arguments still running a century later, is conditioned by that non-arrival, and the reader who holds the fact steadily will find the camps' literatures rearranging themselves around it: the traditionalists prosecuting an act whose authors expected it to be overtaken, the revisionists defending a movement whose horizon vanished, and the evidence column recording, as always, what the documents show.

## The night the city went to the theatre

Every witness of the seizure, whatever their allegiance, reaches sooner or later for the same disconcerting detail: while power changed hands, Petrograd amused itself. Theatres played to full houses on the evening of the 25th; the ballet performed; restaurants and cinemas did their ordinary trade; trams ran; couples promenaded on the Nevsky within earshot of the occasional rifle shot and treated the sound as weather. Sukhanov, hurrying between the soviet and the streets, recorded the incongruity with a memoirist's delight, and Reed's narrative pauses on the same contrast, the revolution proceeding in one set of rooms while the city, in the adjoining set, declined to attend it. On the following morning the capital woke to find itself governed by a committee it had not elected, announced in posters and newspapers, and the bulk of its inhabitants responded with the shrug that the traditionalists have quoted ever since: no rising in defence of the fallen, no festival for the victors, a population reserving its energy for the queues.

The camps have fought over the shrug as energetically as over any document of the year. For the coup reading, the theatre audience is the perfect emblem: a nation absent from its own alleged revolution, power transferred in committee rooms while the people watched a ballet, the very definition of an event that happened to a population rather than through one. For the social reading, the same evening proves the opposite with equal neatness: a seizure that requires no mobilisation because the mobilisation already happened, across eight months, in the institutions that did the deciding; the city's calm is what a completed transfer of allegiance looks like, the old power so thoroughly vacated that its removal disturbed nobody's evening, the new one so expected that its arrival read as a formality. Both readings draw a true feature of the scene into a total explanation, and the separation does its usual work: the normality of the night is a fact about the mechanism's final hours, which were indeed quiet, administrative and unrepresentative of the year; converted into a fact about the year itself, it becomes false, since the year had contained crowds enough for a dozen revolutions, in February, in April, in July, and at every soviet election that mattered. The theatres were full on the night because the drama had already been performed, in instalments, in the factories and barracks and villages, and what remained for October was the signing. Whether a signature so preceded deserves the name of the play is, for the final time, the question this article exists to keep open and legible.

## The empty treasury: the material state the victors inherited

One stratum of the crisis is quoted less often than the others because it lacks a human face, and it conditioned everything the new power attempted: the state's finances had collapsed before its politics did. The war had been financed by borrowing abroad, by taxation that never caught expenditure, and increasingly by the printing press, so that by the autumn of 1917 the paper rouble had lost most of its prewar value and the Provisional Government's own hastily printed notes circulated at a discount under a nickname taken from the premier's name. Prices in the capital had multiplied across the year; wages chased them and lost; the bread queue was, among other things, a monetary phenomenon, since grain existed in the countryside that would not move toward a currency its holders despised at prices the state had fixed below the cost of transport. The treasury the committee inherited contained, in usable terms, almost nothing: the state bank's officials refused the new government its own funds for a fortnight, foreign credit was extinguished by the repudiation quarrels to come, and the gold reserve sat in vaults whose keys were a more honest symbol of sovereignty than any decree.

The fiscal collapse contributes to both questions in ways the camps have underused. For the process column, it explains the tempo of the old order's desertion better than any pamphlet: officials, officers and suppliers do not remain loyal to an authority that pays them in depreciating paper for goods it cannot deliver, and the Provisional Government's authority evaporated partly along its supply lines, in ministries where salaries bought nothing and orders arrived without the means of execution. For the mechanism column, it reframes the seizure's famous ease: the committee did not so much storm a functioning fiscal state as occupy its address, and the struggle of the following winter, the desperate expedients, the requisitions, the printing presses now turned to the revolution's own notes, was the sound of the new authority discovering that the address was the least of the inheritance. Historians of the economy who work this stratum tend to write about October with a distinctive dry sympathy for everyone involved in it, since the books they keep show no government of 1917, of any colour, possessed of the means to honour the promises its survival required. That dryness is a useful corrective to the moral literatures of both camps, and a final demonstration of the case study's method: some of the year's outcomes were chosen in committee rooms, and some were arithmetic, and the ledger that cannot tell the difference will misjudge the men in the rooms in both directions.

## The organiser's fortnight: Trotsky and the soviet envelope

The seizure's operational signature belongs neither to Lenin, who supplied the decision, nor to the committee's rank and file, who supplied the detachments, but to the man who fused the decision to the institution: Trotsky, chairman of the Petrograd Soviet from September, lately returned from American exile by way of a British detention camp, and the revolution's most effective public voice in its decisive weeks. His contribution was a doctrine of timing and dress. Where Lenin's letters demanded the rising as an act of the party, openly and at once, the chairman of the soviet constructed the slower approach this article has described: the defence committee, the countersignature, the confrontation provoked by the government's own moves, the transfer consummated under the congress's authority on the day the congress met. The difference was not cosmetic. It determined who obeyed the orders, since regiments that would have mutinied against a party summons in October marched willingly under a soviet mandate framed as the revolution's self-defence, and it determined how the event could be narrated afterward by everyone, including its enemies, since a transfer ratified in a congress hall resists the vocabulary of the barracks-room plot. When the moderate delegates walked out of that hall, it was Trotsky who answered them from the platform with the consigning phrase that attached itself to the walkout in every history since, the promise that their road led to history's rubbish heap; the cruelty of the phrase, from the pen of a man who would himself be written out of the revolution's official portrait within the decade, has not escaped the literature's notice. The organiser's fortnight is the mechanism column's most personal entry: an insurrection whose form was one man's solution to the problem of legitimacy, adopted because the party's leader, for all his insistence, could not himself stand in the soviet's chair and lend the operation its envelope.

## The sealed train and the German gold: a charge examined

No case study of the seizure is complete without weighing the accusation that shadowed it from the first weeks: that the Bolsheviks were Germany's instrument, bought with Berlin's money to take Russia out of the war. The documented kernel is real and was never secret for long. Lenin's return across Germany in the famous sealed train was a German facilitation, granted because Berlin expected, correctly, that his agitation would weaken Russia's war effort; the train's sealed status was itself a legalism, and the passengers' contacts at the journey's stops were argued over at the time. Beyond the kernel, the charge grows less solid the closer it is examined. The Provisional Government's own investigators in the summer assembled a case that collapsed for want of evidence; the celebrated documents later published in America purporting to prove that the party's leaders were paid German agents, the Sisson papers, were dismantled by subsequent scholarship and are generally treated as forgeries; and German records, when they became available, showed a foreign ministry scattering subsidies among many anti-war and separatist currents in Russia, of a scale historians continue to dispute and none has shown to have purchased the soviet majorities of the autumn. The party's enemies needed the charge because it converted a political defeat into a police matter; its endurance in popular memory owes more to that convenience than to the archives. The ledger therefore enters the facilitation as established, the funding as unproven at any decisive scale, and the inference from either to the character of the revolution as a move the evidence column does not license, since garrisons and villages did not vote as they did for Berlin's reasons but for their own.

## For students and exam candidates

This is a set topic at every level, and examiners reward the distinction this article is built on: separate the seizure from the process before judging either. A strong answer states the mechanism plainly, a planned operation by a party committee executed through a soviet defence committee with limited fighting in the capital, and then states the process plainly, eight months in which soviets, garrisons, factory committees and villages transferred their allegiance away from the Provisional Government. Candidates lose marks in predictable places: by repeating the storming scene as fact, by claiming the Bolsheviks had no support or a national majority, when the documents refute both, and by omitting the Constituent Assembly, whose election results and dispersal are the evidence markers most want to see handled. Quote the ranges rather than inventing percentages, name at least one historian from each camp, and let your conclusion be as asymmetrical as the evidence: a defensible verdict names the stratum it is judging.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The episode teaches best as a dispute with documents attached. Set the ledger exercise directly: give the seminar the five contested claims and require each group to argue one column from sources before the evidence column is revealed, then let the class discover how much of the fourth column both sides must accept. The calendar puzzle opens the first session painlessly and smuggles in the lesson that even dates arrive packaged. Sukhanov against Reed, assigned in parallel extracts, teaches source criticism better than any lecture on bias, because the two men watched the same night and wrote different events. Handle the assembly with particular care in the room: students arrive with the dispersal either as their whole indictment or as a detail they have been taught to skip, and the teaching task is to return it to full size inside a narrative that also contains the soviets' votes. The counterfactuals listed in this article make reliable essay titles, provided the marking scheme rewards the labelling of counterfactual as instrument rather than as finding.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

For researchers, the live frontier is no longer the capital's night but the connective tissue around it: the provincial archives that complicate the two-capital story, the electoral geography at district level, the army's committee records read as a continuous plebiscite, and the borderlands treated as revolutions in their own right rather than as echoes. The post-archival literature has settled much of the texture and left the weights open, which makes the field a good one for work that quantifies carefully: mandates, turnovers of delegates, the timing of land seizures against the timing of resolutions. For historians writing synthesis, the discipline this article practises is offered as a transferable protocol: separate mechanism from process, state the electoral facts early and whole, carry contested numbers as attributed ranges, and let no camp's vocabulary do unpaid work in your own sentences. For journalists, the usable yield is a single accurate sentence and the knowledge of what it costs: October was a planned seizure of power by a party that had won the institutions where the crisis was decided and then dispersed the institution the whole nation had elected. Any shorter version sacrifices one of the clauses that makes it true, and the anniversary coverage that recycles either the storming or the sneer can be corrected, on the record, from the ledger set out above.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the October Revolution?

The transfer of power in Russia in the autumn of 1917, when the Bolshevik party, acting through the Petrograd Soviet's Military Revolutionary Committee, displaced the Provisional Government on the night of 25 October by the old calendar and had the transfer ratified by the Second Congress of Soviets. It was the second act of the revolutionary year that had begun with the monarchy's fall in February, and it opened the period of soviet government whose consolidation, civil war and settlement fill the years that followed. Its character, coup or revolution, is the disputed question this article separates into the mechanism of the seizure and the social process that carried it.

### Q: Was the October Revolution a coup?

In its mechanics, substantially yes: a party committee decided the rising, a small organised force executed it against a government whose defenders had melted away, and the capital changed hands with limited fighting. In its preconditions, the description is incomplete, because the executing party had spent the preceding months winning open majorities in the soviets of the two capitals and commanding the allegiance of the garrison, the railways and the fleets. The article's two-question separation exists for this case: the seizure fits the coup description at the level of technique while resting on a social process that no mere conspiracy could have manufactured in eight months.

### Q: Did the Bolsheviks have popular support?

Yes, in specific places and institutions, and no, as a claim about the whole nation; the precision matters more than the answer. Through the autumn of 1917 the party won majorities in the Petrograd and Moscow soviets, led opinion in the industrial districts, the Baltic Fleet and the armies on the fronts nearest the capital, and rode genuine mass demands for peace, land and workers' control. Across the country as a whole, the November elections to the Constituent Assembly placed the Socialist Revolutionaries first with roughly two fifths of the vote and the Bolsheviks second with roughly a quarter. Support was real, growing and strategically concentrated; it was not a national majority.

### Q: What happened to the Constituent Assembly?

It was elected in November 1917, in the freest vote Russia had held, with the Socialist Revolutionaries the largest party and the Bolsheviks second. When it met in Petrograd in January 1918, the majority refused to subordinate itself to the soviet government and its decrees. The Bolshevik and allied delegates withdrew, the guard commander closed the sitting in the small hours on the ground that the guard was tired, and the deputies returned the next day to find the palace shut and guarded. A soviet decree dissolved the assembly formally. Demonstrations in its support were dispersed, and no force in the country attempted its restoration by arms.

### Q: How much fighting was there during the seizure of power?

In the capital, remarkably little: the bridges, stations, telegraph and ministries were taken mostly without resistance, the Winter Palace was entered after a protracted and largely theatrical bombardment of blanks and a handful of live shells, and the dead in Petrograd numbered, on the accounts historians credit, in handfuls rather than hundreds. Moscow was a different story: there the transfer was contested by organised forces, fighting around the Kremlin lasted the better part of a week with artillery used inside the city, and the dead numbered in the hundreds by most estimates. The contrast between the two cities is itself evidence about how far the old authority had dissolved in each place.

### Q: Was Russia ready for socialism by Marxist theory?

On the orthodox reading held by the Mensheviks and by senior European theorists, no: a peasant empire with a small industrial working class had first to pass through a bourgeois democratic stage that would develop capitalism and its proletariat, and a socialist seizure in such conditions would be forced into coercion against the majority or collapse. The Bolshevik answer revised the timetable: the war had compressed the stages, the soviets offered an alternative power, and the theory of permanent revolution, developed independently a decade earlier, held that the democratic tasks would fall to the working class and drive it beyond them. Whether October vindicated the revision or violated the doctrine remains the tradition's open argument.

### Q: Why is it called October if it happened in November?

Because Russia still used the old Julian calendar, which in the twentieth century ran thirteen days behind the Gregorian calendar used in the West. The insurrection fell on 25 October by the reckoning of the city where it happened, which was 7 November by the reckoning of London or New York. The revolution keeps the name its participants used, and the new government deepened the irony by adopting the Gregorian calendar the following February, leaving its own founding date stranded in a calendar the state had abolished. Some Western accounts call it the November revolution, dating the event by the reader's calendar instead.

### Q: What did the Kornilov affair change?

Almost everything except the formal government. When the commander in chief moved troops on the capital in late August and the defence fell to the soviet organisations, the Bolsheviks emerged from the repression that had followed July as the revolution's armed defenders: their prisoners were released, their supporters received rifles that were never given back, and their central accusation, that the moderate course ended in military dictatorship, appeared confirmed by events. The affair also demonstrated, in rehearsal, the mechanics of October: railwaymen misrouting troop trains, agitators dissolving advancing regiments by argument, and a state discovering that its orders were obeyed only where the committees allowed.

### Q: What do historians disagree about regarding the seizure of power?

Less, after a century, about the sequence of events than about the weights to assign its causes and the word that should govern it. The traditional school, represented by historians such as Leonard Schapiro and Richard Pipes, reads October as a coup by a disciplined minority and centres the party's organisation and the assembly's fate. The revisionist social historians, represented by Alexander Rabinowitch and S. A. Smith, read it as the crest of a mass movement and centre the soviets' elections and the factories' and regiments' resolutions. Post-archival scholarship has complicated both, settling much of the texture, the party's internal disorder, the soviets' genuine electoral life, while leaving the proportion between design and process, and the judgement on the settlement, open.

### Q: Was the storming of the Winter Palace real?

Not in the form the world remembers. The palace was taken in the early hours of 26 October after an ultimatum, a signal shot and a small number of live rounds from the fortress, hours of infiltration through unguarded entrances, and the arrest of ministers found waiting at a table; its defenders, cadets and a women's battalion abandoned by the Cossacks, melted or surrendered in stages. The massed assault under fire, waves of attackers crossing a square swept by machine guns, was manufactured a decade later for the revolution's tenth anniversary, in a dramatised reconstruction and a celebrated film that employed more participants and artillery than the original night contained, and it is the reconstruction that popular memory kept.

### Q: How does the two-question separation change the October debate?

It dissolves the debate's false unity. Instead of asking whether October was a coup or a revolution as though one word must win, the separation asks two questions with different evidence: how power was taken, a question about committees, detachments and resisted or unresisted buildings; and what made the taking possible and durable, a question about soviets, garrisons, villages and the collapse of every rival authority's credit. Answered separately, the questions stop competing: the seizure was a minority operation in technique, and it rested on a social process of revolutionary depth. Most published quarrels on the subject turn out, on inspection, to be one question's evidence deployed against the other question's answer.

### Q: What should a historian check in the claim that October was a coup?

Three things. Whether the claim accounts for the preceding eight months, in particular the open votes that transferred the Petrograd and Moscow soviets and the garrison's refusal to defend two successive authorities, since a coup reading that cannot explain them has described a night and called it a year. Whether it distinguishes the party of the documents, argumentative, factional and repeatedly outvoted by its own base, from the monolith the claim usually requires. And whether it gives the Constituent Assembly's election and dispersal their full weight, because that episode, not the quiet night in the capital, is where the coup description earns its strongest and most durable support.

### Q: Why did the soviets matter to the revolution reading?

Because they are the documentary bridge between the seizure and the society. The soviets were elected, mandated and recallable bodies rooted in factories, regiments and fleets; their resolutions, re-elections and changes of majority across 1917 constitute a continuous record of opinion moving, institution by institution, toward the demand that power be transferred to them. When the Bolsheviks finally acted in the soviets' name and through a soviet committee, and had the transfer ratified by a soviet congress, the revisionist reading can point to a chain of authorisation that no conspiracy narrative should be able to supply. The chain's limits, two capitals and the organised minorities, are what the rival reading answers with.

### Q: How do casualty and crowd figures get handled safely in this case?

By refusing them a precision the sources cannot fund. Crowd sizes at demonstrations, membership totals claimed by parties and casualty counts for contested episodes all arrive carrying the fingerprints of the camps that transmitted them, and honest practice states them as attributed ranges, in orders of magnitude, or qualitatively where the numbers are smallest and most disputed. In this article the capital's deaths during the seizure are described as limited rather than counted, because the credited accounts agree on the scale and disagree on the digits; Moscow's are given as hundreds by most estimates; vote shares are given as rough fractions of the standard tabulations. No civil war or terror figures appear here at all, by the protocol that assigns them to the regime's own treatments.

### Q: What did the Constituent Assembly's dispersal decide for later readings?

It fixed the point on which the event's democratic legitimacy would be argued for a century. Every subsequent defence of October has had to answer for the night an elected national parliament was closed by armed men weeks after the vote; every subsequent prosecution has rested its case there more than on the seizure itself. Within the Marxist tradition the dispersal became the founding exhibit of the internal critique, stated by Rosa Luxemburg from prison in the revolution's first year and renewed by every democratic socialist argument since, that a socialism which begins by annulling an election it demanded will spend its authority faster than it can replenish it. The episode is therefore not an epilogue to the case study but one of its load-bearing walls.
