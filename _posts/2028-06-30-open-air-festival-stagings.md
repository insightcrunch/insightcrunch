Open-air and festival stagings of Romeo and Juliet are not a decorative branch of the play's performance history but its main line. The tragedy was composed for a roofless amphitheatre, an afternoon sky, and a crowd that stood in the same light as the actors, and every park, courtyard, and festival stage that has housed the play since has been recovering, knowingly or not, the conditions of its birth. The verdict argued through this article is plain: the open-air stage is the play's most faithful interpreter, because it restores the two things the indoor stage took away, a shared light between stage and audience and a public that can see itself. What the sky gives the tragedy is not atmosphere in the weak sense, a prettier backdrop for the balcony, but structure. Verona is a city of public places, its catastrophe begins with a brawl in a square at noon and ends with a prince addressing a crowd in a churchyard, and a play built out of crowds plays best before one.

![An open-air festival staging of Romeo and Juliet beneath a summer sky - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The evidence runs in a long chain. The first performances took place under the sky in Shoreditch around 1595. The park tradition that London built in Regent's Park from 1932 gave the play seven productions across nine decades and made an uncovered auditorium a permanent institution rather than a novelty. The festival circuit that Angus Bowmer founded at Ashland in 1935, that Tom Patterson founded at Stratford in Ontario in 1953, and that Verona had founded five years earlier in a Roman theatre above the Adige turned the summer staging of the play into an annual obligation for whole towns. Against that record stand the real costs, and this article weighs them without flinching: weather that stops shows and smoke that cancels them, voices spent against open space, texts trimmed to fit a dusk deadline, and the standing suspicion, argued most sharply by Dennis Kennedy, that festival Shakespeare is tourism wearing the play as a souvenir. The costs are genuine. They are also the price of the one transaction no roofed house can offer, a tragedy of public violence and private love performed before a public that has gathered in the open, under a sky that keeps changing while the words stay the same.

The argument proceeds from conditions to institutions to complications. First, the original afternoon itself: what the Shoreditch amphitheatres imposed on the text, and what daylight does to a play whose lovers keep describing a night that nobody on the first stage could see. Then the two modern institutions that rebuilt those conditions without the archaeology, the permanent park theatre and the festival town, with the documented productions of the tragedy that each has accumulated. Then the complications in ascending order of seriousness: the weather that stops the show, the acoustics that tax the voice, the dusk that edits the running time, and the tourism that threatens to convert the whole enterprise into a souvenir. The survey table gathers the evidence in one place so the verdict can be checked against it row by row. A note on the evidence itself: open-air theatre generates legend even more freely than the indoor kind, because every spectator's memory of a perfect evening is also a review. This account therefore builds on the documents the form leaves behind, foundation records, festival production histories, published policies, and dated notices, and it reports the memoir material as memoir where that is what survives. A tradition this old can afford to be audited; its best stories are the ones that survive the audit.

## Was Romeo and Juliet Written for the Open Air?

The play was written for the open air, and its first home was not the Globe. Scholarship on the Chamberlain's Men places the tragedy, composed around 1595, at the Theatre in Shoreditch or at the Curtain nearby, the two great roofless amphitheatres that served the company before the Globe rose south of the river in 1599. Andrew Gurr, the theatre historian whose account of the early modern stage remains the standard survey, reconstructs the conditions those houses imposed: performances began in the afternoon, the stage thrust into a yard where standing spectators surrounded it on three sides, galleries rose behind them, and no artificial light mediated between the fiction and the day. Actor and audience occupied one continuous illumination. When Romeo says that Juliet is the sun, the first audiences heard the line from a man standing in sunlight, and the compliment had to survive the comparison happening in front of them.

The Chorus prepares that audience for a civic spectacle before any lover appears: "In fair Verona, where we lay our scene" (Prologue.3, in the Oxford edition prepared by Jill Levenson). The verb matters. The scene is laid, placed before a gathering, in a city defined by its public spaces. The play's first action is not an embrace but a street fight between servants, and its first great speech is a magistrate's address to a crowd about crowds. The Prince enters over the bodies of a brawl and speaks the language of public order to people who have been behaving as a mob: "If ever you disturb our streets again, / Your lives shall pay the forfeit of the peace" (1.1). A magistrate sentencing street violence needs witnesses, and the amphitheatre supplied them by architecture. The yard was full of the same class of gathering the fiction kept indicting, apprentices and citizens standing in daylight, visible to the Prince's actor and to each other. The scene did not depict a crowd to an audience seated in private darkness. It arraigned one crowd before another.

The house that received the play was itself a recent invention. The Theatre, raised by James Burbage in Shoreditch in 1576, was the first permanent purpose-built playhouse in England, a polygonal timber amphitheatre with a roofed gallery ring and an open centre, and the Curtain, its near neighbour, followed within a year or two. Gurr's reconstruction of the playing conditions in these houses gives the afternoon its social shape. The yard held its standing spectators for a penny; the galleries charged more for a seat and a roof over the sitter's head, though never over the stage or the yard, so that the cheapest places in the house were also the wettest when it rained and the best lit when it shone. A trumpet announced the performance, a flag flew over the house while it lasted, and the playing occupied the early afternoon, timed to end before the light failed and the audience's working evening began. The company played through the London year in these conditions, which means the first runs of the tragedy included performances in which the fifth act's pretended dawn was spoken as the real December light was already leaving the sky at four in the afternoon. The fiction's hours were imaginary in every season. Only the direction of the disagreement between the text's clock and the sky's changed with the calendar.

The audience so assembled was not a cross-section of silent consumers but the city's leisure public in its own clothes, apprentices on an afternoon's liberty, citizens, visitors, pickpockets, and the sellers of the nuts and ale that the yard consumed during the performance. Gurr's account stresses what the architecture made unavoidable: this public was part of the spectacle for itself. A tragedy about a city's factions, performed to a factional city gathered in daylight, did not need to reach for relevance. Relevance was the admission price. When the Prince banishes Romeo and sentences the next brawlers to death, the sentence is delivered to a yard containing the very demographic, young men of the trading classes with their honour up, that the fiction shows killing each other in the street an hour earlier. Modern productions spend fortunes on design to manufacture that short circuit between stage and stalls. The open-air house gets it from the sky and the seating plan, free of charge.

One further condition of the first performances belongs to the record, because the play itself preserves it. The tragedy was composed in the middle of the decade after the great plague closure of 1592 to 1594, when London's playhouses stood shut by order and the companies scattered to the provinces; the amphitheatre was a regulated space in a city that counted its plague deaths weekly and closed its theatres when the count rose. Inside the fiction, the catastrophe turns on exactly that machinery. Friar John, sent with the letter that would have saved the lovers, reports that the searchers of the town, "suspecting that we both were in a house / Where the infectious pestilence did reign, / Sealed up the doors, and would not let us forth" (5.2). The first yard heard that speech as recent memory, not as plot machinery: the sealed house, the stopped messenger, the letter that never arrived were the ordinary instruments of the city's government of disease, and the open-air theatre in which the speech was delivered existed on the same sufferance, one bad week of the bills of mortality away from closure. No indoor era has reproduced that pressure. The park and festival stages, playing in the open by civic permission in public space, are the only modern houses that still perform the play under a licence the city can revoke for the public health, as the smoke season at Ashland demonstrated in another element's name.

That shared visibility is the original condition every later open-air staging recovers, whether it knows the scholarship or not. The rebuilt Globe in London, whose own productions of the tragedy are surveyed in the companion study of [the Globe reconstruction's productions of the play](/2027/11/26/globe-reconstruction-productions/), reopened the experiment in 1997 with Henry V and reached the tragedy in its original-practices season of 2004, and its yard reproduces the Shoreditch geometry of thrust, standing room, and common sky. What the reconstruction proved, and what the park and festival stages had been proving for decades without the archaeology, is that the play's rhetoric assumes a hearer who can be pointed at. Soliloquy in daylight is not confession overheard. It is testimony delivered to witnesses who are lit well enough to be caught listening.

The indoor tradition that displaced these conditions was a long parenthesis. The patent theatres of the Restoration and the pictorial stages of the nineteenth century roofed the play, darkened the house, and taught audiences to receive its nights as painted effects and its crowds as choreographed supernumeraries. The tragedy survived that parenthesis handsomely, and great indoor Romeos and Juliets fill other articles in this series, but the parenthesis should not be mistaken for the norm. Measured against four centuries, the roofed, darkened, proscenium performance is the experiment. The afternoon amphitheatre is the control condition, and the park and festival stages of the twentieth century were less an innovation than a reversion that had to be argued for as if it were new.

## What Does Daylight Do to the Play's Night Scenes?

Daylight does the opposite of what a lighting designer might fear: it makes the play's nights legible as acts of imagination performed in public. The text is saturated with light that the first stage could not show. Romeo in the orchard, played at mid-afternoon, looks up at a window and announces, "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks? / It is the east, and Juliet is the sun" (2.2.2-3). Juliet, alone, summons a darkness that the sky refused to provide: "Come, night; come, Romeo; come, thou day in night" (3.2.17). On the original stage these lines were not descriptions of anything visible. They were instructions to the audience's imagination, issued in broad day, and the audience obeyed them the way it obeyed the Chorus's instruction to see Verona in a wooden circle of London timber. The verbal scenery that built the city, the wall, and the orchard out of speech alone did the same work for the hours of the day, and the companion study of [Verona built out of words on a bare Elizabethan stage](/2028/02/18/verona-on-a-bare-stage/) maps that system in full.

The consequence for the actor is a discipline that indoor playing relaxes. In a darkened house, the lighting plot tells the audience that night has fallen, and the performer need only behave consistently with the plot. In daylight, the performer must manufacture the night against the evidence of every eye in the yard, by voice, by stillness, by the fiction's own repeated naming of the darkness everyone is pretending to inhabit. The night scenes keep naming their own darkness precisely because the first stage could not stop naming the discrepancy away. Critics of the early stage sometimes treat this as a deficiency the text heroically overcame. The performance record argues the reverse. A night that must be spoken into existence is a night the audience helps to make, and complicity of that kind is the tragedy's native element: the lovers' world is a conspiracy of two against a city, and the daylight audience is recruited into the conspiracy every time it agrees that the sunlit stage is dark.

### What does a park audience do during the street brawls that a seated indoor audience cannot?

It watches itself being a crowd. An indoor audience sits in darkness, invisible to the actors and to itself, receiving the brawls as pictures of public disorder framed at a distance. A park audience occupies benches and grass in full view, rustling and shifting in the light, visibly a gathering of the same kind the fiction keeps indicting.

When the Prince sentences the street fighters, the sentence lands on a gathering that has just been behaving, in miniature, like the gathering onstage. The fiction's question, who will keep the peace of Verona, is addressed to people visibly constituting a public. That visibility is not a distraction from the play. It is the play's first condition, restored.

The ball scene shows the same mechanics working on joy instead of violence. The lovers meet in public, at a feast, inside a shared sonnet spoken before a room full of dancers, and the scene's danger is entirely a function of visibility: Tybalt recognizes a voice, Capulet performs hospitality for the watching town, and two young people conduct the most famous courtship in the language within earshot of the feud. Romeo's approach is made in the open, "My lips, two blushing pilgrims, ready stand" (1.5), and the kiss that follows is negotiated under the eyes of the company like a treaty witnessed. In a darkened auditorium the scene contracts into a private romance overheard. In daylight, on a thrust stage, before a crowd that is itself dressed for a summer evening and visible to the actors, the scene keeps its social physics: love as a public act with public consequences, performed in a city that is watching. The open-air house does not add that meaning to the scene. It declines to subtract it.

The soliloquy obeys the same law, and the orchard scene is its proof. Romeo's speeches beneath the window are confidences, the thinking aloud of a man who believes himself unheard, and their theatrical life depends on a paradox the daylight handles better than the dark: the confidence must be shared with the audience without ceasing to feel private. In a darkened house the paradox resolves toward intimacy; the spectator receives the speech as a whisper in the ear, and Romeo dwindles into a voice. In the yard the paradox resolves toward complicity. A thousand visible people hold the secret together, in full view of one another, while the man on the stage pretends the night hides him from everyone but the woman at the window. The audience's share in the fiction is the larger for being collective, and the tragedy that follows, in which a private love is destroyed by a public world, has been rehearsed in the audience's own behaviour from the moment it first kept the lovers' secret in a crowd. Shared light makes confidants of a public. Darkness makes eavesdroppers of individuals, and the play, which ends by arraigning the public and forgiving the lovers, was written for confidants.

The play's own company eventually acquired the alternative, and the alternative's limits are instructive. In 1609 the King's Men added the Blackfriars to their houses, a roofed, candlelit hall where the cheapest seat cost more than the dearest gallery at the amphitheatre and the audience sat in upholstered proximity to a smaller stage. The tragedy thereafter lived a double life, played in daylight to thousands in summer and by candlelight to hundreds in winter, and the indoor tradition that dominates its modern performance descends from the second of those lives. Nothing in the double life suggests the text preferred the hall. The play's public scenes, its processions, its crowd of watchers at the tomb, are scaled to the amphitheatre's population; the hall received a tragedy built for a city and seated it in a drawing room. When the modern festival stage restores the larger population, it is not inflating the play. It is returning the play to the company of hearers its rhetoric counts on, the thousand-headed Verona that the Prince addresses and the feud requires.

The tomb is the daylight system's severest test, and its quiet triumph. The fifth act is the play's darkest fiction: a vault at night, a page dismissed with a torch, men fighting in a charnel-house among the bones, a friar stumbling in the dark. Paris states the condition the afternoon most flatly contradicts: "Give me thy torch, boy. Hence, and stand aloof" (5.3). Played at two o'clock, every word of that darkness must be built by agreement between stage and yard, the actor naming a torch the sun makes invisible, the audience supplying the night the text insists on. That labour is not a primitive makeshift awaiting the invention of the lighting rig. It is the tragedy's deepest theatrical mechanic, the same imaginative conscription the Chorus announces in the prologue of Henry V and this play practises without announcing: the audience as co-author of the visible world. An indoor tomb, for all its achievable gloom, does the audience's work for it, and the work, done for it, stops being felt. In the daylight yard the vault is the one place where every spectator knows exactly what the open-air contract costs and exactly what it buys, because the darkness they are imagining together is the darkness the lovers die in, and no one in the yard can pretend it was provided by anyone but themselves.

The brawls gain most from this restoration because they are arguments about spectatorship. Verona's young men fight for an audience of each other; honour in the play is a performance that requires witnesses, and the duel in the third act draws a crowd inside the fiction before it draws blood. When Mercutio falls cursing both houses, the curse is spoken to the onstage watchers as much as to the houses named. A modern audience under a sky, itself a visible assembly in a public place, completes the circuit the text designs. It is not merely watching young men perform violence for reputation. It is supplying the reputation market in which the violence trades, and the discomfort of that recognition, available in a park in a way it never quite is in the dark, is among the sharpest pleasures the open-air form offers.

There is a limit, and honesty requires naming it inside this section rather than in the verdict. Daylight forgives the love scenes and taxes the tomb. The last act's gloom, the torches, the vault's imagined depth, ask the audience to imagine not just night but enclosure, and a bright afternoon resists enclosure harder than it resists darkness. Evening festivals, which begin in light and end in dark, solve the problem by scheduling, and their solution is examined below. The afternoon houses accept the tax as the price of the complicity. Either way, the tax is a staging problem inside a form that fits the play, not evidence against the form. No indoor convention is taxed at all, because none of them is tested against the conditions the text was built in.

## Regent's Park and the Invention of the London Park Tradition

The modern park tradition begins with an emergency. In the spring of 1932 the producer Robert Atkins and the critic and impresario Sydney Carroll were presenting a play by Benito Mussolini at the New Theatre in London, and its early closure left the house dark. Their replacement, a black-and-white production of Twelfth Night, succeeded, and on 12 July 1932 they transferred it to a makeshift theatre in Regent's Park for four performances, arranged with the Office of Works. The theatre's own heritage record marks the significance of the arrangement: it was the first time the state had arranged for a Shakespeare play to be presented in a public park. The four performances lost money, a deficit of £560 by the heritage account, and the loss did not dissuade the founders. A first full season followed in 1933, the theatre was formally opened that June, and the Open Air Theatre in Regent's Park became what it remains, by its own description the oldest professional permanent outdoor theatre in Britain, seating some 1,304 people in a wholly uncovered auditorium and playing an eighteen-week summer season to more than 140,000 spectators a year.

The institution then survived its founders and its century's emergencies. The theatre's management record shows Carroll's impresario years ending in 1939 and Atkins carrying the direction alone from 1940 to 1961, which means the park's seasons continued through the war years under the man who had staged the first Twelfth Night, in a city whose indoor life was governed by the blackout, and continued through the austerity decades that followed. A summer novelty does not behave like that. The park theatre had become part of London's theatrical infrastructure, the place where the city kept the classical repertoire in the open while the roofed houses pursued their own economies, and its postwar permanence is the premise without which the later history, the rebuilding of the auditorium in permanent form and the artistic regimes of the modern era, cannot be understood.

### Did anyone play Shakespeare in Regent's Park before the theatre existed?

Yes, a generation earlier. The Theatres Trust record notes that Ben Greet's Woodland Players performed in Regent's Park as early as 1900, part of the actor-manager's campaign to take Shakespeare out of the patent houses and into gardens, college grounds, and open spaces on both sides of the Atlantic.

Greet's park performances were touring visits by a company built for the road. What Carroll and Atkins added in 1932 was not the idea of Shakespeare among trees but an institution: a fixed address, a resident company, and a season that returned every summer until the park itself became the argument.

Romeo and Juliet reached the park in 1934, two years after the founding, and the theatre's heritage archive preserves the production's most famous accident of casting: a twenty-nine-year-old Greer Garson, later a Hollywood star, played in the ensemble. The archive then traces a line of productions across the century that reads like a index of the British theatre's generations. The play returned in 1971 and again in 1986, when Declan Donnellan directed Ralph Fiennes as Romeo opposite Sarah Woodward's Juliet, Fiennes having made his professional debut at the same theatre the year before. The sequence of the seven productions, read as a casting document, shows the park house performing one of its least discussed functions: the play serves as a registry of careers at their thresholds. A Romeo a year into his professional life in 1986; the theatre's heritage archive recording a stage debut in the small part of Benvolio in 2002; a director opening his artistic regime with the tragedy in 2008, as Sheader did, staking a new management's credit on the play before any other. Institutions choose their proving pieces for a reason. The park's management, across ninety years and every change of fashion, has repeatedly reached for this tragedy when something, a career, a regime, a reopened theatre, needed to be tested in public, because the play combines the largest popular draw in the repertoire with the most unforgiving exposure of young talent. A festival's Romeo and Juliet is where its future is auditioned. Judi Dench, the theatre's patron, directed the production the heritage archive dates to 1993. Dominic Hill's 2002 production is recorded by the archive as the occasion of Benedict Cumberbatch's stage debut, as Benvolio. When Timothy Sheader became artistic director, he opened his first season, in 2008, with the tragedy in a 1950s setting, a new regime announcing itself with the park's oldest property. The seventh production, directed by Kimberley Sykes, opened the theatre's post-pandemic season in 2021, played without an interval and, by Alastair Macaulay's account in his review of it, cut the fourth and fifth acts harder than its cast deserved, sacrificing most of Romeo's forty-six-line death speech. Macaulay's review is worth pausing on, because it shows the park tradition examining itself. He praised the first three acts as adding greatly to his understanding of the play, and he registered the production's casting, which placed Black actors in the roles of Juliet, Lord Capulet, Benvolio, and Escalus and recast Tybalt and Benvolio as women, as the supposedly conventional house plunging into the casting arguments of the moment. The unbroken span of the performance is itself a datum for this survey: an evening production that must gather its tragedy inside the light has reason to refuse the interval, and the refusal shapes the text it can carry. The dusk is a dramaturg at Regent's Park whether the director invites it or not, and Sykes's production, praised and faulted in the same review for what it kept and what it surrendered, is the record of a negotiation with that dramaturg conducted in public. Seven productions in eighty-seven years is not saturation. It is a rhythm of return, each generation of the park's audience meeting the play at the age the play demands.

What the park contributes, beyond its own history, is a standing refutation of the idea that open-air Shakespeare is fair-weather light entertainment. The venue's repertoire has always mixed the tragedy with musicals and comedies, and the tragedy has kept its place in the rotation through regimes of every taste. The auditorium is a shallow bowl of benches among trees; the stage sits against planting rather than architecture; the audience arrives through a garden and takes its seats in the same weather the actors will play in. Macaulay, reviewing the 2021 production, remarked that the Open Air was long known as the most safely conventional of Shakespeare theatres, and the remark is a useful measure of the distance the venue has travelled: the park that began as a respectable summer diversion ended the century as a place where a director could strip the lovers' world to a bare platform and let the dusk do the designing. The trees behind the stage are not a view. They are the orchard, the street, and the churchyard by turns, and the audience that has walked through a real garden to reach them has already been taught how little scenery the play needs.

## The Picnic and the Public: How an Open-Air Audience Assembles

Every account of open-air staging dwells on the stage and neglects the hour before it. Yet the form's decisive transaction happens before a word of the play is spoken, in the assembly of the audience itself, and the play is unusually dependent on that transaction because its fiction is about a public that has failed to assemble itself into a city. Verona's tragedy is set in motion by servants gossiping in a street and consummated at a feast to which the whole town's marriageable youth is summoned. The fiction keeps gathering people. A performance of it properly begins, therefore, not with the Chorus but with the gathering of the crowd that the Chorus will address, and the open-air houses are the only ones that stage that gathering as part of the event rather than as a queue for it.

### Why do the gates open long before an open-air performance begins?

Because at an open-air theatre the gathering is part of the performance. Regent's Park opens its gates ninety minutes before the performance, and Ashland's Green Show entertains the arriving crowd free on the courtyard stage before the evening's play. The audience picnics, settles, and becomes a crowd in the open air before the fiction asks it to become Verona.

The Regent's Park visit, as the theatre's own guidance describes it, is built around that long threshold: spectators take seats on the grass banks directly on the ground, buy picnic blankets and ponchos at the theatre, eat in the garden, and watch the light begin its long withdrawal while the company warms up behind the planting. At Ashland the Green Show, free entertainment on the courtyard stage six nights a week before the evening performance, performs the same function with music and dance, a deliberate survival of the festival's founding populism. At the Delacorte the assembly takes the form of the queue: tickets are distributed free on the day of performance, and the line that forms for them is itself a New York institution, hours of civic loitering in which the audience sorts itself into a public before it is allowed to sit down. Playbill's account of the Delacorte's fiftieth anniversary put the cumulative figures at more than five million tickets handed out across more than 150 productions, each of those tickets preceded by the same ritual of waiting in a park among strangers who were about to become, for one evening, a city.

The indoor house abbreviates all of this to a foyer and a bell. Its audience arrives as individuals and couples, sits in darkness among people it cannot see, and disperses without ever having constituted itself. The open-air house cannot abbreviate, because its architecture has no inside into which the crowd can be processed; the crowd is already in the place where the play will happen, in the weather the play will happen in, eating what it brought, watching the stagehands and the sky with the same attention. By the time the Chorus asks the gathering to attend to two households in fair Verona, the gathering has rehearsed the part it is about to play: a body of citizens at leisure in a public place, whose peace the fiction is about to break. The picnic is not an amenity attached to the form. It is the form's first act, played by the audience, without lines.

This is also the answer to the standing sneer that park Shakespeare is theatre for people who are not really attending. The assembly is a discipline of attention of a different kind: slower, social, exposed to distraction, and paid for in time rather than in the coin of the hushed dark. The play was written for a yard that ate nuts and drank ale through the performance and talked back to the clowns; its attention economy was always the economy of a crowd at liberty, not of a congregation at prayer. The festival and park audiences are not a dilution of the play's first public. They are its closest living relatives, and the resemblance is not accidental. It is what four hundred dollars of Oregon city money and a London park bench were always buying.

The assembly also explains the festival audience's most distinctive economic trait, its habit of return. The Oregon festival organized itself as a membership association within two years of its founding, and its modern public is famously a returning one, households that drive the same mountain roads to the same bowl each summer and treat the season as a fixture of the year rather than an excursion. A returning public is the opposite of a tourist flow. Tourists consume a place once; citizens of a festival renew it annually, argue about its productions with the proprietary anger of stakeholders, and measure each new Romeo against the Romeos of their own past summers in the same seats. The open-air season thus manufactures the very thing the indoor repertoire struggles to keep, an audience with a memory, seated in the open where it can watch itself remembering.

Even the interval obeys the form. Indoors, the interval releases the crowd into a foyer, a bar, a different and brighter fiction, and the play must win its audience back from the lobby's chatter. In the park and the bowl the interval happens in the playing place itself: the audience stands, stretches, buys ice cream, watches the light finish changing over the stage it has just left, and talks about the lovers in the weather the lovers are fictionally enduring. The spell is never fully broken because there is nowhere to break it into. When the second half begins, it begins among people who have spent the pause inside the tragedy's element, and the tomb scene inherits an audience that has been standing in the darkening air for twenty minutes, rehearsing the cold the fiction is about to name.

## The Festival Circuit: Ashland, Stratford, Verona

A festival is not a tour that stays still. A touring company carries a finished production to strangers, as the companion survey of [touring and regional productions of the play](/2028/04/21/touring-regional-productions/) records; a festival summons strangers to a place and makes the place a co-author of every performance. The play's festival history is therefore a history of places that decided to be known by it. Three foundings define the circuit, and each was an act of civic self-invention in which the tragedy, sooner or later, took a central part.

Ashland, Oregon, came first, and it inherited more than it invented. The town already possessed a summer assembly culture and the building to prove it: the Chautauqua movement, the great circuit of summer lectures, music, and moral entertainment, had raised its hall in Lithia Park, and the town's park-going habits, band concerts and outdoor gatherings in the same green valley, long predated the festival. Bowmer's genius was to see that the roofless ruin of that assembly tradition was, in shape, the theatre his own tradition had begun in. The festival did not import a foreign ritual to Ashland. It reoccupied the town's own ritual ground and changed the liturgy from lectures to plays. Angus Bowmer, a drama teacher at the Southern Oregon Normal School, persuaded his small town to revive its Fourth of July celebrations in 1935 with two Shakespeare plays on the roofless shell of the old Chautauqua building, whose surviving wall reminded him of an Elizabethan stage. The city lent a sum not to exceed four hundred dollars and stipulated that afternoon boxing matches be added to cover the expected losses. The Oregon Encyclopedia's account preserves the founding's perfect irony: the plays succeeded beyond expectation, and the revenue from the theatre tickets covered the losses of the boxing. Twelfth Night and The Merchant of Venice opened the first season on 2 July 1935. Romeo and Juliet joined in the second season, 1936, directed by Bowmer, and returned in 1937, again under his direction; the festival's production history records further stagings in 1949 and 1956, and the play has remained in the rotation through the festival's repeated completions of the canon. The outdoor Elizabethan stage, rebuilt and renamed over the decades and known in its modern form as the Allen Elizabethan Theatre, stayed the festival's flagship, a summer house whose season runs under the stars from June into October.

The pattern in that early Ashland sequence recurs at every house in the survey, and it is worth naming. A young festival reaches for this tragedy early, in Ashland's case in its second season, as a proving piece: the play tests a company's verse, its crowd scenes, its young casting, and its audience's seriousness all at once, and a festival that can stage it under the sky has announced the scale of its ambition. The later returns, 1949 and 1956 at Ashland, the 1971 revival at Regent's Park, the second Stratford mounting in 2017, are the same announcement renewed by an institution that has grown into its claim. The tragedy's open-air history is therefore also a history of festivals measuring themselves, and the measurements, taken at the founding and at each maturity, are the most reliable index of what each house believed it had become.

Between the founding and the modern festival stands a record of stubborn continuity. The festival closed for the war years, 1941 to 1946, and returned to become, by the Oregon Historical Society's account, the only theatre in the United States to stage all thirty-seven of the plays separately, completing the full cycle for the first time in 1958 and for the fourth time in 2016. In its modern form it presents some eleven productions a season across three theatres to an annual audience in the hundreds of thousands, in a town whose population a single season's attendance exceeds many times over. The outdoor stage remained the emblem of the enterprise through all that growth, the image on the poster and the seat the returning audience asks for first. A festival that began as a town's gamble on two plays had become the largest resident acting company in the country without surrendering its founding wager, that Shakespeare belonged outdoors in the summer, in a bowl open to the weather, before a crowd that had driven through the mountains to be there.

The 2018 Ashland production shows what the festival conditions let a director do with this particular play. Dámaso Rodríguez directed, with Emily Ota as Juliet and William Thomas Hodgson as Romeo, and the festival's own description of the production states its governing idea with unusual precision: it was inspired by the natural mirror of the play's progression from light to darkness. An indoor production must manufacture that progression with a lighting plot. An evening festival production inherits it from the sky. The performance opened at eight in the long light of an Oregon June evening, played the brawls and the ball in something like the day the fiction claims, and let the real dusk arrive in step with the banishment, the potion, and the tomb. The festival's archive of the play thus closes its documented Ashland arc with a production whose central effect no roofed theatre in the world could have supplied, because the effect was the latitude of southern Oregon in summer, arriving on schedule.

Stratford, Ontario, founded its festival as an economic rescue. The railway industry that had sustained the town was pulling out in the early 1950s, and the journalist Tom Patterson proposed a Shakespeare festival as the instrument of revival; the festival's own timeline records that the city council's first contribution was a grant of $125 for a research trip. Tyrone Guthrie agreed to direct, and with the designer Tanya Moiseiwitsch he built a thrust stage inside a concrete amphitheatre and housed it, for the inaugural season and the three that followed, under a giant canvas tent. Richard III opened the festival on 13 July 1953, with Alec Guinness in the title role, before some 1,500 spectators and eighty-five critics. The tent came down for the last time in 1956, and the permanent Festival Theatre, designed by Robert Fairfield in consultation with Guthrie, opened in 1957 with the tent's stage preserved at its heart.

### Why does the Stratford stage in Ontario keep the shape of a tent it no longer uses?

Because the tent decided the geometry. Guthrie and Moiseiwitsch arranged the audience around a thrust stage in a steep amphitheatre bowl, approximating the configuration of the early modern playhouse, and the arrangement worked so well that when canvas gave way to a permanent roof, the stage, the rake, and the encircling seats were rebuilt where the tent had put them.

Stratford's indoor stage is an outdoor amphitheatre that acquired a roof. Its openness is structural, not meteorological: the crowd still surrounds the action in one shared volume of air.

The tent's first night fixed the festival's self-image. Robert Cushman's history of the festival's fifty seasons describes the response when the first Richard III ended: a standing ovation, not then the automatic event it has since become, lasting five minutes, an eternity in theatre time, before an audience that included eighty-five critics from across the continent. The detail matters to this survey because it records the assembly working as designed: a town that had raised a tent by subscription and last-minute donations, gathered under canvas in a bowl dug into the ground, discovering in one evening that the experiment had succeeded. The Festival Theatre that replaced the tent seats more than 1,800, the largest of the survey's permanent houses, and its size is the tent's ambition made permanent: Stratford did not shrink its gathering when it roofed it.

The play's documented Stratford life runs on that fossilized sky. Robert Cushman, reviewing Tim Carroll's 2013 production for the Globe and Mail, noted that its set was Stratford's original Moiseiwitsch arrangement of steps and balconies with thatched additions that suggested both the early modern Globe and the modern London reconstruction at which Carroll had often directed, with Sara Topham as Juliet. The irony of that set deserves a moment's attention, because it reverses the survey's usual direction of travel. Elsewhere the open-air houses quote the indoor tradition, borrowing its stars, its directors, its designs. At Stratford in 2013 the indoor house quoted the yard: thatch over the festival stage, the steps and balconies of a designer imagining an Elizabethan tiring-house, the whole apparatus of a permanent theatre pretending, for the tragedy's sake, to be the temporary wooden structure its own tent had replaced. When the most established festival stage in North America dresses itself as an amphitheatre to play this play, the argument of this article no longer needs to be made. It needs only to be reported. Scott Wentworth's 2017 production, with Sara Farb and Antoine Yared, was filmed for the festival's high-definition cinema series, so that the most place-bound of forms, the festival staging, ended by circulating to cinemas as a record of a particular summer in a particular Ontario town. The camera can export the performances. It cannot export the bowl of spectators that the tent first gathered, which is the part of the production Stratford itself would say mattered.

New York's Delacorte Theater is the circuit's urban proof, an amphitheatre planted in a great metropolitan park and given away free. It opened on 18 June 1962 with The Merchant of Venice, directed by Joseph Papp with George C. Scott as Shylock, on a site above a small lake with a little castle on the far shore, and it holds some 1,800 spectators in the open air. Papp directed the play there himself in 1968, with Martin Sheen as Romeo and Susan McArthur as Juliet, six years after the house opened; the tragedy then waited thirty-nine years for its next full Delacorte production. Michael Greif's staging of 2007, with Oscar Isaac and Lauren Ambrose, Camryn Manheim as the Nurse, and Austin Pendleton as the Friar, set the action in and around a shallow pool of water at the centre of Mark Wendland's design, and the notices divided along the seam the design opened. TheaterMania's reviewer found Ambrose's Juliet reason enough to attend and the surrounding production disappointing; the New York Theatre Scene's critic called the evening headlong and worth seeing, its lovers sloshing through their tragedy in a Verona that kept suggesting Venice. The division is the park form in miniature. A design that conscripts the actual element, water under real trees with the city's towers beyond, strikes one spectator as the place joining the play and another as the place distracting from it, and no indoor production can even pose the question. The Delacorte's scarcity with this play is itself a datum. In the theatre's first fifty years the Public mounted it in full production only twice, in 1968 and 2007, returning to it in 2012 for a single benefit reading that paired Meryl Streep with Kevin Kline under Daniel Sullivan's direction. The tragedies the Delacorte lives on are the histories and the great solo vehicles; the Verona play, with its crowds, its feast, its dependence on a civic ensemble, arrives there as an event rather than a staple, and its rarity concentrates the house's meaning when it comes. A park audience that has queued all day for a play its theatre seldom offers is not consuming a repertory item. It is attending an occasion, and the play, whose own occasions, a feast, a duel, a wedding, a funeral, are the hinges of its plot, understands occasions better than any other in the canon.

Verona is the circuit's extreme case, a festival held inside the fiction. The Estate Teatrale Veronese mounted its first Shakespeare festival in the summer of 1948 in the city's Roman theatre, a stone semicircle of the first century at the foot of the San Pietro hill above the River Adige, and the play it opened with was this one: Roberto Simoni directed Romeo and Juliet, with the young Giorgio Strehler serving as an assistant director. No other festival can make that claim on the text. Ashland and Stratford imported the playwright to dignify a place; Verona staged the tragedy in the city the tragedy names, in a theatre built when the story's supposed century was still recent history, before spectators sitting on stone that predates the fiction by a millennium and a half. The theatre itself is the oldest building in this survey by two thousand years: a Roman cavea cut into the hillside in the first century, its stone semicircle facing the stage across the width of the Adige valley, so that the audience watches the play against the hill of San Pietro and the river traffic below. Across the festival's decades the house has hosted the Italian stage's defining names, Vittorio Gassman and Carmelo Bene among the actors, Zeffirelli among the directors in Playbill's summary of the record, and the festival has returned to the tragedy as its signature ever since. Its fiftieth edition, in 1998, opened with the Royal Shakespeare Company's touring production, directed by Michael Attenborough with Ray Fearon and Zoe Waites as the lovers, closing an international tour with a run of performances in the original setting of the story, as Playbill's report of the season put it.

The 1998 visit deserves a paragraph of its own, because it is the survey's richest single event and the purest test of what a place does to a production. Playbill's description of Attenborough's staging records a designed Verona of stripped Tuscan walls, decaying and rotting, with brick and tile underfoot and a single pipe and tap where women sat to do the washing in alabaster bowls: an artificial Italian street, built in England, toured through Bogotá, Rotterdam, Brussels, Tokyo, and Singapore, and finally erected inside a real Italian city, in a theatre built by the Romans, within a short walk of the house the city calls Juliet's. Everywhere else on the tour, the set had been the production's Italy. In Verona the set was a quotation, and the stones around it were the text. Reports of the visit treat the location as the event, the production's last performances framed by the setting rather than the staging, and the framing is the argument of this article in one anecdote: put the play in its place under the open sky, and the place joins the company, takes the largest role, and receives the largest share of the applause. A purist may call that a distraction from the direction. The Verona audience of 1998, watching English actors perform Verona to Verona in a Roman theatre, was experiencing the condition the play's first audiences knew by default, a fiction staged inside the community it described, with no fourth wall of geography between the story and the stones. Verona's festival is the strongest evidence for the prosecution in the tourism argument heard below, and the strongest evidence for the defence: the place is the attraction, and the play is the place's own story told back to it under the sky where its imaginary events are set.

The circuit's other species deserve their sentences. San Diego's Old Globe represents the fairground line of descent, and its genealogy is a parable of the form's temptations. The theatre built in Balboa Park in 1935 for the California Pacific International Exposition was modelled, on the Old Globe's own history, on Shakespeare's London house by way of a copy erected for the Chicago Century of Progress fair: a copy of a copy, raised in thirty-one days, its centre open to the sky like the original's, and its repertoire cut to fit an exposition's attention. During the fair it presented fifty-minute abridgements of the plays to strolling crowds, Shakespeare as one attraction among the midway's many. That the building survived its fair at all was the work of local citizens who leased it from the city and roofed its yard for year-round use; the open-air line reasserted itself a generation later, when Craig Noel founded the summer Shakespeare festival in 1949 and the festival stage, built outdoors in emergency in 1978, became the permanent Lowell Davies house. The fairground species shows what the open-air form becomes when the gathering is an exhibition crowd: the plays shrink to samples. The festival species, at Ashland and Stratford and the Delacorte, is the same form rescued by duration, a whole summer, a whole town, the whole text.

Boulder's Colorado festival, founded in 1958, plays in the Mary Rippon Outdoor Theatre, a stone shell completed in 1939 on the university campus. Cedar City's Utah festival, founded in 1962 on a makeshift platform stage built by its own company, acquired a Globe-patterned open-air house in 1977. Campus, fairground, and courthouse-square foundings differ in everything except the operative fact: in each, a community chose the open-air performance of these plays as the public act by which it would be known, and the tragedy of Verona was among the instruments nearest to hand.

Two rules of reading govern the table's use. The first is that a row records a house, not a performance: the productions named are the documented anchors of a tradition that in every case includes unlisted revivals, festival returns, and school and amateur stagings beyond the archive's reach, and the count given for any house is a floor, not a total. The second is that the column of the sky is a column of causes. Where the table says the dusk times the parting or the place serves as the scenery, it is making the article's central claim in compressed form, that in these houses the conditions of performance are not the frame around the interpretation but part of the interpretation, as material to the meaning as the casting of the Nurse.

The survey's exclusions are as deliberate as its rows. Three houses discussed in the text, Cedar City's Globe-patterned stage, San Diego's festival stage, and Boulder's campus shell, stand outside the table because this article could document their Shakespeare traditions but not a production of this tragedy with the precision the table demands, and a survey that guesses at its central column is worth nothing. The discipline matters beyond tidiness. The open-air tradition is exactly the field in which souvenir history flourishes, in which every festival town's brochure claims the play for its stage whether the play was staged there or not, and the first service a survey can render the tradition is to show its working: these houses, these productions, these documents, and no others.

Read as a single document, the survey exposes the pattern this article argues. The houses divide into two families. The first family is architectural: the amphitheatres, from Shoreditch to Ashland to Cedar City, which rebuild the original geometry of thrust stage, encircling crowd, and open sky. The second family is locational: the park clearing, the Roman ruin, the lakeside bowl, where the place contributes what no set can, trees, stone, water, and a horizon. The tragedy thrives in both families because both restore its founding condition, a public gathered in the open around a fiction of public life. The survey also records the form's reach across the economics of theatre: a state-arranged park season in London, a four-hundred-dollar civic loan in Oregon, a tent raised by a railway town in Ontario, a free amphitheatre won in court in New York. No two of these houses finance the sky the same way. All of them discovered that the sky, once admitted, starts directing.

## Weather: Atmosphere Against the Elements

The complication must be faced at full strength. An open-air theatre sells its audience a ticket whose terms include the possibility of the performance being stopped by rain, shortened by cold, cancelled by smoke, or played through a downpour that soaks the front rows and turns the prompt copy to pulp. The brief for this article demands that atmosphere be weighed against the elements, and the weighing begins with a fact about the fiction itself: the play's Verona has superb weather. Its catastrophe is scheduled for the hottest days of the year, and Benvolio opens the fatal third act with a forecast: "For now, these hot days, is the mad blood stirring" (3.1). The lovers meet, marry, and die inside a heat wave that the text treats as a contributing cause. A play whose own climate is a clear sky and dangerous heat is, of all the canon, the one whose outdoor staging risks the least dissonance between the weather described and the weather endured. The audience fanning itself with a programme during the duel is not distracted from the fiction. It is corroborating it.

The working theatres have converted that exposure into a code of practice, and the code's fullest statement is the weather policy of the Open Air Theatre in Regent's Park. The theatre's published terms state that the auditorium, stage, and lawn are completely uncovered, that a performance may have to stop and restart, and that a performance is never cancelled before its advertised start time, on the stated reasoning that rain all day may clear in time for the action and that bad weather is often local. Tickets are not refunded when weather cancels a show; they are exchanged, without time limit, for another performance. The same policy page observes that the point at which dusk turns to night is an integral part of the experience, and that some of the most electric performances happen in the wake of rainfall. The figures behind the code are reassuring in aggregate: reporting of the theatre's policy puts the share of performances completed each season at an average of ninety-four per cent. The remaining six per cent is the weather's fee, known in advance, priced into the ticket's terms, and accepted by audiences who return the following summer in undiminished numbers.

Heat levies the afternoon's version of the same fee, and the theatres' guidance to their audiences documents the bargain from the spectator's side. The Regent's Park policy advises its afternoon audience to bring its own shade and warns that the end of an evening performance needs a jumper even in July, since London cools quickly once the sun goes down; the house sells the ponchos and picnic blankets with which its public equips itself. An audience that packs for the weather has accepted the form's premise in its body before the play begins. It will shiver through the tomb scene in the same air that the fiction's Verona, at the height of its summer, never feels, and the shiver is a form of knowledge no thermostat can give: the knowledge that the performance is happening in the world, at a temperature, on a date, once.

Individual entries in the record give the fee its human scale. At the Delacorte in 2007, the first preview of Michael Greif's production, with Oscar Isaac and Lauren Ambrose, was scheduled for 5 June and delayed a day by rain, as Playbill reported at the time; the production then played its free run to the customary queues. At Ashland in 2018, the hazard was not water but air: the Shakespeare Newsletter's review of the festival season records that wildfire smoke and poor air quality forced the cancellation of more performances in the outdoor Allen Elizabethan Theatre than in any previous season, and that the performance of this play the reviewer was scheduled to attend was among them. The elements that interrupt the open-air tragedy have thus expanded, in the documented record, from the shower that soaks a park bench to the regional fire that closes a theatre a county away from the flames. Stratford's founding generation knew the milder version intimately. Alec Guinness, remembering the first season for the Toronto Star in 1968, described rehearsing in a tin-roofed hall where every bird flew in and twittered and every raindrop crashed loudly on the roof; the production and the theatre were ready, he said, largely by an act of faith.

The economics of the exposure were priced at the very first founding in the survey, and the pricing took a form no accountant would predict. Ashland's council, authorizing Bowmer's experiment, stipulated afternoon boxing matches to cover the losses the plays were expected to make; the town hedged its Shakespeare with prizefighting. The hedge inverted within the first season, the plays' profit paying the boxing's losses, and the inversion is the festival economy in parable form: the risk that the sky imposes is real, it can be calculated, and the history of every house in the survey is the history of an audience's willingness to keep paying it. The modern instruments are politer, exchange policies, memberships, subscriptions sold a year ahead, but they are the same instrument, a public underwriting the weather in advance because the evenings that succeed repay the ones that fail.

The original open-air house met the extreme form of the same exposure, and its company's response set the precedent every successor has followed. On 29 June 1613, during a performance of Henry VIII at the Globe, a stage cannon fired for the king's entrance set the thatch of the roof alight, and the house burned to the ground; the eyewitness account preserved in Sir Henry Wotton's letter reports the fire consuming the timbers within the hour, with the audience escaping unhurt. The Globe was an amphitheatre with an open centre, and its flammable crown was the price of its galleries' shelter. The company's answer was not retreat to a safer form. It rebuilt the house on the same foundations, in the same shape, within the year. Fire at the wooden O in 1613, fire at the wooden O in San Diego in 1978: across three and a half centuries the open-air company's instinct is identical, to rebuild the sky into the design and play on. The elements are not an argument the form keeps losing. They are a hazard the form keeps accepting, because what the hazard buys cannot be had under a roof.

### Why did San Diego's Globe acquire an outdoor stage in 1978?

Fire, not philosophy. The Old Globe's own history records that an arson fire destroyed the landmark indoor theatre on 8 March 1978, weeks before the summer's San Diego National Shakespeare Festival, and the company's answer was to build an outdoor festival stage in haste so that the season could proceed.

When that stage was itself destroyed by arson in 1984, it was rebuilt and opened in 1985 as the Lowell Davies Festival Theatre, a permanent open-air house of some 620 seats. San Diego's outdoor tradition is an improvisation that hardened into an institution, the elements admitted by emergency and retained by success.

The weighing, then, comes out as the theatres themselves have weighed it. Weather is not noise around an open-air performance; it is a term of the contract, disclosed in the ticket's conditions, that purchases everything the form exists to provide. A company that cannot tolerate the six per cent fee has a remedy available, a roof, and several houses in the survey have taken it, Stratford most fatefully. A company that pays the fee receives in exchange the one collaborator no designer can hire: a sky whose changes are unwritten, unbilled, and unrepeatable, breaking through cloud during a reconciliation or darkening over a tomb with a timing no stage manager would dare to cue. The elements do not merely threaten the atmosphere. On the evidence of the theatres' own testimony, they periodically are the atmosphere.

## Sound and Space: The Voice in the Open Air

The second practical demand is acoustic. A park or festival amphitheatre seats between thirteen hundred and eighteen hundred people in the open, with no ceiling to return the actor's voice to the back rows and no walls to keep the world's noise out. The question is whether the spoken word can survive the conditions the form celebrates, and the best evidence is instrumental rather than anecdotal. A measured acoustic survey of the rebuilt Globe, published in the Journal of the Acoustical Society of America, found that despite the theatre's open-air construction, the early reflected energy reaching most audience areas produced reasonable levels of speech intelligibility, while achieving an adequate speech sound level was the greater problem, a problem the survey notes is exacerbated by the open-air design and by the house's position beneath the main flight path into Heathrow Airport. That pair of findings, clear words but hard-won volume, is the acoustic signature of the entire form. The shape of an amphitheatre delivers the consonants. The actor must supply the power.

The scale of the demand should be stated numerically, because the numbers correct a lazy assumption. The modern open-air houses are not intimate alternatives to the big indoor stages; several are larger than the rooms in which the play is usually heard. Regent's Park seats 1,304 in the open; the Delacorte seats some 1,800; Stratford's Festival Theatre, the tent's heir, seats more than 1,800; and the standard estimates for the first Globe put its capacity near three thousand, apprentices standing in the yard at the actor's feet and gentry in the galleries above. The tragedy's vocal writing, its formal addresses, its Chorus that must quiet a crowd before it can instruct one, is engineered for populations of this order. A Juliet scaled to a studio theatre is a reduction of the part. A Juliet whose "Gallop apace" must reach the back of a park bowl in the wind is the part at its design size, and the strain audible in the attempt is not a failure of the form but information about what the verse was built to do.

This is why the open-air tradition and the verse-speaking tradition are the same tradition. A line of pentameter is a breath organized for distance; its beat parcels speech into units that carry, and its syntax suspends sense across the line so that a listener twenty rows back receives the completed thought a fraction after the words. The first actors of the tragedy played to a yard and two galleries without reinforcement, in houses whose dimensions the festival amphitheatres roughly reproduce, and the play's crowd scenes, soliloquies, and formal addresses are built at the scale of that delivery. The modern houses divide on whether to assist the voice electronically. Reinforcement, discreetly engineered, can rescue audibility at the bowl's rim; it can also detach the voice from the body that the audience sees, flattening the very presence the shared light exists to create. The acoustic survey's central finding points at the honest middle: intelligibility in an amphitheatre comes from early energy arriving from the stage itself, from the surfaces around the actor, and any system that relocates the apparent source of the voice to a loudspeaker spends the form's one acoustic asset. The voice must remain findable in a person standing under the sky.

The voice also competes with the place's own soundscape, and the competition is older than amplification. The first audiences heard the play against the noise of the city that surrounded the amphitheatre, bells, traffic, the cries of the streets beyond the walls, and the fiction is written by a man who expected that accompaniment: Verona in the text is a sounding city, of watchmen and street brawls, of music at the feast and the lark at the window, and its quietest scene, the orchard, is quiet by contrast with a world the audience could still hear. The modern park reproduces the condition without trying. The Delacorte plays against the hum of New York and the weather in the trees; the London houses play, as the acoustic survey drily records, beneath an airport's approach; Ashland plays against a mountain town settling into its summer night. Regent's Park's own weather page makes the same point from inside the bargain, remarking that the sound of gentle rain in the park is a special thing in itself: the house sells the noise of its own weather as part of the evening's texture. Directors who treat these sounds as contamination misunderstand the assignment. The play does not ask for silence. It asks for a city, and a city is what an open-air audience hears when it listens past the stage.

Space taxes the production in a second way, through sightlines rather than sound. An amphitheatre audience surrounds the action, so no backdrop can be built that does not have its back to somebody; the park stage answers with planting, the Roman theatre with its own ruin, the Elizabethan yard with the tiring-house facade, and all three answers concede the same principle, that in the round and in the open the setting must be a place rather than a picture. The tragedy's settings are unusually equal to that demand. A street, a hall, an orchard wall, a cell, a shop, a vault: the play's locations are announced in speech and furnished by a handful of objects, and a production that trusts the announcements discovers that the largest open-air stage in the circuit needs less scenery than the smallest indoor one. What the space takes in acoustic power it repays in freedom from the pictorial, which is the bargain the bare-stage tradition struck indoors at far greater effort.

## The Aubade at Real Dusk

One exhibit concentrates the whole argument, and it is given a section of its own at exhibit length: the dawn parting of the third act, played by an evening festival as the real light fails. The staging of that scene in its full history belongs to another article in this series; its collision with the actual sky belongs here. Juliet opens the scene resisting a dawn the text insists is not yet present: "Wilt thou be gone? It is not yet near day" (3.5). In an indoor theatre the line is played against a lighting state, blue deepening toward grey, that the audience accepts as the fiction's early morning. In an open-air evening performance the line is played against the sky itself, which is travelling in the opposite direction. The fiction says day is coming. The heavens over the amphitheatre say night is. The audience, which has watched the light drain from the trees through the banishment scene and the potion scene, believes the heavens, and the lovers' argument for one more hour of darkness is conducted in a darkness that is visibly arriving to protect them a moment too late to matter.

The parting scene has, in consequence, three documented weathers in the open-air repertoire, and a complete account of the form should name them. The original weather is the afternoon, in which the dawn is contradicted by a sky at its brightest and the lovers' debate about the lark and the nightingale is a fiction maintained by agreement. The matinée weather, which the park houses still play, reproduces that condition almost exactly, and a spectator who wants to know how the scene worked in 1595 should see it at two in the afternoon with the sun standing on the stage. The evening weather, the festival specialty, inverts the contradiction: the dawn is contradicted by a sky performing the opposite journey, and the fiction's argument that day is coming is refuted by the evidence of the senses in the direction of greater safety and greater sorrow. Indoor staging knows none of these weathers. It knows a lighting state, identical at every performance, and the loss is measurable in the scene's stakes: where the sky is a collaborator, the parting is an argument the audience can check; where it is a cue, the parting is a picture the audience can only receive.

The evening houses know the value of the collision and schedule for it. Ashland's performances on the outdoor stage begin at eight, and the festival built its 2018 production on the stated mirror between the play's progression from light to darkness and the sky's own; Regent's Park's weather policy names the point at which dusk turns to night as an integral part of the experience its audience buys. The Friar's entrance speech, "The grey-eyed morn smiles on the frowning night" (2.3), is in these conditions a small masterpiece of misalignment, a dawn described at dusk by candlelight logic, and the misalignment does not embarrass the production. It certifies it. A text written for the afternoon, when every hour named in the fiction had to be imagined against a contradicting sky, is least betrayed by the sky that contradicts it in the opposite direction. The indoor stage, which can synchronize its lights to the fiction's clock exactly, achieves accuracy at the price of making the audience forget that the clock was ever imaginary. The open-air stage keeps the transaction visible, and the parting scene, the play's purest exercise in wishing the light were other than it is, is where the visibility pays its highest dividend.

The scene's end sharpens the point. The parting is not concluded by the lovers but interrupted from the world outside the window: the Nurse's warning that Juliet's mother is coming, the entrance of Lady Capulet with the day's fatal news, the machinery of the household closing over the night's conspiracy. In the evening amphitheatre the interruption arrives doubled, because the audience's own world is also closing in, the light gone, the air cooling, the walk back through the park or the festival town waiting beyond the exits. The lovers are not the only people whose hour of darkness has ended. When the house lights of an indoor theatre rise, they restore a neutral brightness that belongs to no story. When an open-air performance ends, it releases its crowd into the same night into which Romeo rides for Mantua, and the fiction's weather follows the audience home.

## Festival Shakespeare: A Public, or Tourists?

The strongest case against everything argued so far was made by Dennis Kennedy in his essay "Shakespeare and Cultural Tourism", published in Theatre Journal in 1998 and first delivered as a keynote at the Scaena conference in Cambridge the previous summer. Kennedy, a theatre historian whose subject is the spectator, argues that the postwar festival culture and the heritage industry built around the plays recruit the audience into the identity of the tourist, a spectator whose relation to the performance he characterizes as visiting the exotic, consuming the foreign, watching the great universal show. The tourist's spectatorship is, in his account, ethically uncommitted: the play becomes a sight among sights, certified by its venue, consumed as evidence of having been somewhere, and the festival town becomes a machine for converting a text into an itinerary. His essay's targets include the rebuilt Globe as a commodified heritage property, but the argument covers the whole circuit this article has surveyed, and it deserves the compliment of being answered from the record rather than from sentiment.

The editorial test for such an argument is the one Jill Levenson, the play's Oxford editor, applies throughout her performance history of the tragedy: a production is a reading, to be judged by what it knows of the text and what its conditions let it say. Judged by that test, Kennedy's charge divides the survey against itself. Apply the test to the survey's hardest row and it discriminates cleanly. Verona in 1948 passes it: Simoni's production, mounted in the first summers of Italy's postwar reconstruction with the young Strehler learning his trade in the company, was a reading of the play by the culture and the city that owned its story, an act of repossession performed in a Roman ruin before the neighbours. The Verona of the balcony trade fails it, not because visitors come, but because nothing is read: the courtyard ritual asks the play for a photograph, not an interpretation. Kennedy's essay cannot tell these two Veronas apart, because its category, the tourist, describes the journey and not the attention. Levenson's category, the reading, describes the attention and not the journey, and it is the attention that decides what a festival is.

Consider the foundings. Ashland's festival was a town's own holiday before it was anyone's destination: four hundred dollars of municipal money, a teacher's company of students and neighbours, boxing matches imposed as insurance, and the plays paying for the boxing. The crowd in the Chautauqua shell in 1935 was not consuming the foreign. It was watching its own schoolteacher play Shylock. New York's free Shakespeare is the limiting case in the other direction from Verona: Joseph Papp fought the city's parks commissioner, Robert Moses, in court in 1959 rather than charge admission, and the Delacorte that opened in 1962 charges nothing still. A queue for free tickets is a public assembling, not a market clearing. Against these stands the evidence Kennedy would cite first, and the record supplies it honestly. Fred Adams founded the Utah festival in 1962 with the declared purpose of persuading motorists on the new Interstate 15, bound for the national parks, to stop in Cedar City for the night; the festival was a tourism instrument in its first intention, documented by its own historians. Bowmer himself acknowledged by 1938, three seasons in, that financing his festival required attracting audiences from well beyond the Rogue Valley, and his friend Gordon Claycombe's publicity plan for 1939 targeted the western states and the San Francisco World's Fair. Even Ashland, that is, began courting the visitor almost as soon as it had finished inventing itself for the town. Stratford, Ontario, supplies the case that cuts both ways at once. Patterson's founding purpose, in the festival's own telling, was to breathe new life into his native city's economy after the railway pulled out: the festival was an economic instrument before the first rehearsal, tourism by charter. Yet what the town built with that instrument was a resident company, a repertory, and a stage whose geometry made the audience a participant public, and seventy years of Stratford summers have made the festival the town's identity rather than its attraction. The instrument was tourism; the institution became a citizen. And Verona stages the play in the city of the play, in a Roman theatre, to an audience that has spent the afternoon photographing a balcony the city attached to a thirteenth-century house in the twentieth century, the house itself having been bought by the municipality in 1905 as the relic trade gathered force: Kennedy's mechanism at its most complete, the shrine and the souvenir in one itinerary.

The adjudication this article reaches is that Kennedy identifies the gravitational pull of the festival form exactly, and mistakes the pull for the essence. The documentary record shows the form beginning, at Ashland and at the Delacorte, as a public act, owned by a town or defended in court on behalf of a city's citizens, and shows tourism arriving afterward as the financing temptation of every successful festival, resisted precisely where the house kept the crowd's standing as a public rather than a clientele. The distinction is visible in the architecture. A tourist consumes a view; a public surrounds an action. The amphitheatres in the survey, the yard at Ashland, the tent bowl at Stratford, the free seats at the Delacorte, the benches in Regent's Park, put the crowd around the play in one another's sight, and a crowd that can see itself is harder to reduce to an audience of excursionists than a crowd dissolved in indoor darkness. Where the form fails Kennedy's test, it fails in the Verona direction, when the place becomes the performance and the play its caption. Where it passes, it passes because the sky and the circle keep reconstituting the spectators as the thing the play is about: a city, gathered, watching itself behave. Kennedy is therefore sustained as a warning and overruled as a definition. The festival is not tourism by nature. It is a public by design, with tourism as its standing temptation, and the productions that read the play, on Levenson's test, are the ones whose crowds leave having been a public for three hours.

## The Verdict: What the Sky Restores, and What It Costs

The sky restores three things. It restores the shared light in which the fiction's night must be spoken into existence by actors and audience together, recovering the complicity for which the text's light imagery was written. It restores the tragedy's clock: the brawls in something like noon, the parting against a failing light that no plot can fake, and the Prince's closing sentence on the catastrophe, "A glooming peace this morning with it brings; / The sun, for sorrow, will not show his head" (5.3), spoken under a sky that has either obliged the metaphor by setting or refused it by shining, and either way has taken part in the performance. And it restores the crowd to itself, the visible public whose presence the feud's street world presupposes and whose assembly, in a park, a courtyard, or a festival bowl, is the last surviving piece of the original production that no scholarship has to reconstruct, because it reassembles itself every summer evening at the gates.

The costs stand in the record beside the restorations. The weather's fee, six per cent of a park season abandoned or cancelled on the London figures, with smoke invoiced alongside rain. Voices spent against open space, and the electronic assistance that rescues them at a price in presence. Texts trimmed against the dusk deadline, as when the park's 2021 production cut the last acts hard enough to draw a named critic's public complaint; the cut is the sky's editing, and it has been the form's habit since the abridged exposition versions of 1935. The two trimming traditions should be distinguished, because they indict different things. The exposition's fifty minutes were cut for a strolling crowd's patience, an audience that had not assembled for the play at all; the dusk cut is made for an audience that has assembled for nothing else, and its measure is not patience but light. The first is commerce editing art. The second is a condition editing a text that was itself written under a harder version of the same condition, since the first company also played against the failing afternoon and shaped its two hours of traffic accordingly. The festival cut, in other words, is not a modern vandalism. It is the recurrence of the original negotiation between a long play and a short day, and the record suggests the play has survived the negotiation for four centuries without losing the argument. And the standing temptation Kennedy diagnosed, to let the place play the tragedy while the tragedy poses. None of these costs is imaginary, and a survey that hid them would forfeit the trust its table asks for.

The verdict can be tested against its exceptions, and the exceptions confirm the mechanism. The indoor productions of the play that achieve the force this article credits to the sky are, on inspection, the ones that import the open-air conditions indoors: thrust stages that surround the action with a visible crowd, shared-light stagings that refuse to dissolve the audience into darkness, festival-bowl geometries of the Stratford kind that keep the public in one another's sight. What those productions restore is never the weather. It is the public. The sky is the historical guarantor of the public, the condition that made the first audiences visible to themselves and keeps making modern ones so every time a park darkens around a bench of strangers who have eaten together and will applaud together. Where the guarantee cannot be had, its effects can be simulated, and the simulation's very existence, in the practice of the best indoor directors, is the tribute the roof pays to the yard.

The play's ending ratifies the settlement from inside the text. Verona closes not with the lovers but with the city: a prince pardoning and sentencing in one breath, two fathers competing in golden statues, and a crowd instructed to go hence and have more talk of these sad things. The tragedy hands its final minutes to the public that failed, and asks that public to remain assembled long enough to hear the verdict on itself. An open-air audience is the only modern audience that experiences that ending in kind. It has been a visible public all evening, gathered by a town or a park authority under an open sky, and it disperses into the streets as citizens disperse, carrying the story back into the community that lent the theatre its ground. The indoor audience disperses too, but into a foyer and a night it experiences as individuals. The festival audience goes home as Verona goes home, in company, and the comparison between the two dispersals is the shortest statement of what the open-air form has preserved across four centuries of weather.

Set against them is the comparison the whole record enforces. The indoor stage pays no weather fee and receives no sky. It buys control of every variable and spends the proceeds on a privacy the play was not written for, an audience dissolved into darkness, watching a city of crowds from outside the human condition the city represents. The forms that move the audience through the action, treated in the companion study of [immersive and promenade productions of the play](/2028/06/23/immersive-promenade-productions/), dissolve the public in the opposite direction, into participants. The open-air festival stage keeps the oldest settlement of all: the crowd sits still, in the light, together, and is a public. For a tragedy whose first speech is a prince sentencing a crowd and whose last is a prince begging one to listen, that settlement is not a staging preference. It is the play's home address, and the parks and festivals that keep it have not been preserving a quaint survival. They have been keeping the address occupied.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch open-air survey, and what does it record about each venue?

The InsightCrunch open-air survey is the table in this article that sets the documented open-air and festival houses of the play side by side. For each venue it records the year the tradition begins, the character of the sky above the stage, the tragedy's documented productions in that house, and the specific work the open air does to the play there. Its discipline is exclusion as much as inclusion: houses whose connection to the tragedy rests on legend or undated anecdote are left out, so that every row can be checked against a programme, a production history, or a theatre's own archive.

### Q: When was Romeo and Juliet first performed at Regent's Park?

The play was first performed at the Open Air Theatre in Regent's Park in 1934, two years after Sydney Carroll and Robert Atkins founded the theatre. The theatre's heritage archive preserves the production chiefly for an accident of casting: Greer Garson, then twenty-nine and later a Hollywood star, played in the ensemble. The production began a line that the archive traces through 1971, 1986, 1993, 2002, 2008, and 2021. The tragedy reached the park early in its life: the theatre was two seasons old, and the play took its place beside the Twelfth Night that had founded the house and the Dream whose first production the same young company had given in 1933.

### Q: Who played Romeo and Juliet at Regent's Park in 1986?

Ralph Fiennes played Romeo and Sarah Woodward played Juliet in the Open Air Theatre's 1986 production, directed by Declan Donnellan. Fiennes had made his professional debut at the same theatre the previous year, as Curio in Twelfth Night. The theatre's heritage archive groups the production with those of 1971 and 2002 under the heading of performers seen in the park before they were famous, a pattern the archive treats as one of the venue's recurring functions. The staging was the third of the seven productions of the play in the theatre's documented line, falling almost exactly at the midpoint of the park's history with the tragedy.

### Q: How many productions of the play has the Open Air Theatre in Regent's Park staged?

The theatre's heritage archive counts six productions before its seventh, directed by Kimberley Sykes, opened the 2021 season: the stagings of 1934, 1971, 1986, the production directed by Judi Dench and dated by the archive to 1993, Dominic Hill's production of 2002, and Timothy Sheader's of 2008, with which Sheader opened his first season as artistic director. Seven productions across eighty-seven years gives the park a rhythm of generational return rather than saturation. The count is itself the archive's quiet argument: a park theatre that revives the tragedy roughly once a generation treats the play as a measuring instrument, the fixed text against which each new company, and each new audience of Londoners on the benches, can take its own measure.

### Q: Why does Regent's Park refuse to cancel a performance before its advertised start time?

Because its published weather policy holds that rain all day frequently clears in time for the action and that bad weather is often local. The policy states that a performance may be stopped and restarted as conditions require, but is never cancelled before the advertised start time. The reasoning is empirical and commercial at once: a cancellation announced in the afternoon would strand the many evenings on which the sky relents, and the theatre reports that on average ninety-four per cent of each season's performances are completed.

### Q: What happens to a ticket when rain abandons a performance at Regent's Park?

The ticket is exchanged, not refunded. The theatre's terms state that tickets are non-refundable in the event of cancellation because of the weather, but may be exchanged, without time limit, for seats of the nearest value at another performance in the main repertoire. The same terms provide for abandonment if the temperature during a performance exceeds forty degrees. The arrangement prices the weather risk into the ticket from the start, which the theatre treats as the founding bargain of the uncovered auditorium.

### Q: In which season did Angus Bowmer first stage the play at Ashland?

Bowmer first staged the play in the festival's second season, 1936, directing it himself, and he directed it again in 1937. The first Ashland season of 1935 had consisted of Twelfth Night and The Merchant of Venice, the two plays the city had financed with a loan of four hundred dollars and a stipulation that boxing matches be added to cover the expected deficit. The tragedy's arrival in the second season marks the moment the festival's repertoire widened from the founding pair to the canon it would spend decades completing.

### Q: What idea governed the 2018 Ashland production of the play?

The production was governed by the sky. The Oregon Shakespeare Festival's own description of Dámaso Rodríguez's staging, with Emily Ota as Juliet and William Thomas Hodgson as Romeo, states that it was inspired by the natural mirror of the play's progression from light to darkness. Beginning at eight on a June evening on the outdoor Allen Elizabethan stage, the performance let the real dusk fall in step with the fiction, so that the banishment, the potion, and the tomb arrived with the dark they describe.

### Q: Why were performances cancelled at the Allen Elizabethan Theatre in 2018?

Wildfire smoke cancelled them. The Shakespeare Newsletter's review of the 2018 festival season records that poor air quality from regional fires forced the cancellation of more performances in the outdoor Allen Elizabethan Theatre than in any previous season, and that the performance of this play the reviewer was scheduled to attend was among those lost. The episode extended the documented list of the open-air stage's elemental risks beyond rain and cold to the condition of the region's air itself. The cancellations fell on the festival's flagship house in a season when the outdoor stage carried three productions in repertory, so the smoke closed the Allen Elizabethan repeatedly rather than once.

### Q: Which visiting company opened Verona's fiftieth Shakespeare festival with the play?

The Royal Shakespeare Company opened the fiftieth Verona festival with the play in July 1998. Michael Attenborough's production, with Ray Fearon and Zoe Waites as the lovers, arrived at the end of an extensive tour and played the open-air Roman theatre from 1 to 5 July. Playbill's report of the season noted the symmetry: the production's last performances were given in Verona, the original setting of the story. The festival had itself opened, fifty years earlier, with the same play.

### Q: Who assisted the director of Verona's first festival production of the play?

Giorgio Strehler assisted Roberto Simoni, who directed the Romeo and Juliet with which Verona's Shakespeare festival opened in 1948. Strehler, then at the start of his career, went on to become the defining Italian stage director of his generation and a central figure in the European afterlife of the plays. The detail is preserved in Playbill's history of the festival's fiftieth edition and is a compact emblem of the festival's founding generation: the play's own city gave the century's Italian Shakespeare one of its first apprenticeships.

### Q: How long did the Delacorte Theater wait between its two productions of the play?

Thirty-nine years separated the Delacorte's two full productions of the play in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries' first decades: Joseph Papp's staging of 1968, with Martin Sheen as Romeo and Susan McArthur as Juliet, and Michael Greif's staging of 2007, with Oscar Isaac and Lauren Ambrose. The Public Theater marked the interval in its own publicity for the 2007 production, noting that the tragedy had not played its Central Park stage since 1968. A one-night gala reading in 2012, led by Meryl Streep and Kevin Kline, later added a ceremonial third entry.

### Q: Why did the first preview of the 2007 Delacorte production start a day late?

Rain delayed it. Playbill reported that the first preview, originally scheduled for 5 June 2007, was put back a day because of the recent rain, and performances began on 6 June. The production, directed by Michael Greif with Oscar Isaac as Romeo, Lauren Ambrose as Juliet, Camryn Manheim as the Nurse, and Austin Pendleton as the Friar, then played its scheduled free run through 8 July. The delay is a routine entry in the open-air record and a useful one: the form's weather risk falls on the first nights as impartially as on any other.

### Q: Why are tickets to Shakespeare in the Park free?

Because Joseph Papp fought to keep them free. When the parks commissioner Robert Moses ruled in 1959 that the park Shakespeare would have to charge admission and share the proceeds, Papp took the case to court and won, and the city built the 1,800-seat Delacorte amphitheatre that opened in 1962. The Public Theater has distributed tickets without charge on the day of performance ever since. The free seat is the institutional form of Papp's argument that the park audience attends as citizens rather than customers.

### Q: What did Dennis Kennedy argue about festival Shakespeare in 1998?

In his essay "Shakespeare and Cultural Tourism", published in Theatre Journal in 1998, Kennedy argued that festival and heritage Shakespeare recruits the spectator into the identity of a tourist, whose spectatorship he characterizes as visiting the exotic, consuming the foreign, and watching the great universal show. On his account the performance becomes a sight among sights, consumed as evidence of having been somewhere, and the festival town a machine for converting a text into an itinerary. The essay, first delivered as a conference keynote in 1997, remains the sharpest statement of the case against the festival form.

### Q: Why was the Utah Shakespeare Festival founded?

The Utah festival was founded, on its own historians' account, to intercept tourists. Fred C. Adams, a professor at the College of Southern Utah, proposed a Shakespeare festival in the early nineteen-sixties partly because the new Interstate 15 threatened to divert travellers bound for Zion and Bryce Canyon national parks past Cedar City without stopping. A festival might persuade them to exit the freeway and stay the night. The first season followed in 1962, on a makeshift outdoor platform the company built itself, with a budget under a thousand dollars and an audience of some three thousand.

### Q: How do open-air theatres use the real sunset during an evening performance?

They schedule the tragedy against it. Evening houses such as Ashland's and Regent's Park begin in daylight or long dusk, so that the play's day scenes play in something like day and its night scenes gather real darkness as they proceed. Directors exploit the progression deliberately: the Oregon festival described its 2018 production as inspired by the mirror between the play's movement from light to darkness and the sky's own. The sunset thus becomes an unbilled member of the design team, performing the lighting plot that the text's sequence of scenes already assumes.

### Q: What does an unamplified voice have to overcome in an open-air amphitheatre?

It has to overcome distance without the help of a ceiling. A measured acoustic survey of the rebuilt Globe, published in the Journal of the Acoustical Society of America, found that early reflected energy gave most audience areas reasonable speech intelligibility, while achieving an adequate sound level was the greater problem, exacerbated by the open-air design and by the flight path overhead. The amphitheatre's shape delivers the words clearly; the actor's trained breath must deliver them loudly enough, which is why the form and the verse-speaking tradition developed together.

### Q: Did the first audiences of the play watch it indoors or outdoors?

Outdoors. The tragedy was composed around 1595 for the Chamberlain's Men and first played at the Theatre in Shoreditch or the Curtain nearby, the company's roofless amphitheatres, in afternoon daylight before the Globe was built in 1599. Performances began in the afternoon, the stage thrust into a yard of standing spectators, and no artificial light stood between the fiction and the day. The darkened indoor auditorium in which the play is most often seen is the later invention, not the original condition.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch open-air survey to argue that the sky is a member of the company?

By treating the survey's last column as evidence rather than decoration. An essay can take two houses from different families of the table, the amphitheatre at Ashland and the park clearing in Regent's Park, and show from their documented records that the sky performed identifiable work in each: timing the parting in one, vetoing performances and timing dusk in the other. Set against Kennedy's tourism argument, which the essay should concede at its strongest, the records support the conclusion that the open-air house casts the weather as surely as it casts the lovers, and casts it in a role the text wrote.
