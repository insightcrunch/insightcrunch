The hardest deterrence problem Poland faces is not the one that gets the headlines. It is the problem of fighting below the threshold: how a state answers a campaign of sabotage, jamming, border pressure, and information manipulation that is designed from the outset to stay under the line at which an alliance would treat it as armed attack. Deterring an invasion is conceptually simple, whatever its practical difficulty, because the act would be unmistakable and the alliance response is written down. Deterring deniable coercion is different in kind. The instigator has arranged matters so that no single act clears the bar, so that each incident can be shrugged off as a criminal matter or an accident or somebody else's doing, and so that the defender is left choosing between a response that looks disproportionate and a response that looks like nothing at all.

That choice is the subject of this article. The question is not whether gray-zone pressure is happening; the campaign framing belongs to the cluster pillar, and readers who want the full account of what Russia has been doing across the Polish approaches should start with [the pillar assessment of Russia's gray-zone war on Poland](/2026/12/11/russias-gray-zone-war-on-poland/). The question here is the response side: what actually imposes cost on deniable coercion, which tools work through denial and which through punishment, how a defender keeps a calibrated answer from becoming an escalation ladder, and why the toolkit that deters open war performs badly when it is pointed at a campaign that never intends to cross the line.

![How Poland fights below the threshold, a deterrence and posture analysis of gray-zone response options - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The central argument advanced here can be stated in a sentence. A campaign that depends on ambiguity loses most of its value the moment its acts are attributed and exposed, which makes attribution the sharpest weapon a defender holds below the threshold, and which means the strategic objective of a sub-threshold posture is not to punish each incident but to make the whole class of activity unprofitable by stripping away the deniability that makes it cheap. Call it the deniability-denial rule. It is not a claim that exposure alone solves the problem. It is a claim about sequencing: attribution is the enabling move that makes every other tool, from resilience to sanction to diplomatic cost, work harder than it otherwise would, and a defender who cannot attribute is reduced to absorbing.

## What Fighting Below the Threshold Actually Requires

Start with the structural asymmetry, because everything downstream follows from it. An open attack forces a decision on the alliance. It is visible, it is attributable, it is unambiguous, and the machinery of collective defense is built for exactly that case. The instigator of an open attack knows this, which is why the calculation is dominated by the expected alliance response, and why forward-deployed forces, reinforcement plans, and the credibility of the treaty commitment do most of the deterrent work. Readers who want that side of the ledger in full should consult [the assessment of what deters Russia from attacking Poland](/2025/02/11/what-deters-russia-attacking-poland/), which owns the open-war deterrence question and works through the tripwire logic in detail.

Sub-threshold pressure inverts almost every term. The act is small, the attribution is contested, the legal characterization is arguable, and the alliance machinery is not designed to grind into motion over a severed cable or a burned warehouse. The instigator is not trying to win a war; the instigator is trying to accumulate friction, test seams, gather reactions, and raise the cost of the defender's normal life without ever presenting the defender with a case clean enough to justify a hard answer. Each individual act is calibrated to be beneath the effort of a serious reply. The campaign as a whole may be worth a serious reply, but campaigns are harder to prove than incidents and much harder to respond to proportionately, because the proportionate answer to a campaign looks disproportionate to any one of its parts.

This produces what might be called the aggregation trap, and it is the first thing a below-threshold posture has to solve. If the defender responds incident by incident, each response is necessarily small, because each incident is small. Small responses do not deter a campaign; they price it. The instigator learns the cost of each move and budgets accordingly, and the campaign continues at whatever tempo the budget supports. If instead the defender responds to the campaign as a whole, the response is proportionate to the aggregate but appears wildly out of scale to whatever incident happened to precede it, which hands the instigator a ready-made narrative of overreaction and creates domestic and allied friction the defender did not need. The trap is real and it has no elegant escape. What a serious posture can do is shift the terms: build a public, documented record that establishes the campaign as a campaign before the response arrives, so that when a firmer answer comes it is visibly a reply to a pattern rather than a spasm over a single event.

### What does deterrence below the threshold actually mean?

It means making a class of deniable activity unprofitable rather than punishing each act. The defender works to deny the instigator the ambiguity, the disruption, and the political effect the campaign is meant to buy, using attribution, resilience, and measured cost imposition together, so that the pressure yields diminishing returns over time.

That definition is deliberately modest, and the modesty is analytically important. A great deal of commentary on hybrid pressure fails at the first step because it imports expectations from open-war deterrence, where success looks like an adversary declining to act at all. Below the threshold, success almost never looks like that. The activity does not stop; it becomes less useful. Cables still get damaged, drones still stray, networks still get probed, and unattributed fires still start in inconvenient places. The measure of a working posture is whether those events still produce what they were meant to produce: political disruption, public confusion, demonstrated impunity, and useful intelligence about how the defender reacts. If they produce a rapid attribution, an unimpressed public, a restored service, and a cost the instigator did not want to pay, the posture is working even though the activity continues.

The second requirement is that the response has to be organized around denial rather than reciprocity. Reciprocity is intuitive and mostly wrong here. A defender that answers deniable coercion with deniable coercion of its own is entering a competition on the instigator's chosen terrain, which is exactly the terrain the instigator selected because the balance of advantage there favors the side with a higher tolerance for lawlessness, a weaker attachment to attribution norms, and less to lose from an ambiguity-rich environment. An open society with a functioning legal system, a free press, and treaty commitments is structurally disadvantaged in a race to the bottom of the deniability well. It is structurally advantaged in a competition over evidence, exposure, and resilience, because those are the things it can do at scale and the instigator cannot easily counter.

The third requirement is escalation control, and it deserves more weight than it usually gets. Every below-threshold response carries an escalation coefficient, meaning the probability that the response itself becomes a rung on a ladder rather than a closing of the incident. Some tools have a coefficient near zero: hardening a substation, publishing an attribution, prosecuting a recruited saboteur. Others carry real coefficients: expulsions at scale, sanctions aimed at the security services, cyber operations against the instigator's own infrastructure. None of the higher-coefficient tools is off the table by definition, and a posture that rules them out in advance advertises a ceiling the instigator will simply operate beneath. But a defender who reaches for them without a clear theory of what happens next is not conducting deterrence; the defender is improvising in a domain where the other side has been rehearsing.

## The Posture Poland Has Built and What It Is Designed to Do

Poland's sub-threshold posture is best understood as the accumulated product of a decade in which a series of specific pressures forced specific institutional answers, rather than as a designed architecture rolled out from a strategy document. That matters for assessment, because it means the posture is strong where pressure was felt most and thinner where it was not.

Four layers are visible in the open record. The first is the internal security and counter-intelligence layer, which carries most of the counter-sabotage burden. This is the layer that investigates suspicious fires and infrastructure damage, that runs down recruitment networks, and that produces the evidence packages on which any public attribution eventually rests. Its work is slow, unglamorous, and largely invisible until a prosecution surfaces. Its strategic significance is that it is the engine of the deniability-denial rule; without a functioning investigative capacity, attribution is assertion, and assertion does not carry the weight that evidence does either domestically or with allies.

The second layer is critical-infrastructure protection, spanning energy nodes, rail, ports, telecommunications, and the physical plant that connects Poland to the alliance and to Ukraine. This layer is where the defensive investment is most visible and where the logic is closest to classical denial: harden the target, build in redundancy, shorten restoration times, and the disruption that a successful act produces shrinks toward the point where the act is not worth the risk of conducting it. The policy detail belongs to [the treatment of building resilience to sabotage](/2027/03/11/building-resilience-to-sabotage/), which owns the resilience question and works through the hardening choices at length; the point here is what resilience does deterrently rather than how it is built.

The third layer is the border and migration management apparatus on the eastern frontier, which developed under sustained pressure and which functions as both a physical control system and a demonstration of political durability. Its deterrent contribution is less about the barrier than about what the barrier signaled: that the pressure would be absorbed institutionally rather than producing the political crisis it was designed to produce.

The fourth layer is the information and attribution function, which is the most contested and the least mature. It spans official communication about incidents, the pace and confidence of public attribution, cooperation with allied services on joint attribution, and the harder problem of maintaining public trust in official accounts in an environment where the instigator is actively working to degrade that trust. This layer is where the sharpest tool sits, and it is also where the institutional habits of most Western states are least well adapted, because the incentives inside investigative and intelligence bureaucracies push toward caution, delay, and protection of sources, all of which are reasonable in isolation and collectively fatal to a posture whose central weapon is speed of exposure.

### Is Poland's below-threshold posture defensive or offensive?

It is overwhelmingly defensive by design. The visible architecture concentrates on investigation, infrastructure hardening, border control, and public attribution rather than on reciprocal action. That reflects both alliance considerations and a strategic judgment that an open society competes better on evidence and resilience than on deniability.

The strategic intent behind that architecture is worth stating precisely, because it is frequently mischaracterized as passivity. The intent is to make the Polish operating environment expensive and unrewarding for deniable action: expensive because the probability of detection, investigation, and exposure is high enough to consume the instigator's networks faster than they can be rebuilt, and unrewarding because the disruption achieved is small, brief, and does not generate the political effect it was meant to generate. That is a denial strategy in the classical sense, applied to a domain where classical denial is rarely discussed.

It is also a strategy with a distinctive time signature. Denial in open war is immediate: either the defense holds at the point of attack or it does not. Denial below the threshold is cumulative. Its effects show up in the attrition of recruited networks, the shortening of the interval between an incident and its attribution, the falling political payoff of each new act, and eventually in a shift of the instigator's effort toward targets and methods that are cheaper. That last effect is genuinely double-edged, and honest assessment has to say so: a successful denial posture in one area displaces effort rather than ending it, and a defender who declares victory because sabotage attempts have fallen while information operations have intensified has misread a substitution for a success.

The fourth thing the posture is designed to do, and the one most often missed, is to preserve the alliance's decision space. Every sub-threshold incident is a small test of whether the alliance will treat gray-zone pressure as a collective problem or as a national one. A national response that is measured, well evidenced, and clearly proportionate keeps the incident inside the space where allies can support it. A national response that is loud, thinly evidenced, or visibly emotional pushes allies toward distance, which is precisely the outcome the pressure is designed to produce. A defender working below the threshold is therefore always managing two audiences at once, and the audience that matters more is not the instigator but the alliance.
## Denial and Punishment in the Gray Zone

The denial-versus-punishment distinction is the oldest organizing idea in deterrence theory, and it survives the move below the threshold better than most imported concepts, provided it is adjusted rather than transplanted whole.

Denial in this domain means making the act fail to achieve its purpose. It is not about preventing the act physically, which is usually impossible across a country's worth of vulnerable nodes, but about ensuring that a successful act yields little. A cable cut that is repaired within hours against a routed and redundant network denies the outcome even though the cut succeeded. A jamming campaign that meets a population and an aviation system already adapted to intermittent signal degradation denies the disruption even though the jamming continues. A recruited arsonist who is arrested before acting, or identified within days of acting, denies the impunity even though the fire happened. In each case the physical event occurred and the strategic product did not.

Punishment means imposing a cost the instigator would rather not bear, after the fact, in a way that is connected to the act in the instigator's own accounting. Below the threshold this is available but constrained. The available instruments are diplomatic (expulsions, downgraded representation, formal attribution in multilateral settings), legal (prosecution of recruited actors, indictment of handlers, asset action), economic (targeted restrictions aimed at the entities and individuals implicated), and reputational (the exposure of methods, networks, and personnel in enough detail to burn them). Each of these imposes real cost. None of them imposes cost at anything like the scale of the deterrent threat that governs open war, which is why the punishment leg below the threshold can shape behavior at the margins but cannot by itself carry a deterrent.

The important analytical point is that the ratio between the two legs inverts as you cross from open war to gray-zone competition. In open war, punishment dominates: the threat of alliance retaliation is the load-bearing element, and denial (holding terrain, defeating the initial thrust) supports it by making the punishment credible and the gains uncertain. Below the threshold the weights reverse. Denial carries the load, because it is available, repeatable, escalation-safe, and cumulative, while punishment plays a supporting role by ensuring that denial is not free, that the instigator's networks are consumed rather than merely frustrated, and that the defender is not signaling infinite tolerance.

### Which works better below the threshold, denial or punishment?

Denial does most of the work. Resilience, redundancy, fast restoration, and rapid investigation reduce the payoff of every act at low escalation cost, and they compound over time. Punishment matters as a supporting instrument that prevents the campaign from being cost-free, but its available scale below the threshold is too small to carry a deterrent alone.

There is a further asymmetry inside the punishment leg that deserves attention, because it explains much of the frustration analysts express about sub-threshold responses. Punishment works when the cost lands on the actor whose decision you are trying to influence. In deniable coercion the actor who decides and the actor who acts are separated by design, often by several intermediate layers: a service that tasks, a cut-out that recruits, and a local hire who is paid a few hundred units of currency to photograph a rail junction or set a fire. Arresting and prosecuting the local hire imposes almost no cost on the decision-maker. The person is cheap, replaceable, and was recruited precisely because losing them costs nothing. Punishment that stops at the bottom of the chain is a treadmill.

This is why the burn matters more than the arrest. The strategic value of a counter-sabotage prosecution is not the sentence handed down; it is the evidentiary trail that leads upward, the exposure of the recruitment method so it can be defended against, the identification of the cut-out so it can be disrupted, and the demonstration to future recruits that the pipeline is compromised. A defender who treats each case as a discrete criminal matter to be closed has converted a strategic instrument into a law-enforcement statistic. A defender who treats each case as a source of exposure has begun raising the true cost of the campaign, because recruitment networks are the scarce resource in deniable operations, far scarcer than the money or the targets.

The recruitment layer also explains why societal awareness belongs in the denial column rather than in the soft-power column where it is often filed. A population that recognizes a recruitment approach and reports it converts the instigator's cheapest input into its riskiest. That is denial in the most direct sense: it attacks the supply of the thing the campaign runs on. It is also one of the few interventions that scales without cost and without escalation, which makes it among the highest-value items in the whole toolkit.

A final note on punishment and proportionality. There is a persistent view that below-threshold responses should be strictly reciprocal, matching domain for domain and magnitude for magnitude. The logic is that reciprocity communicates clearly and limits escalation. In practice, strict reciprocity is a poor rule for the defender, for two reasons. First, it concedes the choice of domain to the instigator, who selected the domain because the exchange rate there favors them. Second, it makes the defender's response predictable, which allows the campaign to be planned around it, converting the response from a deterrent into an operating cost. A defender is better served by a doctrine of proportionate but cross-domain response, in which the answer to pressure in one domain may arrive in another, is proportionate in aggregate cost rather than in kind, and is deliberately not fully specified in advance.

## Why Attribution Is the Sharpest Weapon Below the Threshold

The deniability-denial rule rests on a specific claim about the economics of gray-zone activity, and the claim is worth setting out carefully because a great deal follows from it.

Deniable coercion is cheap in absolute terms. The direct expenditure on a recruited saboteur, a drone probe, or an influence account network is trivial next to the cost of military operations. But the campaign is not cheap in the currency that actually constrains it. What constrains it is political risk: the risk of being caught in a way that forces an official response, hardens the target state's politics, unifies the target's allies, and converts a low-cost pressure campaign into a diplomatic and economic liability. That risk is what deniability is purchased to avoid. Every element of the tradecraft, the cut-outs, the local hires, the plausible accidents, the timing chosen to coincide with genuine industrial failures, exists to keep the political risk near zero while the friction accumulates.

Attribution attacks that constraint directly. It does not make the act more expensive to conduct; it makes the act more expensive to have conducted. Once an incident is publicly and credibly attributed, the instigator's cost-benefit calculation on that entire class of activity changes, because the political liability that was supposed to be avoided has been incurred anyway. Repeat the exercise enough times, and the deniability premium the instigator was paying for (in operational complexity, in slower tempo, in accepting less effective methods because they were more deniable) has been paid for nothing.

That is the sharpest edge a defender holds, and it is sharp for an unusual reason: it is the one instrument whose effectiveness the instigator cannot easily degrade. Hardened infrastructure can be routed around by finding softer targets. Border control can be tested at new points. Public awareness campaigns decay. But an adversary who wants deniability cannot make the defender's attribution capability irrelevant without abandoning deniability itself, which means abandoning the whole logic of operating below the threshold. Attribution therefore sits at a structural chokepoint in the instigator's strategy.

### Why is attribution the sharpest sub-threshold weapon?

Because the campaign is purchased with deniability. Credible public attribution imposes exactly the political cost the instigator paid operational overhead to avoid, and it is the one defensive capability the adversary cannot neutralize without abandoning sub-threshold operations altogether, which makes it a structural rather than a tactical advantage.

Four conditions determine whether attribution actually delivers that effect, and all four are institutional rather than technical.

The first is speed. Attribution that arrives months after an incident has lost most of its value, because the political moment has passed, the public has moved on, and the connection between act and consequence is no longer legible to anyone. The instigator's tempo is fast and the defender's evidentiary process is slow, and closing that gap is the single highest-leverage institutional reform available in this domain. This does not mean lowering evidentiary standards, which would be self-defeating for reasons covered below. It means building the capacity to reach a defensible public judgment on a timescale measured in weeks rather than seasons, which is a resourcing and process question more than an intelligence one.

The second is confidence calibration. An attribution that overstates certainty is a gift to the instigator, because a single instance of a claim that later collapses discredits the entire attribution function and hands the information operation a durable talking point. An attribution that understates certainty into meaninglessness delivers no political cost at all. The discipline required is the ordinary discipline of good intelligence assessment applied in public: say what is judged, say the confidence level, say what the judgment rests on in general terms, and say what would change it. Publics handle calibrated uncertainty far better than institutions expect, and calibrated attribution that proves durable builds the credibility that makes the next attribution land harder.

The third is corroboration. Attribution by one state is a claim; attribution by several states, or by a state and its allies acting on a shared evidentiary basis, is closer to a finding. The difference in political cost imposed is substantial, because a coordinated attribution forecloses the divide-and-doubt response that a single-state claim invites. This is one of the concrete places where alliance membership pays a below-threshold dividend, and it is a dividend that requires deliberate cultivation: shared standards, pre-agreed processes, and the willingness of allies to accept some cost for a partner's attribution rather than treating each incident as a bilateral matter between the affected state and the instigator.

The fourth is what might be called the exposure differential, meaning how much of the method is revealed alongside the conclusion. There is a real tension here between two goods. Publishing detail makes the attribution more convincing and burns the method, denying its reuse. Withholding detail protects sources and preserves the defender's own collection advantage. There is no universal right answer, but the balance in most Western practice has historically sat too far toward withholding, for understandable institutional reasons, and the strategic cost of that placement is systematically underweighted. Sources are finite and precious; so is the political capital that drains away when a public sees incidents accumulate and nobody ever says who did it.
## The Below-Threshold Response Framework

The tools available to a defender below the threshold do not form a ladder, and treating them as one is a common and consequential error. A ladder implies that each rung is a stronger version of the one beneath it and that the sensible move after a failed response is to climb. The tools available here are better understood as three functionally distinct families that operate simultaneously and on different timescales: attribution and exposure, which strips deniability; resilience and denial, which strips payoff; and calibrated cost imposition, which ensures the campaign is not free. A posture that runs all three concurrently is doing something structurally different from a posture that escalates through them sequentially.

The framework below sorts the principal categories of response into those three families and rates each on two dimensions that matter for real decisions: the effect it plausibly achieves against a deniable campaign, and the escalation risk it carries. Both ratings are analytical judgments, not measurements, and both are relative to the class of activity under discussion rather than absolute. They are offered as a decision aid, and readers with different weightings will reasonably rate some entries differently.

| Response category | Family | Plausible effect on a deniable campaign | Escalation risk | What it depends on |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rapid public attribution of an incident | Attribution and exposure | High: removes the political-risk discount the campaign was purchased with | Low to moderate | Investigative speed, confidence calibration, willingness to publish |
| Coordinated multi-state attribution | Attribution and exposure | High: converts a contestable claim into a finding and forecloses divide-and-doubt | Low to moderate | Pre-agreed allied standards and process |
| Public exposure of recruitment methods and networks | Attribution and exposure | High: attacks the campaign's scarcest input directly | Low | Willingness to burn collection for strategic effect |
| Prosecution of recruited actors with an evidentiary trail upward | Attribution and exposure | Moderate: low direct cost to the decision-maker, high value as exposure | Low | Treating cases as intelligence products, not closed files |
| Infrastructure hardening and redundancy | Resilience and denial | High and cumulative: shrinks achievable disruption across the whole target set | Very low | Sustained capital investment, not crisis spending |
| Fast restoration and continuity planning | Resilience and denial | High: converts a successful act into a brief inconvenience | Very low | Rehearsed procedures, spare capacity, clear ownership |
| Societal awareness and reporting culture | Resilience and denial | High relative to cost: turns the cheapest input into the riskiest | Very low | Sustained, non-alarmist public communication |
| Signal and navigation resilience measures | Resilience and denial | Moderate: reduces disruption without ending the interference | Very low | Technical adaptation and operator training |
| Counter-messaging and pre-bunking of information operations | Resilience and denial | Moderate and contested: effect is real but hard to measure | Low | Credibility of the messenger, which is the binding constraint |
| Diplomatic measures such as expulsions and downgraded contact | Calibrated cost imposition | Moderate: real cost to the instigator's platform, symmetrically answerable | Moderate | Allied coordination to avoid isolated exposure |
| Targeted economic measures against implicated entities | Calibrated cost imposition | Moderate: cost lands closer to the decision layer than prosecutions do | Moderate | Evidentiary standard sufficient for legal durability |
| Formal attribution in multilateral and legal forums | Calibrated cost imposition | Moderate and slow: builds a durable record and normative cost | Low | Patience and consistency across incidents |
| Restrictions on movement, transit, or diplomatic access | Calibrated cost imposition | Moderate: tangible and reversible, useful as a graduated signal | Moderate | Clear articulation of what would reverse it |
| Reciprocal action in the same domain | Calibrated cost imposition | Low to moderate: competes on the instigator's chosen terrain | High | A theory of the next three moves, which is often absent |

Three readings of that framework carry most of its analytical value.

The first reading is vertical: the top of the effect column is dominated by attribution and resilience measures, and the escalation-risk column for those same measures is near the bottom. That combination is unusual in security policy and it is the strongest argument for the deniability-denial rule. The tools that do the most work are also the tools that are safest to use, which means the binding constraint on a good sub-threshold posture is institutional capacity and political willingness rather than escalation risk. States that describe themselves as constrained by escalation concerns are frequently constrained by something else: slow investigative processes, cautious publication cultures, or an absence of sustained infrastructure investment.

The second reading is horizontal, along the dependency column. Almost every high-effect entry depends on something that has to be built in advance and cannot be improvised during a crisis. Attribution speed depends on investigative capacity built over years. Restoration speed depends on rehearsed procedures and spare capacity. Coordinated attribution depends on allied processes agreed before they are needed. This is the strongest practical argument for treating sub-threshold competition as a standing condition requiring standing investment rather than as a series of incidents requiring responses. A defender who begins building the toolkit when the campaign starts has already lost several years of compounding.

The third reading concerns the bottom row. Reciprocal action in the instigator's chosen domain rates lowest on effect and highest on escalation risk, and it is nonetheless the response that political pressure most reliably generates after a salient incident. That gap between what the analysis recommends and what politics demands is the central management problem of a sub-threshold posture, and it is not solved by better analysis. It is managed by building the higher-effect tools into visible, publicized institutional form, so that a government under pressure to act has something substantive and rapid to point to that is not the highest-risk item on the menu.

## The Credibility Problem in Sub-Threshold Deterrence

Credibility is the hinge of all deterrence, and below the threshold it takes an unfamiliar form. In open-war deterrence the credibility question is whether the defender would really do the enormous thing it has threatened. Below the threshold the threatened acts are small and obviously within the defender's capacity, so the doubt is not about capability or resolve in the usual sense. The doubt is about consistency.

An instigator running a deniable campaign is not asking whether the defender could expel a diplomat or publish an attribution. Of course it could. The instigator is asking a different question: whether this defender reliably does so, at what latency, against what evidentiary bar, and with what allied backing. Those are questions about institutional behavior over many repetitions, and they are answered by the observed record rather than by declarations. A defender with a strong record of rapid, calibrated, allied-backed response has a credible sub-threshold posture even if each individual response is modest. A defender that responds hard to one incident and ignores the next five has an incredible posture no matter how severe the one response was.

This inverts a familiar intuition. In open-war deterrence, a single dramatic demonstration can shore up credibility. Below the threshold, dramatic demonstrations do relatively little and consistency does almost everything, because the instigator is running a repeated game and is optimizing against the defender's average behavior rather than its peak behavior. A campaign is planned around what happens usually, not around what happened once.

### How does a defender build credibility below the threshold?

Through consistency rather than severity. An instigator running a repeated campaign optimizes against the defender's average response, not its strongest one, so a predictable pattern of rapid attribution and measured cost across many incidents deters more effectively than one dramatic reaction followed by long stretches of silence.

There is an important qualification. Consistency in response does not mean predictability in the specific instrument chosen, and conflating the two produces a weak posture. What should be consistent is the fact of response, its latency, and the evidentiary process behind it. What should not be fully specified in advance is which instrument arrives and in which domain, because an instigator that can predict the exact reply can price it into the operational plan and treat it as overhead. The right shape is a declared and observed commitment to respond to attributed acts, coupled with deliberate ambiguity about the form the response takes. That is the mirror image of open-war deterrence, where the commitment is clear and the response is broadly specified in advance.

A second dimension of the credibility problem is domestic rather than external, and it is arguably the harder one. A sub-threshold posture depends on public confidence in the state's account of events. Every attribution is a claim the public has to accept for the political cost to land. Every resilience investment is a budget line that has to survive competing demands. Every reporting-culture initiative depends on a population that trusts the institution it is being asked to report to. An information operation aimed at degrading that trust is therefore not merely one line of pressure among others; it is an attack on the foundation the entire response architecture rests on. This is why the information layer, which looks like the softest part of the problem, is in fact load-bearing for the hard parts.

The third dimension is alliance credibility, and here the picture is genuinely mixed. Alliance membership contributes enormously to the attribution leg, through shared evidentiary work and coordinated statements, and it contributes to resilience through common standards and pooled investment. It contributes much less to the punishment leg, because collective decision-making below the threshold is slow, consensus-bound, and vulnerable to the argument that an incident is a national matter. An instigator who understands this will calibrate acts to fall clearly on the national side of that line, which is one of the sharper design features of a well-run campaign and one of the reasons the threshold question itself deserves separate treatment; the analysis of where the line sits and what crossing it means belongs to [the examination of when gray-zone pressure becomes open war](/2027/02/11/when-gray-zone-becomes-open-war/).
## Applying the Framework Across Four Recurring Categories

The three families in the response framework are not equally useful against every kind of pressure, and a posture that applies them uniformly wastes effort. Four broad categories recur across the open record of sub-threshold pressure on Poland and its neighbors, and each has a distinctive response profile. What follows is a category-level treatment; the specifics of each threat belong to the specialist articles in this cluster, and the purpose here is to show how the response logic shifts across them.

Physical damage to infrastructure is the category where resilience does the most work and attribution is hardest. The acts are cheap, the targets are numerous, the physical evidence is often degraded by the act itself, and the chain from a local hire back to whoever tasked the operation is deliberately long. Because the disruption is the point, hardening, redundancy, and restoration speed attack the payoff directly and immediately, and they do so across the entire target set rather than one node at a time. Attribution in this category is slow and frequently inconclusive, which argues for weighting the resilience family heavily and treating each successful attribution as a bonus that supports the pattern case rather than as the primary response mechanism. The corollary is that response expectations should be set accordingly: a public that has been told attribution follows every incident will be disappointed, while a public that understands the emphasis is on making damage inconsequential will read a fast restoration as the success it is.

Airspace and maritime incursions invert several of those terms. The acts are more visible, the technical attribution is often far easier because the object itself is recoverable or trackable, and the disruption caused is typically modest relative to the political signal sent. Here the exposure family does most of the work, because a clearly attributed incursion carries political cost immediately and does not require months of investigative chaining. The characteristic difficulty is different: distinguishing deliberate probing from genuine navigational failure, since both occur and the instigator relies on the ambiguity. The response question is therefore less about capability and more about the standard of evidence applied and the willingness to state a judgment when the balance of evidence supports one without being conclusive. A defender that requires certainty before speaking will be silent in exactly the category where speaking is cheapest and most effective.

Signal and navigation interference is the category where the response toolkit is thinnest and honest assessment matters most. The interference is often broad rather than targeted, its effects are diffuse, and attribution to a specific decision is complicated by the fact that the emissions may serve purposes unrelated to any campaign against the affected state. Resilience through technical adaptation and operator training reduces the disruption, and multilateral processes create normative cost slowly, but the punitive toolkit has little purchase because the act is difficult to characterize as an attack on anyone in particular. This is the clearest case in which the honest answer is that the defender absorbs and adapts while working the diplomatic and technical channels patiently, and pretending otherwise sets up expectations that cannot be met.

Information manipulation is the category most often misdiagnosed, because the intuitive response, rebutting false claims, addresses the surface rather than the mechanism. The strategic function of sustained manipulation is to degrade confidence in the institutions that the rest of the response architecture depends on, particularly the credibility of official attribution. Rebuttal treats each claim as the problem; the more effective posture treats institutional credibility as the asset being attacked and defends it through the accumulated reliability of official communication rather than through argument. Exposure of the manipulation apparatus, its methods, funding, and coordination, does more than contesting its content, because it shifts the public frame from a debate about claims to a recognition of a campaign. The resilience family contributes through the slow work of building a public that reads confidently rather than through any single intervention.

### Does the same response work against every kind of pressure?

No. Physical sabotage rewards resilience investment because attribution is slow; airspace incursions reward fast exposure because attribution is easy; signal interference has a genuinely thin toolkit and mostly demands adaptation; information manipulation requires defending institutional credibility rather than rebutting individual claims. Uniform responses waste effort in three of the four.

The cross-cutting lesson from that comparison is that a defender should match the response family to where the instigator's vulnerability actually sits in each category, rather than applying a standard package. In physical sabotage the vulnerability is the payoff, so attack the payoff. In incursions the vulnerability is the visibility, so attack the deniability. In interference the vulnerability is normative and slow, so build the record. In information operations the vulnerability is the exposure of the apparatus, so expose the apparatus rather than arguing with its output. A posture organized around this kind of matching will be more efficient than one organized around a single doctrine applied everywhere, and it will also be more legible to the public, which is a benefit in its own right.
## When Below-Threshold Deterrence Holds and When It Fails

Deterrence below the line is not a binary that switches off when an incident occurs. Incidents occur continuously in a functioning gray-zone competition, and treating each one as a deterrence failure produces a permanently despairing assessment that describes nothing useful. A better test asks what the campaign is achieving relative to what it costs, and whether that ratio is moving in the defender's favor or the instigator's.

By that test, sub-threshold deterrence holds under four conditions that recur across the open record of hybrid competition in Europe and beyond.

The first condition is that the payoff curve is flat. When each additional act produces roughly the same small effect as the last, and that effect is well below what a comparable investment would buy elsewhere, the campaign is being denied even though it continues. Flatness is the signature of effective resilience: the instigator cannot find the escalation within the sub-threshold band that produces a step change in disruption, because the targets are hardened, the restoration is fast, and the population has adapted.

The second condition is that the exposure rate is high and rising. When a growing proportion of incidents are attributed publicly, and the interval between act and attribution is shrinking, the deniability premium is being destroyed. This is the clearest single indicator that the deniability-denial rule is operating, and it is measurable in a way most deterrence effects are not.

The third condition is that the political effect is absent. Deniable coercion is ultimately aimed at political outcomes: division between a government and its public, friction between a state and its allies, a shift in policy on some specific question, or simply the demonstration that pressure can be applied with impunity. When incidents occur and the political needle does not move, the campaign is failing at its actual purpose regardless of how much physical disruption it achieved.

The fourth condition is that the recruitment base is contracting. This is the least visible indicator and one of the most important, because it speaks to the sustainability of the campaign rather than its current tempo. A campaign that has to pay more, wait longer, and accept lower-quality recruits is a campaign approaching a resource constraint.

Deterrence below the threshold fails under a different and mostly separate set of conditions, and they are worth naming precisely because several of them are self-inflicted.

It fails when the response latency is long enough that acts and consequences become disconnected. If the typical interval between an incident and any visible official response runs to many months, the instigator faces no meaningful feedback loop, and the campaign is being conducted against a defender who is, from the instigator's decision-making perspective, not responding at all.

It fails when the defender's answer is confined to declaration. Statements of concern, summoned ambassadors, and expressions of resolve without accompanying material consequence are the most common form of sub-threshold response and among the least effective. They cost the instigator nothing while consuming the defender's political stock of things it can say, so that later statements carry less weight.

It fails when resilience investment is episodic. Infrastructure hardening funded in the aftermath of a salient incident and then allowed to lapse produces a defense that is strong at the point of the last attack and weak everywhere else, which is precisely the pattern an adaptive campaign is built to exploit.

It fails when the defender over-responds in a way that generates more political cost for itself than for the instigator. This is the failure mode that gets least attention in commentary that treats hard responses as self-evidently correct, and it is entirely real. A response that fractures allied unity, that is later shown to have rested on thin evidence, or that hands the information operation a genuine grievance to work with has done the instigator's work for it more efficiently than the original act did.

And it fails, most fundamentally, when the defender misreads the purpose of the campaign. If the pressure is preparatory rather than coercive, that is, if it is intended to map vulnerabilities, calibrate response times, and shape the environment for something larger rather than to extract a policy concession, then denying its coercive effect while ignoring its reconnaissance value is a partial success at best. The distinction between a campaign that is an end in itself and one that is groundwork matters enormously for how much weight the defender should place on the response question versus the warning question.

### Can gray-zone deterrence ever be judged successful?

Yes, but by a different standard than open-war deterrence. Success is not the absence of incidents; it is a flat payoff curve, a rising exposure rate, an absent political effect, and a contracting recruitment base. Activity continuing while its returns fall is what a working sub-threshold posture looks like.

The reason those four conditions matter more than incident counts is that incident counts are the metric the instigator can most easily manipulate. A campaign can generate a burst of low-value activity cheaply, producing an alarming statistic that says nothing about effectiveness, and it can go quiet for a period without reducing its long-term intent at all. Metrics that track the campaign's returns rather than its volume are harder to game and closer to what a defender actually needs to know.

## The Escalation-Control Problem

Every discussion of responding to deniable coercion eventually collides with escalation control, and the collision is usually handled badly in both directions. One school treats escalation risk as a reason for near-paralysis, concluding that because any firm response might escalate, the defender should confine itself to absorbing. The other treats escalation concern as timidity, arguing that the instigator's campaign is itself made possible by the defender's fear of escalation and that the fear is therefore the problem. Both readings contain something true and both are incomplete.

The paralysis reading is right that escalation risk is real and that some responses carry it. It is wrong in assuming that inaction is escalation-neutral. A defender that visibly absorbs is teaching the instigator the location of the ceiling, and a campaign that learns the ceiling is high will expand toward it. The relevant comparison is never between a risky response and a safe non-response; it is between the escalation risk of responding and the escalation risk generated over time by demonstrated tolerance. Non-response has an escalation coefficient too, and it is not zero. It is simply deferred and harder to attribute to any particular decision.

The timidity reading is right that excessive caution has costs and that the instigator exploits it. It is wrong in treating escalation dynamics as a bluff to be called. Deniable campaigns are frequently run by services with their own institutional incentives, their own appetite for demonstrating value, and their own imperfect control from above. A response that produces a reaction inside that system may generate a counter-response that neither political leadership intended, which is the classic mechanism by which competitions escalate without anyone deciding to escalate. The problem is not that the instigator is unwilling to escalate; it is that escalation below the threshold can happen without a decision, through institutional reflex on both sides.

That observation points to the practical rule that matters most. Escalation control below the threshold is achieved less by choosing mild responses than by controlling the interpretation of responses. An action that the instigator reads as a proportionate closing of an incident produces a different downstream path than the identical action read as the opening of a new campaign, and the difference lies almost entirely in framing, timing, channel, and whether the defender has communicated what the response is for and what would end the exchange.

### How does Poland respond firmly without triggering escalation?

By making the purpose of each response unmistakable. A measure that is publicly tied to a specific attributed act, proportionate to it, and accompanied by a clear statement of what would end the matter reads as closure rather than as opening. Ambiguity about purpose, not firmness of measure, is what drives sub-threshold exchanges upward.

Three practical implications follow.

The first is that a response should be legible. That means the defender says what the response answers, on what evidentiary basis, and what conduct would make further measures unnecessary. Silence about purpose is the most escalatory choice available, because it forces the other side to guess, and guessing under uncertainty in a competitive relationship tends toward the pessimistic reading.

The second is that the response should be terminable. A measure with no visible off-ramp becomes a permanent feature of the relationship, and permanent features do not deter; they become the new baseline against which the instigator plans. A measure that can be lifted in response to changed conduct retains its deterrent value because it continues to represent a cost that could be avoided.

The third is that the channel matters as much as the content. A response delivered through established diplomatic channels alongside a public statement carries a different signal than one delivered only through public denunciation, because the private channel allows the instigator to absorb the message without the domestic cost of visibly backing down, which is often the difference between de-escalation and a reciprocal move made for face-saving reasons. This is unglamorous statecraft and it is where a great deal of the actual escalation control in sub-threshold competition happens.

There is a final consideration that cuts across all three. The escalation risk of a response is not a fixed property of the response; it depends heavily on the state of the broader relationship at the moment it is delivered. The same expulsion carries different risk in a period of relative stability than in a period of acute military tension elsewhere on the continent. A defender operating a sub-threshold posture therefore needs a live sense of where the broader relationship sits, which is one of several reasons why below-threshold response and strategic warning cannot be run as separate functions by separate institutions that do not talk to each other.
## The Two Schools: Deny Deniability or Impose Cost

Serious analysts disagree about the response question, and the disagreement is substantive rather than semantic. Presenting it fairly matters, because a reader who only meets one school will mistake a contested judgment for a settled one.

The deny-deniability school holds that the campaign's center of gravity is ambiguity, that the defender's comparative advantage lies in evidence and resilience, and that the response effort should therefore concentrate overwhelmingly on attribution, exposure, and hardening. On this view, cost imposition is useful but secondary, and reaching for punitive measures before the attribution architecture is mature is putting the cart before the horse: punishment without credible attribution is either ineffective, because it cannot be publicly justified, or counterproductive, because it invites the accusation of acting on assertion. The school's strongest argument is structural. It identifies the one thing the instigator cannot work around without abandoning the whole approach, and it directs effort there. Its strongest empirical support comes from the observable adaptation of gray-zone campaigns to environments where attribution has become fast and routine: methods shift toward greater deniability, tempo often slows, and the effort required per unit of effect rises.

The calibrated-cost school holds that attribution without consequence teaches the instigator that exposure is survivable. On this view, a defender that names the actor and then does nothing has established that the political risk the instigator feared was overstated, which cheapens deniability rather than pricing it. The school argues that the deterrent effect comes from the pairing, and that the pairing has to be reliable enough for the instigator to model it. Its strongest argument is about the repeated game: a defender who exposes without imposing is running a policy the instigator can absorb indefinitely, because reputational cost decays and operational cost does not. Its strongest empirical support comes from cases in which sustained, materially costly responses have visibly changed an actor's calculus in a specific domain, and from the observation that purely declaratory responses have a poor record almost everywhere they have been tried.

The disagreement is narrower than it first appears, and locating the actual point of difference is more useful than adjudicating a false binary. Both schools agree that attribution is necessary. Both agree that resilience is valuable and underfunded. Both agree that purely declaratory responses fail. The real difference is about sequencing and resource allocation under constraint: when institutional capacity and political capital are finite, does the marginal unit go into building faster and more credible attribution, or into ensuring that attributions already made are backed by material consequence?

The assessment offered here leans toward the deny-deniability school on sequencing while accepting the calibrated-cost school's central warning. The reason for the lean is that attribution is an enabling capability while cost imposition is an applied one. A defender with fast, credible, allied-backed attribution can impose cost whenever it chooses, with legal durability and allied support. A defender with strong punitive instruments and weak attribution cannot use them well, because every application is contestable and every contestation is fuel for the information operation. Capability that unlocks other capabilities generally deserves earlier investment, which is a general principle of force development, not a claim specific to this domain.

The warning that should be accepted from the other side is that attribution unaccompanied by consequence has a shelf life. The first several credible attributions carry real political cost. If the pattern settles into naming without consequence, the instigator learns that exposure is an acceptable operating condition, and the deniability premium partially recovers. The synthesis is therefore not a fifty-fifty split but a sequenced one: build attribution first because it is enabling, but ensure from early on that attribution is reliably followed by something material, even if modest, so that the linkage is established before the pattern of costless exposure sets.

### Should Poland respond harder or smarter to hybrid pressure?

Smarter first, in a specific sense: the highest-return investments are faster attribution and deeper resilience, which are also the lowest-escalation options. Harder responses have a role, but they work best applied to acts already publicly and credibly attributed, where the cost lands with allied backing and a defensible justification.

That verdict carries an implication that cuts against a common instinct. Political pressure after a salient incident pushes toward the visible and the punitive, because those are legible as action. The analysis points toward investments that are largely invisible, that pay off over years, and that are difficult to point to in the aftermath of an incident. Managing that gap is a governance problem more than an analytical one, and the practical answer is to build the invisible capabilities into visible institutional form: named units, published standards, regular public reporting on attribution latency, and periodic accounting of resilience investment. Making the denial architecture legible is what allows a government to resist the pull toward the highest-escalation item on the menu when an incident demands a response.

## The Alliance Dimension of a Sub-Threshold Answer

Collective defense was designed for armed attack, and the gap between that design and the problem of deniable coercion is one of the more consequential structural features of the whole competition. The instigator understands the gap and operates inside it deliberately. Understanding what the alliance does and does not contribute below the line is therefore essential to any honest response assessment.

What the alliance contributes most is evidentiary and normative weight. A coordinated attribution by several states rests on a broader evidentiary base, is far harder to dismiss as politically motivated, and imposes correspondingly greater cost. Shared technical standards for infrastructure protection raise the floor across the whole set of allied targets, which matters because an adaptive campaign hunts for the softest node in a connected system rather than attacking the hardest one. Pooled situational awareness shortens the interval between a pattern emerging in one country and being recognized in another, which is precisely the interval a campaign exploits when it rotates methods across borders.

What the alliance contributes least is speed of decision below the threshold. Consensus mechanisms are deliberately slow, and slowness is a virtue when the decision is whether to go to war and a serious liability when the decision is whether to respond to a severed cable within a politically useful window. A campaign designed to stay national in appearance can generate incidents faster than a consensus body can process them, which means the practical burden of timely response falls on the affected state, with the alliance arriving later to add weight.

There is a further complication that deserves honest statement rather than diplomatic softening. Below-threshold pressure is applied unevenly across the alliance, and states that experience little of it have different incentives than states that experience a great deal. That asymmetry produces genuine differences of view about how much collective effort the problem warrants, differences that are not bad faith but a predictable consequence of unequal exposure. A frontline state seeking allied backing for a sub-threshold response is therefore doing something harder than invoking a shared obligation; it is making a case, and the case is stronger when it rests on documented pattern rather than on a single incident, which is another argument for the evidentiary architecture the deniability-denial rule requires.

### What does the alliance add below the threshold?

Weight rather than speed. Coordinated attribution, shared protection standards, and pooled awareness make a national response more credible and harder to dismiss, but consensus decision-making is too slow for incident-level reply, so the timely response burden stays national with allied reinforcement arriving afterward.

There is one further alliance function that is rarely discussed and may be the most important of all: the alliance sets the interpretive frame within which incidents are read. Whether a severed cable is understood publicly as an accident, a crime, or an element of a state campaign is not determined by the physical facts alone; it is determined by whether the states that matter converge on a shared reading. That convergence is a political achievement, it takes sustained work, and it is worth far more than any individual punitive measure, because it determines whether the next incident lands in an environment of assumed innocence or assumed pattern. A defender that has done the work to establish the pattern reading has changed the political price of every future act without taking any action against any of them.

This is also where the connection between the response question and the threshold question becomes operationally important rather than merely conceptual. An alliance that has converged on a shared reading of a campaign is better positioned to recognize a genuine change in its character, and a defender who has documented the pattern has the baseline against which a departure from it becomes visible. The response architecture and the warning architecture are therefore complements: the same evidentiary work that makes attribution fast is what makes an anomaly legible when the campaign's character shifts.
## The Domestic Foundations of a Below-Threshold Posture

Almost every element of the response architecture terminates in a domestic requirement, and this is the part of the problem that defense-focused analysis tends to skip. The instruments that matter most are not military. They are investigative, regulatory, communicative, and civic, and their effectiveness depends on conditions inside the defending society rather than on anything the instigator does.

Consider what the deniability-denial rule requires in practice. It requires an investigative apparatus capable of building court-quality evidence quickly, which is a question of staffing, forensic capacity, prosecutorial priority, and the legal framework governing what can be collected and how fast. It requires a publication culture willing to accept the residual risk of stating a judgment before every question is closed, which is a question of institutional incentives and political cover. It requires a public that receives an attribution and finds it credible, which is a question of accumulated trust that no security service can generate on its own. And it requires infrastructure operators, most of them commercial, to invest in redundancy and hardening whose payoff is entirely counterfactual, which is a regulatory and financing question rather than a security one.

Each of those is a place where a below-threshold posture can quietly fail without anyone identifying a security shortcoming. A country can have excellent counter-intelligence and still attribute slowly because prosecutorial processes were designed for ordinary criminality. It can have fast attribution and a public that discounts it because trust in official accounts has eroded for reasons unconnected to the campaign. It can have strong regulations and thin compliance because the cost of hardening falls on operators whose commercial incentives point elsewhere. The campaign does not have to defeat the response architecture; it only has to find the seam where the architecture was never actually built.

That observation reframes what an information operation is doing. Read narrowly, it is an attempt to change opinions about a particular question. Read strategically, its more damaging function is to degrade the credibility of the institutions the entire response architecture depends on, because a population that discounts official attribution has neutralized the defender's sharpest instrument without a single act of physical sabotage. This is why the information layer is load-bearing and why treating it as a matter of countering individual false claims underestimates the problem. The defense is not primarily rebuttal; it is institutional credibility built over long periods through accurate, calibrated, non-alarmist official communication that proves reliable when it can be checked.

There is a related and uncomfortable tension around public communication about incidents. Saying more builds the pattern, supports attribution, and prepares the public for the response. Saying more also amplifies the disruption, because a substantial part of the effect of a gray-zone act is the attention it receives, and a defender that treats every incident as major news is doing part of the instigator's work. The resolution is not a formula but a posture: consistent, factual, low-drama communication that treats incidents as manageable events being handled by functioning institutions, paired with periodic pattern-level reporting that establishes the campaign as a campaign. The tone is the substance here. A state that reports incidents calmly and attributes them firmly denies the psychological effect while preserving the political one.

The last domestic foundation is the awareness and reporting culture, and it deserves emphasis because its cost-effectiveness is unusual. Recruitment for low-level tasks depends on finding people willing to accept a small payment for an ambiguous errand. A population that recognizes the approach, understands what it is, and knows how to report it makes that recruitment expensive and risky at essentially no cost to the state beyond sustained communication. The design constraint is that the communication has to avoid producing either paranoia or fatigue, both of which degrade reporting quality. That is achievable, and the models that work generally emphasize normality and civic contribution rather than threat, treating vigilance as an ordinary shared responsibility rather than an emergency measure.

## How a Below-Threshold Response Actually Unfolds

Abstract frameworks obscure the sequencing problem, so it is worth walking through the shape a serious response takes from incident to closure, in general terms and without operational specifics. The value of the walk-through is that it shows where the delays and the decision points genuinely sit, which is usually not where commentary assumes.

The first phase is characterization, and it starts before anybody knows whether an incident is hostile. A fire, an outage, a signal disruption, or a damaged cable begins as an ambiguous event, and the great majority of such events are exactly what they first appear to be: accidents, failures, and ordinary crime. The initial task is not attribution but triage, distinguishing the event that merits investigative attention from the background rate of ordinary failure. This is harder than it sounds and it is where the campaign's design pays off, because acts are chosen to resemble the background. A defender's ability to triage well depends on knowing the background rate precisely, which requires data most states have not historically collected in usable form. Baseline knowledge is an underrated defensive asset.

The second phase is investigation, which is where most of the elapsed time goes. The technical questions are usually resolvable; the harder questions concern the chain from the physical act back through whoever arranged it. This phase produces one of three outcomes: a chain that leads to a state actor with confidence sufficient to act on, a chain that terminates in ambiguity, or a finding that the event was what it appeared to be. Honest assessment requires acknowledging that the middle outcome is common, and a response architecture that only functions when the chain is complete will be idle much of the time.

The third phase is the attribution decision, which is a political judgment informed by an evidentiary one and should not be confused with the evidentiary judgment itself. The questions are whether to state a conclusion publicly, at what confidence, with how much supporting detail, and whether to do so alone or with allies. Each of those has costs. Publishing burns collection. Waiting for allied alignment costs time. Stating high confidence risks the credibility of the function if the judgment later needs revision. This is the decision point where institutional culture matters most and where the difference between a fast posture and a slow one is generally decided.

The fourth phase is the response decision, and by the time it arrives the political environment has usually hardened around expectations that may or may not match what the analysis supports. This is the moment at which the pull toward the highest-visibility option is strongest and the argument for the highest-effect option is weakest, because the highest-effect options are mostly investments that will not produce visible results within the news cycle. A government that has prepared the ground, by publicizing its resilience programs and its attribution standards in advance, has a materially easier decision here than one that has not.

The fifth phase is closure, which is the phase most often skipped entirely. Closure means communicating what the response was for, what it addressed, and what would end further measures, and then actually ending them if the conduct changes. Skipping closure converts responses into permanent conditions, which drains their deterrent value and removes the instigator's incentive to change anything. It also removes the defender's own ability to signal, because a relationship in which everything is already at maximum restriction has no remaining vocabulary.

### How long should a below-threshold response take?

Weeks rather than months for attribution and initial response, if the effect is to register. The technical phase is rarely the constraint; investigative chaining and the publication decision are. States that shorten the interval do it through prepared processes and pre-agreed allied standards rather than through faster forensics.

Two features of that sequence deserve emphasis because they are commonly misunderstood. The first is that the binding constraints are institutional rather than technical almost the whole way through. Better sensors and better forensics help at the margin; prepared decision processes, standing allied arrangements, and clear publication authorities help far more. The second is that the sequence is not linear in practice. Attribution work on one incident overlaps with characterization of the next, pattern analysis runs continuously beneath both, and the campaign is generating new events throughout. A response architecture built to process discrete incidents one at a time will be permanently behind a campaign designed to generate them faster than they can be closed, which is another argument for treating the pattern rather than the incident as the primary analytical unit.
## The Time Dimension: Why a Below-Threshold Posture Compounds

Deterrence of open attack is evaluated in the present tense. Either the posture in place today would give an aggressor pause today, or it would not, and changes to it register fairly quickly, since a deployed brigade is a deployed brigade the month it arrives. Sub-threshold deterrence behaves differently. Almost every element of it compounds, and almost every element of it decays when funding lapses, which gives the whole problem a time signature that budget cycles and political attention spans handle badly.

Attribution capacity compounds because each completed case improves the next one. Investigators learn the recruitment patterns, the intermediaries, the tradecraft signatures, and the evidentiary routes that work, and that accumulated knowledge shortens the interval on subsequent cases. A service that has worked twenty cases attributes faster than one working its fifth, holding resources constant. The same is true of allied coordination: the second joint attribution is far easier than the first because the process questions have been settled, and the tenth is close to routine. This is why investment in attribution architecture pays a return that rises over time rather than staying flat, and why a defender that starts building it early is not merely earlier but permanently ahead.

Resilience compounds differently, through coverage. Each hardened node reduces the set of soft targets available, and because an adaptive campaign selects the softest available option, the marginal value of hardening rises as coverage grows: the first hardened substation displaces pressure to the next one, but the fiftieth begins to exhaust the easy alternatives. This produces a nonlinear payoff curve that argues against the common pattern of funding resilience in bursts after salient incidents. Burst funding produces islands of hardening in a soft sea, which redirects rather than reduces pressure. Sustained funding eventually produces coverage, which reduces it.

Public awareness compounds least reliably and decays fastest, which is why it needs the steadiest attention. Recognition of recruitment approaches fades within a year or two of the last serious communication effort, and populations subject to intermittent alarm develop fatigue that degrades reporting quality below where it started. The models that hold up treat awareness as an ordinary civic habit maintained continuously at low intensity rather than as a campaign run at high intensity when something happens.

Set against all of that is the instigator's own compounding. Recruitment networks, once built, are cheaper to reuse than to rebuild. Mapped vulnerabilities stay mapped. Established narratives are easier to reactivate than to create. A campaign that is allowed to operate without serious friction for several years accumulates infrastructure that outlasts any particular operation, which means the cost of beginning a serious response rises the longer the beginning is deferred. Both sides compound, and the side that starts building first has an advantage that widens.

### Why does starting early matter so much below the threshold?

Because both sides accumulate. Attribution capacity, allied processes, and resilience coverage all improve with use, while an unopposed campaign builds durable recruitment networks and mapped vulnerabilities. The gap between an early start and a late one widens over time rather than staying constant, which makes deferral expensive in a way that is easy to underestimate.

The allocation implication follows directly. If the highest-effect tools compound and the lowest-effect tools do not, the correct bias under budget constraint is toward the compounding ones even when they are less politically legible. Expulsions and restrictions produce their effect and stop; they leave behind no capability. A shortened attribution process, an established joint-attribution arrangement, an additional decade of infrastructure investment, and a maintained reporting culture all leave behind capability that makes every subsequent response better. That is the strongest available argument for treating sub-threshold competition as a standing condition requiring standing investment, and against the instinct to fund it as a series of responses to events.

There is one caution attached to the compounding argument, and it should be stated plainly rather than left implicit. Compounding logic can be used to justify indefinite deferral of any visible response, on the reasoning that resources are better spent on capability. That reasoning is sound at the margin and unsound as a policy, because a defender that never imposes cost has, as discussed, taught the instigator that exposure is survivable and has thereby degraded the value of the very capability it was building. The allocation should be biased toward compounding investment, not devoted entirely to it, and the linkage between attribution and consequence should be established early enough that it becomes an expected feature of the environment rather than an occasional surprise.
## Reading the Instigator's Design Choices

A campaign built for deniability leaks information about itself through its own construction, and a defender who reads those choices carefully learns where to concentrate effort. This is a diagnostic exercise rather than a response one, but it belongs in a response assessment because it tells the defender what the campaign is optimizing for and therefore what would hurt it most.

Consider what deniability costs the side buying it. Working through intermediaries means slower tasking, weaker control over execution, and results that are often crude. Choosing methods that resemble accidents means accepting lower effect, since the most damaging options are usually the least deniable. Recruiting locally for small payments means accepting unreliable people who may not perform, may talk, and may be caught. Spacing acts to avoid a legible pattern means a slower tempo than the resources would support. Each of those is a real sacrifice of effectiveness, accepted in exchange for ambiguity. A defender looking at that ledger should conclude that ambiguity is not a convenience for the instigator but a requirement, and requirements are where pressure works.

The pattern of target selection carries a second layer of information. A campaign that concentrates on targets whose disruption is visible to the public but militarily marginal is optimizing for political effect. A campaign that concentrates on connective infrastructure, transport nodes, and logistics is doing something closer to reconnaissance and shaping, and the appropriate weighting between response and warning shifts accordingly. A campaign that begins probing categories it previously avoided, particularly ones carrying higher attribution risk, is either testing a new threshold or has become less concerned about attribution, and both readings warrant attention. The response toolkit does not change much across these readings, but the urgency and the relative weight given to the warning function do.

A third signal is how the campaign responds to being exposed. If attribution produces a shift toward more deniable methods, slower tempo, or displacement to other target categories, the exposure is working and the deniability-denial rule is confirmed in that case. If attribution produces no observable adaptation at all, one of two things is true: either the instigator has concluded that exposure carries no cost, which is a judgment about the defender's follow-through and a warning that attribution has become a ritual, or the campaign has passed into a phase where deniability matters less to it, which is a far more serious indicator and belongs to the warning literature rather than the response literature.

That last distinction is worth dwelling on, because it is where response analysis hands off to warning analysis. A campaign that stops caring about deniability has changed its character. It may be signaling deliberately, it may be preparing an environment, or the political constraints on it may have loosened. Whatever the cause, a defender whose entire posture is built on denying deniability has just been told that its central instrument is losing purchase, and the correct response is not to push harder on attribution but to reassess what the campaign has become. The question of what that shift looks like and how to recognize it belongs to [the analysis of when gray-zone activity becomes open war](/2027/02/11/when-gray-zone-becomes-open-war/), which owns the threshold question and treats the indicators in detail.

The practical value of reading design choices this way is that it converts the response problem from a reactive one into an analytical one. A defender who only asks what to do about the last incident is always behind. A defender who asks what the campaign's construction reveals about its constraints can direct effort at those constraints rather than at the incidents they produce, which is the difference between managing symptoms and treating the mechanism.
## The Honest Gaps in Poland's Below-Threshold Posture

A response assessment that only catalogs strengths is advocacy. Five gaps are visible from the open record, and naming them is more useful than assuming they have been closed.

The first gap is latency. The interval between an incident and a credible public attribution remains long enough, across most European states including Poland, that the connection between act and consequence is weak from the instigator's perspective. This is not a criticism of investigators, whose standards are appropriate to the criminal process they operate within. It is an observation that the criminal process was not designed to serve a deterrent function on a strategic timescale, and that no separate process with a different tempo and a different evidentiary threshold has been fully established alongside it. The reform that would matter most is institutional rather than technical: an explicit, resourced pathway for reaching a publishable strategic judgment on a timescale independent of the prosecutorial one, with clear authority to publish and clear standards for confidence.

The second gap is the aggregation problem, which remains largely unsolved everywhere. States handle incidents; they handle campaigns poorly. There is no widely adopted mechanism for formally recognizing a set of individually minor acts as a single campaign, attributing the campaign as a whole, and responding to its aggregate weight. Without such a mechanism, the defender is structurally condemned to under-response, because every response is scaled to an incident while the harm is scaled to a pattern. Building a formal pattern-attribution practice, and normalizing it with allies so that a pattern finding carries collective weight, is probably the single highest-value institutional innovation available in this space.

The third gap is uneven resilience across the target set. Investment concentrates where attacks have occurred and where ownership is public. Privately held infrastructure, secondary nodes, and the connective tissue between systems attract less attention, and an adaptive campaign hunts exactly there. The resilience question is treated at length in [the policy treatment of building resilience to sabotage](/2027/03/11/building-resilience-to-sabotage/), which owns the hardening agenda; the point relevant here is that the deterrent value of resilience depends on its evenness rather than its peak, because the instigator selects the softest available node rather than testing the hardest.

The fourth gap is the alliance seam already described: the mismatch between the speed a sub-threshold response requires and the speed collective decision-making delivers. Nothing about this is likely to change fundamentally, since the deliberateness of consensus is a feature of collective defense rather than a defect. What can change is preparation: pre-agreed frameworks for coordinated attribution, standing arrangements for joint evidentiary work, and prior political agreement on what categories of act warrant collective response would substantially narrow the gap without touching the consensus principle itself.

The fifth gap is measurement. States running sub-threshold postures generally cannot say with confidence whether their posture is working, because the metrics in use track activity rather than returns. Incident counts, arrests, and expenditure are all measures of effort. The four conditions set out earlier, payoff flatness, exposure rate, political effect, and recruitment contraction, are measures of outcome, and they are largely uncollected. A defender that cannot measure outcomes cannot allocate effort rationally between the three families in the framework, and will default to whichever family is most legible politically, which is usually cost imposition and usually the wrong answer.

None of these gaps is unique to Poland. Every state facing sustained deniable pressure shows a version of them, and Poland's posture is, on the open record, among the more developed in Europe on the investigative and infrastructure legs precisely because the pressure has been sustained and the institutional learning has been real. The gaps are named here because a posture built by accumulated response to pressure will be strong where pressure was felt and thin where it was not, and the thin places are where the next phase of a campaign will be aimed.

## The Verdict on Fighting Below the Threshold

Three judgments close this assessment.

The first is that the gray zone is deterrable, contrary to a widely repeated claim, but only against a definition of deterrence appropriate to it. The claim that deniable coercion cannot be deterred is usually smuggling in the open-war standard, under which success means the adversary declines to act. By that standard nothing below the threshold is deterrable and the conclusion is trivially true and analytically useless. Under the standard that matters, whether the campaign's returns can be driven below its costs, the answer is clearly yes, and the mechanisms are known: strip the deniability, flatten the payoff, ensure exposure is not free, and do all three continuously rather than episodically. The deniability-denial rule states the priority among those mechanisms, and the reason it holds is structural rather than tactical, since attribution attacks the one thing the campaign cannot operate without.

The second judgment is that the binding constraint on a better posture is institutional capacity, not escalation risk. The framework earlier in this assessment makes the point visible: the highest-effect tools cluster at the low end of the escalation column. What limits their use is investigative tempo, publication culture, sustained capital investment, and the difficulty of measuring outcomes, none of which has anything to do with fear of the adversary's reaction. States that describe themselves as boxed in by escalation concerns should examine whether the real box is administrative. This matters practically, because escalation risk is treated as an external constraint to be endured while institutional capacity is a variable the defender controls.

The third judgment is that the response question cannot be separated from the warning question, and that separating them institutionally is a recurring error. The evidentiary infrastructure that makes attribution fast is the same infrastructure that establishes the baseline pattern against which a change in the campaign's character becomes visible. A defender who has documented a campaign thoroughly enough to attribute it has, as a by-product, built the reference point that makes a shift toward preparation for something larger legible. A defender who has treated incidents as discrete criminal matters has no baseline and will see the shift late, if at all.

A fourth judgment follows from the first three and is worth stating separately because it bears directly on how the problem should be resourced. Sub-threshold competition is a standing condition rather than a sequence of episodes, and the posture appropriate to a standing condition is an investment program rather than a response doctrine. Almost everything that works here compounds with use and decays with neglect, which means the decisive variable is continuity of effort rather than intensity of reaction. A defender that funds attribution capacity, resilience coverage, and civic awareness steadily across a decade will be in a materially stronger position than one that spends more in total but spends it in bursts triggered by salient incidents, because the bursts leave islands of capability that an adaptive campaign simply routes around. That conclusion is unexciting, which is precisely why it needs stating: the analysis points toward patience and continuity in a policy environment that rewards visible reaction, and closing that gap is a governance task that no amount of analytical clarity performs on its own.

What should a reader carry away into a decision or a debate? Three things. That the correct measure of a sub-threshold posture is the campaign's return on investment rather than the number of incidents, and that anyone quoting incident counts as evidence of failure or success is measuring the wrong thing. That the argument between responding harder and responding smarter resolves in favor of sequencing rather than choosing, with attribution capacity built first because it unlocks everything else, and material consequence attached early enough that exposure never becomes visibly costless. And that the most valuable investments in this domain are unglamorous, slow to pay off, and nearly impossible to point to in the aftermath of an incident, which means the governance challenge is at least as hard as the analytical one.

The last observation is the one most worth holding onto. A campaign built on deniability is a campaign that has told you, through its own design choices, exactly what it fears. It spends operational overhead, accepts slower tempo, and settles for less effective methods, all to preserve ambiguity. A defender who understands that has been handed the target. Everything else in the toolkit is support for the move that matters, which is taking the ambiguity away.
## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How can Poland deter gray-zone pressure without escalating?

By concentrating effort on the tools that carry high effect and low escalation risk, which are the same tools. Faster attribution, deeper infrastructure resilience, quicker restoration, and a strong public reporting culture all reduce what a deniable campaign achieves without giving the instigator anything to react against. Where firmer measures are used, escalation is controlled less by choosing mild instruments than by making the purpose of each measure unmistakable: publicly tied to a specific attributed act, proportionate to it, and accompanied by a clear statement of what conduct would end the matter. Ambiguity about purpose, not firmness of measure, is what pushes sub-threshold exchanges upward. A response that reads as closure of an incident produces a very different downstream path than an identical response that reads as the opening of a new campaign.

### Q: Why is denying deniability the sharpest gray-zone weapon?

Because deniability is what the campaign is purchased with. An instigator running deniable operations accepts real costs to preserve ambiguity: operational overhead, slower tempo, cut-outs and intermediaries, and less effective methods chosen because they are more plausibly accidental. All of that expenditure buys one thing, which is avoidance of the political liability that would follow from being publicly and credibly identified. Attribution imposes that liability anyway, which means the premium was paid for nothing. What makes this structural rather than tactical is that the instigator cannot neutralize the defender's attribution capability without abandoning sub-threshold operations altogether. Hardened targets can be bypassed by finding softer ones and border controls can be tested at new points, but there is no way to keep operating below the line while making exposure irrelevant.

### Q: How does attribution impose cost on gray-zone coercion?

Attribution does not raise the price of conducting an act; it raises the price of having conducted it. Once an incident is credibly and publicly tied to a state actor, the consequences the operation was designed to avoid begin to accrue: diplomatic friction, a hardening of the target state's politics, allied convergence on a shared reading of events, and a legal and evidentiary record that makes later measures durable and defensible. The cost compounds across incidents. A single attribution is a data point; a consistent record of rapid attribution changes the expected value of the entire class of activity, because the instigator can no longer assume that ambiguity will hold. That shift in expectation is where the deterrent effect actually lives, rather than in any individual consequence imposed.

### Q: What calibrated responses fit below-threshold gray-zone pressure?

The available instruments are diplomatic, legal, economic, and reputational: expulsions and downgraded contact, prosecution of recruited actors with the evidentiary trail followed upward, targeted restrictions on implicated entities and individuals, formal attribution in multilateral settings, and the deliberate exposure of methods and networks in enough detail to render them unusable. Each imposes genuine cost and none approaches the scale of the threats that govern open-war deterrence, which is why calibrated cost imposition supports a sub-threshold posture rather than carrying it. The design rule that matters most is that responses should be legible, meaning the defender says what the measure answers and on what basis, and terminable, meaning there is a visible path by which changed conduct lifts it. Permanent measures stop deterring and become the new baseline.

### Q: Can the gray zone be deterred at all?

Yes, but only against a standard appropriate to it. The claim that it cannot be deterred usually imports the open-war test, under which success means the adversary declines to act, and by that test the answer is trivially no. Under the standard that matters, whether the campaign's returns can be driven below its costs, the answer is clearly yes. Success looks like continuing activity with falling payoff: a flat disruption curve because targets are hardened and restoration is fast, a rising and accelerating exposure rate, an absent political effect because the public and the alliance are not moved, and a contracting recruitment base because the pipeline has become risky. Activity persisting while its returns decline is what a working posture looks like, and expecting silence instead guarantees a permanently pessimistic and unhelpful assessment.

### Q: How does Poland avoid both under-response and escalation to hybrid acts?

The two errors have a common root, which is treating each incident as the unit of response. Incident-scaled responses are necessarily small, so a campaign simply prices them and continues, which is under-response by construction. Campaign-scaled responses are proportionate to the real harm but look wildly out of scale next to whichever incident preceded them, which invites the overreaction narrative and allied friction. The way through is to establish the pattern publicly before the response arrives, so a firmer measure is visibly an answer to a documented campaign rather than a spasm over one event. That requires a formal practice of pattern attribution, which most states have not built, and it is probably the highest-value institutional innovation available in this domain.

### Q: Why does countering gray-zone pressure differ from deterring open war?

The weights of the two deterrence legs reverse. In open war, punishment carries the load: the threat of alliance response dominates the calculation, and denial supports it by making gains uncertain. Below the line, the punishment available is too small to carry a deterrent, so denial does most of the work through resilience, redundancy, fast restoration, and rapid investigation, all of which are repeatable, escalation-safe, and cumulative. Credibility also works differently. Open-war credibility can be shored up by a single convincing demonstration; sub-threshold credibility comes almost entirely from consistency, because the instigator is running a repeated game and optimizes against average behavior rather than peak behavior. One dramatic reaction followed by long silence deters less than a predictable pattern of modest replies.

### Q: What role does resilience play in deterring gray-zone pressure?

Resilience is the load-bearing element, and it deters through denial rather than through threat. A cable cut repaired within hours against a redundant network, a jamming effort meeting systems and operators already adapted to intermittent degradation, an arson attempt against a facility with rehearsed continuity procedures: in each case the act succeeded physically and produced nothing strategically. That is what denial means here. Its distinctive property is that it is cumulative rather than immediate, showing up over time as a flattening of the payoff curve across the whole target set. Its deterrent value depends on evenness rather than peak strength, because an adaptive campaign hunts for the softest node in a connected system. Uneven resilience redirects pressure; even resilience reduces it.

### Q: How does exposing a gray-zone act reduce its value?

Deniable operations generate value on two tracks: the physical disruption and the demonstration of impunity. Exposure attacks the second directly and often matters more, because the demonstration is frequently the real product. An act that is quickly and credibly tied to its sponsor stops functioning as a display of reach and becomes evidence in a documented pattern, which is the opposite of what it was meant to be. Exposure also burns inputs. Publishing recruitment methods and network structures makes those methods harder to reuse and makes future recruits harder to find, which attacks the campaign's scarcest resource. There is a real tension here, since publishing detail costs collection advantage, but Western practice has historically weighted that tradeoff toward withholding and has underpriced the strategic cost of silence.

### Q: Are hard responses always right against gray-zone pressure?

No, and the assumption that they are is a common analytical error. A response that fractures allied unity, that later proves to have rested on thin evidence, or that hands an information operation a genuine grievance has done the instigator's work more efficiently than the original act did. Reciprocal action in the instigator's chosen domain is the clearest example: it rates lowest on plausible effect and highest on escalation risk, because it competes on terrain selected precisely because the exchange rate there favors the side with less attachment to attribution norms. That said, the opposite error is equally real. Visible absorption teaches the instigator where the ceiling sits, and a campaign that learns the ceiling is high will expand toward it. Non-response is not escalation-neutral; its risk is simply deferred.

### Q: Which gray-zone countermeasures carry the greatest escalation risk?

Reciprocal action in the same domain sits highest, followed by measures aimed directly at the instigator's security services, large-scale expulsions, and restrictions with no articulated off-ramp. What raises the risk is rarely the severity of the measure itself; it is the interpretive ambiguity. A measure whose purpose is unstated forces the other side to guess, and guessing under uncertainty in a competitive relationship tends toward the pessimistic reading. Two further factors matter. Deniable campaigns are often run by services with their own institutional incentives and imperfect control from above, so a reaction can be generated that political leadership never chose. And the risk attached to any given measure depends heavily on the state of the broader relationship at the moment it lands, which changes with conditions elsewhere.

### Q: How does a defender know a below-threshold answer worked?

Not by counting incidents, which is the metric the instigator can most easily manipulate through cheap bursts of low-value activity or deliberate quiet periods. Four outcome measures matter instead. Whether the payoff curve has flattened, meaning additional acts no longer produce step changes in disruption. Whether the exposure rate is rising and the interval between act and attribution is shrinking. Whether the political effect is absent, since coercion aims at division, allied friction, or demonstrated impunity rather than at physical damage for its own sake. And whether the recruitment base is contracting, which speaks to sustainability rather than current tempo. Most states collect effort measures such as arrests and expenditure instead, which is why so few can say with confidence whether the posture is working.

### Q: Why do gray-zone responses so often arrive too late?

The delay is institutional rather than technical almost the whole way through. Technical forensics on an incident are usually the fastest part. What consumes time is the chain from the physical act back through the intermediaries who arranged it, and then the publication decision, which is a political judgment informed by an evidentiary one and involves genuine costs on every side: publishing burns collection, waiting for allied alignment costs weeks, and stating high confidence risks the credibility of the function if the judgment later needs revision. Most European states run this process through prosecutorial machinery built for ordinary criminality, which has appropriate but slow standards. The reform that would matter most is a separate, resourced pathway to a publishable strategic judgment with clear authority and calibrated confidence standards.

### Q: Can legal and economic tools answer deniable coercion?

They can, within limits worth being precise about. Legal action is most valuable not for the sentences it produces but for the evidentiary trail it generates and the exposure it enables, because prosecution that stops at the recruited local actor imposes almost no cost on the decision layer, which selected that person precisely because losing them costs nothing. Economic measures land closer to the decision layer when aimed at implicated entities and individuals, and they carry the advantage of durability once the evidentiary basis is solid. Both depend entirely on the attribution architecture. Without a credible public finding, legal measures are contestable and economic ones are politically fragile, which is the strongest argument for building attribution capacity before expanding the punitive toolkit rather than after.

### Q: What does a calibrated answer communicate to the instigator?

Ideally three things at once: that the act was seen and correctly understood, that the reply is proportionate to that act rather than to accumulated grievance, and that a specific change in conduct would end the matter. The third element is the one most often omitted and the most valuable, because a measure with no off-ramp offers no incentive to change anything and quickly becomes a permanent background condition rather than a live cost. Channel matters as much as content. A measure delivered through established diplomatic contact alongside a public statement lets the other side absorb the message without the domestic cost of visibly backing down, which is frequently the difference between an exchange that closes and one that produces a reciprocal move made for reasons of face.
