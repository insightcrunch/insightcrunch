US consumer protection law has no single founding statute. No act of Congress created a general right to safe products, honest selling, fair credit, or private information for every buyer in every market. Protection arrived instead after particular harms became impossible to ignore: a sweater that flashed into flame, a child trapped inside a discarded refrigerator, a poison stored under a sink without an adequate warning, a loan whose price could not be compared with the price of a rival loan, a credit file whose errors the subject of the file could neither see nor correct. Each harm produced a statute, and each statute reached the industry in which the harm had appeared and stopped at the boundary of that industry. The field therefore grew as a sequence of bounded answers rather than as a code written outward from a general principle, and the sequence, running from the first product statutes of the nineteen fifties to the creation of a dedicated financial regulator in 2010, supplies the structure of the account that follows. The pattern inside the sequence explains both the real strength of the protections that exist and the gaps a reader encounters between one protected transaction and a neighbouring transaction that no statute reached.

![A mid-century American kitchen appliance beside a credit card, a warranty booklet, and a smartphone, arranged on a table](/assets/images/blog/blog-69.webp)

The organising claim of the account can be named in a single phrase: protected by industry, not by right. A buyer in the American system held enforceable federal protection because a purchase fell inside a category Congress had legislated for, not because buyer status alone carried a bundle of federal rights into every transaction. A credit card holder received error resolution rights that a payer by another instrument lacked. A cable subscriber received privacy duties that a reader of books did not receive from any federal statute. A child received online privacy protection that ended at a thirteenth birthday. In each case the decisive legal question was classification: which product, which record, which seller, which channel. The harm a buyer suffered mattered less, as a matter of legal architecture, than the box into which the transaction fitted, and two buyers suffering closely similar harms could stand in entirely different legal positions because one transaction had attracted a statute and the other had not.

The turning points of the sequence mark changes in the instruments Congress trusted, even as the bounded method endured. The presidential message of 1962 gave the scattered field a charter, four rights stated for every buyer, and the labelling statutes that followed tested how far disclosure alone could carry those rights. The decade from 1968 to 1978 then built institutions and substantive codes: a credit code assembled title by title inside a single act, a product safety commission with authority across most household goods, and a trade commission armed with power to write rules for entire industries. The settlement of 1980 reversed the direction of trust, restraining the trade commission and teaching Congress to legislate practice by practice rather than principle by principle. Privacy law developed under that discipline, arriving one industry at a time through cable, video rental, telephone, and children online statutes, and the financial legislation of 2009 and 2010 closed the period by gathering the credit statutes under one dedicated regulator without generalising the rights those statutes contained.

A reader who follows the sequence in order gains a usable map of a field that otherwise presents itself as a shelf of unrelated acts. The map shows why the earliest federal measures governed fabric, refrigerator doors, and package labels rather than products in general, why credit law grew by appending titles to a 1968 statute rather than by fresh enactments, and why a commission created in 1972 could order a recall of a defective toy while holding no authority over the automobile or the pesticide that presented comparable risks. The map also shows why the strongest protections in the system tend to sit inside the oldest legislative settlements, and why each new technology, from electronic fund transfers to commercial email, had to wait for a statute of its own before federal duties attached to the practices the technology made possible.

The evidence throughout is statutory. Public law numbers, effective dates, definitions, and remedies carry the argument, because the pattern under examination lives in the drafting choices Congress made: the product definition that fixed a statute jurisdiction, the exclusion that sent a category to another agency, the private action granted in one title and withheld in the next, the preemption clause that traded stronger state protection for national uniformity. Read at that level, the seventy year record supports a precise conclusion rather than a lament. American consumer law between 1950 and 2019 was extensive, technically sophisticated, and deliberately incomplete, a body of law that protected buyers thoroughly inside named industries and left the spaces between industries to state law, to agency enforcement discretion, and to the common law remedies that the federal statutes had supplemented without replacing.

## The Law Before the Consumer Era and the First Product Statutes

A reader opening the federal statute books in January 1950 would have found no chapter addressed to consumers as a class. No statute declared a general right to safe products, truthful selling, or fair contract terms for household buyers, and no federal agency carried a mandate written around the household buyer as such. Protection arrived in fragments built for other purposes. One fragment policed competition between businesses and only gradually turned toward deception practiced on buyers. Another policed adulteration and misbranding in a defined set of ingestible and applied products. Around those fragments lay state contract, tort, and fraud law, doctrine inherited from the nineteenth century and shaped by assumptions about bargaining between parties of roughly equal knowledge. The consumer era, as a matter of federal legislation, began from that fragmented baseline, and the first statutes of the 1950s preserved the fragmentation even as they added new federal duties.

The shape of the baseline mattered because it determined what a complaint had to look like before any public official could act. A deceptive advertisement, standing alone, was not automatically a federal case. A dangerous household product was not automatically a federal case either, unless the product fell inside a named category such as food or drugs. Injury to a buyer and unfairness in a market were related ideas in ordinary speech, but pre-1950 federal law treated them as separate inquiries, and the distance between the two explains much of the legislation that followed after 1950.

### What did federal consumer law look like before the period opened?

Before 1950 federal consumer law consisted of two narrow pillars without a general statute. The Federal Trade Commission policed unfair competition and, after 1938, deceptive practices through individual cases, while the Food and Drug Administration enforced adulterated and misbranded product rules for food, drugs, devices, and cosmetics.

The first pillar began with the Federal Trade Commission Act, signed on September 26, 1914. The Act created the Federal Trade Commission and, in Section 5, declared unfair methods of competition in commerce unlawful. Congress declined to supply an exhaustive definition of unfairness, reasoning that an enumeration would invite evasion. The Commission therefore received an open textured standard and a procedural model. Proceeding case by case, the Commission investigated a practice, held a hearing, and entered a cease and desist order when the standard and the public interest supported an order. Courts reviewed those orders, and the meaning of Section 5 accumulated through adjudication rather than through a code of prohibited acts announced in advance, denying buyers a predictable list of seller duties.

The competition language also fixed the direction of the inquiry. Section 5, as written in 1914, asked whether a method injured competition, and early Commission practice reflected that orientation even when the conduct under examination involved misleading claims addressed to the public. Deceptive branding drew Commission attention in part because a seller gaining sales through deception diverted trade from honest rivals. The Supreme Court reinforced the competitive framing in Federal Trade Commission v. Raladam Co., decided in 1931 and reported at 283 U.S. 643. The case concerned an obesity preparation marketed with claims the Commission treated as false and dangerous. The Court held that the Commission lacked authority because injury to competitors had not been shown, even though findings supported a conclusion that the preparation could endanger health. Deception of the public, without a demonstrated competitive injury, fell outside Section 5 as the Court read the statute.

Congress answered Raladam with the Wheeler-Lea Act, enacted as Public Law 75-447 and signed on March 21, 1938. The Wheeler-Lea amendments rewrote the operative reach of Section 5 by declaring unfair or deceptive acts or practices in commerce unlawful alongside unfair methods of competition. Commissioner R. E. Freer, speaking in May 1938, described the purpose of the added language as freeing the Commission from any need to prove injury to a competitor. The amendment also provided civil penalties for violations of final orders, and related provisions addressed false advertising of food, drugs, devices, and cosmetics. After March 1938, a practice could be a federal violation because it deceived buyers, whether or not a rival lost a sale. Consumer facing authority, authority justified by harm to consumers themselves, entered Section 5 on that date.

The 1938 expansion had limits that defined enforcement for the rest of the pre-1950 period. The Act created no private right of action for a deceived buyer, so enforcement depended on Commission priorities and on a Commission judgment that a proceeding served the public interest. The characteristic remedy remained the cease and desist order, directed at future conduct, rather than damages for past purchases; monetary redress as a general Commission tool belonged to later legislation. The Commission also lacked broad substantive rulemaking power under the 1914 Act as amended in 1938, so guidance to whole industries emerged from sequences of individual orders and guides rather than from legislative rules binding an entire trade at once. Section 5 after Wheeler-Lea was therefore broad in language and narrow in machinery.

The second pillar was the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt on June 25, 1938, the same legislative season that produced Wheeler-Lea. The 1938 Act replaced the Pure Food and Drugs Act of 1906. Cosmetics and medical devices came under federal authority, factory inspection received express authorization, and injunctions joined seizure and criminal prosecution among the enforcement tools administered through the Food and Drug Administration and the courts. For drugs, the statute introduced a premarket safety gate: a new drug could not be marketed lawfully unless an application had become effective on evidence bearing on safety under the conditions prescribed in the labeling. The statute prohibited false therapeutic claims in drug labeling, while advertising jurisdiction over drugs rested largely with the Federal Trade Commission under the Wheeler-Lea framework.

A catastrophe supplied the political force behind the drug provisions. In 1937 the S. E. Massengill Company marketed Elixir Sulfanilamide, a liquid preparation that dissolved sulfanilamide in diethylene glycol, a toxic solvent. The Food and Drug Administration later recorded that more than one hundred people died, many of them children. Federal officers in 1937 could seize the preparation only through a misbranding theory tied to the word elixir, because pre-1938 law imposed no premarket safety requirement for new drugs. The deaths demonstrated that the 1906 framework punished some false statements after products reached the market while leaving the safety of a novel formulation largely to the manufacturer. The 1938 Act closed that gap for new drugs, illustrating the reactive method of the period: a defined failure produced a defined answer, bounded by the category in which the failure had appeared.

Boundedness appeared throughout the 1938 Act. The statute addressed adulterated and misbranded food, drugs, devices, and cosmetics, leaving most other household products outside any comparable federal safety regime. A defective toy, a flammable textile, and a caustic cleaning preparation raised no equivalent premarket question under federal law in 1938 or in 1950. The Act also stopped short of an efficacy requirement for drugs. Safety under labeled conditions was the 1938 test; proof that a drug worked for the claimed purpose became a federal premarket condition only with the Drug Amendments of 1962. Enforcement remained a market removal system built on seizure, injunctions, and criminal penalties, rather than a compensation system. An injured person looked principally to state tort and warranty law for damages, and state doctrines often made recovery difficult.

State law supplied the background rules for most purchases, and two inherited limitations structured that background. The first was caveat emptor, the maxim that a buyer bore the risk of defects in the absence of fraud, express warranty, or a specific statutory duty. Fraud doctrine and food and drug regulation qualified the maxim, but the maxim expressed a default allocation of risk. Sellers had no general duty to inspect for latent dangers, to disclose unfavorable information, or to design against foreseeable misuse. The second limitation was privity of contract. Warranty claims traditionally required a contractual relationship between claimant and defendant, so a person injured by a product bought through a retailer could face a doctrinal obstacle in suing the manufacturer whose design had caused the injury. Negligence offered a path around privity in many jurisdictions, yet negligence required proof of fault against a distant manufacturer, proof dependent on records and expertise individual plaintiffs rarely possessed.

The combined federal and state structure produced recognizable enforcement habits by 1950. Federal consumer protection proceeded predominantly through agency adjudication and court actions aimed at stopping a practice or removing a product, category by category and respondent by respondent. Standards announced in one case bound the parties and guided the trade by example, but no federal safety commission held authority to survey household products as a field, set standards across product lines, order recalls as a routine remedy, or require premarket clearance outside the drug framework. Congress had legislated against specified evils, adulteration, misbranding, unfair competition, and after 1938 deception, without enacting a general consumer protection or product safety statute. That absence is the starting point for the 1950s, because the statutes of that decade repeated the older method in new subject areas.

The Flammable Fabrics Act of 1953, enacted as Public Law 83-88 on June 30, 1953, addressed burns caused by clothing that ignited readily and burned with exceptional speed. Reports from the 1940s concerning brushed rayon sweaters, described in contemporary discussion as torch sweaters, and long pile rayon cowboy chaps worn by children had made a specific textile hazard politically visible. Brushed rayon raised a loose fiber surface that could flash on contact with a match or a stove, and the interval between ignition and serious injury could be too short for a wearer or a nearby adult to beat out the flames. The 1953 Act prohibited the introduction into interstate commerce of articles of wearing apparel and fabrics for wearing apparel that were so highly flammable as to be dangerous when worn. Congress incorporated a test drawn from a Department of Commerce commercial standard, Commercial Standard 191-53, so the statute operated through a named testing method rather than a general delegation to write textile safety rules. Enforcement rested with the Federal Trade Commission, through exclusion from commerce, injunction and condemnation proceedings, and penalties, with a guaranty mechanism allowing a seller in defined circumstances to rely on a supplier assurance of compliance.

Flammable fabrics legislation protected clothing wearers without creating any federal authority over burn hazards generally. The Act reached wearing apparel and fabric intended for wearing apparel, within a test framed around rapid and intense burning, and the Act did not reach furniture, draperies, bedding, or building materials in the form enacted in 1953. Expansion into interior furnishings arrived through amendment in 1967. A child burned by a sweater fell inside the problem Congress had named, while a child burned by upholstery fabric confronted generally no federal flammability standard at all in 1953. The line was intelligible as a response to the incidents that had prompted hearings, and arbitrary as a map of household burn risk, since ignition sources crossed the product categories the statute kept apart.

The Refrigerator Safety Act of 1956, enacted as Public Law 84-930 on August 2, 1956, addressed a hazard created by a design feature rather than by a material. Household refrigerators of the period commonly used latch doors that could be opened only from the outside. Discarded refrigerators attracted children playing in yards and vacant lots, and a child climbing inside could pull the door shut and become trapped in an airtight compartment. Suffocation deaths followed despite public campaigns urging owners to remove doors from discarded units. Voluntary exhortation failed predictably, because the hazard depended on an owner discarding an appliance and on a child encountering the appliance later, with no transaction between the two and no practical opportunity for warning. Congress responded by making the appliance itself carry the protection: introduction into interstate commerce became unlawful for a household refrigerator manufactured after the statutory effective date unless the door could be opened from the inside through a device conforming to standards prescribed under the Act. Industry compliance converged on the magnetic gasket closure, which yielded to pressure from inside and replaced the mechanical latch as the standard closure for household refrigerators made for the American market.

Refrigerator legislation again drew a boundary exactly around the named product. The statute concerned household refrigerators and the single failure mode of entrapment behind a latch door. The statute conferred no authority over other appliances in which children could become trapped, or over appliance hazards involving electricity, tipping, or mechanical injury. Enforcement rested on Commerce Department standards, the prohibition on commerce in noncomplying units, and misdemeanor penalties. No injured family received a federal damages remedy, and no agency received a mandate to examine household appliances as a group. A door openable from inside removed the lethal feature from new production regardless of owner behavior at disposal, and that success demonstrated the cost of the method: each additional entrapment hazard in a different product required a fresh legislative campaign.

The Federal Hazardous Substances Labeling Act of 1960, enacted as Public Law 86-613 on July 12, 1960, widened the field from a single product to a class of substances while retaining the category method. The Act regulated packages of hazardous substances intended or suitable for household use, a class reaching solvents, cleaners, polishes, adhesives, and fuels whose danger arose from toxicity, corrosiveness, irritation, strong sensitization, flammability or combustibility, or generation of pressure. A package lacking the required cautionary labeling counted as misbranded and subject to exclusion from commerce. Required labeling elements included the name and address of the manufacturer, packer, or distributor, the common or usual name of the hazardous contents, a signal word such as danger, warning, or caution, a statement of the principal hazard, precautionary measures, handling and storage instructions, and first aid information where the statute called for first aid statements. The premise differed from the refrigerator statute. A door could be redesigned so that a trapped child could escape without information, whereas a solvent retained legitimate uses dependent on an adult reading and following directions, so Congress regulated the information accompanying the product rather than the composition of the product.

Information regulation carried limits that the statutory text made visible. A label could warn against a hazard that a ban would have eliminated, and the Act in the form enacted in 1960 relied principally on disclosure rather than on authority to prohibit substances whose risks labeling could not reasonably control. Labeling also assumed literacy, attention at the moment of use, and safe storage after purchase, assumptions that fitted adult purchasers imperfectly and children encountering a stored product hardly at all. Amendments in 1966 transformed the statute into the Federal Hazardous Substances Act and added banned hazardous substance authority. Within the 1950 to 1960 sequence, the labeling statute was the broadest of the three measures and the measure most dependent on consumer conduct. Congress had moved from fabric composition, to appliance design, to package information, selecting a different instrument for each hazard without creating a framework for choosing among design duties, standards, bans, and warnings across household products as a whole.

Read together, the statutes of 1953, 1956, and 1960 established the pattern that organized federal consumer legislation for the era that followed. Congress acted after deaths or injuries had concentrated public attention on a recognizable scene, a sweater flashing into flame, a child trapped behind a refrigerator door, a poison or corrosive stored under a kitchen sink without an adequate warning. Hearings then translated the scene into a product definition, and the product definition became the jurisdiction of the statute. Each measure named a product class, prescribed a test, a device, or a label for that class, and assigned enforcement to an existing institution: the Federal Trade Commission for flammable fabrics, the Department of Commerce for refrigerator doors, and the Food and Drug Administration for hazardous substances labeling. Each measure rested on a claim that a specific article, as designed, made, or packaged, had produced a demonstrable harm.

The omissions were as consistent as the interventions. None of the three statutes created a standing product safety commission, and none conferred general authority to set safety standards for consumer products as a field. None required premarket approval in the manner of the 1938 drug framework, and none authorized routine recall orders, mandatory reporting of deaths and injuries across industries, or a federal private damages remedy. Remedies leaned on exclusion from commerce, seizure or condemnation where a statute provided seizure, injunctions, and criminal penalties, remedies that removed goods or punished sellers without compensating an injured buyer. A hazard falling between definitions awaited a new statute, however closely the hazard resembled one Congress had addressed in a neighboring product.

The contrast with later legislation gives the 1950s statutes their place in a longer account of American consumer law between 1950 and 2019. Reform proposals of the 1960s and early 1970s attacked precisely the features of the 1953, 1956, and 1960 measures: product by product coverage, dependence on a precipitating tragedy, fragmented administration, and remedies centered on market exclusion rather than compensation or systematic prevention. Defenders of the older method, including trade associations appearing in congressional hearings of the period, treated narrow coverage as a virtue, arguing that a legislature informed about one product could regulate with precision while a general commission would burden lawful commerce. Consumer advocates in the hearing records of the following decade answered that a hazard by hazard method guaranteed delay, since proof of harm accumulated through injuries before legislation could begin. Whether protection should remain a series of answers to demonstrated disasters, each confined to the product that had produced the disaster, or should become a standing system for finding hazards before a disaster supplied the evidence, was the question Congress confronted after 1960.

## A Presidential Charter and the Labelling Decade

The consumer legislation of the nineteen sixties acquired a charter before it acquired a code. On 15 March 1962, President John F. Kennedy sent to Congress a Special Message on Protecting the Consumer Interest, a document that proposed particular bills on drugs, food, cosmetics, lending, and packaging while also performing a different and more durable kind of work. The message treated consumption as a universal civic condition rather than a private misfortune. Shoppers in the expanding postwar market bought packaged foods whose contents were hard to compare, borrowed money under contracts whose true cost was hard to calculate, used cosmetics and household products whose testing was uneven, and drove automobiles whose design choices were made far from public view. No single statute reached across all of those transactions, and no single agency held jurisdiction over all of them. The Federal Trade Commission policed deception, the Food and Drug Administration policed food and drugs, the Department of Agriculture policed meat inspection, and state law supplied a patchwork of weights and measures rules, warranty doctrines, and fraud actions. Kennedy offered a vocabulary that could travel across those boundaries, and the vocabulary outlasted most of the individual bills named beside it.

The four rights at the centre of the message were framed as protections owed to every buyer. The right to safety meant protection against the marketing of goods hazardous to health or life. The right to be informed meant protection against fraudulent, deceitful, or grossly misleading information, advertising, and labelling, together with access to the facts required for an informed choice. The right to choose meant access, wherever possible, to a variety of products and services at competitive prices, and, where competition was not workable and government regulation stood in its place, an assurance of satisfactory quality and service at fair prices. The right to be heard meant that consumer interests would receive full and sympathetic consideration in the formulation of government policy and fair and expeditious treatment in administrative tribunals. Each right paired a market failure with a government duty, and each was stated at a level of generality that allowed legislators, administrators, journalists, and voluntary associations to invoke the same sentence for very different programmes.

### What was the Consumer Bill of Rights?

The Consumer Bill of Rights was the name later given to the four protections President John F. Kennedy set out for Congress on 15 March 1962: protection against hazardous goods, protection against deceptive information and access to facts for choice, access to variety at competitive prices, and consideration of consumer interests in government policy.

A presidential message became the organising rhetoric of the era for reasons rooted in institutional arithmetic. A statute binds the transactions it covers and leaves neighbouring transactions untouched, whereas a declaration of rights can be quoted in a committee hearing on lending in one session and a hearing on automobile design in the next without amendment. Kennedy held no majority for a single omnibus consumer code, and the administration programme arrived in pieces, among them the drug amendments enacted in October 1962 after the thalidomide episode had concentrated congressional attention on safety and efficacy testing. The message supplied the connective argument for those pieces. It also shifted the burden of justification. A manufacturer resisting a labelling rule after March 1962 was resisting a named presidential right rather than a bureau proposal, and an agency official seeking appropriations could cast routine enforcement as fulfilment of a White House promise. Rhetoric of that kind did not decide votes by itself, and the message deserves no credit for bills that passed on separate coalitions and separate evidence. The influence was quieter and more structural: a shared heading under which scattered grievances could be filed as one public question.

Follow-through inside the executive branch gave the rhetoric an address. Kennedy created a Consumer Advisory Council in 1962, attached to the machinery of economic advice, and charged private citizens drawn from outside government with advising on matters of broad economic policy affecting consumers. The council embodied the right to be heard in miniature, because it converted consumer representation from an occasional invitation to testify into a standing seat in deliberation, even though it held no power to compel any department to act. President Lyndon B. Johnson gave the arrangement firmer form on 3 January 1964, when Executive Order 11136 created the President's Committee on Consumer Interests and placed the Consumer Advisory Council within that structure. Interdepartmental coordination, complaint handling, consumer education, and the preparation of legislative proposals acquired a recognisable home, modest in staff and budget yet visible enough for the press, for Congress, and for organised consumer groups to petition, criticise, and measure.

The person appointed to lead that machinery embodied the coalition the charter had imagined. Esther Peterson became the first Special Assistant to the President for Consumer Affairs on 3 January 1964 and served as chairman of the President's Committee on Consumer Interests from 1964 to 1967. Peterson arrived with credentials from both the labour movement and the Kennedy administration, having served as Director of the Women's Bureau in the Department of Labor from 1961 and as Assistant Secretary of Labor for Labor Standards, and having acted as executive vice chairman of the President's Commission on the Status of Women between 1961 and 1963. In the consumer post, Peterson pressed the administration programme on truth in packaging and truth in lending, fielded complaints that individual departments had left unanswered, and argued in public that honest labels and honest credit terms belonged to the same policy family. The appointment mattered symbolically as well as administratively. A named official accountable for the consumer interest made the fourth Kennedy right tangible, and Peterson used the office to insist that household purchasing, long treated as a private and largely female sphere of competence, raised questions of national economic policy.

The legislative harvest of the middle nineteen sixties reflected the most tractable of the four rights, the right to be informed. Information remedies attracted broad coalitions because information remedies preserved the transaction while correcting its presentation. A label rule did not set a price, ban a product, or prescribe a design. A label rule told the seller to disclose and left the buyer to decide, which allowed members of Congress with very different views of regulation to support the same bill for different reasons. The labelling statutes that followed therefore carried a double character from the start. Each statute compelled speech that sellers had declined to volunteer, and each statute stopped well short of the judgements that compelled speech was meant to inform. The cigarette law of 1965 and the packaging law of 1966, read together, show how much disclosure Congress was willing to mandate and how carefully Congress limited the mandate at the moment of granting it.

Cigarettes supplied the sharpest test because the evidence against the product was scientific, public, and unusually stark. On 11 January 1964, Surgeon General Luther L. Terry released the report of the Advisory Committee on Smoking and Health, which concluded on the basis of more than seven thousand articles in the biomedical literature that cigarette smoking caused lung cancer and laryngeal cancer in men, probably caused lung cancer in women, and ranked as the most important cause of chronic bronchitis. The Federal Trade Commission responded in 1964 with a proposed trade regulation rule that would have required a strong health warning in cigarette advertising as well as on packages. Members of Congress from tobacco growing states attacked the Commission proposal in severe terms; Representative L. H. Fountain of North Carolina called the decision capricious, arbitrary, unreasonable, and unsupported by facts. Jurisdiction over the question moved from the agency to Congress, and the statute that emerged bore the marks of that transfer in every operative clause.

The Federal Cigarette Labeling and Advertising Act of 1965, Public Law 89-92, was signed by President Johnson on 27 July 1965 and took effect on 1 January 1966. The Act made it unlawful to manufacture, import, or package for sale or distribution within the United States any cigarettes whose package failed to bear, in a conspicuous place and in conspicuous and legible type, a caution statement to the effect that cigarette smoking may be hazardous to health. The Act required no health statement in cigarette advertising for packages labelled in conformity with its provisions. The Act also required regular reporting to Congress, with the Secretary of Health, Education, and Welfare reporting on the health consequences of smoking and the Federal Trade Commission reporting on the effectiveness of cigarette labelling and on advertising and promotion practices, each with recommendations for legislation. The warning was genuine and national, printed on every pack sold in the country, and the reporting duties kept the evidence before Congress on a recurring schedule.

The other side of the bargain was written into the preemption section, and candour about the statute requires both sides to be stated in full. The Act provided that no statement relating to smoking and health, other than the statement required by the Act, would be required on any cigarette package, and that no statement relating to smoking and health would be required in the advertising of cigarettes whose packages complied. In practical effect, states and federal agencies were barred for a period of years, a preemption that ran until 1969, from demanding stronger or different health statements on packages or in advertisements. The statutory declaration of policy made the commercial purpose explicit beside the health purpose: commerce and the national economy were to be protected to the maximum extent consistent with the declared policy and were not to be impeded by diverse, nonuniform, and confusing cigarette labelling and advertising regulations. The tobacco industry gained uniformity, delay, and the soft modal verb of the caution, may be hazardous, in place of the firmer causal language that the Surgeon General's committee had used and that the Federal Trade Commission had proposed. Health gains and industry gains were not sequential concessions around the statute. Health gains and industry gains were the statute, fused in a single text that informed smokers of a hazard in hedged language while forbidding every other level of government from using stronger language.

The Fair Packaging and Labeling Act of 1966, Public Law 89-755, signed by President Johnson on 3 November 1966, applied the information remedy to the ordinary supermarket shelf rather than to a single hazardous product. The Act declared that informed consumers were essential to the fair and efficient functioning of a free market economy and that packages and labels should enable consumers to obtain accurate information as to the quantity of contents and should facilitate value comparisons. For each package of a consumer commodity distributed in commerce, the label had to state the identity of the commodity, the name and place of business of the manufacturer, packer, or distributor, and a separate statement of the net quantity of contents in terms of weight, measure, or numerical count. Qualifying words or phrases that exaggerated quantity were barred from accompanying the net quantity statement. Rulemaking authority was divided along familiar lines, with the Secretary of Health, Education, and Welfare responsible for commodities that were food, drugs, devices, or cosmetics under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, and the Federal Trade Commission responsible for other consumer commodities. Additional regulations could address deceptive size characterisations, claims of price advantage based on package size, ingredient listing for many nonfood commodities, and the nonfunctional slack filling of packages, where a container was filled substantially below capacity for reasons other than protection of the contents or the requirements of packaging machinery.

The limits of the packaging statute were structural, and the limits were visible in the text rather than imposed by later neglect. The definition of consumer commodity expressly excluded meats, poultry, and tobacco, along with prescription drugs, alcoholic beverages, agricultural and vegetable seeds, and certain poisons and animal biologics, categories governed by other statutes or left to other regimes. The more consequential disappointment concerned package sizes. Campaigners for truth in packaging, Peterson among them, had confronted shoppers with shelves of odd weights and fractional quantities that defeated mental arithmetic and made unit comparison a chore, and many reform proposals contemplated a restricted set of standard sizes for staple commodities. Congress chose a voluntary route instead. The Act directed the Secretary of Commerce, upon finding undue proliferation of weights, measures, or quantities that impaired value comparison, to request manufacturers, packers, and distributors to participate in developing voluntary product standards, and the Act expressly withheld any construction that would authorise a limitation on the size, shape, weight, dimensions, or number of packages used to enclose a commodity. Disclosure of quantity was mandatory. Rationalisation of quantity was a request. A shopper after 1966 could read the net weight on every covered package with greater confidence and confront almost as many competing sizes as before.

While Congress refined the label, a parallel argument held that some hazards could not be read away. Traffic deaths had risen by nearly thirty percent between 1960 and 1965, and the dominant explanations that blamed careless drivers sat uneasily beside evidence that vehicle design converted survivable collisions into fatal ones. Ralph Nader, a young lawyer who had studied automobile litigation, published Unsafe at Any Speed: The Designed-In Dangers of the American Automobile in November 1965. The opening chapter examined the Chevrolet Corvair and its rear suspension and handling characteristics, and later chapters ranged across dashboards, steering assemblies, windshields, and an industry design culture that Nader charged with subordinating safety to styling, power, and annual model change. General Motors responded in part by hiring investigators to follow Nader and probe his private life, an episode aired in congressional hearings in 1966 that damaged the company far more than the book alone had done and that ended, after litigation, in a settlement for invasion of privacy. The substantive claim of the book survived the scandal intact: buyers could not inspect crashworthiness in a showroom, and no label affixed to a dashboard could disclose the behaviour of a suspension under stress.

Congress answered that claim with standards rather than warnings. The National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act of 1966, signed by President Johnson on 9 September 1966 alongside companion highway safety legislation, directed the federal government to establish motor vehicle safety standards protecting the public against unreasonable risk of accidents arising from design, construction, or performance, and against unreasonable risk of death or injury when accidents occurred. Administration began in the Department of Commerce and passed shortly afterward to the new Department of Transportation. The distinction from the labelling statutes was one of kind rather than degree. A standard governed the product whether or not any buyer understood it, comprehended the underlying engineering, or had a safer alternative within reach at a comparable price. Disclosure assumed that the decisive moment was the moment of purchase and that an informed buyer would discipline the market. Performance standards assumed that the decisive moment might be the moment of collision, years after purchase, involving passengers and bystanders who had chosen nothing at all. The automobile statute therefore marked the point at which the consumer programme of the nineteen sixties outgrew the information remedy that had supplied its first statutes, without abandoning information as one instrument among several.

The bridge from episodic safety statutes to a general product safety regime was built by a study commission. Public Law 90-146, enacted on 20 November 1967, created the National Commission on Product Safety and charged the commission with a comprehensive study of the scope and adequacy of measures protecting consumers against unreasonable risks of injury associated with hazardous household products. President Johnson appointed the seven member bipartisan commission in March 1968. The questions put to the commissioners reached beyond any single product category: the measure of exposure to unreasonable hazards, the categories presenting such hazards, the adequacy of industry self regulation, and the adequacy of federal, state, and local authority and of the common law. The commission delivered its final report to the President and to Congress in 1970 and concluded that the public faced many unreasonably dangerous products and that product liability litigation, state and local regulation, industry self regulation, and earlier federal safety laws had failed to supply adequate protection. The central recommendation was institutional: an independent federal Consumer Product Safety Commission empowered to set mandatory consumer product safety standards, to secure compliance through a range of methods and sanctions, to gather and publish injury information through a clearinghouse, and to bar noncomplying imports. Congress enacted the core of that recommendation in the Consumer Product Safety Act of 1972, and the commission model of investigation first and architecture afterward closed the decade that the Kennedy message had opened.

The governing idea of the labelling era can be stated plainly. Honest information, prominently displayed and uniformly required, would convert private confusion into public discipline, because a buyer equipped with facts would reward the honest seller and desert the deceptive one, and competition would then enforce standards that government had merely described. The statutes of 1962 to 1967 gave that idea durable form, in a presidential charter with no legal force of its own, in a cigarette caution hedged by preemption, and in a packaging law that standardised statements while leaving package sizes to voluntary agreement. The limits of the idea were inscribed in its premises. Information remedies assumed a reader with time to read, literacy and numeracy sufficient for comparison, alternatives genuinely available at prices within reach, and a hazard that knowledge alone could avoid. A shopper choosing between two detergents met those assumptions tolerably well. A smoker confronting addiction, a passenger in a borrowed automobile, and a parent buying a toy whose defect was invisible met those assumptions hardly at all. The decade therefore informed consumers far more than it empowered consumers, and the imbalance was not an accident of drafting but the predictable result of choosing the least coercive remedy first and discovering, product by product, where coercion alone would serve. The credit and safety statutes of 1968 to 1977 took up that tension directly, pairing disclosure with substantive prohibitions, private rights of action, and agencies built to set standards rather than merely to publish facts.

## The Credit Statutes Build a Code Inside One Act

Much of the federal law of consumer credit lives inside a single statute enacted on 29 May 1968. The Consumer Credit Protection Act, Public Law 90-321, began as a disclosure measure with several companion titles, and over the following decade Congress treated the Act as an open vessel. When a new credit problem reached the legislative agenda, the response was another title, numbered in sequence and inserted into the 1968 Act. Between 1968 and 1978 that habit produced a disclosure law for loans, an accuracy law for credit reports, an antidiscrimination law for credit decisions, a conduct law for debt collectors, and an error resolution law for electronic transfers. The arrangement was not planned as a system in 1968. It accumulated, and the accumulation shaped each new protection, because each title inherited the definitions and the enforcement habits of the titles that preceded it.

The original Act contained more than its famous first title. Title I carried the Truth in Lending Act. Title II addressed extortionate credit transactions through amendments to the federal criminal code. Title III restricted the garnishment of wages and barred discharge of an employee by reason of garnishment for a single indebtedness. Title IV created the National Commission on Consumer Finance to study the consumer finance industry and report on the need for further regulation. Title V held general provisions, among them a severability clause. The numbering left room: a statute ending at Title V could receive a Title VI, and then a Title VII, without disturbing text that lenders, regulators, and courts had already learned to use.

Title I, the Truth in Lending Act, answered a specific failure of the pre-1968 credit market: a borrower could not reliably compare the price of one loan with the price of another. Creditors quoted the cost of credit in forms that resisted comparison. One lender stated a monthly rate, another stated an add-on charge computed on the original balance for the full term, and another stated a discount rate. The hearings that preceded enactment, conducted over several Congresses and associated in the Senate first with Senator Paul Douglas of Illinois and then with Senator William Proxmire of Wisconsin, returned repeatedly to the arithmetic problem. A rate stated on a declining balance and a rate stated on the original balance could describe identical dollars of cost in numbers that looked far apart, and a borrower quoted the smaller number had no dependable way to know that two offers cost the same.

Congress answered with standardization rather than price control. The Truth in Lending Act set no ceiling on the price of consumer credit and left the substantive terms of loans largely to contract and state law. Instead, the statute required a creditor to disclose, in a uniform vocabulary, the finance charge and the annual percentage rate. The finance charge captured, in dollars, the sum of charges imposed by the creditor as an incident of the extension of credit. The annual percentage rate expressed that finance charge as a yearly rate computed by the actuarial method prescribed in the statute. The Federal Reserve Board issued the implementing regulation as Regulation Z, and the central chapters of the title took effect on 1 July 1969.

The disclosure scheme distinguished between closed end and open end credit because the two products presented different information problems. For a closed end transaction, such as an installment loan with a fixed number of payments, the creditor disclosed the amount financed, the finance charge, the annual percentage rate, the number and amount of payments, and the total of payments. For open end credit, such as a revolving charge account, the creditor disclosed the periodic rate and the method of determining the balance on which a finance charge was computed. Advertising received its own chapter, built on trigger terms: an advertisement that stated one term from a prescribed list had to state the additional terms needed to keep the single figure from misleading a reader.

Enforcement of Title I combined administrative supervision with a private damages remedy scaled to the finance charge, together with criminal liability for willful and knowing violations. A creditor could price credit expensively and comply fully with Title I, provided the price was stated in the prescribed form, and could price credit cheaply and violate Title I by stating the price in the wrong unit. The statute regulated the language of the bargain rather than the bargain, and [the Truth in Lending Act guide](/2018/10/15/truth-in-lending-act-1968-guide/) traces how that decision, disclosure in place of rate control, governed the growth of the title. Amendments approved on 26 October 1970 as part of Public Law 91-508 illustrated the title in motion, barring unsolicited credit cards and capping cardholder liability for unauthorized use of an accepted credit card at fifty dollars, the first of many occasions on which Congress repaired a credit problem by editing the 1968 Act rather than by writing on a clean page.

### Why did consumer credit law grow title by title inside one act?

Congress reused the 1968 Act because each new credit problem arrived separately, shared the same committees, definitions, and enforcement machinery, and fit an existing consumer credit code more easily than a freestanding statute, so legislators appended a numbered title and left the earlier titles undisturbed in place.

The mechanics behind that answer were practical. Consumer credit bills moved through the Senate Committee on Banking, Housing, and Urban Affairs and the House Committee on Banking, Finance, and Urban Affairs, and the staff and witnesses for one credit bill reappeared for the next. A new title could borrow settled definitions of creditor, consumer, and consumer credit, and could assign enforcement to agencies already supervising the earlier titles. A freestanding statute invited a general fight over the federal role in credit markets, while an amendment to an act that banks already obeyed presented the new protection as a completion of accepted work.

The Fair Credit Reporting Act arrived by that route. By the late 1960s, reporting agencies assembled files on millions of consumers and sold reports to creditors, insurers, and employers. The subject of a file often had no right to see the file, no notice that a report had caused a denial of credit, and no procedure for correcting an error that might belong to a different person with a similar name. The harm differed from the harm addressed by Title I. Disclosure at the moment of lending could not cure a false record compiled years earlier by a business the borrower had never dealt with, so the new problem required regulation of the record itself.

Public Law 91-508, approved on 26 October 1970, was principally a banking measure, and Title VI of that public law amended the Consumer Credit Protection Act by adding a new Title VI, the Fair Credit Reporting Act, which took effect upon the expiration of one hundred and eighty days following enactment. The new title defined its central product with care. A consumer report was a communication by a consumer reporting agency bearing on creditworthiness, credit standing, credit capacity, character, general reputation, personal characteristics, or mode of living, used or expected to be used in establishing eligibility for credit, insurance, or employment.

The first set of rules governed release. Section 604 listed the permissible purposes for which a reporting agency could furnish a consumer report, confining lawful distribution to defined occasions such as a credit transaction involving the consumer, underwriting of insurance, an employment purpose, a court order, or the written instructions of the consumer. A report furnished for no permissible purpose was unlawful regardless of accuracy. The second set of rules governed contents. Section 605 barred specified obsolete information, in general preventing most adverse items from being reported after seven years, with a longer period allowed for cases under the bankruptcy laws. The third set governed process inside the agency. Section 607 required reasonable procedures to limit reports to persons with a permissible purpose and to assure the maximum possible accuracy of consumer reports.

The fourth set of rules gave the consumer a procedural foothold. A user of a report who took adverse action based in whole or in part on the report had to notify the consumer and identify the reporting agency, converting a silent denial into a traceable event. The consumer could obtain disclosure of the information in the file, learn its sources and the recipients of reports, and dispute the completeness or accuracy of any item. Upon dispute, Section 611 required the agency to reinvestigate and to delete information found to be inaccurate or that could not be verified, with notice of the result to the consumer. Civil liability attached on two tracks, for willful noncompliance and for negligent noncompliance, and administrative enforcement was divided among the Federal Trade Commission and the banking agencies. The statute left the furnishing side of the system lightly regulated in 1970. The governing idea of the reporting regime was set in 1970: the consumer report was a regulated product, sold about a person who was not the customer, and the law therefore imposed duties of accuracy, limits on distribution, and rights of inspection and correction that had no counterpart in ordinary contract law.

The Equal Credit Opportunity Act extended the credit code from information to judgment. The problem presented to Congress in the early 1970s concerned the standards by which creditors evaluated applicants, and in particular the treatment of women. Evidence presented to the National Commission on Consumer Finance, whose 1972 report recommended federal action against credit discrimination, and to congressional committees described creditors discounting or disregarding the income of married women, especially women of childbearing age, and requiring a husband to cosign applications.

Public Law 93-495, approved on 28 October 1974, added the Equal Credit Opportunity Act to the Consumer Credit Protection Act as Title VII, through Title V of the enacting public law, and the new title took effect one year after enactment. As enacted in 1974, the prohibition was confined to discrimination on the basis of sex or marital status, and the Federal Reserve Board implemented the prohibition in Regulation B. The Equal Credit Opportunity Act Amendments of 1976, Public Law 94-239, approved on 23 March 1976, rewrote and broadened the title. The amended statute barred discrimination on the basis of race, color, religion, national origin, sex, or marital status, or age, provided the applicant had the capacity to contract, and barred discrimination because all or part of an income derived from a public assistance program, or because an applicant had in good faith exercised any right under the Consumer Credit Protection Act. The 1976 amendments converted a statute about the treatment of women into a general charter against discrimination in credit.

The operating rules of Title VII reached beyond a simple command against unequal treatment. Regulation B restricted inquiries into marital status, required that credit histories be reported in the names of both spouses where both were liable or both used an account, limited the circumstances in which a creditor could require the signature of a spouse, and barred discouraging applications on a prohibited basis. The 1976 amendments also required a creditor taking adverse action to notify the applicant within thirty days and either to give specific reasons or to disclose the right to obtain those reasons. Enforcement was dispersed among the federal banking agencies and the Federal Trade Commission, and an aggrieved applicant received a private action for actual and punitive damages subject to statutory limits, together with attorney fees. The design differed from Title I in a fundamental respect. Truth in lending assumed a lawful bargain badly described. Title VII identified bargains that disclosure could not redeem, because an applicant denied credit for a prohibited reason gained nothing from a clearer statement of the terms of a loan that was never offered.

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act addressed the stage of the credit relationship at which the borrower had already failed to pay. Collection of consumer debts in the 1970s was divided between creditors collecting debts owed to themselves and a specialized industry of agencies that collected debts owed to others. Congressional findings in the statute recited abundant evidence of abusive, deceptive, and unfair practices, and connected those practices to personal bankruptcies, marital instability, loss of jobs, and invasions of privacy. The practices described in committee hearings included calls at unreasonable hours, threats of arrest, disclosure of debts to employers and neighbors, simulated legal process, and repeated telephone contact intended to exhaust a debtor into paying a disputed amount.

Public Law 95-109, approved on 20 September 1977, amended the Consumer Credit Protection Act by adding Title VIII, the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, which took effect six months after enactment, on 20 March 1978. The title regulated a defined actor rather than the collection of debts in general. A debt collector was, in the statutory definition, a person who regularly collected debts owed to another, or who operated a business the principal purpose of which was the collection of debts. Original creditors collecting debts owed to themselves, in their own names, stood largely outside the title, as did officers and employees of a creditor collecting in the name of the creditor. The exclusion carried a deliberate consequence: identical words spoken by an agency collector and by the creditor who owned the debt could receive different treatment under federal law, with the creditor left principally to state law. Congress legislated against the third party collector because the collector had no continuing customer relationship to protect.

Within its chosen field, Title VIII regulated conduct in detail. Section 805 restricted communications, treating communication before eight o clock in the morning or after nine o clock in the evening at the consumer location as an inconvenient time, and giving effect to a written notice that the consumer refused to pay or wished communication to cease, subject to narrow exceptions. Section 806 prohibited harassment or abuse, including threats of violence, obscene language, and causing a telephone to ring repeatedly with intent to annoy or harass. Section 807 prohibited false or misleading representations, including false claims to be an attorney or a government official, false threats of arrest or of legal action that was not intended or lawful, and misrepresentation of the character, amount, or legal status of a debt. Section 808 prohibited unfair practices, including collection of amounts not authorized by the agreement or by law.

The procedural center of the title was validation. Within five days after an initial communication, unless the information appeared in that communication, a debt collector had to send a written notice stating the amount of the debt, the name of the creditor, and the consumer rights to dispute the debt within thirty days and to obtain verification of the debt or a copy of a judgment, together with the name and address of the original creditor if different from the current creditor. If the consumer disputed the debt in writing within the thirty day period, the collector had to cease collection of the disputed debt until verification was obtained and mailed to the consumer. Silence could not be construed by any court as an admission of liability. The remedy structure made the private action the principal engine of compliance. A debt collector who failed to comply was liable for actual damages, for additional damages not exceeding one thousand dollars in an individual action, and for costs and a reasonable attorney fee. The title directed the enforcement habits of the earlier titles at behavior rather than at documents: the question in a case under the title was rarely whether a form had been completed correctly, and usually whether a collector had said or done something the statute forbade.

The Electronic Fund Transfer Act closed the decade by confronting a payment method that barely existed in consumer hands when the 1968 Act was written. Electronic fund transfer systems moved money by instructions transmitted through electronic terminals, telephones, computers, and magnetic tape, and by the middle 1970s automated teller machines were spreading through American banking while point of sale debit transactions remained limited and experimental, even though the National Commission on Electronic Fund Transfers had recommended a statutory allocation of rights and liabilities in a final report delivered in 1977. Congress found, in the text of the new title, that the application of existing consumer protection legislation to electronic systems was unclear and that the rights and liabilities of consumers, financial institutions, and intermediaries were undefined. A paper check left a physical record and a signature. An electronic debit left machine records controlled by the institution, and a consumer who disputed a transfer faced an institution holding nearly all of the evidence.

The Electronic Fund Transfer Act became Title IX of the Consumer Credit Protection Act, added by Title XX of Public Law 95-630, the Financial Institutions Regulatory and Interest Rate Control Act of 1978, approved on 10 November 1978. The title defined an electronic fund transfer as a transfer of funds, other than a transaction originated by check, draft, or similar paper instrument, initiated through an electronic terminal, telephonic instrument, computer, or magnetic tape so as to order, instruct, or authorize a financial institution to debit or credit an account, and excluded wire transfer systems used primarily for transfers between financial institutions or businesses. The Federal Reserve Board implemented the title in Regulation E.

The substantive scheme rested on documentation, error resolution, and a graduated allocation of loss. Financial institutions owed initial disclosures of the terms of electronic transfer services, including liability, fees, and limitations on transfers, and owed receipts at electronic terminals and periodic statements identifying each transfer. A recurring preauthorized debit required written authorization, and a consumer could stop payment by notifying the institution up to three business days before a scheduled transfer. Section 909 built the error resolution procedure. A consumer who believed that an error had occurred, a category that included unauthorized transfers, incorrect amounts, and omitted transfers, could notify the institution within sixty days after the periodic statement was sent. The institution then had to investigate and report the results, ordinarily within ten business days, or provisionally credit the account and complete the investigation within forty five days, correcting any error found promptly after determining that an error had occurred. The burden of proof on whether a transfer was authorized rested on the financial institution rather than on the consumer.

Liability for unauthorized transfers followed a timetable. A consumer who notified the institution within two business days after learning of the loss or theft of an access device bore liability of no more than fifty dollars. A consumer who delayed beyond two business days faced liability capped at five hundred dollars. A consumer who failed to report an unauthorized transfer appearing on a periodic statement within sixty days faced unlimited liability for transfers occurring after that period that timely notice would have prevented. The structure differed sharply from the rule governing credit cards, where cardholder liability for unauthorized use rested on a flat statutory cap of fifty dollars under the Truth in Lending Act, later reduced in practice by the voluntary zero liability policies that the card networks adopted. An electronic transfer removed money that already belonged to the account holder, and the statute placed the risk of silent delay on the consumer in a way that credit law, dealing with money lent by the issuer, did not.

By 1978 the design consequence of the decade of insertions stood complete. A borrower harmed by consumer credit had to begin analysis not with the fact of harm but with the classification of the product and the identity of the actor who caused it. A loan described in misleading terms raised a disclosure claim under Title I, measured against the finance charge and the annual percentage rate that the creditor had stated. A false record held by a reporting agency raised accuracy, permissible purpose, and reinvestigation claims under Title VI, against agencies and users rather than against the creditor who had made the loan. A denial of credit for a prohibited reason raised a discrimination claim under Title VII, with its own notices, regulators, and damages rules. Abusive collection by an agency raised a conduct claim under Title VIII, while abusive collection of the same debt by the creditor who owned it fell outside that title. A mistaken or unauthorized electronic debit raised an error resolution claim under Title IX, governed by deadlines that had no equivalent in the paper based titles. Across the borders, protection changed character completely. The sectoral pattern of American consumer law, in which the applicable rule follows the category of product or institution rather than the experience of the consumer, appeared in the credit cluster in its purest form, assembled title by title inside an act that had begun with a modest demand that the price of credit be stated in numbers a borrower could compare.

## Safety Becomes an Institution and Warranty Law Arrives

By the opening of the 1970s, federal consumer law had accumulated statutes aimed at particular dangers without acquiring an institution charged with product safety as a general subject. A fabric could be regulated for flammability and a household chemical could be labeled for toxicity, yet no single federal body owned the larger question of whether ordinary goods presented unreasonable risks of injury. The legislation of 1970 to 1975 answered that gap in pieces. Congress first placed a physical barrier between small children and poisonous substances, then created an independent commission for product safety across most of the household market, and then rebuilt warranty law while giving the Federal Trade Commission a defined power to write substantive trade regulation rules.

The Poison Prevention Packaging Act of 1970 (Public Law 91-601), signed by President Richard M. Nixon on 30 December 1970, grew out of a narrower record than the general debates over credit and advertising. Aspirin had become a leading cause of accidental poisoning among children under five years of age, and flavored children aspirin presented a particular danger, because tablets made palatable for a sick child could also be eaten in quantity by a toddler who reached an open bottle. Pediatricians including Dr. Jay Arena of Duke University had persuaded some manufacturers during the 1950s to adopt safety closures voluntarily and to limit the number of tablets in a container. Voluntary adoption left competing products without comparable protection, and education directed at parents could not change the fact that a conventional screw cap offered no resistance to a determined child.

The statute answered with packaging rather than with a ban on any medicine or chemical. Enacted as an amendment to the Federal Hazardous Substances Act, the act authorized standards for special packaging, defined as packaging designed to be significantly difficult for children under five years of age to open or to obtain a toxic or harmful amount of the contents within a reasonable time, while not being difficult for normal adults to use properly. The definition held two requirements in tension. A closure that defeated adults would invite workarounds such as leaving caps loose or transferring medicines into unmarked containers, whereas a closure that adults found effortless would rarely defeat a child for long. Testing protocols therefore exposed prototype packages to panels of young children and to adults, requiring high rates of adult success alongside high rates of child failure.

Coverage followed findings of hazard rather than a fixed list written entirely into the statute. The Secretary of Health, Education, and Welfare could require special packaging after finding that such packaging was required to protect children from serious personal injury or serious illness resulting from handling, using, or ingesting a household substance. Administration rested at first with the Food and Drug Administration, within the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare. Aspirin became the first and most consequential application, and requirements extended to other listed substances that included prescription drugs, acetaminophen, turpentine, methanol, sulfuric acid, and furniture polish. The statute preserved narrow outlets, including packaging supplied at the direction of a prescribing physician and, for some nonprescription products, one size in conventional packaging labeled for households without young children.

The measured record gave the packaging statute a place that few disclosure laws could match. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, summarizing Consumer Product Safety Commission and poison control data, reported that deaths among children under five years of age from aspirin and aspirin substitutes fell from 58 in 1970 to 18 in 1978, a reduction of 69 percent. A study published in the Archives of Pediatrics and Adolescent Medicine, analyzing the years 1973 to 1990 and controlling for changes in aspirin consumption and for the association between aspirin and Reye syndrome, estimated that child resistant packaging had reduced the aspirin related child mortality rate by about 34 percent relative to the projected rate without such packaging, equating to about 90 fewer child deaths during that period. Economist W. Kip Viscusi, in a 1985 analysis, found no statistical association between child resistant packaging and aspirin poisoning rates. Even with that disagreement in the record, the mortality series made the packaging law the clearest success case of the era, because success could be counted in deaths that did not occur. In 1973, jurisdiction over the act transferred to the newly formed Consumer Product Safety Commission, recasting packaging as one instrument within a general portfolio covering toys, appliances, fabrics, and household equipment.

The Consumer Product Safety Act of 1972 (Public Law 92-573), signed by President Nixon on 27 October 1972, supplied that institution. The groundwork had been laid by the National Commission on Product Safety, a seven member study commission authorized in 1967 by Public Law 90-146. Its final report, issued in 1970, concluded that Americans were exposed to many unreasonably dangerous products and that product liability litigation, state and local regulation, industry self regulation, and earlier federal safety laws had failed to provide adequate protection. Litigation compensated some injured plaintiffs after harm had occurred, but depended on proof of causation under defenses that varied by state. Voluntary standards depended on manufacturer participation and lacked a federal backstop. Earlier federal statutes addressed categories one at a time, leaving gaps that regulators could fill only by returning to Congress.

Congress responded by creating the Consumer Product Safety Commission as an independent regulatory agency, governed by five commissioners appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate for staggered terms, with a limit designed to prevent more than three commissioners from belonging to the same political party. A freestanding commission could set an agenda organized around injury reduction, without subordination to a department charged with promoting business or with administering health and welfare programs. The detailed treatment in [the Consumer Product Safety Act pillar](/2018/10/01/consumer-product-safety-act-1972/) traces the statutory architecture that followed from that choice.

### Why did Congress create a separate product safety commission?

Congress created a separate commission because product hazards crossed the jurisdictions of older, single subject statutes and agencies, and the National Commission on Product Safety concluded in 1970 that litigation, voluntary standards, and fragmented federal programs had left unreasonable risks unaddressed in ordinary household goods.

The statute defined a consumer product broadly, as an article or component produced or distributed for sale to a consumer for use in or around a household or residence, a school, in recreation, or otherwise, or for the personal use, consumption, or enjoyment of a consumer in those settings. Under that definition, commission jurisdiction extended to thousands of product categories, from cribs, toys, and children sleepwear to lawn mowers, power tools, bicycles, cigarette lighters, and household appliances. The commission received authority to prescribe safety standards for performance, composition, design, packaging, or labeling, and to require warnings or instructions. Where a standard could not reduce a risk adequately, the commission could declare a product a banned hazardous product. The statute also directed the commission to rely on a voluntary standard where compliance would adequately reduce the risk and substantial compliance was likely.

Information powers matched the standard setting powers, because a commission responsible for thousands of categories could not wait for injuries to arrive through lawsuits. The commission operated the National Electronic Injury Surveillance System, which drew reports from a sample of hospital emergency departments and allowed staff to estimate national injury patterns. Section 15 then placed an affirmative reporting duty on manufacturers, distributors, and retailers. Under Section 15(b), a firm that obtained information reasonably supporting a conclusion that a product failed to comply with an applicable safety rule, contained a defect that could create a substantial product hazard, or created an unreasonable risk of serious injury or death was required to inform the commission immediately, unless the commission had been adequately informed already. Silence after acquiring reportable information became an independent violation.

Recall authority completed the enforcement sequence. Section 15 defined a substantial product hazard to include a failure to comply with an applicable safety rule that created a substantial risk of injury, and a product defect that, because of the pattern of the defect, the number of products distributed, the severity of the risk, or other considerations, created a substantial risk of injury. After notice and a hearing, the commission could order a manufacturer, distributor, or retailer to give public notice and to elect, as applicable, to bring the product into conformity, repair the product, replace the product, or refund the purchase price less a reasonable allowance for use. Separate authority allowed the commission to seek federal court action against an imminently hazardous product. In practice, many recalls proceeded through negotiated corrective action, in the shadow of a hearing and a refund remedy.

Consolidation accompanied creation. Section 30 transferred functions under several earlier statutes to the new commission, including the Flammable Fabrics Act of 1953, the Federal Hazardous Substances Act of 1960 (Public Law 86-613), the Refrigerator Safety Act of 1956 (Public Law 84-930), and the Poison Prevention Packaging Act of 1970. Those transfers gathered the textile flammability program, the hazardous substances labeling program expanded during the 1960s, the refrigerator door standard enacted after children suffocated inside abandoned appliances, and the packaging program into one agency. Consolidation did not erase the distinct histories of those statutes, each of which retained separate codification. Consolidation instead placed enforcement and injury data under one roof, so that a hazard presenting as a labeling failure under one statute and a design failure under another could be evaluated as one product problem.

The exclusions from commission jurisdiction were as deliberate as the grant, and the exclusions wrote a sectoral pattern into the charter. The statutory definition excluded tobacco, motor vehicles and motor vehicle equipment, pesticides, articles subject to the federal firearms and ammunition excise tax, aircraft, boats subject to federal boat safety regulation, and articles regulated as food, drugs, cosmetics, or medical devices under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act. Each exclusion corresponded to an existing regulatory home. Motor vehicles remained with the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, food and drugs remained with the Food and Drug Administration, pesticides remained principally with the Environmental Protection Agency, and firearms remained subject to tax, licensing, and criminal provisions administered by the Department of the Treasury and shaped by the Gun Control Act of 1968. Defenders of the exclusions presented the arrangement as avoidance of duplicative regulation, whereas consumer advocates including Ralph Nader warned that excluded categories included some of the most lethal products in American life.

Product safety addressed physical injury, but a second class of complaint concerned the promises attached to goods after sale. A refrigerator or an automobile could be safe in operation and defective in performance, and the warranty document often determined whether repair would be free. Warranty documents of the postwar decades drew sustained complaint for dense print, undisclosed exclusions, and advertising that suggested broader coverage than the formal terms delivered after purchase. Congressional hearings during the early 1970s, held by committees associated with Senator Warren G. Magnuson of Washington and Representative John E. Moss of California, assembled evidence that buyers could not compare warranties before purchase and could not enforce a promise without spending more on counsel than the product had cost.

The Magnuson-Moss Warranty: Federal Trade Commission Improvement Act of 1975 (Public Law 93-637), signed by President Gerald Ford on 4 January 1975, addressed that record without requiring any manufacturer to offer a warranty at all. Once a supplier chose to offer a written warranty, however, federal disclosure rules attached. The warranty title required a warrantor to disclose fully and conspicuously, in simple and readily understood language, the terms and conditions of the warranty, to the extent required by Federal Trade Commission rules. Commission rules specified matters that included the identity of the warrantor, the products or parts covered, the procedure a buyer had to follow to obtain performance, and the period during which the warrantor would perform. Related rules required that warranty terms be available before purchase, so that comparison could occur at the point where comparison could influence a choice.

The designation system converted disclosure into a signal that a buyer could read at a glance. A written warranty could be designated as a full warranty only if the warrantor met federal minimum standards that required remedy of a defect within a reasonable time and without charge, imposed no unreasonable conditions, required repair and, if repair proved impossible or unsuccessful after a reasonable number of attempts, required replacement without charge or refund of the purchase price. A full warranty also barred exclusion of consequential damages unless that exclusion appeared conspicuously on the face of the warranty, and barred any limitation on the duration of an implied warranty. A warranty that failed any element had to be designated as a limited warranty. Consumer advocates praised the designation as a discipline on inflated advertising language, whereas manufacturer representatives warned that few firms would risk the obligations attached to the full label. Limited warranties thereafter predominated, and full warranties appeared rarely.

Implied warranties and tie in sales received separate treatment. Section 108 provided that a supplier offering a written warranty could not disclaim or modify an implied warranty arising under state law, although a supplier offering only a limited warranty could limit the duration of an implied warranty to the duration of the written warranty where that limitation was conscionable and stated in clear and unmistakable language on the face of the warranty. Federal law did not create the implied warranty of merchantability, which remained a creature of state law. Section 102(c) prohibited a warrantor from conditioning a warranty on a buyer using an article or service identified by brand, trade, or corporate name, unless the warrantor provided that article or service without charge or obtained a waiver from the Federal Trade Commission. A waiver required a showing that the condition benefited the public and was needed because a specified article or service was necessary for the product to function properly.

Enforcement design recognized that individual warranty claims were usually too small for conventional litigation. The statute directed the Federal Trade Commission to prescribe minimum standards for informal dispute settlement mechanisms and encouraged warrantors to establish procedures through which complaints could be resolved fairly and quickly. Where a warrantor incorporated such a mechanism into a written warranty and the mechanism met commission standards, a buyer generally had to resort to that mechanism before pursuing a legal remedy under the federal statute, although the outcome did not bind a dissatisfied buyer. For claims that proceeded to court, Section 110 created a federal cause of action for a consumer damaged by failure of a supplier, warrantor, or service contractor to comply with a statutory obligation, a written warranty, an implied warranty, or a service contract. A consumer who finally prevailed could recover court costs and reasonable attorneys fees, in addition to damages and other legal or equitable relief. Fee shifting altered the economics that had made warranty promises difficult to enforce.

The same public law carried a second institutional transformation. The second title amended the Federal Trade Commission Act by adding Section 18, codified at 15 United States Code Section 57a, which authorized the commission to promulgate trade regulation rules defining with specificity acts or practices that were unfair or deceptive. Before 1975, the commission had proceeded primarily through case by case adjudication, and the Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit had upheld substantive rulemaking under an older provision in National Petroleum Refiners Association v. Federal Trade Commission in 1973. Section 18 settled the existence of the power while surrounding the exercise of the power with hybrid procedures that exceeded ordinary notice and comment rulemaking. A proceeding required a statement of reasons, an opportunity for oral presentation, a rulemaking record, cross examination on disputed issues of material fact in defined circumstances, and judicial review under a substantial evidence standard. The commission also had to have reason to believe that the practices addressed were prevalent.

Procedure was both a grant and a warning. Supporters of Section 18 in Congress, including Senator Magnuson, presented rulemaking as a fairer alternative to adjudicating the same practice firm by firm, because a rule could announce obligations in advance and apply obligations evenly across an industry. Business organizations opposed to expansive rulemaking presented the procedures as evidence that Congress expected restraint and a record capable of surviving demanding review. Commission staff and commissioners treated Section 18 during the later 1970s as authority to address practices across entire markets, opening proceedings concerning funeral industry practices, used motor vehicle sales, credit practices, eyeglasses advertising, vocational schools, and advertising directed at children. The backlash followed from that breadth, and a 1978 editorial in The Washington Post described the commission as a national nanny, a phrase that opponents repeated as a political claim about government supervision of ordinary choices. Under Chairman Michael Pertschuk, appointed by President Jimmy Carter in 1977, the commission defended an expansive reading of unfairness, whereas industry groups and members of Congress opposed to that reading maintained that unfairness without a deception anchor invited commissioners to substitute personal values for market judgments. Congress answered in the Federal Trade Commission Improvements Act of 1980 by imposing additional notice requirements, attaching a legislative veto mechanism later invalidated in Immigration and Naturalization Service v. Chadha in 1983, and barring a rule for children advertising based on a finding of unfairness. The 1980 statute did not repeal Section 18. Rulemaking authority endured, and the political conditions for exercising that authority at the tempo attempted during the later 1970s did not.

The closing institutional point belongs to the decade as a whole. Between 1970 and 1975, Congress built three different machines for three different failures of the consumer market. A packaging regime used performance testing to keep poisonous substances away from small children and then passed to a commission for products, empowered to gather injury data, set standards, demand reports, ban hazardous products, and order notice, repair, replacement, or refund. A disclosure code for credit used standardized information to make the price of borrowing comparable across lenders. A strengthened Federal Trade Commission received warranty disclosure rules, a federal warranty remedy supported by fee shifting, and Section 18 authority to define unfair or deceptive practices for an entire trade. No statute attempted to merge those machines into a single consumer protection agency, and proposals for a broader consumer agency failed in Congress during the 1970s.

## The Backlash and the Statute That Restrained the Regulator

The authority that produced the confrontation had been granted only a few years earlier. The Magnuson-Moss Warranty: Federal Trade Commission Improvement Act, Public Law 93-637, signed by President Gerald Ford on 4 January 1975, gave the Federal Trade Commission express power under Section 18 of the Federal Trade Commission Act to issue trade regulation rules defining, with specificity, acts or practices that were unfair or deceptive. A rule carried the force of law across an industry, a sharp contrast with a complaint against a single firm, which bound the respondent and left competitors to be reached case by case. Congress surrounded the power with hybrid procedures heavier than ordinary notice and comment, but the grant signalled an expectation that the Commission would move from episodic prosecution toward general standards for whole trades.

President Jimmy Carter appointed Michael Pertschuk chairman in April 1977. Pertschuk had served as chief counsel and staff director of the Senate Committee on Commerce, Science, and Transportation, where the consumer legislation of the preceding decade had been drafted. He chaired the Commission from 1977 to 1981 and remained a commissioner through 1984. Under his leadership the agency treated the 1975 grant as a mandate to examine practices that individual enforcement could not reshape, and its dockets reached trades whose customers were dispersed, poorly informed, or under acute pressure at the moment of purchase.

The funeral industry proceeding illustrated the method and the grievance it created. A proposed rule for funeral industry practices had been published on 29 August 1975, before Pertschuk took office, and the proceeding examined a market in which families arranged an expensive, unfamiliar purchase within hours of a death. Commission staff documented sales offered as inseparable packages, prices for individual items that were difficult to obtain before selection, and representations about legal requirements for embalming or caskets that could steer a family toward expenditure no law demanded. The remedy centred on disclosure: itemised price information in advance and by telephone, and freedom to select only the goods and services a family wanted. Funeral directors objected that a federal template misunderstood a local, professional service, and carried that objection to Congress with considerable effect.

Other proceedings widened the circle of affected trades. The used motor vehicle proceeding grew out of a direction in Title I of the 1975 act that the Commission initiate rulemaking on warranties and warranty practices in used motor vehicle sales, and it examined defect disclosure and the gap between sales talk about condition and written terms disclaiming responsibility. A proceeding on proprietary vocational schools examined recruitment by schools selling occupational training, including representations about accreditation, completion, and the employment awaiting a graduate whose tuition often depended on federal student assistance. Proceedings on standards and certification activities and on credit practices added further constituencies, each asking an unelected commission to settle, for an entire trade, how much disclosure was enough.

The children's advertising proceeding, known as KidVid, converted that unease into a national argument. Action for Children's Television petitioned the Commission in April 1977 for rules against televised advertising aimed at children, and the Center for Science in the Public Interest filed a companion petition directed at sugared snack foods and heavily sugared breakfast cereals. In early 1978 Commission staff presented recommendations of a breadth that surprised even sympathetic observers: a ban on all television advertising directed at children under eight, on the ground that children that young lacked the capacity to understand that an advertisement was designed to persuade; for children from eight to twelve, a ban on television advertisements for the sugared products most likely to cause tooth decay; and balancing messages about nutrition and dental care from advertisers of other sugared products. The Commission authorised a rulemaking proceeding in 1978, and hearings followed in early 1979.

The theory beneath the staff proposal was unfairness rather than falsity. An advertisement for a sugared cereal might make no demonstrably false statement, and yet, in the account offered by Action for Children's Television and the Center for Science in the Public Interest, the practice exploited an audience unable to recognise selling intent or to weigh a health consequence arriving years later. Pertschuk framed the inquiry, in his 1981 lecture, as an extension of older legal restraints on the commercial exploitation of minors. Broadcasters and advertisers answered that the proposal treated truthful speech as contraband because of the age of part of its audience. Vincent Wasilewski of the National Association of Broadcasters argued that responsibility for a child's purchases rested with parents, and advertising organisations argued that a ban would withdraw the revenue supporting children's programming while leaving parental authority diminished rather than protected.

On 1 March 1978 The Washington Post published an editorial headed "The FTC as National Nanny," condemning the contemplated restrictions as a preposterous intervention that would turn the agency into a national nanny and make parents less responsible rather than more. The phrase proved durable because of its source as much as its wording: the Post was generally sympathetic to federal consumer protection. Pertschuk later wrote that ridicule from that quarter signalled to the broadcast, grocery manufacturing, and advertising industries that the proceeding was fair political game, and to members of Congress that the political risk of intervening was minimal.

Business mobilisation after 1978 was broad, organised, and directed principally at Congress rather than at the rulemaking record. The United States Chamber of Commerce, the Association of National Advertisers, the American Association of Advertising Agencies, the National Association of Broadcasters, grocery manufacturers, food companies, toy manufacturers, funeral directors, and automobile dealers each had a proceeding to oppose. Pertschuk, in his 1981 lecture, recalled a Chamber spokesman describing universal membership in a society of victims of the Federal Trade Commission, and he recorded that business coalitions petitioned Congress to foreclose the children's advertising inquiry, the funeral rule, the used car rule, and other measures one by one. Some industries sought outright exemption from Commission jurisdiction. The Association of National Advertisers and the American Association of Advertising Agencies contended that advertising restrictions burdened protected commercial speech; the National Association of Broadcasters contended that parents and broadcasters were better placed than commissioners to judge children's viewing; and the Chamber contended that an agency armed with a general prohibition on unfairness could reach virtually any practice a majority of commissioners disliked.

Representative James Broyhill of North Carolina gave that last contention its canonical form, stating in 1978 that, under the Commission's very broad authority over conduct that was unfair or deceptive, the agency could regulate virtually every aspect of commercial life in the United States, and that Commission rules were broad policy pronouncements which Congress had an obligation to study and review. Consumers Union, Action for Children's Television, and the Center for Science in the Public Interest answered that Congress had considered the breadth of the unfairness standard when it enacted the 1975 improvements and had chosen rulemaking precisely because case by case adjudication left widespread practices untouched.

### What did the backlash statute do to the Federal Trade Commission?

The Federal Trade Commission Improvements Act of 1980 subjected every Commission trade regulation rule to disapproval by concurrent resolution of both houses of Congress, barred a children's advertising rule founded on unfairness, imposed advance notice and regulatory analysis duties, and reauthorised the agency only after a funding dispute closed it on 1 May 1980.

Congress answered with the Federal Trade Commission Improvements Act of 1980, Public Law 96-252, 94 Stat. 374, which President Carter signed on 28 May 1980. The act was at once an authorisation and a restraint. For the first time in three years, as Carter noted in his signing statement, a specific authorisation statute was in effect for the Commission, under funding that ran through fiscal year 1982. Carter signed despite deep objection to one provision: in that statement he called the two house congressional veto of Commission rules both unwise and unconstitutional, said that he signed because the very existence of the agency was at stake, and recorded that he looked forward to a court challenge under the provision for expedited judicial review.

The legislative veto stood at the centre of the settlement, and [the account of how the FTC writes consumer rules](/2019/01/15/ftc-consumer-protection-rulemaking/) sets out the procedures into which the veto was inserted. Section 21 of the act, codified at 15 U.S.C. 57a-1, required a final trade regulation rule to be transmitted to Congress and provided for disapproval if both houses adopted a concurrent resolution of disapproval within the period the section prescribed, described in contemporary legal analysis as ninety days after promulgation. A concurrent resolution was not presented to the President, the source both of the device's attraction for Congress and of the objection Carter recorded. Supporters in Congress presented the veto as a proportionate answer to delegation: a legislature that had written a standard as open as unfairness retained a mechanism for recalling a rule without enacting a new statute over a presidential veto. Opponents, including the Carter administration, presented the mechanism as lawmaking outside the procedure Article I prescribes.

The children's advertising provision was narrower and more absolute. The act barred the Commission from adopting a rule in that proceeding, or in a substantially similar proceeding, on the theory that advertising directed at children was an unfair act or practice. The bar addressed the legal theory rather than the subject alone: a rule against false or deceptive advertising remained within ordinary authority. Related amendments also denied the Commission authority, for three years in the first instance, to begin a new rulemaking restricting commercial advertising on an unfairness theory, a restriction continued through appropriations legislation until 1994, according to the Commission's later account. KidVid itself ended without a rule: the Commission terminated the rulemaking in 1981 after its staff concluded that a workable remedy could not be framed for the youngest children, whose inability to understand persuasive intent had supplied the strongest factual claim.

Procedure supplied the quieter half of the restraint. Before issuing a notice of proposed rulemaking, the Commission had to publish an advance notice describing the objectives under consideration and inviting comment, and to submit that notice to the Senate Committee on Commerce, Science, and Transportation and the House committee with jurisdiction over the agency. It also had to prepare regulatory analyses for proposed and final rules, setting out need, alternatives, and costs and benefits; Carter's signing statement praised the direction to choose the least burdensome method of achieving a stated goal. The requirements lengthened a process the 1975 act had already made deliberate. The act also restrained authority over standards and certification activities, and it dealt with the funeral proceeding through money: Section 19 barred use of funds authorised for fiscal years 1980, 1981, or 1982 to issue the funeral rule proposed in 1975 unless the final rule met specified requirements and procedures. A funeral rule did issue in 1982 and took effect in 1984, narrowed to itemised price disclosure, and a used motor vehicle rule issued in 1984 requiring a window disclosure of warranty terms. Rulemaking had been channelled rather than abolished.

The funding lapse of May 1980 gave the dispute a physical form. Attorney General Benjamin Civiletti had advised President Carter in April 1980 that, under the Antideficiency Act, an agency whose appropriations had lapsed could spend funds only as necessary to bring about an orderly termination of its functions. When the Commission's funding expired after 30 April 1980 while Congress disputed the terms of its authorisation, the opinion was applied for the first time to close a federal agency. The Commission shut its doors on 1 May 1980, furloughing about 1,600 employees for one day at a cost estimated at 700,000 dollars, largely in lost labour. Congress approved funding that evening, Carter signed the measure, and the Commission reopened the following day.

The legislative veto survived the settlement by three years. In Immigration and Naturalization Service v. Chadha, 462 U.S. 919 (1983), the Supreme Court held unconstitutional a provision of the Immigration and Nationality Act that allowed one house of Congress to overturn the Attorney General's suspension of a deportation. Chief Justice Warren Burger wrote for the Court that action legislative in purpose and effect had to follow the single procedure Article I prescribes: passage by both houses and presentation to the President. The reasoning reached beyond the one house device in Chadha, because a concurrent resolution disapproving a Commission rule likewise altered legal rights without presentation. Justice Byron White, dissenting, treated trade regulation as evidence for the opposite conclusion, quoting Representative Broyhill's 1978 statement and arguing that the veto had preserved congressional authority over the Commission's broad rulemaking mandate. The majority prevailed, the veto provision was left without constitutional effect, and the remainder of the 1980 act, including the advance notice and analysis requirements and the children's advertising bar, stood. Congress retained other instruments: after 1980 the Commission received no new authorisation statute for fourteen years, a fact the Commission itself later recorded, so appropriations and oversight carried the burden authorising legislation would ordinarily have borne.

Retreat inside the agency began, in one respect, before Congress finished acting. On 17 December 1980 a unanimous Commission adopted a policy statement on unfairness that narrowed the doctrine on which the expansive rulemakings had rested. The statement made unjustified consumer injury the primary focus of the standard and articulated a three part test: the injury had to be substantial, it had not to be outweighed by countervailing benefits to consumers or competition, and it had to be an injury consumers could not reasonably have avoided. The Commission disavowed reliance on the older formulation that a practice might be condemned as immoral, unethical, oppressive, or unscrupulous as an independent ground, and it confined public policy to an evidentiary role in most cases. A doctrine framed in the children's advertising recommendations as a judgment about exploitation became a test of demonstrable injury net of benefits.

Under James C. Miller III, who chaired the Commission from 1981 to 1985 after appointment by President Ronald Reagan, the change acquired an affirmative programme. Miller, an economist, wrote that the agency had strayed far beyond the vision of open, competitive markets associated with President Woodrow Wilson and had embraced an interventionist, paternalistic approach Congress had rejected in 1914. The Miller Commission pressed economic analysis into case selection, favoured prosecution of fraud and collusive conduct over industry wide rulemaking, and expanded competition advocacy: comments and testimony before federal, state, and local bodies urging removal of restrictions on entry, advertising, and professional practice that raised prices without a corresponding public benefit. Timothy Muris, who directed the Bureau of Consumer Protection from 1981 to 1983, later described the construction of an anti fraud programme built on Section 13(b), which allowed the Commission to seek injunctions and monetary relief in federal court against scams that rulemaking addressed poorly.

The consequences for federal consumer legislation were generational in shape. After 1980 the measures Congress enacted tended to address a single industry, technology, or practice, often directing a rule for that subject rather than enlarging the Commission's general authority. Congress legislated directly on children's television in the Children's Television Act of 1990, which limited advertising time in children's programming and was administered by the Federal Communications Commission, rather than reviving a Commission rule founded on unfairness. Congress addressed children's privacy online in the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act of 1998, which directed the Commission to issue a rule for a defined category of operators and data. The pattern was a preference for statutes that named the practice and the remedy, leaving the contested judgment to the legislative text rather than to a commission applying a general standard.

Activity migrated in part to the states, along channels cut earlier. State unfair and deceptive acts and practices statutes, commonly called little FTC acts because many were modelled on Section 5 of the federal act, had been enacted in most states from the mid 1960s to the mid 1970s, as the National Consumer Law Center recorded in its survey of those statutes. During the 1980s state attorneys general became the most visible enforcers in much of the field, building consumer protection divisions, suing national sellers under state law, and coordinating multistate investigations. The state statutes differed from the federal act in the feature that mattered most to a wronged consumer: the Federal Trade Commission Act gave a consumer no private right of action under Section 5, while state statutes authorised private damage actions and, as the National Consumer Law Center's survey recorded, many provided attorney fees for a prevailing consumer and punitive, treble, or minimum damage awards.

The weighing that the episode requires has never produced a settled verdict, because the two accounts disagree about the meaning of the same events. For the United States Chamber of Commerce, the Association of National Advertisers, and Representative Broyhill, the 1980 act corrected an excess of delegation: a commission had treated a general prohibition on unfairness as authority to propose restrictions on truthful advertising heard by children, to restructure funeral selling, and to prescribe warranty disclosure for an entire trade, and elected legislators rather than commissioners were the proper authors of decisions turning on paternalism, family responsibility, and the acceptable cost of a rule. For Michael Pertschuk, the same act punished the Commission for using authority Congress had granted in 1975 in terms broad enough to invite general rules, and it did so, in his 1981 account, after opponents had shunned debate on the substance of most rules and cases in favour of a collective portrait of an agency out of control. Consumers Union and Action for Children's Television treated the children's advertising bar as a decision that a documented harm to a defined audience would go unremedied at the federal level because the theory that fit the harm had been withdrawn. Defenders also pointed to the destination of the abandoned work: national practices addressed thereafter by fifty state statutes of uneven scope, with private remedies the federal act withheld. Each account identifies a genuine cost in the arrangement the other produced, and the record of the 1980s supports the factual premises of both without supplying a common measure by which the two could be reconciled.

## Privacy One Industry at a Time, Then the Digital Statutes

Privacy entered federal consumer law attached to statutes about something else, and that pattern shaped everything that followed. Congress rarely began with a general proposition about personal information applied across the economy. Congress began with a particular service, a particular record, and a particular complaint, and the resulting statute protected the record generated by that service and left neighboring records to other statutes, to state law, or to no statute at all. A cable subscriber received a federal privacy provision in 1984 because cable operators held viewing and billing records. A video renter received a stronger provision in 1988 because a nomination fight had exposed rental records to a newspaper. Telephone households received protection against machines that dialed and faxed without restraint, children received protection on commercial websites directed at children, and credit card holders received conduct rules for issuers.

The Cable Communications Policy Act of 1984 (Public Law 98-549) supplied an early example, as [the Cable Communications Policy Act profile](/2017/08/15/cable-communications-policy-act-1984/) sets out in the full franchising and communications setting. Section 551, codified at Section 551 of Title 47 of the United States Code, restricted what a cable operator could collect, use, and disclose about a subscriber. At the time a subscriber entered into an agreement for cable service, and at least once a year thereafter, the operator had to provide a separate written notice describing the nature of personally identifiable information collected, the nature of any use of that information, the nature, frequency, and purpose of any disclosure, the period during which the operator would maintain the information, and the times and place at which the subscriber could gain access to the information. Collection through the cable system generally required the prior written or electronic consent of the subscriber, apart from collection necessary to render the service or to detect unauthorized reception. Disclosure likewise generally required prior consent, subject to exceptions for disclosures necessary to render a service or conduct a legitimate business activity, for disclosures under a court order meeting the safeguards stated in the section, and for limited disclosures of names and addresses where the subscriber had received an opportunity to prohibit disclosure. The subscriber received access, a reasonable opportunity to correct error, and destruction of information when the information was no longer necessary for the purpose for which the information had been collected. A subscriber aggrieved by a violation could sue in federal district court, with actual damages subject to a liquidated floor of one hundred dollars a day for each day of violation or one thousand dollars, whichever was higher. A governmental entity seeking subscriber information through a court order had to offer clear and convincing evidence of the grounds stated in the section, and the subscriber received an opportunity to contest the claim. The protection stopped at the boundary of the cable relationship, however. A physician, a librarian, and a video store each held records capable of revealing as much or more, and Section 551 imposed no duty on any of those holders.

The Video Privacy Protection Act of 1988 (Public Law 100-618) showed how quickly Congress could move when a privacy violation acquired a famous name, and the episode also showed how narrow the resulting protection could be. During the confirmation hearings for Judge Robert Bork, nominated to the Supreme Court in 1987, reporter Michael Dolan of the Washington City Paper obtained the video rental records of Judge Bork and his family from Potomac Video, a local rental store, and the newspaper published the list under the headline of the Bork tapes. The list contained 146 films and revealed ordinary family viewing rather than scandalous material, and the absence of scandal strengthened the legislative reaction. Legislators who disagreed sharply about the nomination could agree that a store record system should not place a household viewing history in a newspaper. Senator Patrick Leahy of Vermont, a leading sponsor of the response, framed the disclosure as an invasion of the freedom to be left alone, and Congress enacted the statute in 1988, roughly a year after the publication.

The statute prohibited a video tape service provider from knowingly disclosing personally identifiable information concerning a consumer, including information that identified a person as having requested or obtained specific video materials, except under the conditions stated in the statute. Disclosure with informed written consent was permitted, as were disclosures incident to the ordinary course of business, disclosures of names and addresses where the consumer had received an opportunity to prohibit disclosure, and disclosures under warrants, subpoenas, and court orders governed by the procedures stated in the statute. A person aggrieved by a violation could sue in federal district court, and the court could award actual damages but not less than liquidated damages of 2,500 dollars, punitive damages, and reasonable attorney fees. The statute also required destruction of personally identifiable information as soon as practicable but no later than one year from the date on which the information was no longer necessary for the purpose for which the information had been collected. Why video rentals received that remedy in 1988, while other records waited or went without, has no single explanation. The Bork episode gave the issue a named victim and a named newspaper, and video rental presented a bounded category that Congress could define without regulating an entire information economy. The bill as introduced by Senator Leahy on 10 May 1988 covered library materials and services alongside video materials, and the statute as enacted dropped the library language and retained the video category alone. Medical records received a federal privacy framework only through the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996, addressed to defined health entities rather than to every holder of health related information, and library records received protection principally through state confidentiality laws adopted state by state. The disparity was not a judgment that video viewing was more sensitive than a diagnosis or a reading list. The disparity was an artifact of sequence, salience, and legislative convenience.

### Is there a general federal consumer privacy law?

No. Through 15 February 2019, Congress had enacted no general federal consumer privacy statute covering personal information across industries. Federal protection rested on sector specific laws for credit, cable, video, telephones, children online, financial records, and health records, supplemented by Federal Trade Commission enforcement against unfair or deceptive practices.

The answer requires one qualification. The Federal Trade Commission Act gave the Federal Trade Commission authority over unfair or deceptive acts or practices, and the Commission used that authority against companies that misrepresented privacy practices, broke promises stated in privacy policies, or failed to provide reasonable security for sensitive information in circumstances that the Commission treated as unfair. The Commission proceeded case by case, however, and no Commission action created a single set of collection, use, retention, access, and disclosure rights applicable to every company holding consumer information.

Congress turned from records held by a store to intrusions delivered through a network in the Telephone Consumer Protection Act of 1991 (Public Law 102-243), enacted on 20 December 1991 and codified principally at Section 227 of Title 47 of the United States Code. The statute responded to automatic dialing equipment capable of generating calls in volume, to prerecorded and artificial voice messages delivered without a human caller, and to unsolicited advertising sent to facsimile machines at the expense and interruption of the recipient. Congress restricted the use of an automatic telephone dialing system and an artificial or prerecorded voice, restricted telephone solicitations, and prohibited unsolicited advertisements to facsimile machines, subject to the exceptions stated in the statute and in regulations issued by the Federal Communications Commission. A person could bring an action under the private right of action stated in the statute to enjoin a violation and to recover actual monetary loss or five hundred dollars in damages for each violation, whichever was greater, and a court could increase the award to not more than three times that amount where the defendant had willfully or knowingly violated the applicable requirements. State attorneys general and the Federal Communications Commission held enforcement roles as well, so each unlawful call or fax became a measurable legal event rather than a mere annoyance. The telephone statute protected the household against a channel of intrusion, and the statute left other channels, including postal marketing and commercial email, to other legal instruments.

The commercial World Wide Web produced the first federal privacy statute written for online collection as such, and Congress again chose a defined population rather than a general rule for the internet. The Children's Online Privacy Protection Act of 1998 was enacted as Title XIII of Division C of the Omnibus Consolidated and Emergency Supplemental Appropriations Act, 1999 (Public Law 105-277), signed on 21 October 1998. The statute applied to operators of websites and online services directed to children under thirteen and to operators with actual knowledge that a child under thirteen was providing personal information. Before collecting, using, or disclosing personal information from a child, an operator had to provide notice of information practices and obtain verifiable parental consent, subject to exceptions stated in the statute and in the rule issued by the Federal Trade Commission. Parents received rights to review information collected from a child, to refuse further use or collection, and to seek deletion. The statute barred an operator from conditioning participation by a child in a game, a prize offer, or another activity on disclosure of more information than was reasonably necessary for participation. The statute directed the Commission to issue regulations, the Commission published the Children's Online Privacy Protection Rule in 1999, and the rule became effective on 21 April 2000. That effective date in April 2000, verified in Commission releases and in the Federal Register history of the rule, marked the point at which operators faced an enforceable federal code for collection of information from children online.

Three measures of 2003 then showed the sectoral method operating across credit, email, and telemarketing. The Fair and Accurate Credit Transactions Act of 2003 (Public Law 108-159), signed on 4 December 2003, amended the Fair Credit Reporting Act in response to identity theft and to disputes about accuracy and access in consumer reports. The statute gave a consumer the right to one free disclosure of a consumer file from each nationwide consumer reporting agency during each twelve month period, a right that the Federal Trade Commission summary describes as a right to one free credit report a year from the credit reporting agencies. A consumer who suspected fraud or identity theft could place a fraud alert in a file, and the statute provided initial and extended alerts for identity theft victims, together with procedures for blocking information that resulted from identity theft. Persons that accepted credit or debit cards could print no more than the last five digits of a card number on an electronically printed receipt, and the statute barred printing of the expiration date on such a receipt. The statute also addressed credit scores, risk based pricing notices, disposal of consumer information, and procedures for detection of identity theft patterns known as red flags.

The Controlling the Assault of Non-Solicited Pornography and Marketing Act of 2003, known as the CAN-SPAM Act of 2003 (Public Law 108-187), signed on 16 December 2003 and effective on 1 January 2004, chose a different architecture for commercial email. Where the children's statute required consent before collection from a child, the email statute permitted commercial email to a recipient who had not consented, provided that the sender complied with the duties stated in the statute and honored a subsequent decision to opt out. A commercial email could not contain false or misleading header information or a deceptive subject heading, the message had to contain a clear notice of the opportunity to decline future email and a valid physical postal address, and the sender had to honor an opt out request. Enforcement rested principally with the Federal Trade Commission, other agencies within jurisdiction, and internet service providers, and the statute gave no general private right of action to an individual recipient. Pre-emption made the email statute the most contested of the three measures of 2003. The Act superseded any state statute, regulation, or rule that expressly regulated the use of electronic mail to send commercial messages, except for state provisions that prohibited falsity or deception and except for specified state laws of general application. California had enacted an anti spam statute scheduled to take effect at the beginning of 2004 that would have required opt in consent and would have provided a private cause of action, and the California State Bar account of the federal statute recorded that the California law never took effect because the federal statute preempted state anti spam legislation. Marc Rotenberg, executive director of the Electronic Privacy Information Center, stated in Senate testimony that a weak federal statute preempting stronger state laws would reduce the level of consumer protection and facilitate the continued growth of spam. The enacted statute sided with uniformity and with opt out, and through pre-emption a sectoral statute could prevent a state from selecting a higher level of protection for the same practice.

The national do-not-call registry of 2003 supplied the third technique, since no single new consumer statute created the registry in the manner of a conventional enactment. The Federal Trade Commission created the registry by amendment of the Telemarketing Sales Rule, announced in December 2002 as part of the amended rule. Registration opened in the summer of 2003, telemarketers received access in September 2003 to remove registered numbers from call lists, and the Commission and the states began enforcement in October 2003, with violators subject to a fine of up to 11,000 dollars per violation under the schedule announced by the Commission in 2003. Funding and fee authority arrived through separate legislation. The Do-Not-Call Implementation Act (Public Law 108-10), enacted on 11 March 2003, authorized the Commission to promulgate regulations establishing fees sufficient to implement and enforce the do-not-call provisions of the Telemarketing Sales Rule and directed coordination with the Federal Communications Commission, which administered parallel rules under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act of 1991.

The Credit Card Accountability Responsibility and Disclosure Act of 2009, known as the Credit CARD Act of 2009 (Public Law 111-24), returned the narrative to the credit cluster and to conduct rules for a product that earlier federal law had treated principally through disclosure. The statute amended the Truth in Lending Act to restrict the circumstances and timing of interest rate increases, required advance notice of specified changes in terms, limited penalty fees and charges, restricted over limit fees to circumstances in which the consumer had affirmatively consented to over limit transactions, and imposed protections for younger consumers. The Federal Trade Commission summary describes provisions that prescribe open end credit lending procedures and enhanced disclosures, limit related fees and charges, increase related penalties, and establish constraints and protections for issuance of credit cards to minors and students. For consumers under twenty one, the statute required either an application cosigned by a person over twenty one with a means to repay or documentation of an independent means to repay, and the statute restricted prescreened offers and marketing inducements directed at students. Penalty fees had to be reasonable and proportional to the violation under regulations issued pursuant to the statute. Congress answered within the law of open end credit, addressed to credit card accounts, and the protections therefore applied because a transaction took a particular product form, not because a general statute governed consumer contracts.

The Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010 (Public Law 111-203) appeared to break the pattern at the level of institutions while preserving the pattern at the level of legal coverage, as [the account of how the CFPB was created and empowered](/2013/12/01/cfpb-creation-and-powers/) explains in institutional detail. Title X created the Bureau of Consumer Financial Protection, commonly called the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, as a bureau within the Federal Reserve System with a director appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate. Title X transferred to the Bureau rulemaking, supervision, and enforcement authority under the enumerated consumer laws, a defined list that included the Truth in Lending Act, the Equal Credit Opportunity Act, the Fair Credit Reporting Act, the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, the Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act, and the Electronic Fund Transfer Act. The designated transfer date was 21 July 2011, and the Bureau formally began operating on that date, one year after enactment of the Dodd-Frank Act on 21 July 2010. Consolidation of administration should not be confused with consolidation of substantive law. Before July 2011, authority over the enumerated consumer laws had been divided among seven federal agencies and departments, with the result that consumer protection competed inside several agencies against other missions. Title X placed a single dedicated regulator in charge of the federal law of consumer finance. The substantive rights of a borrower, a cardholder, and a checking account customer continued to arise from separate statutes, however, each with separate definitions of a covered person, a covered transaction, and an available remedy. The Bureau administered the credit cluster as a cluster. Title X created no bureau for privacy across industries and no general consumer protection agency empowered to write a unified code for every market. A sector specific super regulator therefore strengthened the sector that received the regulator.

By 15 February 2019, the record across seven decades supported a direct answer. Congress protected the data and the transaction in front of Congress, industry by industry, and no Congress enacted a general consumer protection statute or a general consumer privacy statute of the kind adopted in the European Union through the General Data Protection Regulation of 2016, Regulation (EU) 2016/679, adopted on 14 April 2016 and applicable from 25 May 2018. The European regulation applied across sectors, stated general principles for lawful processing, and conferred general rights on data subjects. Scholarly assessment divided over whether fit was a virtue or whether fragmentation was a defect. Paul M. Schwartz of the University of California, Berkeley, School of Law and Daniel J. Solove of the George Washington University Law School, in Reconciling Personal Information in the United States and European Union, published in the California Law Review in 2014, described American privacy law as a set of narrow sectoral laws focused on specific industries or specific contexts, in contrast to European omnibus law, and the same authors in earlier work criticized the disjointed, inconsistent, and often overly narrow definitions that resulted from statute by statute drafting. In that earlier work, Schwartz and Solove also developed an approach built on a continuum of risk of identification that, in their description, permitted tailored legal protections built around different levels of risk, and defenders of the sectoral method drew on that logic of tailoring to argue that a health record, a credit report, a communication, and a marketing file present different risks and different expectations, so that a rule fitted to one setting may misfit another. The criticism answered the defense at the level of coverage. Schwartz and Solove treated the incongruity between the American and European regimes as a source of confusion and of impediments to information flow and use. Definitions differed from statute to statute, consent meant opt in in one setting and opt out in another, a private action existed for a video disclosure and for an unlawful telephone call and did not exist for an individual recipient under the email statute, and a record of equal sensitivity could fall outside every federal category because the holder belonged to no regulated sector. Joel R. Reidenberg of Fordham University School of Law, quoted in later privacy scholarship for a formulation first published in the Federal Communications Law Journal in 1992, stated that the American legal system did not contain a comprehensive set of privacy rights or principles that collectively addressed the acquisition, storage, transmission, use, and disclosure of personal information within the business community. That sentence remained an accurate description of the architecture in February 2019 even though the quantity of federal protection had grown considerably after 1992. Growth had occurred by addition, and the sum was a body of law extensive in volume, exact in particulars, and incomplete by design.

## The Periods of the Field and the Case for the Patchwork

The seventy year record divides into periods with distinct legislative habits, and a periodization earns a place in the account only if the divisions explain choices that a simple chronology leaves mysterious. Six periods fit the evidence. Each period opened when Congress settled on a governing instrument, and each period closed when the limits of that instrument produced a different settlement. The boundaries fall at enactments rather than at round dates: 1953, 1962, 1968, 1980, 1984, and 2009. What changed across those boundaries was never the underlying method of legislating industry by industry. What changed was the scale of the unit Congress legislated for, from a single product, to a labelled package, to a whole transactional code, to a single communications channel, and at last to a whole sector of finance gathered under one regulator.

The first period, the age of single product safety statutes in the nineteen fifties, treated each hazard as a property of one article. The flammable fabrics legislation of 1953 answered clothing fires with a flammability test for wearing apparel. The refrigerator legislation of 1956 answered entrapment deaths with a door standard for one appliance. The hazardous substances labelling legislation of 1960 widened the unit modestly, from one article to a class of household substances, while keeping the instrument informational. No period statute created an institution, a reporting system, or a private remedy, and no period statute authorised any official to ask whether a neighbouring product presented the same risk. The legislator of the nineteen fifties trusted the incident report and the product definition, and distrusted delegation.

The second period, the charter and labelling age opened by the presidential message of 1962, supplied a vocabulary before supplying machinery. The four rights of the Consumer Bill of Rights gave advocates, administrators, and committees a shared heading under which unrelated grievances could be argued as one public question. The statutes of the period, the cigarette labelling act of 1965 and the packaging act of 1966, worked through compelled disclosure, and each statute disclosed the limits of disclosure in the same text that granted the duty: a hedged caution paired with preemption of stronger statements, a mandatory net quantity statement paired with a voluntary approach to package sizes. The automobile safety act of 1966 broke the pattern from inside the period by choosing performance standards over labels, and the National Commission on Product Safety, created in 1967, prepared the institutional answer that the next period enacted.

The third period, the institution building decade from 1968 to 1978, assembled the durable architecture of the field. Congress enacted the Consumer Credit Protection Act in 1968 and then treated that act as an open vessel, appending the Fair Credit Reporting Act in 1970, the Equal Credit Opportunity Act in 1974 and 1976, the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act in 1977, and the Electronic Fund Transfer Act in 1978. Congress created the Consumer Product Safety Commission in 1972, gathered the older safety statutes under that commission in 1973, and armed the Federal Trade Commission in 1975 with warranty jurisdiction and with Section 18 rulemaking power over entire trades. Private rights of action, fee shifting, administrative rulemaking, injury surveillance, and recall authority all entered the system during the decade. The sectoral method survived the transformation intact: the safety commission charter excluded automobiles, pesticides, firearms, and food and drugs, and the credit titles divided protection by product and by actor, but the units of legislation had grown from products to industries and institutions.

The fourth period, the restraint age opened by the Federal Trade Commission Improvements Act of 1980, narrowed the ambitions of the generalist agency while leaving the sectoral statutes untouched. The 1980 settlement subjected trade regulation rules to congressional disapproval, barred a children advertising rule founded on unfairness, layered advance notice and regulatory analysis over rulemaking, and closed the Commission for a day in a funding dispute that dramatised the new relationship. Federal lawmaking after 1980 narrowed to single industries and single practices, and general enforcement energy migrated to state attorneys general working under state unfair practices statutes that, unlike the federal act, carried private remedies. The restraint age therefore redistributed authority rather than repealing protection: downward to the states, and inward to narrower federal texts.

The fifth period, the sectoral privacy and digital age from 1984 to 2003, applied the oldest method in the system to the newest subject. Cable subscriber records received duties of consent, access, and destruction in 1984. Video rental records received stronger duties and liquidated damages in 1988, after a Supreme Court nomination exposed the records of the nominee. Telephone intrusion received a private action in 1991, children online received a consent regime in 1998, and the measures of 2003 divided among credit file accuracy, commercial email, and telemarketing. No period statute stated a general principle for personal information, and the consent concept changed meaning from statute to statute, opt in for a child website, opt out for an email sender, written consent for a video disclosure.

The sixth period, the financial consolidation of 2009 and 2010, gathered the oldest code in the field under a new roof. The Credit CARD Act of 2009 converted parts of credit card law from disclosure to conduct rules, restricting rate increases, penalty fees, and over limit charges. Title X of the Dodd-Frank Act then transferred rulemaking, supervision, and enforcement under the enumerated consumer laws to the Bureau of Consumer Financial Protection, which began operating on 21 July 2011. Consolidation was administrative rather than substantive. The bureau inherited the credit titles as separate statutes with separate definitions and remedies, and no parallel bureau arrived for product safety, privacy, or general consumer protection. The period closed the arc at the point of greatest institutional concentration the sectoral method had produced, a dedicated regulator for one sector.

The counter reading deserves statement in the strongest form, because the patchwork had serious defenders and produced serious law. Sectoral drafting allowed Congress to fit duties to the practice of an industry: error resolution deadlines suited to bank records, packaging tests suited to the strength of a five year old child, consent rules suited to the records a cable operator actually kept. A single statutory definition of consumer harm, stretched across banking and toys and telemarketing, would have fitted each setting worse, and general standards delegated the hard questions to agencies and courts in any event, as the fate of the unfairness standard after 1980 demonstrated. Paul Schwartz and Daniel Solove, the scholars whose work supplied much of the vocabulary for the debate, developed a defence along these lines in an approach built on a continuum of risk, under which tailored legal protections calibrated to different levels of risk could match the varied expectations attaching to a health record, a credit report, and a marketing file. On that account, variety among statutes registered genuine variety among the underlying practices.

The same scholars supplied the criticism with equal force. Schwartz and Solove also described the product of statute by statute drafting as disjointed and inconsistent coverage, with definitions of personal information and of consent shifting from one law to the next and with protection turning on classifications that bore no relation to the sensitivity of the record. Joel Reidenberg stated the coverage objection in a sentence from 1992 that the following decades did not falsify: the American legal system contained no comprehensive set of privacy rights or principles addressing the acquisition, storage, transmission, use, and disclosure of personal information within the business community. Weighing coherence against fit produces no automatic winner. Fit explains why particular regimes, child resistant packaging, credit reporting procedure, video rental confidentiality, worked well inside their boundaries. Coherence explains why a buyer harmed at a boundary, by a record in an unregulated sector or a practice shared across sectors, found the boundary itself to be the obstacle. The record supports the narrower conclusion that tailoring was a genuine virtue purchased at a genuine price, and that Congress paid the price knowingly in statute after statute.

The honest limits of the system follow from the architecture rather than from any failure of drafting skill. A sectoral method structurally misses harms arising in industries Congress has not reached, and arrival of a statute historically required a precipitating incident vivid enough to sustain a legislative campaign, so unglamorous and diffuse harms waited longest. A sectoral method also misses data categories that no statute covers, because duties attach to defined records held by defined actors, and a holder outside every definition owed no federal duty of care, access, or deletion however sensitive the information became in aggregation. Enforcement fragmentation compounded the coverage gaps. Authority over the consumer field sat divided among the Federal Trade Commission, the product safety commission, the financial bureau, the communications commission, the Food and Drug Administration, the banking agencies, and fifty state attorneys general, each with separate priorities, budgets, and jurisdictional edges, so a practice crossing agency lines could be every regulator second concern and no regulator first concern. Those limits were not defects that better administration could cure. Those limits were the sectoral settlement itself, restated from the standpoint of a person whose harm fell between the statutes.

## What the Sequence Adds Up To

The sequence supports the claim with which the account began, stated as a conclusion rather than as a premise: American buyers between 1950 and 2019 were protected by industry, not by right. Every major protection in the record arrived attached to a named product, a named transaction, a named record, or a named institution, and every protection stopped where the definition stopped. The flammable fabric was regulated and the upholstery fabric waited. The third party collector was regulated and the creditor collecting an owned debt stood outside the title. The video rental record carried liquidated damages and the library record depended on state law. The pattern repeated across safety, credit, warranty, privacy, and finance for seventy years, through administrations and Congresses of every composition, because the pattern was the method by which federal consumer law got enacted at all: a grievance became legible to Congress as an industry problem, and an industry problem received an industry statute.

The conclusion carries a practical instruction for anyone reading federal consumer law. The first question is never whether a practice seems unfair in the abstract, and never whether a neighbouring transaction received protection. The first question is classificatory: which statute, if any, names the product, the actor, and the practice, and what remedy that statute provides, private damages, agency enforcement, a disclosure duty, or no federal duty at all. Mastery of the field consists largely of mastery of those boundaries, the exclusions inside the safety commission charter, the actor definition inside the debt collection title, the holder definitions inside the privacy statutes, and the enumerated list of laws transferred to the financial bureau in 2010. A student of the sequence can therefore read a new consumer controversy by locating the gap or the title into which the controversy falls, and can predict, from the history, that any federal answer will arrive bounded in the same manner as the answers that came before.

Depth on any single law belongs to the statute articles of the cluster, which treat each enactment at full length: the drafting history, the operative sections, the enforcement record, and the amendments that later Congresses appended. The era account and the statute accounts serve different purposes, and a reader tracing one law through the decades will move between the two levels, using the sequence to understand why a statute took the shape the statute took, and using the statute text to test the general claim against particular language. Keeping the two levels aligned demands orderly notes, and a reader can [keep your statute notes, citations, and case chronologies together free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html) while moving between the public law number in a table, the section analysis in a statute article, and the period argument of an era survey.

The seventy year record ends, at the boundary date of February 2019, with the settlement intact and extensively built out. Protection in the American consumer economy was deep where Congress had legislated, uneven where legislation had arrived late, and absent where no incident had yet forced a category into being. That unevenness was not an accident awaiting a tidier draftsman. That unevenness was the accumulated result of hundreds of discrete legislative judgments, each defensible on the evidence of the harm that prompted the judgment, and the sum of those judgments is the field itself.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What consumer protection laws has Congress passed since 1950?

Congress passed statutes industry by industry rather than a single general code. The sequence began with product measures: the Flammable Fabrics Act of 1953, the Refrigerator Safety Act of 1956, and the Federal Hazardous Substances Labeling Act of 1960. Labelling and credit measures followed, among them the cigarette labelling act of 1965, the Fair Packaging and Labeling Act of 1966, and the Consumer Credit Protection Act of 1968. The nineteen seventies added the Fair Credit Reporting Act, the Consumer Product Safety Act, the Equal Credit Opportunity Act, the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act, the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, and the Electronic Fund Transfer Act. Later decades added sectoral privacy statutes for cable, video rentals, telephones, and children online, the Credit CARD Act of 2009, and Title X of the Dodd-Frank Act of 2010.

### Q: Why is US consumer protection law a patchwork?

The patchwork resulted from the method by which Congress legislated. A specific harm, a clothing fire, a trapped child, a false credit file, a published rental record, concentrated public attention on one product or practice, and hearings translated that harm into a product definition or an actor definition. The resulting statute governed the category named in the definition and left neighbouring categories to other statutes, to state law, or to no statute. Committees, agencies, and remedies accumulated around each category, which made the next measure easier to draft as another bounded statute than as a general code. Repetition across seventy years, rather than any single drafting decision, produced a system in which protection followed classification.

### Q: What was the Consumer Bill of Rights in consumer protection history?

The Consumer Bill of Rights was the name later given to the four protections President John F. Kennedy set out in a Special Message on Protecting the Consumer Interest sent to Congress on 15 March 1962. The message named a right to safety against goods hazardous to health or life, a right to be informed through protection against deceptive information and access to facts for choice, a right to choose among products and services at competitive prices, and a right to be heard through consideration of consumer interests in government policy. The message enacted nothing by itself. The four rights supplied a shared vocabulary that committees, administrators, and advocates invoked across unrelated bills for decades afterward.

### Q: Why does a video rental privacy law exist in consumer protection?

The Video Privacy Protection Act of 1988 exists because the confirmation hearings for Judge Robert Bork, nominated to the Supreme Court in 1987, exposed rental records to public view. A reporter for the Washington City Paper obtained the rental records of Judge Bork and his family from a local store, and the newspaper published a list of 146 films. The list revealed ordinary family viewing, and the absence of scandal strengthened the legislative reaction, because the episode showed that any renter record could reach print. Congress responded within roughly a year with a statute barring video tape service providers from knowingly disclosing personally identifiable information about a consumer, with liquidated damages for a violation.

### Q: Is there a general federal consumer privacy law in consumer protection?

No general federal consumer privacy statute existed through 15 February 2019. Federal protection rested on sector specific laws governing defined records held by defined actors: credit reports under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, cable subscriber information under the Cable Communications Policy Act of 1984, video rental records under the Video Privacy Protection Act of 1988, children information online under the Children Online Privacy Protection Act of 1998, and health and financial records under separate statutes for those industries. The Federal Trade Commission also proceeded case by case against deceptive privacy promises and unreasonable security practices under the Federal Trade Commission Act. No enactment conferred a single set of collection, access, correction, and disclosure rights across industries.

### Q: What did the 1970s add to consumer protection law?

The nineteen seventies converted a collection of product statutes into an institutional system. The Poison Prevention Packaging Act of 1970 required child resistant packaging for hazardous household substances. The Consumer Product Safety Act of 1972 created an independent commission with authority to set standards, ban hazardous products, gather injury data, and order recalls. Inside the Consumer Credit Protection Act, Congress added the Fair Credit Reporting Act in 1970, the Equal Credit Opportunity Act in 1974, the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act in 1977, and the Electronic Fund Transfer Act in 1978. The Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act of 1975 rebuilt warranty disclosure and enforcement and granted the Federal Trade Commission express rulemaking power over unfair or deceptive practices across entire trades.

### Q: When was the consumer financial agency created in consumer protection?

The consumer financial agency, the Bureau of Consumer Financial Protection, was created by Title X of the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010, Public Law 111-203, enacted on 21 July 2010. Title X established the Bureau within the Federal Reserve System, under a director appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate, and transferred rulemaking, supervision, and enforcement authority under the enumerated consumer laws, including the Truth in Lending Act, the Equal Credit Opportunity Act, the Fair Credit Reporting Act, and the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act. The designated transfer date was 21 July 2011, and the Bureau formally began operating on that date.

### Q: What is the timeline of US consumer protection law?

The timeline runs through six periods. Single product safety statutes opened the record in the nineteen fifties, covering flammable fabrics in 1953, refrigerator doors in 1956, and hazardous substance labels in 1960. A charter and labelling period followed the presidential message of 1962, producing cigarette and packaging labelling statutes in 1965 and 1966. An institution building decade ran from the Consumer Credit Protection Act of 1968 to the Electronic Fund Transfer Act of 1978, adding the product safety commission and trade commission rulemaking. A restraint period opened with the Federal Trade Commission Improvements Act of 1980. Sectoral privacy statutes followed from 1984 to 2003, and financial consolidation closed the record with the Credit CARD Act of 2009 and the Dodd-Frank Act of 2010.

### Q: What did the Flammable Fabrics Act do?

The Flammable Fabrics Act of 1953, Public Law 83-88, prohibited the introduction into interstate commerce of articles of wearing apparel and fabrics for wearing apparel that were so highly flammable as to be dangerous when worn. Congress acted after reports of brushed rayon garments, described as torch sweaters, and long pile rayon cowboy chaps worn by children igniting and burning with exceptional speed. The statute operated through a named testing method drawn from a Department of Commerce commercial standard, Commercial Standard 191-53, rather than through a general delegation of textile rulemaking power. Enforcement rested with the Federal Trade Commission through exclusion from commerce, injunction and condemnation proceedings, and penalties. The Act reached wearing apparel alone and created no federal damages remedy.

### Q: How did the Consumer Product Safety Commission get its powers?

The Consumer Product Safety Commission received powers from the Consumer Product Safety Act of 1972, Public Law 92-573, enacted after the National Commission on Product Safety reported in 1970 that litigation, voluntary standards, and fragmented federal programs had failed against unreasonable product risks. The Act created the Commission as an independent agency governed by five commissioners and defined consumer product broadly across household goods. The Commission received authority to prescribe safety standards for performance, composition, design, packaging, and labelling, to declare products banned hazardous products where a standard could not reduce a risk adequately, to gather injury information through a hospital surveillance system, and, after notice and hearing, to order public notice and repair, replacement, or refund for a substantial product hazard.

### Q: What rights does the Fair Credit Reporting Act give consumers?

The Fair Credit Reporting Act, added to the Consumer Credit Protection Act in 1970, gave a consumer procedural rights against a reporting system that operated without the subject of a file. A consumer could obtain disclosure of the information in a file, learn the sources of information and the recipients of reports, and dispute the completeness or accuracy of any item. Upon a dispute, the agency had to reinvestigate and delete information found inaccurate or unverifiable. A user of a report who took adverse action based on the report had to notify the consumer and identify the reporting agency. The Act also confined reports to permissible purposes and barred most adverse information after seven years, with civil liability for willful and negligent noncompliance.

### Q: Why did Congress restrict the Federal Trade Commission?

Congress restricted the Federal Trade Commission in 1980 after the rulemaking programme of the late nineteen seventies mobilised the trades that programme reached. Under Chairman Michael Pertschuk, the Commission pursued rules on funeral practices, used motor vehicle sales, vocational schools, and advertising directed at children, and a 1978 editorial in The Washington Post described the agency as a national nanny. Business organisations and members of Congress argued that a general prohibition on unfairness allowed commissioners to regulate virtually any practice. The Federal Trade Commission Improvements Act of 1980 answered with a two house legislative veto over rules, a bar on a children advertising rule founded on unfairness, advance notice duties, and regulatory analysis requirements.

### Q: What is a little FTC act?

A little FTC act is a state unfair and deceptive acts and practices statute modelled on Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act, which declares unfair or deceptive acts or practices in commerce unlawful. Most states enacted such statutes from the mid nineteen sixties to the mid nineteen seventies, as the National Consumer Law Center recorded in a survey of the state laws. The state statutes mattered because the federal act gave a consumer no private right of action under Section 5, whereas state statutes authorised private damage actions. Many state statutes also provided attorney fees for a prevailing consumer and punitive, treble, or minimum damage awards, and state attorneys general enforced the statutes through consumer protection divisions.

### Q: What did the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act change for buyers?

The Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act of 1975 changed warranty practice without requiring any manufacturer to offer a warranty. Once a supplier chose to offer a written warranty, federal disclosure rules attached, requiring full and conspicuous disclosure of terms and conditions in simple language and availability of terms before purchase. A warranty could carry the designation full only if the warrantor remedied defects without charge within a reasonable time, imposed no unreasonable conditions, and replaced the product or refunded the price after failed repair attempts. The Act barred disclaimer of implied warranties by a supplier offering a written warranty, restricted tie in conditions, encouraged informal dispute settlement, and created a federal cause of action with recovery of attorney fees for a prevailing consumer.

### Q: How does the Electronic Fund Transfer Act limit losses from unauthorised transfers?

The Electronic Fund Transfer Act of 1978 allocated loss from an unauthorised transfer according to the speed of notice by the consumer. A consumer who notified the financial institution within two business days after learning of the loss or theft of an access device bore liability of no more than fifty dollars. A consumer who delayed beyond two business days faced liability capped at five hundred dollars. A consumer who failed to report an unauthorised transfer appearing on a periodic statement within sixty days faced unlimited liability for transfers occurring after that period that timely notice would have prevented. The Act also required the institution to investigate a reported error, ordinarily within ten business days or with provisional credit during a longer investigation, and placed the burden of proof on authorisation on the institution.

### Q: What did the Fair and Accurate Credit Transactions Act add?

The Fair and Accurate Credit Transactions Act of 2003, Public Law 108-159, amended the Fair Credit Reporting Act in response to identity theft and to disputes about accuracy and access in consumer reports. The Act gave a consumer the right to one free disclosure of a consumer file from each nationwide consumer reporting agency during each twelve month period. A consumer who suspected fraud or identity theft could place a fraud alert in a file, with initial and extended alerts for identity theft victims, and could block reporting of information that resulted from identity theft. Merchants could print no more than the last five digits of a card number on an electronically printed receipt and could not print the expiration date. The Act also addressed credit scores, risk based pricing notices, disposal of consumer information, and red flags procedures.

### Q: Why does the United States lack a single data protection statute?

The absence of a single data protection statute followed from the sectoral history of American privacy law. Congress legislated record by record: credit reports in 1970, cable subscriber information in 1984, video rental records in 1988, children information online in 1998, and health and financial records under statutes for those industries. Each enactment defined a holder, a record, and a remedy, and no enactment generalised the definitions. Joel Reidenberg observed in 1992 that the American system contained no comprehensive set of privacy rights or principles for personal information in the business community, and Paul Schwartz and Daniel Solove described the resulting body of law as narrow sectoral statutes with disjointed and inconsistent definitions. Institutional division reinforced the pattern, because privacy duties sat inside communications, commerce, health, and finance regimes administered by different agencies.

### Q: What did the Credit CARD Act prohibit?

The Credit Card Accountability Responsibility and Disclosure Act of 2009, Public Law 111-24, restricted conduct that the Truth in Lending Act had previously addressed mainly through disclosure. The Act restricted the circumstances and timing of interest rate increases on card accounts, required advance notice of specified changes in terms, and required penalty fees to be reasonable and proportional to the violation under regulations issued pursuant to the Act. An issuer could charge an over limit fee only where the consumer had affirmatively consented to over limit transactions. For consumers under twenty one, the Act required either an application cosigned by a person over twenty one with a means to repay or documentation of an independent means to repay, and restricted prescreened offers and marketing inducements directed at students.

### Q: Which federal agency enforces the most consumer protection statutes?

The Federal Trade Commission held the broadest enforcement portfolio across consumer markets through February 2019. Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act reached unfair or deceptive acts or practices across most of the economy, and the Commission also enforced or administered duties under the Truth in Lending Act, the Fair Credit Reporting Act, the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act, the Children Online Privacy Protection Act, the CAN-SPAM Act, and the Telemarketing Sales Rule, among other instruments. Other agencies held deeper authority inside single sectors: the Consumer Product Safety Commission over household products, the Bureau of Consumer Financial Protection over consumer finance after 2011, and the Federal Communications Commission over telephone practices. Breadth across industries, rather than depth inside one industry, distinguished the Commission portfolio.

### Q: How do state consumer protection laws interact with federal law?

State and federal consumer protection law operated as overlapping layers rather than as a single hierarchy. State unfair and deceptive practices statutes, the little FTC acts, authorised private damage actions that the Federal Trade Commission Act withheld, and state attorneys general sued national sellers under state law, often in coordinated multistate investigations. Federal statutes sometimes set a floor that state law could exceed, and sometimes displaced state law expressly: the cigarette labelling act of 1965 preempted stronger state health statements on packages and in advertising for a period of years, and the CAN-SPAM Act of 2003 superseded state statutes that expressly regulated commercial email, apart from provisions prohibiting falsity or deception. The governing statute in a dispute therefore depended on preemption language as much as on the underlying conduct.
