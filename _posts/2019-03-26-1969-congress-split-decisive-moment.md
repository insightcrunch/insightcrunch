The 1969 Congress split is usually told as a story of clashing wills: a young Prime Minister, Indira Gandhi, against the five ageing bosses of the syndicate, in a fight over who would sit in Rashtrapati Bhavan. The personalities were real, and the quarrel over Neelam Sanjeeva Reddy and Varahagiri Venkata Giri was genuinely bitter. Yet the personality story explains the spark and misses the fire. Between January and November 1969 the Indian National Congress did something no governing party in a parliamentary democracy is supposed to survive doing to itself: it broke the whip on a vote its own candidate had to win, expelled its own Prime Minister, and divided into two parties that then asked the electorate to decide which of them was the Congress. The electorate answered in 1971, but the deeper settlement was institutional. The split taught every later holder of high office that party discipline was a negotiable instrument, that an organisation could be outbid by a direct appeal to voters, and that the great offices of the Constitution could be fought over as factional prizes. Those lessons, once learned, were never unlearned.

The claim defended here can be stated in one sentence. The 1969 split was the structural pivot of post-Independence Indian politics, more consequential than the Emergency that followed six years later, because the split established the precedent that party discipline could be sacrificed for personal authority, and everything from 1975 to 1991 follows from this decision tree. The Emergency of June 1975 was an episode, terrible and finite, which the voters of March 1977 reversed at the ballot box. The split was a change in the operating rules of the system itself. Before November 1969, a Congress Prime Minister governed through an organisation that could, in principle, remove her; the syndicate had installed Lal Bahadur Shastri in June 1964 and Indira Gandhi in January 1966 on precisely that assumption. After November 1969, the Prime Minister owned the party in a sense no predecessor had: its legislators owed their tickets to her, its committees took their membership from her nominees, and its president at the Bombay session of December 1969, Jagjivan Ram, held office by her favour. The personality reading of the split, represented in its most accomplished form by Inder Malhotra's Indira Gandhi: A Personal and Political Biography (1989), captures the proximate trigger with unmatched narrative detail. The structural reading, which Rajni Kothari's Politics in India (1970) made possible and which this article defends, explains why the consequences ran for decades after the personalities had left the stage. Adjudicating between those readings on the primary record, the expulsion resolution of 12 November 1969 preserved in the AICC Papers at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library, the strategic memoranda in the Mohan Kumaramangalam papers, and the Lok Sabha debates of August to November 1969, is the work of the sections that follow.

## The Personality Story, and What It Leaves Standing

The standard telling begins in the summer of 1969 and runs on rails of temperament. Indira Gandhi, installed by the syndicate in 1966 and never forgiven by its members for growing independent, wanted a President who would not threaten her. The syndicate, the informal directorate of K. Kamaraj of Tamil Nadu, S. Nijalingappa of Mysore, Atulya Ghosh of Bengal, S. K. Patil of Bombay and Neelam Sanjeeva Reddy of Andhra Pradesh, with Morarji Desai attached tactically rather than by loyalty, wanted a President who would. President Zakir Husain died in office on 3 May 1969, and the succession to Rashtrapati Bhavan became the terrain on which the two camps fought. The syndicate's candidate was Reddy, a syndicate member himself, Speaker of the Lok Sabha from March 1967 until his resignation to contest the election in July 1969. The Prime Minister's counterweight was Vice President V. V. Giri, a veteran trade union leader who declared his independent candidacy in July 1969 and whom she backed through the famous appeal to a conscience vote. Giri won on 20 August 1969. The syndicate retaliated on 12 November 1969 by expelling the Prime Minister from the party she led in Parliament, and the Congress divided into the Congress (Organisation), promptly shortened to Congress (O), and the Congress (Requisitionists), or Congress (R), around her. Every element of that narrative is true, and the best of the biographical literature, Malhotra's 1989 volume above all, documents it from interviews with the participants themselves.

The syndicate's confidence in the summer of 1969 rested on the memory of January 1966, and the memory deserves scrutiny because it taught the bosses exactly the wrong lesson. When Shastri died at Tashkent on 11 January 1966, the syndicate had managed the succession as a committee operation: soundings in the state capitals, a managed contest in the Congress Parliamentary Party, and a result, Indira Gandhi's defeat of Morarji Desai by 355 votes to 169 on 19 January 1966, that the managers read as proof of their machinery's command over the parliamentary party's mind. The reading confounded two different things: the members' willingness to accept the managers' candidate when no alternative centre of power existed, and the members' obedience once such a centre existed in the person of a sitting Prime Minister of three years' standing, possessed of the cabinet's patronage and a public programme. By 1969 the alternative centre existed, and the machinery's command had quietly lapsed; the tragedy of the syndicate's generalship is that its most recent victory concealed the lapse from the victors. A personnel system that mistakes a managed succession for a permanent constitution will repeat the manoeuvre until the manoeuvre destroys it, and Bangalore was the repetition.

The narrative nevertheless leaves the largest questions standing. A personality conflict explains why these particular people fought in this particular year; it does not explain why the fight could not be contained, as earlier Congress fights had been contained in 1951, in 1959 and in 1963. The Congress had absorbed the resignation of its president Purushottam Das Tandon in September 1951 after Jawaharlal Nehru forced the issue, and it had absorbed the Kamaraj Plan of October 1963, when six Union ministers and six chief ministers surrendered office at the party's request, without splitting. In 1969 the same party, facing a dispute over a largely ceremonial office, split down the middle and stayed split. What had changed was not the temperaments, which were constants, but the structure underneath them: an organisation hollowed by two decades of governing, a parliamentary party whose members had learned to look to the leader rather than the committee, and a Prime Minister who had discovered, in the bank nationalisation of 19 July 1969, that a single dramatic act could manufacture a constituency no committee could veto. The personality framing also fails the simplest test of consequence. If the split had been about persons, the departure of the persons would have closed the question; instead the precedents set in 1969 were available to be used by successors who had never met the syndicate, and they were used, in 1975, in the 1980s and in the decades after.

A further defect of the personality story is that it treats the presidential election as a prize fight when the participants understood it as a constitutional lever. The President of India in 1969 was no cipher in a succession crisis, because the office carried the powers that a disputed future would need: the power to appoint a Prime Minister when no party held a clear majority, the power to return or withhold assent, the power to promulgate ordinances, the power to act on advice whose binding force the country would litigate for years. Giri, as acting President after 3 May 1969, demonstrated the ordinance power in the most concrete way imaginable when he signed the bank nationalisation ordinance on 19 July 1969, hours before resigning to contest the election. A syndicate President in 1975, the syndicate's members calculated, might refuse to sign an emergency proclamation or might dismiss a Prime Minister who had lost a court case; a loyal President would sign. The fight over Reddy and Giri was therefore a fight over the insurance policy for every future crisis, and both camps said so in the language of the time, the syndicate speaking of constitutional propriety and the Prime Minister's camp speaking of a President independent of party bosses. Stanley Wolpert's India (fourth edition, 2009) treats the episode within the Indira-era chapters as a turning point in the centralisation of power, and the judgment of this article follows Wolpert on the turning point while pressing further: the pivot was structural before it was personal, and its mechanism can be dated, month by month, in the record of 1969.

## A Party Already Hollowed: The Congress After the 1967 Election

The split's precondition was an organisation that had been losing its inner life for years. The general election of February 1967 cut the Congress majority in the Lok Sabha to 283 seats out of 520, the party's vote share fell below 41 percent, and Congress governments fell across a belt of states that included Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, West Bengal, Orissa, Madras and Kerala, where Samyukta Vidhayak Dal coalitions of mutually hostile opposition parties took office on the single shared programme of keeping the Congress out. The defeats of 1967 were the electorate's verdict on the settlement whose fragility [the Nehruvian-era institutional fragility framework](/2019/01/25/nehruvian-era-settlement-three-tensions/) reconstructs: a party that had converted itself from a movement into a machine for distributing office, and whose district committees increasingly existed to ratify decisions taken in state capitals. Rajni Kothari had described the older arrangement in his 1964 essay on the Congress system and then in Politics in India (1970) as a system in which the Congress functioned as a party of consensus, absorbing dissent internally through factions that bargained inside one organisation. The corollary Kothari's framework supplied, and which 1969 would test, was that the system depended on the factions preferring internal bargaining to exit. Once a faction calculated that it could win outside, the system had no second line of defence.

Two earlier shocks had already loosened the bolts. The first was [the post-1962 institutional self-confidence breakdown](/2019/02/16/1962-china-war-decline-nehru/), when defeat in the border war destroyed the aura of the founding leadership and taught ambitious men that the centre could err and could be said to err. The second was the syndicate's own remedy for decay, examined in [the Kamaraj Plan's organisational refresh failure](/2019/02/27/kamaraj-plan-1963-congress/): the resignations of October 1963 refreshed the ministry's faces while leaving the committee structure untouched, and Kamaraj himself, moving from the chief ministership of Madras to the Congress presidency, became the first of the bosses to learn that organisational office without governmental office decays into brokerage. By 1969 the syndicate's members held no major ministry among them except through Morarji Desai, who sat in the cabinet as Deputy Prime Minister and Finance Minister on terms of armed truce, and their authority rested on control of state units whose legislatures the 1967 results had already shaken. A directorate that cannot deliver its own states is a directorate negotiating from memory, and the memory was of the two successions, analysed in [the syndicate's two gambles](/2019/03/15/after-nehru-1964-succession-question/), in which they had chosen Shastri and then Indira Gandhi as the candidates every faction could accept and no faction had to obey.

Indira Gandhi's side of the precondition was a parliamentary party transformed in composition. The Congress legislators elected in 1967, and the larger intake that would come in 1971, owed their nominations less to district organisations than to the leader's perceived capacity to win; a member who believes his seat is the leader's gift will follow the leader against the committee when the two collide, which is the precise behavioural fact the conscience vote of August 1969 would reveal. Inside the cabinet and the party forums, a younger cohort, the men and women journalists called the Young Turks, among them Chandra Shekhar, Mohan Dharia, Krishan Kant and others, pressed an ideological programme drawn from the ten-point programme the AICC had adopted at its Delhi session of May 1967: social control of banking, ceilings on urban property, abolition of princely privileges, state trading in foodgrains. The ten-point programme gave the Prime Minister's camp a language in which a power struggle could be redescribed as a mandate, and the redescription was not cynical decoration. It identified the conflict's stakes for audiences outside the committee rooms, in the press and in the state units, and it is why the syndicate's procedural case, however strong inside the Parliamentary Board, collapsed in the country within weeks of the Bangalore decision.

The state-level record of 1967 to 1969 deserves a paragraph of its own, because the split was fought and counted in the states. In Bihar the Samyukta Vidhayak Dal government of Mahamaya Prasad Sinha, sworn in during March 1967, was the first non-Congress government in the state's history and a standing advertisement that coalitions of Jana Sangh, socialist and Communist legislators could hold office together. In West Bengal the United Front governments of Ajoy Mukherjee, first in 1967 and again from February 1969, put a Congress defector in the chief minister's chair in Calcutta itself, governing in harness with the Communist Party of India (Marxist) that the Congress had spent two decades opposing. In Madras the DMK victory of 1967 ended Congress rule permanently in the state that Kamaraj had governed for nine years, and Kamaraj himself lost his own assembly contest at Virudhunagar to a young DMK candidate, a personal defeat that converted the syndicate's most respected figure into a leader whose authority ran through Delhi committees because it could no longer run through a state electorate. Each of these facts weakened the syndicate's central claim in the same way: the organisation it presented as the party's foundation had already been voted out of the foundation's largest rooms.

The erosion had a quieter register inside the Congress itself, in the membership and committee life that the party's constitution imagined and that the 1960s allowed to lapse. Organisational elections within the Congress, the ladder from primary committees through district and Pradesh committees to the AICC, were held irregularly and managed where they were held; enrolment drives produced paper members in blocs controlled by state leaders; and the Working Committee itself, formally the party's highest executive between sessions, drew its weight from the standing of its members rather than from any recent mandate of the membership. None of this made the organisation fake. The Congress of 1969 still possessed the deepest local networks of any Indian party, and in most districts its committee remained the address at which politics was transacted. What had lapsed was the organisation's capacity to renew its own authority by internal contest, and a body that cannot renew its authority cannot easily defend it, because every defence it mounts must borrow authority from an election it has postponed. The requisitioned AICC of November 1969 would expose exactly that borrowing: when the two sides counted heads, the side whose authority came from a parliamentary majority and a popular programme outcounted the side whose authority came from the committee rolls.

## The Machinery the Syndicate Owned

To see what the syndicate was defending, and why its defence took the procedural form it did, the party's constitutional machinery needs a short description, since the crisis of 1969 was at every stage a fight conducted through that machinery's forms. The Congress constitution built the party as a pyramid. Primary members enrolled in local committees; district committees and Pradesh Congress Committees governed the party in the districts and the states; the All India Congress Committee, some seven hundred members drawn from the Pradesh committees, stood as the party's deliberative body between its plenary sessions; and the Congress Working Committee, chaired by the party president, acted as the executive. Beside the pyramid stood two bodies of the parliamentary wing: the Congress Parliamentary Party, comprising the party's members of Parliament under its own leader and executive, and the Congress Parliamentary Board, the small committee that settled questions touching the parliamentary party as a whole, among them, by convention and practice, the choice of the party's candidate for the presidency of India. Candidate selection for elections ran through a parallel channel, Pradesh election committees proposing and the Central Election Committee disposing, in bargaining between the centre and the state bosses.

Every element of that machinery mattered in 1969, and each side's strategy can be read off it. The syndicate's strength sat in the pyramid: Nijalingappa's presidency of the party from January 1968, the Working Committee majority his presidency assembled, the Pradesh committees its members commanded. The Prime Minister's strength sat in the parliamentary wing and outside the constitution altogether: the parliamentary party's confidence, the cabinet's powers, and the programme's appeal to voters who were not members of anything. The Bangalore contest was therefore a jurisdictional dispute before it was a personal one. The Parliamentary Board's claim to settle the presidential candidate was genuine, rooted in the practice of 1952, 1957, 1962 and 1967; the Prime Minister's counter-claim, that a candidate imposed on a divided parliamentary party by a board of bosses was no candidate at all, was equally genuine as politics and considerably weaker as party law. The crisis became a split at the moment each side chose the forum in which it was strongest and refused the other's: the Board voted, the Prime Minister appealed over the Board's head to the legislators, the Working Committee expelled, the AICC by requisition unexpelled. A student of institutions reading the sequence sees not anarchy but hyper-legality, two constitutions being operated at once inside one party, until the Election Commission and then the electorate were left to decide which operation had been the party's own.

One clause of that constitution deserves separate mention, because it supplied the split's very name. The party's rules allowed a meeting of the AICC to be requisitioned by its members, on signatures, when the ordinary convening authority would not summon one; the device existed for emergencies of conscience against a captured executive, and it had slept in the text, unused at the national level, for the whole of the party's governing life. Indira Gandhi's supporters woke it in November 1969, assembling the signatures of a majority of the AICC's members to compel the session that the president would never have called, and the session's decisions, voiding the expulsion and reconstituting the leadership, derived their formal validity from that clause and from nothing else. Her party carried the clause in its name for the rest of its separate existence: Congress (R), the Requisitionists, a faction christened by a procedural device, which is as precise a summary as the decade affords of how the old Congress actually died, by its own forms, operated in earnest by people who had learned that forms follow followings.

## Faridabad: The Groundwork of Two Camps

Faridabad's prehistory matters, because the chair from which Nijalingappa presided over the crisis had itself changed hands in a way that foreshadowed it. Kamaraj, Congress president since October 1963, resigned the presidency after the defeats of 1967, accepting a share of the organisation's responsibility for them, and at the turn of 1968 the party elected S. Nijalingappa, the former chief minister of Mysore, as his successor. The succession transferred the presidency from the one syndicate figure whose authority was national and personal to a figure whose authority was institutional and regional at the moment the institution's authority was entering its steepest decline, and it concentrated in Nijalingappa's hands, as president, the powers of convening, of constituting committees and of speaking for the party, which he proceeded to exercise with a literal fidelity to the party constitution that his predecessor's political weight would have tempered. The AICC session at Faridabad in April 1969 is the first entry in the split's decision tree, and its importance is easy to miss because nothing was decided there. The session met to take stock of a party two years past its worst electoral result, and the stocktaking split along a line that had nothing to do with any candidate. Nijalingappa's presidential address, delivered as Congress president since his election to the office in January 1968, broke with the custom by which a Congress president cleared the text with the Prime Minister before speaking; the address lectured the government on fiscal discipline and on the dangers of populist economics, and the Prime Minister's camp read the omission of clearance as a declaration that the organisation no longer served the government but audited it. Indira Gandhi's reply from the same platform insisted that the party's social programme could not be postponed to a calmer season, and delegates left Faridabad understanding that the question before the party was not a candidate but a hierarchy: whether the elected head of government took instruction from an unelected committee, or the committee from her. The session also displayed the syndicate's residual strength in the only currency it still minted. Its members controlled the arrangements, the delegations and the drafting committees, and the Prime Minister, for all her office, could not have carried a contested vote on that floor in April 1969. The year that followed is the story of her acquiring the means to make such a vote unnecessary.

The memoranda strategy belonged to this groundwork phase, and its documentary trace is the under-cited primary source this article leans on: the papers of Mohan Kumaramangalam, preserved at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library, including the strategic memoranda he prepared for the Prime Minister's circle through 1968 and 1969. Kumaramangalam, a barrister who had come to the Congress from the Communist movement and who would die in office as Minister of Steel and Mines in the air crash of 31 May 1973, wrote as an organiser thinks. The memoranda addressed the mechanics of a coming confrontation: which state units could be relied on, how the parliamentary party's loyalties would behave under pressure, where the organisation's formal claims (its constitution, its elected committees, its president's authority) were vulnerable to a mass-mobilisational answer. That such planning predates Zakir Husain's death is the single most corrective fact in the file for the personality reading. A quarrel that erupts over a vacancy is an accident of timing; a confrontation planned through two seasons, with the vacancy merely supplying the occasion, is a structural event wearing a personal mask. The memoranda do not show a Prime Minister determined to split her party, and this article does not claim they do. They show a camp preparing to win a trial of strength it expected the syndicate to force, by building, outside the committee structure, the instruments the committee structure could not answer: a programme with a popular signature, a candidate-selection process drifting to the centre, and a direct line to the legislators who would vote in any succession.

One further piece of groundwork belonged to neither camp's planning and both camps' misfortune: the disappearance of the mediating generation. The Congress of the 1950s had contained figures whose authority crossed the organisation-government divide and who could have brokered the quarrel of 1969 as earlier quarrels had been brokered. Lal Bahadur Shastri, the last leader acceptable to every faction, had died at Tashkent on 11 January 1966. Gulzarilal Nanda, the twice-acting Prime Minister and the party's senior moralist, held no machine. Kamaraj, the one syndicate figure whose personal standing crossed camps, had been diminished by his 1967 defeat and by the Kamaraj Plan's settlement, which had made him a president without a government; by 1969 his mediation was spent, and at Bangalore he voted with his camp. The institution that loses its brokers does not become freer; it becomes brittle, because disputes that mediation once absorbed at low cost must be settled by the expensive instruments of vote, ordinance and expulsion. The Faridabad session, read against that loss, was the sound of a party discovering that the men in the room could no longer settle its largest question among themselves.

## May 1969: A Death in Rashtrapati Bhavan

President Zakir Husain died in office on 3 May 1969, the first President of India to do so, and the constitutional machinery moved with a smoothness that concealed the stakes. Vice President V. V. Giri was sworn in as acting President the same day under the Constitution's arrangements for a vacancy, and the election of a sixth President had to follow within six months. What converted a succession into a crisis was the arithmetic of the moment combined with the character of the office as both camps had come to understand it. The Congress held power at the Centre on a reduced majority and in a minority of the larger states; the electoral college for the presidency, composed of the members of Parliament and of the state assemblies with votes weighted by population, was the one arena in which the party's organisational strength and its parliamentary strength would be counted together, publicly, with a number attached. A defeat for the official candidate would be a measured demonstration that the organisation, or the government, no longer commanded its own house. Each camp therefore treated the choice of candidate as a proxy war for the leadership itself, and each was correct to do so.

The office also carried the substantive powers rehearsed earlier in this article, and the record of May to July 1969 shows Giri using the acting presidency in ways that advertised them. As acting President he was the formal head under whose signature ordinances issued, and the ordinance that nationalised fourteen banks on 19 July 1969, promulgated on the advice of the Council of Ministers he nominally headed, bore his signature a day before he resigned the acting office to enter the contest. The syndicate read the sequence as proof that a pliable President could be the instrument of a Prime Minister's radicalism; the Prime Minister's camp read it as proof that the presidency, held by a man of independent standing, could legitimate a programme the committees had stalled. When Giri resigned on 20 July 1969, Chief Justice Mohammad Hidayatullah stepped in as acting President under the statute governing such vacancies, and the country acquired, for five weeks, a Chief Justice discharging the presidency while the ruling party fought over who should hold it permanently. The institutional oddity passed without public alarm, which is itself evidence for the structural reading: by the high summer of 1969 the forms of the Constitution were being operated, by all sides, as instruments in a factional contest, and the operating was becoming normal.

The mechanics of the presidential election turned the contest into an audited census of the party's loyalties, which is why both camps fought it with a desperation out of proportion to the office's daily duties. Under Article 55 of the Constitution, the President is elected by an electoral college of the elected members of both Houses of Parliament and of the state legislative assemblies, with each legislator's vote carrying a value weighted by the population of the state, and each member of Parliament's vote carrying the value needed to balance the states' total. Election is by proportional representation through the single transferable vote, so a candidate short of the quota on first preferences can still win on the transferred preferences of eliminated candidates, as Giri would. Two consequences followed. First, the count would reveal, state by state and in published figures, exactly how many Congress legislators had obeyed the organisation: there could be no discreet abstention, because the values would be printed. Second, the transferable vote rewarded the candidate with the broader second-preference coalition, which structurally favoured the independent with support across the left over the official candidate of a divided ruling party. The syndicate, which understood the arithmetic as well as anyone, nonetheless proceeded on the Board's instruction, because to retreat from its own candidate after Bangalore would have conceded the hierarchy question that Faridabad had posed. Pride of institution, the besetting virtue of committee men, carried the organisation into the one arena in which its weakness would be measured to the last vote.

## Bangalore: The Board Chooses Reddy

The AICC met at Bangalore from 11 to 13 July 1969 in an atmosphere the press of the week described as a trial of strength deferred. The session's open business belonged to the Prime Minister: her note of stray thoughts on economic policy, circulated to the delegates, proposed among other measures the nationalisation of the major banks and the abolition of the privy purses and privileges of the former princes, and the session adopted the radical programme with an enthusiasm that startled the organisation's managers, who had expected to contain it in committee. The session's closed business belonged to the syndicate. The Congress Parliamentary Board, the small body charged with settling the party's presidential candidate, met at Bangalore during the session, and there the contest of procedure that Faridabad had previewed was played out in miniature. Indira Gandhi proposed Jagjivan Ram, the senior Dalit leader of her cabinet, as a consensus candidate; the Board declined him. She asked that the decision be postponed so that a consensus might still be built; the Board declined the postponement and voted, and by its majority settled on Neelam Sanjeeva Reddy, who was not merely acceptable to the syndicate but of it, a former chief minister of Andhra Pradesh, a former Union minister, a former Congress president of 1960 to 1963 and the sitting Speaker of the Lok Sabha. The Prime Minister signed Reddy's nomination papers as his proposer, as party discipline required, and filed them; she conspicuously issued no whip and made no appeal for his election. In the grammar of Congress politics, the filed nomination was obedience and the withheld whip was war.

### Which body selected the official Congress candidate for President in 1969?

The Congress Parliamentary Board selected the candidate. Meeting at Bangalore during the AICC session of 11 to 13 July 1969, the Board rejected Indira Gandhi's suggestion of Jagjivan Ram, refused her request for a postponement, and by a majority vote settled on Neelam Sanjeeva Reddy, the Speaker of the Lok Sabha and a leading member of the syndicate.

The Board's decision was formally impeccable, and the syndicate's members believed, with reason on the precedents, that formality would govern. Congress candidates for the presidency had always been settled in committee and then ratified by the college without public dissent from the leadership; Rajendra Prasad's second term in 1957 and Zakir Husain's election in 1967, both arranged in the teeth of private reservations, had observed the convention. What the Board's majority undervalued was the change in the party's centre of gravity since 1967: the legislators whose votes it was disposing of were no longer, in their own understanding, the committee's clients. The Lok Sabha debates of the following weeks, August to November 1969, are filled with members reasoning aloud about where their mandate came from, and the reasoning runs, again and again, to the constituency and the leader who had campaigned in it rather than to the district committee that had once owned the ticket. The Bangalore decision thus stands as the decision tree's second entry and its first irreversible one: a formally correct decision, taken by the wrong body, in a party whose real constitution had already changed.

## The Programme Faction: Young Turks, Ginger Groups and the Uses of Ideology

Any account that divides 1969 between five bosses and one Prime Minister leaves out the third force whose presence made the Prime Minister's strategy viable: the ideological ginger groups inside the Congress, and above all the younger parliamentarians the press had christened the Young Turks. The Congress Forum for Socialist Action, organised in the early 1960s as a study and pressure circle of left-leaning Congressmen, supplied the connective tissue: Chandra Shekhar, Mohan Dharia, Krishan Kant, Chandrajit Yadav and their associates, with Mohan Kumaramangalam as the circle's senior strategist before he entered the cabinet, spent the decade arguing inside party forums that the Congress's survival depended on capturing the programme ground the Communist and socialist parties were taking from it in the states. Their weight in 1969 was not numerical; the Forum's adherents were a minority of the parliamentary party. Their weight was positional and rhetorical. They staffed the committees that drafted the economic resolutions, they wrote the speeches and the pamphlets, they gave the Bangalore session its vocabulary of stray thoughts and ten points, and they provided the Prime Minister with the one asset the syndicate entirely lacked: a public explanation of the quarrel in which her side stood for a programme and the other side stood for itself.

The syndicate's members were not slow to understand the threat, and the organisational record of 1968 and 1969 shows them attempting, too late and with the wrong instruments, to discipline the ginger groups as factions, which the party constitution frowned upon, instead of answering them as arguments. The attempt misread the decade. Factional organisation inside the old Congress had been the normal medium of politics, as Kothari's model of the Congress system described: factions bargained, and the bargaining was the system. What distinguished the Forum's generation was that its faction organised around a doctrine with an external audience, addressed over the committees' heads to students, trade unionists, journalists and the new voters of the state-level opposition victories, so that disciplining it in committee merely advertised it outside. When the crisis came, the Forum's members were the Prime Minister's floor managers in the parliamentary party and her candidates for the requisitioned AICC's rostrum, and their reward after the split, ministries and party offices in the requisitionist Congress and the lasting enmity of the organisation's survivors, recorded the transfer of the party's ideological copyright from the men who had inherited it to the men who had rewritten it. The split, in this register, was also a generational succession that the party's ordinary procedures had been unable to conduct since the Kamaraj Plan consumed the last agreed mechanism in 1963.

## The July Counterstroke: A Ministry Lost and Fourteen Banks Taken

The Prime Minister's answer to Bangalore took eight days and two instruments. The first instrument was the cabinet itself. Morarji Desai, Deputy Prime Minister and Minister of Finance, was the syndicate's man inside the government and the standing obstacle to the economic programme the Bangalore session had cheered. In the week after the session Indira Gandhi took the finance portfolio from Desai, offering him continued office without the ministry that made him a rival centre; Desai resigned from the cabinet, as she had calculated he would, and carried into the syndicate's camp the grievance of a man stripped for his economics on the eve of a vote about discipline. The second instrument was the ordinance. On 19 July 1969 the acting President promulgated the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Ordinance, transferring to public ownership the fourteen largest Indian commercial banks, every bank with deposits above fifty crore rupees, among them the Central Bank of India, the Bank of India, the Bank of Baroda, the Punjab National Bank and the Canara Bank. The reception of the measure beyond the party completed its political design, and the record of that reception survives in quarters the syndicate could not dismiss as the Prime Minister's choir. Jayaprakash Narayan, the Sarvodaya leader whose standing with the old Congress generation was unquestioned and who would within five years lead the movement against her Emergency, praised the nationalisation in 1969 in terms that treated it as the republic's economic coming of age; the organised business houses, whose nominees the acquired banks had substantially been, protested through their federations in the language of property and compensation while conspicuously declining to make Reddy's candidacy their public cause; and the press that had mocked the stray thoughts as a session's rhetoric revised its estimate of the Prime Minister within the fortnight. A committee decision can be reversed by a committee; a decision ratified by the applause of the country's most respected dissenter and by the acquiescence of its richest interests has acquired a constituency that no working committee can vote away, which is the arithmetic the syndicate confronted in August.

The political meaning of the measure was stated by its timing. Announced to a country whose ruling party had just chosen a conservative presidential candidate in committee, the nationalisation told the legislators who would vote in August that the Prime Minister, not the Board, set the nation's agenda, and it told them in the idiom the ten-point programme had prepared. The measure's own politics, its drafting, its invalidation by the Supreme Court in the R. C. Cooper judgment of 10 February 1970 and its re-enactment, are treated in the companion study of the 1969 nationalisation elsewhere in this series; here it matters as preparation for a vote.

### How many banks were nationalised in July 1969?

Fourteen banks were nationalised. The ordinance of 19 July 1969, signed by the acting President V. V. Giri, acquired the fourteen largest Indian scheduled commercial banks, each holding deposits above fifty crore rupees, and transferred their undertakings to new public corporations, with compensation provisions that the Supreme Court would examine in the R. C. Cooper case of February 1970.

The announcement at Bangalore of an intention to end the privy purses completed the counterstroke's architecture. The purses, the tax-free payments guaranteed to the former rulers by the covenants of integration in 1947 and 1948, were a small charge on the exchequer and a large symbol of the old order's survival inside the republic; by proposing their abolition the Prime Minister widened her coalition beyond the party, addressing voters for whom the syndicate's India of committees and covenants had no answer. The syndicate's members understood the manoeuvre and chose to endure it, calculating, as the standard accounts record, that humiliation in July would be repaid when Reddy entered Rashtrapati Bhavan in August and the balance of the Constitution itself tilted their way. That calculation required the college to vote as the Board had instructed, and the calculation was wrong for the reason the whole crisis teaches: the college was composed of legislators, and legislators had begun to count differently.

## August 1969: The Conscience Vote

Giri's candidacy assembled the coalition that the syndicate could not match. The two Communist parties, divided since their own split of 1964, found a rare common cause in the trade unionist who had helped found the All India Trade Union Congress in an earlier life; the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, governing Madras under C. N. Annadurai's successor M. Karunanidhi after Annadurai's death in February 1969, backed him; the Samyukta Socialist Party, sections of the Akali Dal and the Muslim League added their votes, and the opposition's official candidate, the former finance minister C. D. Deshmukh, fielded by the Swatantra Party and the Jana Sangh, drew off the anti-Congress right without touching Giri's left. Against this stood the instruction of the Congress Parliamentary Board, and the decisive act of the election was the Prime Minister's refusal to enforce it. In the days before the poll she appealed publicly to Congress legislators to vote according to conscience, naming no candidate and breaking, in plain sight, the discipline that her own signature on Reddy's nomination had seemed to promise. Presidential elections are held by secret ballot, so the appeal's legal force was nil and its political force was total: it licensed every Congress legislator who wished to vote for Giri to understand the licence as coming from the head of his government rather than from his conscience alone.

### Who won the Indian presidential election of 1969?

Varahagiri Venkata Giri won the election. Polling took place on 16 August 1969 and counting closed on 20 August 1969. Giri led on first preferences with a vote value of 401,515 against Sanjeeva Reddy's 313,548, and after the transfer of C. D. Deshmukh's second preferences he reached 420,077, above the quota of 418,169, with Reddy at 405,427.

The figures repay attention because they measure the discipline that failed. The Congress Parliamentary Party entered the election with a strength of 431 members of Parliament, yet Reddy drew only 268 first-preference votes from that body; the remainder scattered to Giri and Deshmukh under cover of the secret ballot and the conscience appeal. Giri carried the electoral college majorities in eleven of the seventeen states at a time when Congress ministries held office in twelve, which is to say that in at least one Congress-governed state, and in truth in several, Congress legislators in large numbers voted against the candidate their own Board had chosen and their own Prime Minister had formally proposed. The election that followed in the courts prolonged the demonstration. A petition challenging Giri's election on grounds of corrupt practice was carried to the Supreme Court, where the sitting President appeared in person as a witness, an event without precedent in the republic's history, and the Court dismissed the petition and upheld the result. The syndicate had lost in the college and then lost in the court, and the double loss destroyed the last asset its authority rested on, the belief of ordinary members that the bosses' way was also the winning way.

The geography of the defection is worth pausing on, because it maps the party's real constitution more accurately than any committee list. Giri's strength among Congress legislators ran deepest where the organisation's grip had already been broken by events: in states where Congress ministries had fallen in 1967 and members had learned to survive without the machine, in the Madras legislature where the DMK government of M. Karunanidhi delivered a friendly assembly's non-Congress votes and Congress members read the signal, and in the parliamentary party at Delhi, where the Prime Minister's daily patronage, questions of tickets, ministries and access, outweighed a president's episodic authority. Reddy held the blocs where personal machines remained intact and personally commanded, in his own Andhra Pradesh and in pockets of the organisation's heartland, and even there the published values showed leakage that the secret ballot mercifully left unnameable. The standard accounts of the election, Malhotra's biography among them, record individual legislators describing the same calculation in the same words across unconnected states: the member's future ran through the Prime Minister, the Board's instruction ran through men who might not be able to give him a ticket again. A whip that must be weighed against a career is no longer a whip; it is advice, and the college treated it as advice in eleven states out of seventeen.

## September to November: Expulsion and the Formal Split

Between August and November the two camps manoeuvred for the party's carcass with the procedural weapons each still held. Nijalingappa, as Congress president, demanded explanations and issued charges of indiscipline against the Prime Minister and her supporters; the Prime Minister's camp requisitioned a special meeting of the AICC under the party constitution's provisions, gathering the signatures that would let the larger body overrule the Working Committee; state units split into parallel committees issuing rival membership lists. The Lok Sabha's debates of those months, preserved in the official record, show the government legislating through the crisis: the bank nationalisation bill's successors, the privy purse question and the ordinary business of the house carried on by a ministry whose party was dissolving around it, sustained by the votes of the Communist Party of India and the DMK, whose price was the programme the syndicate had tried to bury. On 12 November 1969 the Congress Working Committee, meeting in Delhi with the syndicate's majority in control, adopted the resolution that the AICC Papers preserve: Indira Gandhi was expelled from the primary membership of the Congress for indiscipline, and the Congress Parliamentary Party was directed to elect a new leader.

### Did Indira Gandhi resign from the Congress in 1969?

Indira Gandhi did not resign. She was expelled. The Congress Working Committee, controlled by the syndicate's majority, resolved on 12 November 1969 to remove her from the primary membership of the party for indiscipline and directed the parliamentary party to choose a new leader, a direction the majority of Congress members of Parliament refused to obey.

The expulsion also inverted the one comparable precedent the party possessed, and the inversion is instructive. In September 1951 the quarrel between Prime Minister Nehru and Congress president Purushottam Das Tandon over the party's direction ended with the president's resignation: the head of government, commanding the parliamentary party and the country's attention, forced the organisation's chief to yield, and the party closed ranks around the settlement. In November 1969 the organisation's chief attempted the same manoeuvre in reverse, signing away the head of government's membership, and discovered that the relative weights of the two offices had exchanged places in the intervening eighteen years: the presidency of the Congress had become an office in gift of forces it no longer controlled, while the premiership had accumulated the patronage, the programme and the public. The Working Committee's resolution was legally the same instrument in both decades; in 1951 its substance would have been obeyed before it was drafted, and in 1969 it was waste paper within ten days, because the authority to make such resolutions obeyed had migrated, with the legislators' allegiance, from the committee room to the leader's office and the crowd outside it.

The refusal is the split's formal moment, and its numbers measure the transfer of sovereignty inside the party. Of the 705 members of the AICC, a majority, some 446 by the count her camp published, supported the requisition that convened in Delhi on 22 November 1969, declared the expulsion void and reconstituted the party's authorities around the Prime Minister; of the Congress members of Parliament, 310 of 429 stood with her, and the organisation's parliamentary remnant chose Morarji Desai as its leader in the house. Her government fell into a minority in the Lok Sabha and survived on outside support, a condition that lasted until the dissolution of December 1970 and the election of March 1971 settled the argument in the only court the syndicate could not pack. The Bombay session of her Congress in December 1969 elected Jagjivan Ram its president, completing a full parallel constitution: president, working committee, parliamentary party, state units. The syndicate's Congress, Congress (O), retained the presidency of Nijalingappa, the claim of constitutional continuity and, pending the Election Commission's ruling, an equal claim on the party's name and on its election symbol, the pair of bullocks carrying a yoke, under which Congress candidates had fought every election since 1952.

### Who led Congress (O) after the split?

Congress (O) was led by S. Nijalingappa, the incumbent Congress president whose Working Committee had expelled the Prime Minister, with K. Kamaraj as its most powerful figure. The organisation faction kept the party's machinery, its president and its claim to the bullock-pair symbol, while the parliamentary remnant in the Lok Sabha chose Morarji Desai as its leader.

## Two Congresses in the States

Between November 1969 and the dissolution of December 1970, India contained two Congress parties in a sense more literal than metaphor. In state after state the Pradesh committee duplicated itself: a president recognised by Nijalingappa's AICC and a president recognised by the requisitionists, rival membership registers, rival offices in the same city, and litigation or the threat of it over party funds and premises. The duplication was not administrative untidiness; it was the split's true battlefield, because the faction that held a state's legislators held its government, and the faction that held its committee held the machinery for the next election. Chief ministers became the decisive counters. A chief minister who declared for the Prime Minister carried his ministry, the majority of his assembly party and usually the Pradesh committee's working majority with him, since the same legislators sustained all three; a chief minister who declared for the organisation, or who hesitated, invited his assembly party to count itself, and the counting usually ended his hesitation or his government. The pattern resolved state by state through the winter of 1969 and 1970, and it resolved overwhelmingly in one direction, because the legislators doing the counting faced the same arithmetic their colleagues had faced in August: re-election would be sought under a leader's name, and the name that filled halls was not the organisation's.

The Election Commission's enquiry supplied the official audit of that resolution. Both factions petitioned the Commission over the party's identity and its reserved symbol, and the Commission's method, set out in its order under paragraph 15 of the Symbols Order and defended later before the Supreme Court in the Sadiq Ali record, was to count. It counted the members of Parliament and of the state legislatures who adhered to each group; it counted the delegates and committee members of the organisational wing on the evidence each side filed; it weighed the aims-and-objects test and the party-constitution test and set both aside as inconclusive beside the numbers. The numbers, as the Commission recorded them, gave the Prime Minister's group the majority in the legislative wing overall and, notwithstanding the organisation faction's strength in particular states, the majority in the organisational wing as well. The legal consequence was that the bullock-pair symbol was frozen between the claimants and her group was recognised as the Congress for the purposes of the Order, receiving the cow-and-calf as its symbol for the contest of 1971. The political consequence ran deeper. The republic's election authority had held, and the Supreme Court would confirm, that a political party is, for the law's decisive purposes, the people who compose its following at the count, and not the custodians of its letterhead. The syndicate had spent the autumn arguing from custody; the Commission counted the following, and custody lost.

The government at the Centre lived through these months in the condition the split had made for it: a minority administration sustained from outside by the Communist Party of India and the DMK, legislating its programme with votes borrowed from parties the Congress had historically opposed. The condition is usually treated as an embarrassment, and organisationally it was worse than an embarrassment, because it inverted the founding logic of the Congress system, in which the party in government was supposed to contain the spectrum within itself. A Congress that needed Communist votes to pass its budget in 1970 had already become something the founders would not have recognised: a leader's following in alliance with external parties, rather than a national coalition under one committee's roof. That inversion, and not any resolution or expulsion, is the deepest sense in which the party of 1947 ended in 1969, and it is why the voters of 1971, presented with two claimants, so readily identified the Congress with the faction that had already been governing as something else.

## The Minority Winter: Governing Without a Majority, 1970

The split's consequences were tested in Parliament before they were tested at the polls, and the test of September 1970 belongs in any anatomy of the decision tree, because it shows the new system working exactly as the precedents of 1969 said it would. The abolition of the privy purses, promised at Bangalore and obstructed since, required a constitutional amendment, and the Constitution (Twenty-Fourth Amendment) Bill for the purpose passed the Lok Sabha, where the government's borrowed majority held, and fell in the Rajya Sabha on 5 September 1970, short of the special majority the Constitution requires, by the narrowest of margins in the count of the house. In an earlier Congress dispensation, a defeat of that kind in the upper house would have closed the question for the session and set the committees to work on a compromise. The post-1969 system answered within a day and through a constitutional office: on 6 September 1970 President V. V. Giri, on the advice of the Council of Ministers, issued orders withdrawing recognition from the former rulers, accomplishing by executive act under the Constitution's provisions on rulership what the amendment had failed to accomplish by legislation. The Supreme Court struck the orders down in December 1970, in the Madhavrao Scindia case, holding the mass derecognition beyond the President's power, and the government accepted the judgement and carried the question to the electorate instead. Each step in the sequence is a precedent of 1969 at work: a committee-less executive improvising around an institutional refusal, a constitutional office used as the instrument of the improvisation, a court restoring the boundary, and a leader concluding that the only tribunal certain to vindicate her was the voter.

The dissolution that followed completes the year's logic. On 27 December 1970, thirteen months after her expulsion from the undivided Congress, Indira Gandhi advised President Giri to dissolve the fourth Lok Sabha a year before its term expired, and the President, the man whose election had detonated the split, signed the dissolution of the Parliament in which his opponents had once commanded the ruling party. The mid-term election was itself a novelty the old Congress would not have risked: no previous Prime Minister had voluntarily shortened a Parliament's life to convert a minority into a majority, because no previous Prime Minister had needed to, the organisation having managed its majorities as a going concern. The advice of December 1970 announced that the management of majorities had passed from the organisation to the electorate's periodic verdict on a leader, and the campaign of February and March 1971, conducted as a referendum under the slogan Garibi Hatao, ratified the transfer. When the syndicate's survivors in Congress (O) joined the Grand Alliance whose platform reduced itself to the counter-slogan Indira Hatao, the contrast stated the decade's whole argument in two phrases: one side offered a person and a programme fused, the other offered a person to be removed, and the voters chose as the precedents of 1969 had taught everyone to expect they would.

## The Decision Tree of 1969, Month by Month

The preceding narrative can be compressed into the article's findable artifact. Readers citing this analysis refer to it as the 1969 Congress Split Decision Tree: a six-node sequence in which each event of the year established a named structural precedent, and in which the long-arc consequences traced later in this article attach to the nodes rather than to the personalities who passed through them.

| Month (1969) | Event | Principal actors | Structural precedent established |
|---|---|---|---|
| April | Faridabad AICC session: presidential address delivered without the Prime Minister's clearance | S. Nijalingappa, Indira Gandhi | The organisation's claim to audit the elected government, stated publicly for the first time |
| May | President Zakir Husain dies in office on 3 May; V. V. Giri becomes acting President | V. V. Giri, the Council of Ministers | A constitutional office becomes the terrain of a factional succession contest |

Read down the final column, the table makes the article's argument without commentary. No single row is a rupture; the rupture is the sequence, and the sequence is irreversible because each precedent lowered the cost of the next. Faridabad made Bangalore thinkable, Bangalore made the July counterstroke necessary, July made the conscience appeal winnable, August made expulsion the syndicate's only remaining move, and expulsion made the parallel constitution of December a formality. A decision tree of this kind also disciplines the counterfactuals that the personality reading invites. Had Reddy won in August, the syndicate would have held the presidency and the organisation, and the Prime Minister would have governed on sufferance; the structural question, who owned the Congress, would have been answered the other way for a season. It would not have been abolished, because the conditions that produced it, a hollowed organisation, a leader-dependent parliamentary party and a programme in search of an instrument, were the property of the decade and not of the summer.

## Three Precedents the Split Wrote into the System

The first precedent concerns discipline. Congress legislative behaviour since 1947 had rested on the whip: a member voted as directed, and direction flowed from bodies in which the organisation's managers had the decisive voice. The conscience appeal of August 1969 inverted the flow in full public view. A Prime Minister invited her party's legislators to disobey their party's instruction, they obeyed the invitation, and no sanction followed that any member needed to fear, because the sanctioning body expelled the inviter and was itself repudiated within ten days. The lesson generalised beyond the Congress within the decade. Legislators across parties learned that a whip is binding only when the leadership that issues it can also distribute the tickets for the next election, a lesson whose later career runs through the defections of the 1970s and 1980s and through the eventual, partial answer of the anti-defection provisions inserted into the Constitution by the Fifty-Second Amendment of 1985, which tried to restore by law a discipline that 1969 had shown could no longer be restored by convention. The raw material for the lesson had been accumulating even before the split, in the state assemblies elected in 1967, where floor-crossing became a governing technique: the Haryana legislator Gaya Lal, who changed parties three times within a fortnight in 1967 and was presented to the press by his final patron with the words that gave Indian politics the phrase Aaya Ram Gaya Ram, Ram has come, Ram has gone, became the era's emblem precisely because his crossings were unashamed and unpunished. What 1969 added to that state-level farce was altitude and respectability. When the head of the Union government herself breaks a whip and is rewarded with a party, a majority and a decade of power, the state legislator weighing a crossing no longer weighs it against a norm; he weighs it against a market price, and the norm's defenders are left arguing from a convention whose most famous breach sits at the head of the government that would have to punish him.

The second precedent concerns the source of authority inside a party. The syndicate's claim in 1969 was organisational: it spoke for the committees, the membership and the constitution of the Congress, and on the paper record of both wings the claim was strong. Indira Gandhi's counter-claim was mobilisational: she spoke, she said, for the programme the Bangalore session had adopted and for the voters who would judge it. She framed the contest as one between the people and a ring of vested interests, a framing her opponents denounced as populist and which nonetheless identified the new reality, that a leader with a direct line to the electorate could route around every intermediate body the party possessed. The Election Commission's eventual ruling on the party's identity ratified the routing: applying the test of majority support in the party's legislative and organisational wings under paragraph 15 of the Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order of 1968, the Commission treated the question of which faction was the Congress as a question of numbers, and the Supreme Court, dismissing the appeals in Sadiq Ali v. Election Commission of India on 11 November 1971, upheld that test. A party's identity, in the doctrine the ruling established, belongs to the faction that can count more heads, and heads are counted most convincingly at elections. The organisational constitution the syndicate prized had become, in the decisive forum, a pleading. The same transfer registered in the party's daily practice through the 1970s in a change of vocabulary that participants themselves noticed: Pradesh Congress presidents and district presidents came increasingly to be described, without embarrassment, as nominated, where the constitution contemplated election, and organisational elections across most of the party lapsed for long stretches because a nominated hierarchy has no use for the electorate that might disturb it. Each lapse was defended as temporary, and the defence was sincere; a party preparing for the next general election always has a reason to postpone its internal ones. The cumulative effect, visible by the middle of the decade, was a party whose local leadership held office by the centre's pleasure and whose members encountered their organisation chiefly as an audience for visiting leaders. When the Emergency ended and the party faced the electorate of March 1977, it discovered that an audience cannot canvass, and the discovery, state by state across the north, was the second precedent presenting its bill.

The third precedent concerns the Constitution's own offices. The presidency in 1969 was fought over as an instrument, occupied briefly by a Chief Justice doing double duty, and used, through the ordinance of 19 July, to execute the most contentious economic act of the decade. The split normalised the treatment of high constitutional office as a factional asset inside the ruling party, and the normalisation outlived the quarrel: governorships in the states, the Speaker's chair in legislatures and the presidency itself at later successions were thereafter contested with an openness about their political function that the founding generation's conventions would have forbidden. This precedent is the least discussed of the three and the one with the longest institutional shadow, because it reaches beyond party management into the design of the republic, teaching every future majority that the referees of the system are also players to be selected. The precedent's neatest illustration arrived exactly a decade later, wearing the other camp's colours. In July 1979 the President who dissolved the Janata experiment by inviting Charan Singh to form a government, and who then accepted that government's resignation and dissolved the Lok Sabha in August 1979 amid contested advice, was Neelam Sanjeeva Reddy himself, elected to Rashtrapati Bhavan unopposed in 1977 as the Janata coalition's candidate. The man whose imposed candidacy had split the Congress in 1969 ended the following decade exercising, as President, the very discretionary weight in a succession crisis that both camps in 1969 had fought to control, and exercising it in circumstances, a fragmented majority and rival claims to confidence, that the post-1969 party system produced as a matter of course. No participant's intention connects the two episodes; the connection is institutional, which is the article's whole contention.

## The Long Arc: 1971, 1975, 1991

The 1971 election was the split's first test, and its result is the strongest exhibit of the counter-argument this article must answer. Indira Gandhi advised the dissolution of the Lok Sabha on 27 December 1970, a year early, and fought the election of March 1971 as a plebiscite on herself: her Congress (R), campaigning under the new symbol of a cow with a suckling calf that the Commission had allotted it and under the slogan Garibi Hatao, remove poverty, won 352 of the 518 seats in the Lok Sabha with nearly 44 percent of the vote. The Grand Alliance ranged against her, Congress (O) with the Jana Sangh, the Swatantra Party, the Samyukta Socialist Party and others under the counter-slogan Indira Hatao, won almost nothing between them; Congress (O) fell from 65 seats to 16, and the syndicate's surviving leaders lost their own constituencies or their relevance. The full treatment of that election and of the Bangladesh war that followed it appears later in this series; here the result matters for what it proved about 1969. The voters, asked which faction was the Congress, answered with the organisation's ruins, and the Election Commission's recognition of her group as the Indian National Congress, entitled to the name without suffix, followed the verdict as a formality.

### How many seats did Congress (R) win in the 1971 general election?

Congress (R) won 352 of the 518 seats in the Lok Sabha in the election of March 1971, with nearly 44 percent of the popular vote. Its rival Congress (O), the organisation faction of the 1969 split, fell from 65 seats to 16, and the combined Grand Alliance of opposition parties failed to stop Indira Gandhi's majority.

The counter-argument can be stated at full strength: a split whose organisational consequences were supposedly structural was followed within sixteen months by the most emphatic electoral victory in the party's history, so the consequences were plainly absorbable. The argument confuses two assets that 1969 had separated. Electoral success the party retained, in abundance; what it lost was organisational vitality, the capacity to renew itself, correct its leader and survive her defeats, which are the functions an organisation exists to perform. The victory of 1971 was manufactured by the personal-authority machine the split had built: candidates selected at the centre for loyalty and winnability, a campaign run as a referendum on one name, a parliamentary party that owed its seats to that name and knew it. When the machine's owner was discredited, by the Allahabad judgment of 12 June 1975 that set aside her election, there existed no party structure capable of processing the crisis constitutionally, because processing crises constitutionally is what the structure discarded in 1969 had been for. The Emergency proclaimed on 25 June 1975, examined in its own right later in this series, is therefore correctly read as the split's consummation rather than as a separate catastrophe: the precedent that discipline and committee government could be subordinated to personal authority, established in a cause most of the country applauded, was simply applied in a cause it did not. The mechanics of June 1975 show the precedent operating at the level of procedure, which is where precedents live. On the night of 25 June 1975 the proclamation of Emergency was signed by President Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed, a loyal nominee elevated the previous year, on the advice of the Prime Minister alone; the Union Cabinet, the collective body in whose name the advice constitutionally ran, was informed and ratified the decision the following morning. Every element of that sequence had been rehearsed in 1969: a President treated as the executive's instrument rather than its check, a committee of the governing side presented with a decision already taken, and a party whose legislators and committees possessed neither the machinery nor the standing to reopen the question. The syndicate's men of 1969, watching from the opposition benches or from retirement, could identify the procedure without difficulty, because it was the procedure whose establishment they had contested and lost. That the Emergency's excesses belonged to a darker register than anything contemplated in 1969 does not weaken the lineage; it is the lineage, since the argument of this article has never been that the split intended the Emergency, only that it dismantled, one November resolution at a time, the machinery by which the republic's ruling party might have refused it.

The line runs onward through the return of 1980 on the same personal mandate, through the party's long vacancy of internal elections, to June 1991, when P. V. Narasimha Rao inherited and had to govern through a Congress whose organisational skeleton 1969 had designed: strong at transmitting a leader's authority downward, weak at generating authority upward. The centralisation of the Prime Minister's Office that observers describe in the decades after, including the present Modi era's concentration of initiative in the premiership, operates on this inherited design, whoever occupies the office and whichever party holds the majority.

The fates of the two factions after 1971 close the arc's first loop. Congress (O), denied the name, the symbol and the electorate's recognition, subsisted as a party of eminent persons without a following's machinery, and in 1977 it dissolved itself into the Janata Party, the syndicate's survivors entering the government that defeated Indira Gandhi as partners in somebody else's coalition. Its end carries the structural moral in miniature: an organisation that had defined itself by custody of the party's forms could not survive the discovery that the forms were portable. Her faction's fate was the mirror image. The requisitionist Congress, rebuilt as [the four-phase trajectory of Indira's office-rebuilding](/2019/03/21/indira-gandhi-personality-institution/) describes across the pillar's hub study, split again in January 1978, after the Emergency's defeat, into a Congress (I) that bore her initial and a remnant that claimed the older succession; the Election Commission's counting jurisprudence of 1969 and 1971 supplied the grammar of that dispute too, and the voters settled it in 1980 as they had settled its predecessor in 1971, by following the person. A party that splits twice in nine years along the line of one leader's authority, and is rewarded by the electorate both times, has been taught its constitution by experience, and the lesson it learned in 1969 it did not need to be taught again.

Rao's premiership after June 1991 is the arc's quiet coda, and it confirms the reading from an unexpected direction. Narasimha Rao was the first Congress Prime Minister of the post-1969 order who possessed no personal machine of the kind the order was designed to serve: a scholar-administrator elevated by a party bereaved of its owning family, governing a minority administration for a full term by negotiation, committee management and the patient expenditure of a lifetime's organisational credit. That such a premiership was possible at all shows the design's neutrality; that it was experienced by contemporaries as an anomaly, and that the party spent the decade after his fall searching for the dynastic centre the design presupposed, shows the design's depth. The structure built in 1969 transmits the authority of a leader who has one and exposes the absence in a leader who does not, and a constitution, written or unwritten, that behaves so consistently across such different occupants has earned the name this article gives the event that wrote it.

## The Evidence in the Files: Kumaramangalam, the AICC Papers and the Debates

The documentary base for the structural reading is unusually good, and its best parts are the least used. The 12 November 1969 expulsion resolution survives in the AICC Papers at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library, and its text repays the journey: a Working Committee expelling the head of its own government recites indiscipline in the language of a school register, cataloguing the refusal to campaign for the official candidate and the appeal to conscience as offences against the party's constitution, and the recital is the strongest single proof that the syndicate understood the crisis exactly as this article does, as a contest over whether committee government would survive. The Kumaramangalam papers in the same library supply the other side's planning record, the memoranda of 1968 and 1969 in which the Prime Minister's circle mapped the organisation it expected to fight. The Lok Sabha debates of August to November 1969 supply the public transcript, members reasoning on the record about mandate, whip and conscience as the institution changed around them. The press record supplies the contemporary verdict, and it is more divided than later memory suggests, which is precisely its value as evidence of a genuinely open contest. The Hindu's editorial of 18 November 1969, published in the week of the expulsion under the heading of Congress against Congress, directed its fire at the Prime Minister, judging her reactions since the Bangalore decision hard to understand and asking whether the Parliamentary Board was expected to serve as a rubber stamp for the head of government; the editorial page of the country's most establishment newspaper thus stated the organisation's case at full strength six days after the expulsion, and no honest adjudication can proceed without weighing it. Set against it the reporting and comment of the same weeks in the journals of the left and in the Delhi press that had championed the July programme, where the expulsion figured as the bosses' revenge on a leader for defeating their candidate and obstructing their economics. A reader who works through both files watches the country's political class dividing, in real time, along the line this article draws between procedure and mobilisation, and dividing with arguments on each side that the participants themselves would have recognised as fairly stated. Students assembling these files face a practical obstacle the sources themselves create: the memoranda and resolutions circulate as scanned office documents in mixed formats, and readers assembling a working file from them can open and read the collection, spreadsheets of vote values included, through a free [online office file viewer for Excel, DOCX and PPTX files](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html). Against this base the secondary literature arranges itself by the named disagreement the article set out to judge. Malhotra's biography of 1989, built on interviews with participants on both sides, remains unmatched on motive and sequence. Kothari's Politics in India of 1970, written before the split's consequences had unfolded, supplies the framework that predicted them, and his later essays on the post-1969 party system watched the framework confirmed. Wolpert's India, in the fourth edition of 2009, carries the narrative into the judgement of a general historian. Where the three disagree, they disagree along the line this article has drawn: the closer a treatment stands to the participants, the more personal the split appears; the more it attends to institutions, the more structural.

## The Organisation's Case, Stated at Its Strongest

The charter of this article requires the losing side to be heard at its strongest before judgement, and the syndicate's case deserves that hearing, because it was not the case of cynics defending spoils. It rested, first, on a doctrine of collective government that the founders themselves had practised. The Congress that fought the freedom struggle and then governed was a collective leadership: Nehru, for all his eminence, submitted to the Working Committee's discipline, resigned the party presidency question to negotiation in 1951 when President Tandon's election embarrassed him, and treated the Parliamentary Board's decisions on candidates, including presidential candidates, as binding settlements rather than opening bids. On that doctrine, the events of 1969 reversed in one season the party's constitutional morality. A Prime Minister signed her party's nomination of a presidential candidate, as proposer, and then invited the electors to defeat him; no doctrine of conscience, the syndicate argued, distinguishes that invitation from a breach of faith, and the secrecy of the ballot, designed to protect electors from power, was used to protect a conspiracy of power against the party's own lawful decision. Malhotra's interviews preserve the bitterness with which the older men stated the point, and The Hindu's editorial of 18 November 1969 stated it editorially within the week: if every Board decision binds only until the Prime Minister dislikes it, the Board is a rubber stamp and the party is a court.

The case rested, second, on a reading of the presidency that the Prime Minister's camp never answered on its merits. A President elected as one faction's insurance, the syndicate held, corrupts the office in the act of filling it; the republic needs in Rashtrapati Bhavan a figure whom a future crisis can trust to act without a patron's interest, and Reddy, whatever his loyalties, was a parliamentarian of three decades whose record as Speaker had been marked by rulings against his own party's government. Against that stood Giri's candidacy, arranged in the open as the instrument of the Prime Minister's camp and celebrated by its supporters in exactly those terms. The syndicate's fear, that a presidency so filled would sign what it was sent, acquired its evidence within the very week of the vacancy's climax, when the acting President signed the bank ordinance on his last day in the acting office before resigning to accept the camp's nomination. Men who believed the presidency should stand above the battle could regard that sequence only as confirmation, and their expulsion resolution's language of indiscipline, however schoolmasterly, expressed a constitutional anxiety that later decades, with their contested Governors and Speakers, have done nothing to refute.

The case rested, third, on a warning about the kind of party the Prime Minister's method would build, and here the syndicate's spokesmen, Kamaraj above all, spoke from an organiser's knowledge rather than from nostalgia. A party held together by a leader's popularity and a secretariat's ticket book, Kamaraj's position implied, can win elections and cannot govern a federation: it has no state leaderships with standing to argue with the centre, no internal forum in which a failing policy can be corrected before it becomes a national crisis, and no succession that does not begin as a vacancy crisis. The warning was self-serving, since the men uttering it were the state leaderships whose standing was at stake, and it was also, on the evidence of the two decades that followed, substantially correct in each particular. This article's verdict for the structural reading is therefore not a verdict that the syndicate was wrong about the future; it is a verdict about where the future was decided. The organisation's case failed not because it was weak but because the body that had to be persuaded by it, the parliamentary party and behind it the electorate, had withdrawn the allegiance on which its strength depended, and a case, however strong, cannot be enforced by a court that has adjourned. The syndicate defended the Congress's constitution after the Congress had ceased to be governed by it, and the tragedy of its position, which the personality reading's contempt for the bosses obscures, is that its members understood the destination earlier than their opponents and lacked only the means to turn the vehicle.

## Adjudication: The Trigger Was Personal, the Pivot Was Structural

The pillar to which this article belongs names the scholarly disagreement in its largest form, between the personality reading associated with Bipan Chandra and his collaborators and the institutional reading associated with Granville Austin, and the two readings meet in 1969 as cleanly as anywhere in the series. Chandra's school, in India After Independence (1999), treats Indira Gandhi's leadership, her political instincts and her willingness to subordinate institutions to survival as the engine of the era's transformations; on that reading the split is the moment the engine overpowered the chassis. Austin's method, displayed in Working a Democratic Constitution (1999), follows the institutions and their logics: a party constitution unreformed since the movement years, a parliamentary party whose incentives the spread of competitive state politics had rewritten, an electoral law that would shortly define party identity by counted heads. On that reading 1969 is the year the logics surfaced and the personalities merely announced them. The adjudication offered here finds the Austin method more powerful for this episode for one specific reason: it predicts. An institutional reading of the Congress in 1968, built from the 1967 results, the lapsed organisational elections and the Parliamentary Board's narrowing base, forecasts a rupture over the first succession the bosses tried to impose; a personality reading forecasts only that strong persons will clash, which is true of every year and therefore discriminates among none. Where Chandra's school is indispensable is on the uses to which the rupture was put, because the machine the split built was personal in design and cannot be described in any other vocabulary. The two readings are thus assigned their provinces: institutions decided that the party would break, personality decided what would be built from the pieces.

The verdict follows from the evidence rather than from temperament. On the proximate trigger, the personality reading is correct and this article concedes it without reserve: but for the particular ambitions and resentments of 1969, the succession to Zakir Husain would have been arranged in committee like its predecessors. On everything that made the trigger consequential, the personality reading fails, and it fails on the primary record. It cannot explain why containment, the party's reflex in 1951 and 1963, was unavailable; the hollowness documented in the 1967 results and the decayed condition of the committees explain it. It cannot explain why the losers of August could find no remedy short of expelling their own Prime Minister; the transfer of the legislators' loyalty from committee to leader, measured in Reddy's 268 votes from a parliamentary party of 431, explains it. It cannot explain why the precedents outlived every participant, operating in 1975, in the defection politics that provoked the Fifty-Second Amendment, in the Election Commission's numbers jurisprudence and in the leader-centred Congress that P. V. Narasimha Rao inherited in 1991; only a change in the system's operating rules explains consequences that do not retire when persons do. The strongest counter, that the party absorbed the blow and won in 1971, has been answered on the distinction between electoral success and organisational vitality, and the answer is Kothari's framework vindicated by events: a party of consensus had become a party of command, and command parties win elections until the commander falters, whereupon they discover that nothing underneath remains. The 1969 Congress split was therefore the structural pivot of post-Independence Indian politics, more consequential than the Emergency because the Emergency was one of its consequences, and the claim stands in the form the article undertook to defend: the split established the precedent that party discipline could be sacrificed for personal authority, and everything from 1975 to 1991 follows from this decision tree.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the 1969 Congress split?

The 1969 Congress split was the division of the Indian National Congress into two rival parties during the second half of 1969. The immediate cause was the presidential election of August 1969, in which Prime Minister Indira Gandhi backed the independent candidate V. V. Giri against her own party's official nominee, Sanjeeva Reddy. Giri won, and on 12 November 1969 the Congress Working Committee, controlled by the syndicate of senior leaders, expelled Gandhi from the party. Her supporters repudiated the expulsion at a requisitioned meeting of the All India Congress Committee, and by December 1969 two complete parties existed: Congress (O), the organisation faction, and Congress (R), her requisitionist faction. The election of 1971 settled which of them the voters recognised as the Congress.

### Q: Who were the syndicate?

The syndicate was the informal name, coined by the press, for the group of senior Congress bosses who controlled the party organisation in the 1960s and managed the successions of 1964 and 1966. Its core members were K. Kamaraj of Tamil Nadu, S. Nijalingappa of Mysore, who served as Congress president from 1968, Atulya Ghosh of West Bengal, S. K. Patil of Bombay and Neelam Sanjeeva Reddy of Andhra Pradesh. Morarji Desai, the Deputy Prime Minister and Finance Minister until July 1969, cooperated with the group tactically without being of it. The syndicate's power rested on control of state units, membership lists and candidate selection, and its members had chosen both Lal Bahadur Shastri and Indira Gandhi as prime ministers, expecting each to govern with the organisation's consent.

### Q: Why did Indira Gandhi split the Congress?

Indira Gandhi did not set out with a settled plan to split the Congress, and she was formally expelled rather than resigning. She fought the syndicate because its control of the party organisation threatened her control of her own government: the syndicate's majority on the Congress Parliamentary Board imposed Sanjeeva Reddy as the presidential candidate in July 1969 against her stated preference, and a Reddy presidency would have given her opponents a constitutional lever against her. She answered with the bank nationalisation of 19 July 1969 and the conscience appeal that defeated Reddy in August. When the Working Committee expelled her on 12 November 1969, she chose to build a parallel party around her parliamentary majority rather than submit, judging that the electorate, and not the committees, would decide which faction was the Congress.

### Q: What is the difference between Congress(O) and Congress(R)?

Congress (O) and Congress (R) were the two parties created by the 1969 split. Congress (O), the Organisation faction, kept the incumbent party president S. Nijalingappa, the claim of constitutional continuity within the old party, the support of the syndicate's state machines and, initially, the party's bullock-pair election symbol. Congress (R), the Requisitionists, took their name from the requisitioned AICC meeting of 22 November 1969 that voided Gandhi's expulsion; it held the Prime Minister, the majority of Congress members of Parliament and most of the party's legislators, and it fought the 1971 election under a new cow-and-calf symbol. The Election Commission recognised Congress (R) as the Indian National Congress after its victory in 1971, and Congress (O) dwindled, merging into the Janata Party in 1977.

### Q: Who was V.V. Giri?

Varahagiri Venkata Giri (1894 to 1980) was a veteran trade union leader, a founder figure of the All India Trade Union Congress, a former Labour Minister in Jawaharlal Nehru's cabinet and India's High Commissioner to Ceylon before his election as Vice President in May 1967. On the death of President Zakir Husain on 3 May 1969 he became acting President, and on 19 July 1969, still in that office, he signed the ordinance nationalising fourteen banks. He resigned the next day to contest the presidential election as an independent, backed by Indira Gandhi, the Communist parties, the DMK and the Samyukta Socialist Party. He defeated the official Congress candidate Sanjeeva Reddy, was sworn in as the fourth President on 24 August 1969, and served until 1974.

### Q: How did the 1969 split lead to the Emergency?

The split led to the Emergency by changing what kind of party the Congress was. After 1969 the party's legislators owed their tickets to Indira Gandhi personally, its committees were nominated rather than elected from below, and no internal body retained the standing to refuse her or to manage a crisis constitutionally. When the Allahabad High Court set aside her election on 12 June 1975, a party of the older Congress type might have replaced its leader and governed on; the post-1969 Congress had no mechanism and no personnel for such a transfer, because the split had destroyed the organisation that once supplied alternative leadership. The decision of 25 June 1975 to proclaim an Emergency rather than accept the judgment applied, in a darker cause, the precedent of 1969: that institutional discipline could be subordinated to the leader's survival.

### Q: Who was Mohan Kumaramangalam?

Mohan Kumaramangalam (1916 to 1973) was a Madras barrister, educated at Eton and Cambridge, who passed from the Communist movement into the Congress and became one of Indira Gandhi's closest strategic advisers. During 1968 and 1969 he prepared memoranda for her circle, preserved in his papers at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library, mapping the Congress organisation's loyalties and the tactics of a confrontation with the syndicate; the memoranda are the documentary evidence that the showdown of 1969 was prepared on both sides rather than improvised over the presidential vacancy. After the split he rose with her faction, entered the cabinet as Minister of Steel and Mines in 1971, and died in the Indian Airlines crash near Delhi on 31 May 1973.

### Q: What happened at the Faridabad AICC session in April 1969?

The Faridabad session of the All India Congress Committee in April 1969 was the first open display of the two camps that split the party later that year. Congress president S. Nijalingappa delivered a presidential address critical of the government's economic direction, breaking the custom of clearing the text with the Prime Minister beforehand, and Indira Gandhi replied from the same platform by insisting that the party's social programme could not wait. Nothing was voted and no candidate was at issue, yet delegates left understanding that the real question before the Congress was one of hierarchy: whether the elected head of government took direction from the party's committees or the committees from her. The session also showed the syndicate still commanding the floor through its control of delegations and arrangements.

### Q: What were Indira Gandhi's stray thoughts at the Bangalore session of July 1969?

The stray thoughts were a short written note on economic policy that Indira Gandhi circulated to delegates at the Bangalore AICC session of 11 to 13 July 1969, and that the session adopted with enthusiasm. It proposed, among other measures, the nationalisation of the major banks, tighter control of monopolies and the abolition of the privy purses and privileges of the former princely rulers, extending the ten-point programme the party had accepted in 1967. The note's purpose was political as much as economic. By committing the assembled party to a radical programme in public, it raised the cost to the Parliamentary Board, meeting in closed session during the same days, of imposing a conservative presidential candidate in the name of discipline.

### Q: What was Indira Gandhi's conscience vote appeal in the presidential election of 1969?

The conscience vote appeal was Indira Gandhi's public invitation, issued in the days before the presidential poll of 16 August 1969, to Congress legislators to vote according to their conscience rather than follow the party's direction to support its official candidate, Sanjeeva Reddy. She named no candidate, and because presidential elections are held by secret ballot the appeal carried no legal force; its political meaning was unmistakable, since she had formally proposed Reddy's nomination herself. The appeal licensed Congress members of Parliament and of the state assemblies to vote for the independent V. V. Giri without branding themselves rebels, and it worked: Reddy drew only 268 first-preference votes from a Congress Parliamentary Party 431 strong.

### Q: By what margin did V.V. Giri defeat Sanjeeva Reddy in the presidential election of August 1969?

The margin was narrow enough to make the election the closest presidential contest in the republic's history. On the first count of 20 August 1969, following polling on 16 August, Giri led with a vote value of 401,515 against Reddy's 313,548, with the opposition candidate C. D. Deshmukh third at 112,769, short of the quota of 418,169. When Deshmukh's ballots were distributed by second preferences, Giri rose to 420,077 and crossed the quota, while Reddy reached 405,427. The final gap between the independent backed by the Prime Minister and the official candidate of the ruling party was therefore 14,650 in vote value, produced by the defection of a large part of the Congress's own electoral college.

### Q: Which parties supported V.V. Giri against the official Congress candidate?

Giri's coalition joined the Prime Minister's Congress faction to most of the organised left and to the governing party of Madras. The Communist Party of India and the Communist Party of India (Marxist), divided since 1964, both backed the veteran trade unionist; the Samyukta Socialist Party supported him; the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, which governed Madras under M. Karunanidhi, delivered its legislators; and sections of the Akali Dal and the Muslim League added their votes. Against this combination the official candidate Sanjeeva Reddy relied on the Congress organisation's instruction and on sympathy from the right, whose own candidate was C. D. Deshmukh, fielded by the Swatantra Party and the Jana Sangh. The coalition's breadth showed that the election had become a national referendum on the Congress's internal quarrel.

### Q: When and how was Indira Gandhi expelled from the Congress?

Indira Gandhi was expelled on 12 November 1969 by a resolution of the Congress Working Committee, the party's executive, meeting in Delhi under the control of the syndicate's majority and its president S. Nijalingappa. The resolution, preserved in the AICC Papers at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library, removed her from the primary membership of the Congress for indiscipline, citing her refusal to support the official presidential candidate and her conscience appeal, and it directed the Congress Parliamentary Party to elect a new leader. The direction failed because the parliamentary party's majority refused it: within ten days a requisitioned meeting of the All India Congress Committee declared the expulsion void, and the expelling committee found itself leading the smaller of the two parties the resolution had created.

### Q: What happened at the rival Congress gatherings in Delhi in November 1969?

In November 1969 Delhi hosted two assemblies, each claiming to be the legitimate Congress. The syndicate's Working Committee met first and, on 12 November, expelled the Prime Minister. Her supporters answered with the instrument the party constitution allowed: a requisitioned meeting of the All India Congress Committee, convened on 22 and 23 November on the signatures of members demanding that the larger body judge the dispute. A majority of the AICC's 705 members, some 446 by the count her camp published, backed the requisition, and the meeting declared the expulsion invalid, removed Nijalingappa's authorities and reconstituted the party's leadership around Indira Gandhi, with Jagjivan Ram carried to the presidency at the faction's Bombay session the following month.

### Q: How did chief ministers and state Congress leaders divide between the two factions after the split?

The state units divided by following their legislators. Most sitting Congress chief ministers calculated that their assemblies stood with the Prime Minister, whose name had won their elections, and carried their governments into Congress (R); the parliamentary test the Election Commission later applied found her faction holding the majority of Congress legislators in Parliament and in the state assemblies taken together. The organisation faction held its ground where the syndicate's machines were personal and deep: the Commission recorded Congress (O) majorities in the legislatures of Gujarat and Mysore, Nijalingappa's home state, and Kamaraj kept the allegiance of much of the Tamil Nadu organisation in a state the Congress no longer governed. Several state committees duplicated themselves, issuing rival membership rolls from rival offices.

### Q: How did the Election Commission decide which faction was the real Congress?

The Election Commission decided under paragraph 15 of the Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order of 1968, which empowers it, when a recognised party splits, to determine which rival group is that party for the purposes of the reserved symbol. Both factions claimed the Congress and its pair-of-bullocks symbol. The Commission applied a test of majority support, counting adherents in the party's legislative wing, its members of Parliament and state assemblies, and in its organisational wing, and it found the majority with Indira Gandhi's group; pending and following the decision her faction fought the 1971 election under a new cow-and-calf symbol. Congress (O) appealed, and in Sadiq Ali v. Election Commission of India, decided on 11 November 1971, the Supreme Court upheld the Commission's majority test and its ruling.

### Q: What role did the 1967 general election play in producing the 1969 split?

The 1967 election created the conditions the split required. It reduced the Congress to 283 seats in the Lok Sabha, drove it from office across a belt of states from Punjab to Bengal and in Madras and Kerala, and installed Samyukta Vidhayak Dal coalition governments whose existence proved that the Congress could lose and that its legislators could imagine a politics beyond it. Inside the party the defeats discredited the organisation's claim to be the source of electoral survival, while teaching legislators that the leader's campaigning, and not the district committee, was what their seats would depend on in the next contest. Without 1967, the syndicate's instruction in the presidential election of 1969 would probably have held; after 1967, the audience for a conscience appeal existed.

### Q: How did the 1969 split change the selection of Congress election candidates?

After the split, the selection of Congress candidates migrated from the state and district committees to the centre and, in practice, to the Prime Minister's secretariat. The old procedure, in which Pradesh committees proposed names and the Central Election Committee bargained over them with state bosses who commanded their own followings, depended on bosses strong enough to insist; the syndicate's defeat destroyed that strength. For the election of 1971 the requisitionist Congress chose its candidates through committees its leader controlled, weighting loyalty to her and judgements of winnability above organisational seniority, and the practice hardened into a durable feature of the party: tickets as gifts of the leadership, legislators as the leadership's clients, and state leaders as appointees rather than rivals.

### Q: What precedent did the conscience vote set for party discipline and defection in India?

The conscience vote established that a party whip could be defied openly, by the government's own head, without the defiers paying a price, provided the defiance won. That demonstration lowered the cost of indiscipline and defection across the political system in the years that followed: legislators changed sides for office through the 1970s and into the 1980s with a freedom the pre-1969 conventions had restrained, and governments in the states rose and fell on purchased or persuaded crossings of the floor. Parliament's eventual answer, the Fifty-Second Amendment of 1985, inserted the Tenth Schedule into the Constitution to disqualify defectors, an attempt to restore by law the discipline that the events of August 1969 had shown could no longer be assumed as a matter of convention.

### Q: How do Inder Malhotra and Rajni Kothari read the 1969 split differently?

Inder Malhotra, in Indira Gandhi: A Personal and Political Biography (1989), reads the split through the persons who made it: the syndicate's determination to cage a Prime Minister it had installed, her refusal to be caged, and the presidential election as the duel in which those wills met; his account, built on interviews with the participants, is the fullest record of motive and sequence. Rajni Kothari, whose Politics in India appeared in 1970 and who had named the Congress system in 1964, reads the same events structurally: a party of consensus, which had processed conflict through internal factional bargaining, broke when one faction judged that it could win outside the bargaining, and the system, not merely the party, changed its rules. This article's adjudication accepts Malhotra on the trigger and Kothari on the consequences.
