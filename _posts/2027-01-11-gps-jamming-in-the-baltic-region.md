## The Quiet Pressure That Never Quite Becomes an Attack

GPS jamming in the Baltic region occupies a category that most security thinking handles badly. It is not an attack in any sense a lawyer or a treaty would recognize. It is not an accident in any sense that survives close examination of where and when it happens. It is not a one-off incident that can be logged, investigated, and closed. It is a condition: a persistent, low-grade degradation of the navigation and timing signals that civil aviation, commercial shipping, and military forces across the region around Poland have quietly come to depend on, appearing and receding in patterns that are consistent enough to be noticed and irregular enough to be argued about.

That condition is the subject of this assessment. The question is not whether navigation interference happens, because open aviation and maritime reporting across the region has documented the pattern well enough that the fact is no longer seriously contested. The question is what it means, how much it costs, why it is chosen, and what a state on the receiving end can reasonably do about it. Those are harder questions than they look, because the honest answers pull in opposite directions. The disruption is real and measurable in operational terms. The strategic weight of it is easy to overstate. And the response options are constrained by exactly the property that makes the tool attractive to whoever employs it.

![GPS jamming in the Baltic region, a gray-zone interference analysis - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-106.webp)

This article makes a specific argument. Navigation interference is not merely one gray-zone tool among many. It is close to the theoretical ideal of what a gray-zone instrument should be, and understanding why reveals something general about how below-threshold pressure works. The argument runs through four properties that interference possesses simultaneously and that almost no other coercive tool possesses together: it is cheap to sustain, hard to attribute with the confidence that a response would require, genuinely disruptive to the target, and structurally incapable of crossing the line that would trigger a collective-defense reaction. Call this the ideal-instrument rule. A gray-zone tool approaches its theoretical maximum as the product of disruption and deniability rises while the probability of provoking a response stays flat near zero. Navigation interference sits closer to that maximum than sabotage, closer than drone incursions, closer than disinformation, and far closer than anything involving a person crossing a border with intent.

The broader campaign of which this forms one strand is treated in [the pillar assessment of Russia's gray-zone war on Poland](/2026/12/11/russias-gray-zone-war-on-poland/), which sets out the full repertoire and the logic that binds it together. This article holds itself deliberately to the interference problem: what the disruption does, why it is chosen, how it is argued about, and where the honest limits of both alarm and dismissal lie. It does not attempt to be the resilience article, because protection of critical systems has a canonical owner elsewhere in the series and duplicating it here would produce a worse version of a better treatment.

## What Navigation Interference Actually Is

Two distinct phenomena get filed under one popular label, and the conflation causes confusion that matters analytically. Jamming, in the general sense, means overwhelming a receiver with noise so that it can no longer extract the faint signal it needs. Spoofing means feeding a receiver a plausible but false signal so that it computes a confident and wrong answer. The distinction matters because the two produce entirely different failure modes, invite entirely different responses, and carry entirely different signatures of intent.

A jammed receiver typically knows it is in trouble. It loses lock, reports degraded accuracy, throws a warning, or falls back to whatever secondary source it has. The operator sees a problem. The system degrades in a way that is visible and, in well-designed equipment, graceful. This is disruptive but honest failure. A spoofed receiver, by contrast, fails silently. It reports a position with normal confidence indicators, and the position is wrong. The operator sees nothing unusual until some other cue contradicts the display. That difference, between loud failure and quiet failure, is the single most important technical fact a non-specialist reader should carry away from this subject, because it determines almost everything about how dangerous a given episode is.

### What is the difference between jamming and spoofing?

Jamming drowns the signal so a receiver knows it has lost reliable positioning and warns the operator. Spoofing substitutes a false signal so the receiver reports a wrong position with full confidence and no warning. Loud failure is disruptive but manageable; quiet failure is the more dangerous of the two.

The underlying vulnerability is structural rather than accidental. Satellite navigation works by receiving extraordinarily weak signals from spacecraft in medium orbit and extracting timing information from them with great precision. The physics of that arrangement means the received signal power at the surface is minuscule compared with almost any locally generated emission on the same frequencies. This is not a design flaw that better engineering would have avoided; it is an inherent consequence of transmitting from orbit to a passive receiver with no return link. Every satellite navigation constellation shares the property to some degree, including the European, Russian, and Chinese systems alongside the American one that gave the popular label its name. Referring to the whole phenomenon as a problem with one constellation is a habit of speech rather than a description of the engineering.

Nor is the resulting fragility confined to knowing where you are. Satellite navigation is, at its core, a timing service that happens to yield position as a by-product. The same signals that tell a receiver its coordinates also distribute precise time across systems that never display a map: telecommunications networks that need synchronized handoffs, financial systems that timestamp transactions, power grids that coordinate protection relays across long distances, and broadcast infrastructure that needs frames to align. The timing dependency is deeper and less visible than the positioning dependency, and it is the reason that interference in an air corridor can produce consequences well away from anything that flies.

## Why the Signal Is So Easy to Degrade

The asymmetry between the effort required to disrupt navigation signals and the effort required to protect against disruption is the economic heart of the problem. This assessment will not describe methods, equipment, or techniques, and readers looking for that material will not find it here, because the responsible treatment of this subject stops firmly at effects. What can be said at the analytical level is that the cost curve runs the wrong way for defenders. The effort required to degrade reception across a meaningful area is small and falls over time as commodity electronics improve. The effort required to harden a national aviation system, a merchant fleet, and a military force against that degradation is large, slow, distributed across thousands of independent operators, and never finished.

That asymmetry is what converts a technical nuisance into a strategic instrument. A tool that costs its user very little and costs its target a great deal, repeatedly, without limit, and without a clear moment at which the target can say that a line has been crossed, is a tool that will be used. The rational response to that observation is not outrage but accounting: understanding precisely what the disruption costs, who bears the cost, and whether the accumulated total justifies a change of posture.

There is a second asymmetry that receives less attention and matters more over time. The user of an interference capability suffers the effects too. Any emission that degrades reception for civil aviation degrades it for everything else in range, including the emitter's own forces, its own civil aviation, and its own shipping. This produces a genuine operational constraint on how such capabilities are employed, and it also produces one of the more persuasive arguments in the incidental-spillover reading discussed later in this assessment. A capability that harms its user as well as its target will be employed in bursts, in specific geometries, and around particular activities, rather than continuously and indiscriminately. The observed pattern of episodic rather than constant degradation is consistent with that constraint, and it is consistent with several other explanations as well, which is exactly the analytical difficulty this subject presents at every turn.

## The Ideal-Instrument Rule

The central claim of this assessment can be stated as a rule and then defended. A gray-zone instrument is valuable to the extent that it delivers disruption and deniability at once while remaining structurally incapable of triggering the response mechanism that protects the target. Most tools fail at least one leg of that test. Navigation interference passes all three, and it is worth walking through why, because the reasoning generalizes.

Consider the four properties in turn. The first is cost. Sustained pressure requires a tool that can be maintained indefinitely without straining the budget or the attention of the state applying it. Physical sabotage requires people, and people are finite, expensive, and liable to be caught. Cyber operations require access that is painstaking to acquire and often perishable once used. Disinformation requires a continuous production apparatus and a receptive audience that may or may not exist. Electromagnetic degradation requires equipment that runs, and once installed it imposes little marginal cost per day of operation. It is closer to a standing condition than to a campaign requiring constant fresh investment.

The second property is deniability, and here the tool is close to unmatched. A saboteur can be arrested and interrogated. A drone can be recovered and examined. A network intrusion leaves forensic traces that skilled investigators can follow, sometimes to a named organization. Electromagnetic emissions leave nothing to recover. The evidence of their existence is the absence of a working signal, which is a negative fact, and negative facts are extraordinarily difficult to attribute. Geolocating an emission source requires sustained collection with specialized assets, and even a technically confident geolocation establishes only a rough origin, not an identity, an authority, or an intent. The distance between knowing roughly where a disturbance came from and being able to say publicly who ordered it is enormous, and the accusing party bears the full burden of closing that distance.

The third property is genuine disruption. A tool that is cheap and deniable but harmless is not an instrument of pressure; it is theater. Navigation degradation is not harmless. It imposes real costs on aviation through procedural workarounds, added crew workload, and occasional operational restrictions. It imposes real costs on shipping through degraded situational awareness in confined waters. It imposes real costs on military forces through the requirement to train, plan, and operate as though the service may not be available. Those costs are diffuse and mostly do not appear on any single ledger, which is a separate problem examined later, but they are real.

The fourth property is the threshold relationship, and it is the one that ties the others together. A tool must not merely avoid attribution; it must avoid the category of act that would compel a response even if attributed. This is the deepest point about interference and the one most often missed. Even in the hypothetical case where attribution were certain and publicly conceded, degradation of a navigation signal would not straightforwardly constitute an armed attack. No territory is entered. No person is injured. No property is destroyed. The affected systems continue to function in degraded form. A defender confronting a certainly attributed interference campaign would still face the question of what exactly to invoke and what exactly to do, and would find that the available frameworks fit poorly. That is the structural gift the tool offers its user, and it is why the ideal-instrument rule holds.

## Why This Region and Not Another

The concentration of reported degradation in the Baltic region and the areas around Poland is not incidental to the phenomenon, and the geography explains a good deal about why the instrument works so well here specifically.

Start with the shape of the sea. The Baltic is semi-enclosed, shallow in large parts, and entered through narrow passages. Commercial traffic is funneled into defined routes, ferry services cross those routes at right angles in high volume, and the whole basin is small enough that a vessel is rarely far from a coast, a shoal, or another ship. Every one of those features raises the value of an accurate position and shortens the time available to recover from a wrong one. The same disruption applied to a mid-ocean route would be an inconvenience. Applied here, it operates in an environment where precision has real consequences.

Airspace has a parallel structure. The region contains multiple national airspaces packed into short distances, with international boundaries crossed frequently on routine flights and coordination between control authorities required constantly. Sector transit times are short. Traffic density around the major hubs is high. An aircraft experiencing a navigation anomaly here has less time and less room than it would over a large landmass, and the controllers managing it are working across boundaries that complicate a rapid coordinated response.

Then there is the political geography, which matters more than either. This is a region where the territory of one state that operates significant military capability sits embedded among alliance members, separated from its own main landmass, and adjacent to the narrow land corridor that the series treats in its own cluster. Any electronic activity originating in or around that area affects several countries at once, none of which can address it individually. That multiplicity is not a complication for whoever generates the effects; it is an advantage, because a problem that belongs to six states belongs institutionally to none of them.

Finally, the region is where the alliance's eastern flank meets a maritime environment that is simultaneously a commercial artery, a defense corridor, and the site of energy and communications infrastructure running along and beneath it. Degrading navigation in that space touches military movement, civil commerce, and infrastructure protection at the same time with a single undifferentiated effect. Few other places offer that concentration of consequence for so little effort, which is a sufficient explanation for why the pattern appears here and not in places where the return would be lower.

## The Interference-Impact Matrix

The framework below is the analytical artifact of this assessment. It maps navigation degradation across the user communities it touches, rates each for the severity of disruption and for the ease with which the disruption can be denied, and identifies the reason each category sits below the threshold at which a defender could plausibly respond with force. The ratings are analytical judgments, not measurements, and they are offered so that a reader can argue with them rather than accept them.

| User category | What degrades | Disruption severity | Deniability | Why it stays below the threshold |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Commercial aviation en route | Position accuracy, some approach and terrain systems, crew workload | Moderate: procedures absorb it, cost accrues in delay and workload | Very high: airlines report, states cannot prove origin or intent | No damage, no casualty, flight continues under alternative procedures |
| Commercial aviation on approach | Precision approach aids that depend on satellite augmentation | Moderate to high at specific airfields: diversions and minima changes | Very high: attributed to environment or equipment as easily as to intent | Aviation safety system is designed to degrade to alternatives without incident |
| Merchant shipping in confined waters | Position, autopilot inputs, collision-avoidance display accuracy | Moderate to high in straits and approaches, low in open sea | Very high: maritime authorities issue warnings without naming a source | Ships have visual, radar, and traditional navigation fallbacks by law |
| Small craft, fishing, and leisure vessels | Position and chart overlay, often the only navigation source aboard | High relative to the vessel: fewest fallbacks, least training | Very high: rarely reported, rarely investigated | Individual incidents are too small and too dispersed to aggregate politically |
| Search, rescue, and emergency services | Position reporting, dispatch routing, coordination timing | High when it coincides with an incident, otherwise negligible | High: coincidence rather than causation is always available as an explanation | Effects are conditional and probabilistic rather than direct |
| Military navigation and precision effects | Unprotected receivers, planning assumptions, some munition guidance | Low to moderate: protected receivers and alternatives exist | Very high: forces do not publicize what is degraded or by how much | Degradation of a peacetime capability is not an attack on a force |
| Timing-dependent civil infrastructure | Network synchronization, grid coordination, transaction timestamps | Low in short episodes, potentially high in sustained ones | Very high: failures surface as technical faults, not as attacks | Consequences are indirect, delayed, and hard to trace back to a cause |
| Public confidence and political attention | Perception that the state cannot protect ordinary functions | Cumulative rather than immediate | Very high: the effect is psychological and unprovable | There is no legal category for degraded public confidence |

Reading the matrix from left to right reveals the pattern that makes this instrument attractive. Disruption is real in every row and severe in none, deniability is high in every row without exception, and the threshold column supplies a different but equally solid reason in each case for why no response mechanism engages. A defender looking for the row that justifies escalation will not find one. A defender looking at the column totals, however, sees something different: a persistent tax levied across eight categories of user at once, indefinitely, with no expiry and no mechanism for relief. The correct analytical response to the matrix is neither to point at a single row and declare an attack nor to shrug at the individual severity ratings. It is to notice that the tool is designed so that no single row will ever justify a response, and to plan accordingly.

## Civil Aviation: The Most Visible Affected Community

Aviation reports interference more thoroughly than any other user community, which produces a systematic distortion in public understanding of the phenomenon. Flights are tracked, crews are required to report anomalies, safety authorities collect and publish, and the industry has a culture of documenting degradation rather than absorbing it silently. This is a virtue of the aviation safety system, and it also means that the aviation picture is the picture most people see, which leads them to treat aviation as the primary affected community when it is more accurately the best-instrumented one.

What actually happens to an aircraft in a degraded reception environment is less dramatic than the reporting sometimes implies and more consequential than the reassurances suggest. Commercial aviation was built around ground-based navigation and inertial systems long before satellite positioning existed, and those layers remain in place. An airliner that loses satellite reception does not become lost. It reverts to inertial reference, ground-based aids, radar vectors from controllers, and standard procedures rehearsed in training. The system is designed on the assumption that any single navigation source may fail, and it degrades in a controlled way when one does.

The costs are therefore procedural rather than catastrophic, and they accumulate in three places. The first is crew workload. A flight deck managing a navigation anomaly in a busy sector is doing extra work at precisely the moment when spare capacity is most valuable. Aviation safety thinking has understood for decades that accidents rarely have a single cause and usually involve a reduction in the margin available to handle the next problem. Persistent degradation does not cause accidents directly; it thins the margin, and thinner margins produce worse outcomes across a large number of flights over a long period. That is a real safety argument, and it is also an argument that cannot be converted into a specific attributable harm, which is precisely the pattern this whole subject follows.

The second cost sits with approach procedures at specific airfields. Modern aviation has invested heavily in satellite-based approaches, particularly at airports where installing and maintaining traditional precision aids is expensive or geographically awkward. Where those approaches become unreliable, operators fall back to older procedures with higher weather minima, which means more diversions in poor conditions, more fuel carried against the possibility, and more disruption to schedules. Open reporting in the region around Poland and across the wider Baltic area has documented episodes in which airfields adjusted procedures in response to unreliable reception. The specifics of which airfields, for how long, and with what operational consequence vary and should be confirmed against current aviation information rather than taken from any general account.

The third cost is the one that gets the headlines and deserves the most careful handling: the possibility of a genuinely dangerous event. Aviation safety is a probabilistic discipline. Degraded navigation in a region with dense traffic, complex airspace, and short sector transit times raises the probability of a serious event by some amount that is small in absolute terms and not zero. Responsible analysis says exactly that and stops. It does not say an accident is imminent, because the layered defenses of the aviation system are genuinely robust and have absorbed far worse degradations than this. It also does not say the risk is zero, because that claim requires a confidence about complex systems that no honest safety professional would offer.

### How much does navigation degradation actually endanger flights?

Directly, very little: airliners retain inertial systems, ground-based aids, and controller support, and crews train for the failure. Indirectly, the risk is a thinner safety margin across many flights over a long period, which raises the probability of a serious event by a small but non-zero amount.

## Maritime Shipping: The Quieter and More Exposed Community

Shipping reports far less than aviation and is in several respects more exposed, which inverts the impression that public coverage creates. The maritime picture deserves closer attention than it usually gets.

Merchant vessels are legally required to carry and use traditional navigation methods, and a competent bridge team on a well-run ship can navigate confidently without satellite positioning using radar, visual bearings, echo sounder, and chart work. That statement is true and it is also incomplete, because it describes a standard rather than a universal reality. Bridge teams have thinned over decades of commercial pressure. Electronic chart systems have become the primary display on which navigation is actually conducted, and those displays are populated by satellite-derived position. Traditional skills are trained and certified, but they are exercised less often than they once were, and skills that are not exercised degrade whatever the certificate says.

The geography of the Baltic compounds the problem. This is a semi-enclosed sea with shallow areas, narrow approaches, dense traffic separation schemes, heavy ferry traffic crossing the main commercial flows, and seasonal conditions that reduce visibility for long periods. It is an environment where an accurate position matters more than it does in open ocean and where the consequences of a wrong one arrive faster. A vessel that computes a confident wrong position in a traffic separation scheme in poor visibility is in a materially worse situation than an aircraft in the same circumstance, because it has less time, fewer external observers, and no equivalent of a radar controller watching the whole picture and issuing instructions.

There is a further maritime dimension that connects this subject to the wider gray-zone picture without belonging to this article. Degraded navigation in an area that also hosts undersea cables, pipelines, and energy infrastructure creates ambiguity about vessel behavior that is useful to anyone who wants to operate near that infrastructure without explaining themselves. A ship whose track looks irregular can attribute the irregularity to reception problems. The relationship between navigation ambiguity and infrastructure protection belongs properly to the treatment of [protecting critical infrastructure](/2028/06/21/protecting-critical-infrastructure/), which owns the resilience and protection question for the series, and this assessment defers to it rather than duplicating it.

The reporting asymmetry deserves one final point because it distorts every attempt to measure the scale of the phenomenon. Aviation reports because it must. Commercial shipping reports selectively, since operators have commercial reasons not to advertise navigation difficulties and crews have professional reasons not to log an anomaly they resolved without incident. Fishing vessels and leisure craft, which have the fewest fallbacks and the least training, report almost never. Any dataset assembled from reports therefore under-represents exactly the users most exposed, and any conclusion drawn from such a dataset about who is affected should be treated with that bias in mind.

## The Timing Layer: What Breaks When Nothing Looks Broken

The least visible consequence of navigation degradation is the one with the widest reach, and it has almost nothing to do with knowing where anything is. Satellite navigation constellations are, functionally, distributed clocks. Each satellite carries extremely stable timekeeping and broadcasts it continuously, and any receiver in view can recover time to a precision that would otherwise require expensive laboratory equipment. Over three decades, that free and ubiquitous precision time became load-bearing for infrastructure that has no interest in navigation whatsoever.

Telecommunications networks use it to keep base stations synchronized so that handoffs work and adjacent cells do not interfere with each other. Electrical grids use it to timestamp measurements across long distances so that protection systems can distinguish a genuine fault from a transient and act in the right place. Financial and trading systems use it to order transactions across venues. Broadcast networks use it to align transmissions. Data centers use it to sequence distributed operations. Almost none of these applications advertise the dependency, and many of the engineers maintaining them are unaware of how much of their system's correctness rests on a satellite signal that no one budgets for.

The consequences of degrading that layer are qualitatively different from the consequences of degrading positioning. Position failures are immediate, obvious, and confined to whoever needed the position. Timing failures are delayed, obscure, and propagate. A network element that loses its reference does not fail at once; it free-runs on its internal oscillator and drifts slowly out of tolerance, producing symptoms hours or days later that present as ordinary technical faults with no apparent external cause. By the time the symptom surfaces, the connection back to the original degradation is difficult to establish and frequently never made at all.

This creates an accounting problem that runs through the whole subject. Costs that are never attributed to a cause are never counted, and costs that are never counted never enter the political calculation that would justify a response. A telecommunications operator that spends engineering hours chasing intermittent synchronization faults logs those hours as maintenance. A grid operator that adds redundant timing sources logs the expense as a resilience upgrade. Neither appears in any tally of what electromagnetic pressure has cost the country, and the aggregate of such uncounted costs across an economy over years is plausibly larger than the visible aviation and maritime disruption that dominates discussion. That is an assessment rather than a measurement, and it is offered as an argument for building the measurement rather than as a substitute for it.

Well-run infrastructure is not defenseless here. Holdover oscillators, alternative timing distribution, terrestrial time services, and architectural changes that reduce dependence on a single source all exist and are being adopted. The point is not that the problem is unsolvable. The point is that solving it costs money, takes years, must be done separately by every operator of every dependent system, and is being driven by a pressure that no one can formally attribute to anyone. The party applying the pressure pays nothing toward the remediation and bears no consequence for having caused it.

## Military Effects: Degradation Rather Than Denial

The military dimension is where public discussion most often goes wrong in both directions at once, treating armed forces either as helplessly dependent on a signal that can be switched off or as fully immune through classified protection. Neither is right, and the accurate picture is more useful than either.

Modern forces are deeply integrated with satellite navigation and have been for a generation. Position reporting, blue-force tracking, fires coordination, aviation, logistics movement, and several classes of precision munition all use it. That dependency is real and militaries do not pretend otherwise. What distinguishes military use from civil use is that the dependency was recognized early, has been the subject of sustained investment, and has been designed against. Military receivers include protections that civil receivers do not. Alternative navigation methods are maintained and exercised. Inertial systems provide continuity through periods of degradation. Planning assumes that the service may be unavailable in contested conditions, because any adversary capable of contesting it would be expected to do so from the first hour of a real conflict.

The result is that degradation in peacetime conditions is an operational annoyance and a training input rather than a capability defeat for a well-prepared force. Units train with the assumption of interference. Exercises deliberately degrade the service to build the habit of operating without it. Forces on the eastern flank in particular treat the environment as a permanent feature to be planned around rather than a surprise to be reported. In that sense, sustained peacetime degradation may be doing a defender a modest favor by keeping the training realistic and the assumptions honest, which is a genuinely underappreciated point.

But three military consequences are real and should not be dismissed. The first is that not everything a military operates is a protected system. Support vehicles, contracted logistics, unmanned systems of the smaller and cheaper varieties, and a great deal of equipment procured for cost rather than resilience use ordinary commercial receivers. The gap between the protected tip of a force and its unprotected tail is wide, and logistics is where the vulnerability concentrates. This connects to the reinforcement and movement problem the series treats elsewhere, since moving formations across long distances depends on a transport and coordination layer that is far less hardened than the combat elements it supports.

The second consequence concerns intelligence rather than operations. A sustained interference environment is a laboratory. Whoever operates in it observes how the defender responds: which procedures change, which alternative systems come up, how quickly reporting flows, what gets adjusted and what does not. Every episode is a low-cost probe that returns information about a defender's adaptations. The same logic drives the ambiguity of airspace incursions treated in [the assessment of drones over Poland as accident or probe](/2027/01/01/drones-over-poland-accident-or-probe/), and the two phenomena should be read together as parts of a single learning process rather than as separate irritants.

The third consequence is about the transition to conflict, and it is the most important. In a real conflict, degradation of navigation would be immediate, comprehensive, and combined with every other form of electronic disruption available. Peacetime degradation is therefore a preview of the wartime environment at a fraction of the intensity. That preview has an ambiguous strategic value: it hardens the defender through practice, and it normalizes an environment that would otherwise be an unmistakable indicator. If degraded navigation becomes routine background, then degraded navigation preceding an actual operation loses much of its value as a warning signal. That is a genuine cost to the defender's warning picture, and it is the most strategically serious consequence in this entire assessment.

### Does persistent interference blind a modern military?

No. Protected receivers, inertial systems, alternative navigation methods, and planning that assumes denial mean a prepared force degrades rather than stops. The real military costs are unprotected logistics systems, the intelligence a persistent environment yields to the emitter, and the erosion of degradation as a warning signal.

## The Attribution Problem

Everything difficult about responding to this form of pressure flows from one fact: proving who caused it is genuinely hard, and the difficulty is technical, legal, and political at once.

Start with the technical layer. Establishing that reception is degraded in an area is straightforward; receivers report it and reports aggregate. Establishing where the interfering emission originates is a different task requiring measurement from multiple positions over time with equipment configured for the purpose. States with the relevant collection capability can do this, and the resulting geolocation is a probability region rather than a point. Establishing what generated the emission requires characterizing it. Establishing who controls that emitter requires knowing what is at that location, which may be knowable from other sources or may not. Establishing that the emission was intentional rather than incidental to some other activity requires understanding purpose, which technical collection alone cannot supply.

Each of those steps loses confidence. A chain of five reasonable inferences with high individual confidence produces a conclusion with considerably lower confidence, and the standard a government needs before making a public accusation against another state is high, because the cost of being wrong in public is severe and lasting.

The legal layer compounds the technical one. Even a confident attribution runs into the question of what has been attributed. Radio spectrum is governed by international arrangements built around cooperation and complaint resolution rather than enforcement against a state that is uninterested in cooperating. The mechanisms are designed for accidental interference between well-intentioned users, and they work well for that. Deliberate interference by a state that declines to acknowledge it falls outside what those mechanisms were built to handle. Complaints can be lodged and are; they produce a record rather than a remedy.

The political layer is the decisive one. Suppose attribution were technically solid and legally characterized. A government still faces the question of what to do with it. Public accusation without a follow-on response demonstrates that the pressure can be applied with impunity, which teaches the wrong lesson. Public accusation with a response requires selecting a response proportionate to a harm that is real, diffuse, and difficult to quantify. Private protest through diplomatic channels preserves options and produces no visible result. Doing nothing publicly is the path most governments take most of the time, and it is not obviously wrong, though it does have a cumulative cost in credibility that is rarely priced.

This is the same structural bind that runs through sabotage, cyber intrusion, and every other deniable instrument, and it is why the attribution question is properly the central battleground of gray-zone competition rather than a technical footnote to it. The electronic dimension of that competition connects directly to the wider treatment in [the assessment of the cyber threat to Poland](/2028/08/11/the-cyber-threat-to-poland/), which owns the network and attribution question for the series and should be read alongside this one.

### Why can states not simply name who is responsible?

Because attribution requires chaining several inferences, each of which loses confidence: detecting degradation, geolocating an emission, characterizing it, identifying who controls that location, and establishing intent. Governments need high confidence before public accusation, and even solid attribution leaves the harder question of what response would follow.

## Why GPS Jamming Sits Below the Response Threshold

The threshold question is where analytical rigor matters most, because it is where wishful reasoning is most tempting. A reader who has followed the accumulating costs described above may reasonably conclude that something this persistent and this widespread ought to trigger something. The honest answer is that it does not, and that the reasons it does not are structural rather than a failure of will.

Collective defense arrangements are built around armed attack. That concept has a settled core: the use of force against territory, forces, or population producing damage, injury, or death. Around that core sits a contested margin where states argue about whether particular acts qualify, and the argument has been live for years regarding cyber operations of sufficient severity. The mainstream position among states and lawyers is that effects rather than means determine the answer, so that an operation producing consequences equivalent to a kinetic attack could qualify regardless of how it was delivered.

Apply that test to navigation degradation and the result is clear. There is no physical damage. There is no injury. There are no deaths. Affected systems continue to function using alternative methods. The consequences are degraded performance and increased cost, distributed across many parties, none of whom can point to a discrete harm of the relevant severity. Under any reasonable application of the effects test, this does not reach the armed-attack standard, and no serious legal argument suggests otherwise on the facts as generally reported.

The consultation mechanisms that exist below the collective-defense threshold are a different matter. Allies can and do consult over threats to security that fall short of armed attack, and persistent interference is a legitimate subject for that consultation. But consultation produces coordination and statements, not compulsion. It is a mechanism for organizing a collective political response, and where the collective judgment is that no forceful response is warranted, consultation confirms that judgment rather than overriding it.

There is a second threshold that matters more in practice than the legal one: the political threshold at which a government decides that a response is worth its costs. That threshold sits well above the legal one, and it moves according to what else is happening. Sustained low-grade degradation that has become routine will not cross it. The same degradation coinciding with a serious incident, a casualty, or a crisis elsewhere might. This is why the timing of a response depends less on the interference itself than on the surrounding context, and why an unchanged pattern can produce years of silence followed by a sudden reaction that looks disproportionate to observers who were not tracking the context.

The consequence for the defender is uncomfortable and should be stated plainly. There is no accumulation rule. Nothing in any framework says that a sufficient quantity of sub-threshold pressure eventually becomes a crossing. A tool that stays below the line stays below it indefinitely, however long it is applied and however much it costs in aggregate. That is not a loophole to be closed by clever drafting; it is a consequence of building collective defense around armed attack, which was a deliberate and defensible choice made for good reasons. The question of where the line genuinely lies and what would constitute a crossing is treated directly in [the assessment of when gray-zone pressure becomes open war](/2027/02/11/when-gray-zone-becomes-open-war/), which owns the threshold question for the series.

### Would sustained interference ever justify a military response?

Not on its own. It produces no damage, injury, or death, so it does not meet the armed-attack standard under any mainstream reading, and there is no rule by which accumulated sub-threshold pressure becomes a crossing. A response would follow from surrounding context, not from the interference reaching some quantity.

## Deliberate Pressure or Incidental Spillover

This is the genuine analytical disagreement in the subject, and it deserves a fair hearing on both sides rather than the assertion that usually substitutes for one. Serious people hold both positions, and the evidence does not decisively settle it.

The deliberate-pressure reading holds that sustained degradation across the region is a chosen instrument of coercion applied for effect. Its supporting arguments are substantial. The geographic distribution of documented episodes correlates with areas of strategic interest rather than distributing randomly. The pattern has persisted long enough that an unintended side effect would plausibly have been noticed and moderated by a state that cared about the diplomatic cost of causing it. Any state operating such capabilities knows their effects on civil users, since those effects are neither obscure nor disputed. Continuing regardless is at minimum a decision to accept the consequences, which is a form of intent even if generating the consequences was not the primary purpose. And the whole pattern fits comfortably within a broader repertoire of deniable pressure whose other elements are much harder to explain innocently.

The incidental-spillover reading holds that most degradation is a by-product of legitimate defensive activity rather than a campaign aimed at civil users. Its arguments are also substantial and are too often dismissed. A state that believes itself under threat of air and missile attack has strong reasons to operate defensive electronic systems, and those systems produce effects that do not stop at borders because electromagnetic propagation does not respect them. Protection of high-value locations against navigation-guided weapons is a recognized defensive requirement, not an exotic claim. The episodic rather than continuous pattern is more consistent with activity tied to specific operations or exercises than with a campaign designed for steady pressure. And the emitter suffers the effects too, degrading its own aviation and shipping, which is an odd feature of a tool supposedly chosen for its favorable cost ratio.

Both readings explain the observed pattern. That is precisely the problem, and it is not an accident. The analytical honest position is that the two are not mutually exclusive and that the distinction may be less meaningful than it appears. A state can operate systems for defensive purposes, observe that they impose costs on neighbors, and decline to moderate them because those costs are useful. Under that reading the activity is defensively motivated in origin and coercively sustained in continuation, and asking whether it is deliberate or incidental imposes a binary on something that is genuinely both.

For the defender, this ambiguity is the operative fact regardless of which reading is correct. A defender cannot know intent with confidence, cannot prove it if known, and must design a response that works under both readings. That is a demanding requirement, and it explains why measured responses focused on resilience tend to dominate over responses focused on punishment. Resilience works whether the cause is malice or spillover. Punishment requires being right about which one it is.

### Is the interference deliberate or an accident?

The evidence supports both readings and does not settle between them. Distribution and persistence suggest deliberate pressure; the defensive rationale, the episodic pattern, and the emitter's own losses suggest spillover. The most defensible view is that it may be defensively motivated in origin and coercively tolerated in continuation.

## Normalization by Accumulation

The most consequential effect of sustained pressure of this kind is not any single disruption but the change in what counts as normal, and this deserves separate treatment because it is where the strategic payoff actually sits.

Consider how a defender's perception evolves. The first episodes are anomalies: reported, investigated, discussed, treated as significant. As they recur, the reporting machinery adapts. Procedures are written. Crews are briefed. Advisory notices become routine publications rather than alerts. Analysts stop writing about individual episodes and start writing about the pattern. Eventually the condition is a feature of the operating environment, mentioned in briefings the way weather is mentioned, absorbed into planning assumptions, and no longer capable of generating attention or response.

That transition is the objective, whether or not anyone articulated it as such. Three things are accomplished by it. First, hostile presence in the electromagnetic environment becomes unremarkable, which means the emitter operates freely without generating friction. Second, the defender's warning apparatus loses a category of signal, since a phenomenon that occurs constantly cannot indicate anything by occurring. Third, and most subtly, the population and the political system learn that this class of pressure produces no response, which shapes expectations about the next class and the one after that.

The third effect is the one that connects this subject to the wider gray-zone competition. A campaign that establishes a pattern of costless action against a target is doing more than imposing the specific costs of that action. It is testing and demonstrating the target's response function. The information gained is valuable independent of the disruption caused, and the demonstration effect operates on audiences beyond the immediate target, including allies weighing how much of a fuss to make about pressure applied to someone else.

Against this, an honest assessment must note the counterargument, which is that normalization also produces adaptation. A society that has absorbed persistent degradation has, in the process, built alternative timing sources, trained crews for the environment, written the procedures, and hardened the systems that mattered enough to harden. That is genuine resilience purchased at the cost of the disruption, and it is resilience the defender would otherwise have deferred indefinitely because the business case for it was weak. Whether normalization is net negative for the defender depends on whether the erosion of warning value and political attention outweighs the adaptation, and reasonable analysts differ on that balance.

## The Escalation Risk Nobody Prices

The most serious risk in this domain is not that pressure will continue at its present level. It is that an episode will coincide with something else and produce a consequence that neither party chose.

The mechanism is straightforward. Degradation raises the probability of navigation-related incidents by a small amount across a large number of operations over a long period. Small probabilities multiplied by large exposure over long durations produce events. If a serious incident occurs in a period of documented degradation, the political sequence that follows will be driven by the coincidence rather than by any established causation, because causation in such a case would be extraordinarily difficult to establish and impossible to establish quickly.

Consider how that unfolds. An incident occurs. Reception in the area was known to be unreliable. Investigators say, correctly, that they cannot determine whether degradation contributed. The public and the political system fill the gap with the obvious inference. Pressure builds for a response to something that has now been recharacterized from routine nuisance to lethal act. The state alleged to be responsible denies causation, correctly noting that no causal link has been established, and interprets the demand for a response as opportunistic. Both parties are now in a position neither selected, driven by an event that may not have been anyone's fault.

This is the classic structure by which low-level competition produces high-level crises, and it is the strongest available argument against tolerating persistent degradation on the grounds that its direct costs are modest. The direct costs are indeed modest. The tail risk is not, and it is a tail risk that grows with duration and exposure rather than staying constant. A defender assessing whether to accept the condition should be pricing the tail rather than the mean, and a state applying the pressure should be aware that it is accumulating an exposure it does not control and cannot call back.

There is a symmetric risk running the other way, which fairness requires stating. A defender that responds forcefully to an episode on the basis of inference rather than established attribution creates its own escalation pathway. The pressure is designed to make exactly that mistake attractive by ensuring that certainty never arrives while frustration accumulates. A response based on a wrong attribution, or on a correct attribution of an incidental effect to deliberate intent, would hand the other party a genuine grievance and a public case. The discipline of not responding to provocation is itself a component of deterrence, and it is harder to sustain than it sounds. The posture question of how a state competes in this space without either capitulating or overreacting belongs to [the treatment of how Poland fights below the threshold](/2027/02/21/fighting-below-the-threshold/), which owns the response-design question for the series.

## How the Instrument Compares With Its Alternatives

Placing navigation degradation alongside the other tools in the same repertoire clarifies why the ideal-instrument rule ranks it where it does, and the comparison is instructive in both directions.

Sabotage produces sharper effects than any amount of signal disruption. A damaged facility stops working, and the consequence is immediate, attributable to a location, and severe enough to command attention. But sabotage requires people who can be caught, produces physical evidence that supports investigation, and generates a discrete event that a state can point to and respond against. It buys effect at the price of deniability, which is the trade the tool under discussion here refuses to make. The series treats that trade directly in its assessment of [sabotage on Polish soil](/2026/12/21/sabotage-on-polish-soil/), which owns the physical-disruption question.

Airspace incursions produce visibility rather than effect. An object crossing a border is unmistakable, generates a response, and forces a decision, which is often the purpose. But the object can be recovered, examined, and traced, and the incursion is a discrete event that a defender can prepare for, count, and cite. It buys attention at the price of ambiguity.

Influence operations are cheap and highly deniable but depend entirely on receptive conditions that the operator does not control. A society that is cohesive, well informed, and skeptical absorbs a great deal of manipulation with little effect, and the return on the effort is therefore unpredictable and often disappointing. Electromagnetic effects, by contrast, do not care what the target believes. Physics delivers the result regardless of the audience's disposition, which makes the return far more reliable.

Engineered migration pressure produces political effects of real magnitude and is highly deniable in the sense that the movement of people is always attributable to the people themselves. But it requires an apparatus to organize, generates humanitarian visibility that cuts both ways, and imposes costs on the organizing state that are not trivial. It also has a natural ceiling and a natural end, since the pressure cannot be sustained forever at constant intensity.

Set against all four, signal degradation is weaker on effect and stronger on everything else. It cannot compel a decision, cannot destroy anything, and cannot force a government's hand. What it can do is run continuously at low cost, produce no evidence, generate no discrete event, require no organization, impose no meaningful cost on its user beyond self-interference, and never present a defender with a moment that demands a reply. That combination is why it belongs at the top of any ranking built on the ideal-instrument rule and near the bottom of any ranking built on immediate impact. The two rankings answer different questions, and the mistake most commentary makes is applying the second while thinking it is applying the first. The full comparison of below-threshold pressure against the risk of open conflict is developed in [the assessment of hybrid war against open invasion risk](/2027/03/01/hybrid-war-vs-open-invasion-risk/).

## The Coordination Problem Among Allies

A phenomenon that crosses borders indiscriminately creates a governance problem that is separate from and in some ways harder than the attribution problem, and it receives far less attention than it deserves.

Electromagnetic effects do not stop at frontiers. A single source degrades reception across several countries at once, in different amounts, affecting different sectors in each, and generating reports through separate national systems that use different definitions and different thresholds for what counts as an incident. There is no single authority that experiences the phenomenon whole. Each state sees its own slice, judges the severity by its own exposure, and forms a national view that may differ substantially from its neighbor's.

That fragmentation has three consequences. The first is that severity is systematically underestimated in aggregate, because no institution sums the slices. A state that experiences a moderate problem concludes it has a moderate problem, and six states each concluding the same thing produce a collective judgment of moderate rather than the correct judgment about the total.

The second consequence is that responsibility is diffuse. Aviation safety belongs to civil aviation authorities. Maritime safety belongs to maritime administrations. Spectrum management belongs to communications regulators. Military effects belong to defense ministries. Attribution belongs to intelligence services. Diplomatic protest belongs to foreign ministries. Each of these is a different institution with a different mandate in each of several countries, and the phenomenon falls into the seams between all of them. A problem that is everyone's marginal concern is nobody's priority, and this is a far better explanation for institutional slowness than any theory of governmental indifference.

The third consequence is that a collective response requires a collective judgment that the fragmentation prevents forming. Alliance consultation works when members share a characterization of what has happened. Here the characterization varies by exposure, by sector, and by national temperament, with more affected states inclined toward urgency and less affected ones inclined toward proportion. Reaching a shared position takes time that a persistent, unspectacular condition never forces anyone to spend.

The remedy is unexciting and consists mostly of plumbing: common definitions so that reports from different countries and sectors are comparable, a designated point of aggregation, and a standing mechanism for producing a shared regional picture. None of that requires attribution, accusation, or any decision about response. It requires only that somebody be responsible for seeing the whole, which is currently not the case, and it is the most tractable improvement available in this entire problem set.

## What the Instrument Cannot Do

An honest assessment of a tool should include its limits from the perspective of whoever uses it, and those limits are real enough to constrain how much strategic weight the phenomenon can carry.

Signal degradation cannot take or hold territory, cannot destroy a capability, and cannot compel a government to change a policy. It generates friction rather than outcomes. A state applying it continuously for a decade would find at the end of that decade that its target's alliance membership, force structure, defense budget, and political orientation were unchanged, and possibly reinforced, since visible pressure tends to consolidate rather than divide a society that identifies the source.

It cannot be escalated usefully. Increasing the intensity does not produce proportionally greater effect, because the affected systems fall back to alternatives at a certain point and further degradation changes nothing. Broadening the geography increases self-interference and diplomatic exposure without a corresponding gain. The tool has a low ceiling, and a user that pushed against that ceiling would begin trading its deniability away for effects that remain modest.

It cannot be aimed. Effects fall on everything in range, including the user's own aviation, shipping, and forces, and including neutral parties whose annoyance serves no purpose. A tool that cannot discriminate is a tool that generates diplomatic costs disproportionate to the effects it achieves against the intended target.

And it cannot be concealed indefinitely. Deniability is not invisibility. Everyone knows the phenomenon exists, most informed observers hold a confident private view about its origin, and the deniability operates only at the level of formal public attribution. That is enough to prevent a response, which is the point, but it is not enough to prevent the reputational accumulation that comes from being the state everyone privately blames for a persistent regional problem. Over a long period that accumulation has costs, and they are paid in the currency of how other states characterize the actor when deciding harder questions.

These limits are the reason this assessment does not treat the phenomenon as strategically decisive. It is a well-designed instrument for a narrow purpose: imposing distributed cost, gathering information about a defender's adaptations, normalizing hostile presence, and eroding a warning signal, all without giving anyone a reason to act. Within that narrow purpose it works extremely well. Beyond it, the instrument has very little to offer, and a defender who understands both halves of that judgment will neither panic about it nor ignore it.

## Building Resilience Without Building a Grievance

The response that works under every reading of intent is the one that reduces dependence, and it has the additional virtue of being unprovocative. A state that hardens its systems is not accusing anyone, not escalating, and not requiring attribution it cannot produce. It is simply making itself a less rewarding target, which degrades the instrument's value without giving its user anything to react to.

The architecture of that resilience is treated in depth by [the policy assessment of protecting critical infrastructure](/2028/06/21/protecting-critical-infrastructure/), which is the canonical owner of the protection question across this series, and this assessment defers to it rather than producing a thinner parallel version. What belongs here is the narrower point about how resilience interacts with the interference dynamic specifically, and there are four observations worth making.

The first is that resilience against navigation degradation is unusually well understood technically and unusually badly executed institutionally. Nobody is confused about what would help. Diversifying across constellations, maintaining terrestrial alternatives, improving receiver behavior in degraded conditions, retaining and exercising traditional navigation skills, and reducing single-source timing dependence are all established measures with known costs and known benefits. The obstacle is not knowledge; it is that the measures are expensive, distributed across thousands of independent operators, and justified by a threat that produces no acute crisis to force the decision. This is the standard shape of an infrastructure resilience problem, and it fails in the standard way, through deferral.

The second observation is that resilience must be built before it is needed and cannot be surged. An aviation authority cannot install terrestrial navigation aids during a crisis. A shipping company cannot rebuild bridge-team navigation skills in a week. A telecommunications operator cannot re-architect timing distribution under pressure. Everything useful takes years, which means the decision to invest must be made during exactly the period when the threat looks like a manageable nuisance. This is the recurring pathology of all preparedness spending and the reason such spending is chronically inadequate everywhere.

The third observation concerns the training dividend, which is real and easy to miss. A society operating in a persistently degraded environment develops habits that a society with reliable signals does not. Crews that regularly handle navigation anomalies handle them better. Procedures written for degraded conditions and used routinely work when they are needed for real. Planners who assume the service may be unavailable produce plans that survive its unavailability. If a serious conflict came, the forces and civil systems that had spent years operating in a degraded environment would be materially better prepared than those that had not. The pressure applied over time has therefore purchased the defender something, which is an outcome its user presumably did not intend.

The fourth observation is about what resilience does to the instrument. A tool retains value only while it disrupts. Every alternative timing source installed, every crew trained for degraded operations, every system re-architected to tolerate loss of the signal reduces the disruption a given level of pressure produces. Resilience does not deter in the classical sense of threatening a cost; it deters by devaluation, making the effort not worth its trouble. That is the quietest and most durable form of counter-pressure available in the gray zone, and it is available without attribution, without accusation, and without escalation. For a defender facing an instrument specifically engineered to make retaliation impossible, devaluation is not a second-best option. It is the appropriate one.

Analysts and staff who want to track the pattern rather than react to individual episodes will find a structured record more useful than recollection, and readers building one can [track indicators and maintain a risk checklist on ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/poland-russia-risk-monitor.html) alongside their private notes.

## The Line Between Nuisance and Genuine Danger

Every gray-zone tool sits somewhere on a spectrum from irritant to threat, and locating navigation degradation on that spectrum honestly is the analytical task this section addresses. The temptation runs both ways: toward treating a persistent condition as an act of war because it is persistent, and toward dismissing it as background noise because no one has been hurt. Both are errors and both are common.

Four criteria distinguish nuisance from genuine danger in this domain, and applying them produces a more useful answer than either reflex.

The first criterion is whether direct harm has occurred. On the open record, degradation has caused operational disruption, procedural change, added cost, and increased workload. It has not, on the available evidence, caused a loss of life or a major accident that investigators have attributed to it. That places the phenomenon firmly on the nuisance end of the direct-harm axis, and any assessment that skips past this point to reach a more dramatic conclusion is arguing from something other than evidence.

The second criterion is trajectory. A condition that is stable is a different problem from one that is intensifying. The relevant question is not how bad the disruption is but whether the pattern is broadening in area, lengthening in duration, or extending to user categories it previously spared. A stable nuisance can be managed indefinitely. An expanding one requires a decision at some point, and identifying the point of expansion is more analytically valuable than measuring the current level.

The third criterion is what the phenomenon reveals about the adversary rather than what it costs the target. If degradation is a by-product of defensive activity, it tells you about defensive posture. If it is calibrated pressure, it tells you about a willingness to impose costs on civil populations in peacetime, and that willingness is informative about how other decisions might be made. The signal value about intent may exceed the cost value of the disruption, and a defender that focuses entirely on the cost is discarding the more useful half of what the phenomenon offers.

The fourth criterion is the one already identified as decisive: the effect on warning. A defender whose warning picture has been degraded by normalization has lost something of real strategic value regardless of how modest the day-to-day disruption is. That loss is not visible in any incident report and does not show up in any cost tally, which is precisely why it is chronically underweighted.

Applying all four, the defensible verdict is that navigation degradation is currently a serious nuisance with a significant tail risk and one strategically important second-order effect. It is not an attack and should not be described as one, because describing it that way commits an analyst to a response that is neither available nor warranted and cheapens the language needed for events that genuinely qualify. It is also not harmless, and the framing of it as trivial fails on the tail risk and on the warning erosion. The right posture toward it is the one a competent engineer takes toward a slowly worsening structural problem: not panic, not dismissal, but measurement, monitoring, and scheduled remediation.

## The Measurement Problem

A recurring theme of this assessment is that costs which are not counted do not enter decisions. That observation deserves direct treatment, because building the measurement is the single most useful thing a state facing this pressure could do and almost nowhere is it done well.

The difficulty is structural. The costs are distributed across airlines, shipping operators, telecommunications firms, grid operators, emergency services, fishing fleets, and armed forces, each with its own accounting, its own reporting incentives, and its own reasons to categorize a cost as something other than what it is. An airline logs a diversion as a diversion. A shipping company logs an extra hour on the bridge as normal operations. A network operator logs a synchronization fault as maintenance. No one aggregates, because no institution owns the aggregation, and the sum of costs that nobody adds up is functionally zero for political purposes.

The consequence is a systematic bias toward underestimating the pressure. A government asked whether the disruption justifies action looks for a number, finds none, and reasonably concludes that the problem is smaller than the complaints suggest. That conclusion may even be correct, but it is being reached without evidence rather than from it, and the absence of evidence is a feature of the accounting rather than of the phenomenon.

Building a credible measurement would require three things. It would require a common reporting definition so that a degraded approach, a bridge-team workaround, and a synchronization fault could be recorded in comparable terms. It would require an institution mandated to collect across sectors that do not normally report to a common authority. And it would require the political willingness to publish the aggregate, which is not guaranteed, since a published number that turns out to be small undercuts the case for spending while a number that turns out to be large invites the question of why nothing is being done.

That last difficulty is worth dwelling on because it explains the persistent gap between how seriously operators treat this problem and how seriously governments discuss it. Operators experience the costs directly and adapt. Governments face the awkward position of either acknowledging a cost they cannot make stop or declining to acknowledge it and appearing inattentive. Silence is the path of least resistance, and silence sustains the accounting failure that makes the pressure sustainable.

An analyst working on this subject should therefore treat any confident statement about the scale of the disruption, in either direction, with substantial skepticism. The honest position is that the scale is not known, that the reporting is systematically biased toward the best-instrumented sectors, and that the aggregate cost is plausibly larger than the visible portion by a margin nobody can currently quantify. Confirm any specific figures against current sector reporting rather than relying on general accounts, including this one.

## Who Actually Pays

Following the money in this problem produces an answer that explains a great deal about why so little happens, because the parties bearing the cost are almost entirely different from the parties able to act.

Airlines absorb fuel carried against the possibility of diversion, crew hours spent managing anomalies, schedule disruption when approaches become unavailable, and the training investment required to keep procedures current. None of that is recoverable from anyone. It is priced into tickets and into margins, distributed across passengers who have no idea they are paying it. Shipping operators absorb the equivalent in bridge-team time, route caution, and occasional delay, similarly unrecoverable and similarly invisible. Telecommunications and grid operators absorb engineering hours and capital spending on alternative timing sources, which they book as ordinary resilience investment because there is no category for anything else.

Insurance is where the picture becomes analytically interesting. Marine and aviation policies contain exclusions for war, hostile acts, and the actions of states, drafted long before this particular problem existed and never written with it in mind. If a loss occurred in a period of documented degradation, the question of whether the exclusion applies would turn on characterization: an accident caused by equipment behavior is covered, while a loss arising from a hostile act by a state may not be. Since attribution is precisely what cannot be established, the characterization would be contested, and the contest would be expensive and slow. Underwriters facing that ambiguity respond in the way underwriters always do, by pricing uncertainty into premiums for the affected region. That is a cost falling on every operator in the area, generated by the ambiguity itself rather than by any loss, and paid whether or not anything ever happens.

Governments, meanwhile, bear almost none of the direct cost. They bear the diffuse cost of a slightly less safe transport system, the eventual cost of whatever resilience investment they fund, and the political cost of appearing unable to stop the problem. None of these is acute, none arrives on a schedule that forces a decision, and none can be attributed to a budget line that competes visibly with anything else.

The mismatch is the whole explanation for institutional inertia. The parties paying cannot act, since a shipping company has no diplomatic or military options. The parties able to act are not paying, at least not in a form that registers. Between them sits an accounting system that never sums the total. That is a textbook collective-action failure, and recognizing it as one is more useful than attributing the slow response to indifference or incompetence, because collective-action failures have known remedies while indifference has none.

## Reading the Pattern Rather Than the Episode

Analysts new to this subject usually make the same mistake, which is to treat each reported episode as a discrete event to be explained. Episodes are close to uninformative individually. Any single instance of degraded reception admits several explanations, and the effort spent adjudicating between them for one case is effort wasted. Value lies entirely in the pattern, and reading the pattern well requires attention to five dimensions.

Geography is the first. Where degradation is reported, and how that distribution relates to strategic geography, military installations, exercise areas, and commercial routes, carries more information than any individual report. A distribution clustered around locations that a state would plausibly want protected supports the defensive-spillover reading. A distribution that follows commercial corridors and airfields with no obvious protective logic supports the coercive reading. A distribution that shifts over time in ways that track diplomatic or military developments supports the reading that this is an adjustable instrument rather than a fixed condition.

Timing is the second, in two senses. Within a day or a week, does degradation correlate with exercise periods, with specific flight or shipping activity, or with nothing identifiable? Across months and years, does the pattern respond to events, intensifying around periods of tension and relaxing afterward? A phenomenon that tracks the political weather is being adjusted by someone. A phenomenon that is invariant is more likely a standing arrangement whose operators are not thinking about it day to day.

Breadth is the third. Which user categories are affected, and does the set expand? A pattern that has always affected the same categories to the same degree is stable. A pattern that begins reaching categories it previously spared, or reaching further from its apparent source, indicates either an expansion of capability or a decision to apply more of it. Either is significant and both are observable without any technical collection, since the affected communities report their own experience.

Character is the fourth, and it is the most important for judging severity. Degradation that produces loud, obvious failure is one thing. Degradation that produces confident wrong answers is another and considerably more serious, because it defeats the assumption on which every fallback procedure rests, namely that the operator will know when to fall back. A shift in the observed character of episodes from the loud kind to the quiet kind would be the single most significant development this subject could produce, and it should be watched for specifically.

Response is the fifth. How does the affected state react, and how does the pattern respond to that reaction? This is the dimension that tests whether an instrument is being deliberately managed. A pattern that adjusts after a diplomatic protest, an exercise, or a public statement is being steered. A pattern that ignores all of it is either not being steered or is being steered by someone indifferent to the feedback.

An analyst tracking those five dimensions over time will build a far more defensible judgment than one adjudicating individual incidents, and will be considerably harder to mislead by a single dramatic report. The tradecraft here is the ordinary tradecraft of pattern analysis applied to an unusually noisy signal, and its main requirement is patience.

## The Counter-Case: Why This May Be Overstated

Responsible assessment requires taking seriously the argument that this subject receives more attention than it merits, and there is a real case to be made. Four strands of it deserve a fair hearing.

The first strand is that the direct harm remains genuinely small. After a long period of documented degradation across a substantial region affecting many thousands of flights and voyages, the tally of attributable serious consequences is close to empty. Systems designed with redundancy have absorbed the disruption exactly as they were designed to. That is not luck; it is the aviation and maritime safety architecture working as intended. An honest observer should update toward the view that these systems are more robust than the alarmed commentary assumes, because that is what the evidence has been showing over an extended period.

The second strand is that the attention this subject receives is partly a function of its measurability rather than its importance. Degradation is detectable, reportable, and mappable, which makes it an attractive subject for coverage and analysis in a domain where most activity is invisible. There is a well-known bias toward studying what can be observed rather than what matters most, and this subject is a candidate for it. The pressures that genuinely shape outcomes may be the ones that leave no trace and generate no reports.

The third strand is the defensive-necessity argument taken at full strength. A state that expects to be attacked from the air has legitimate reasons to operate systems that degrade navigation-guided weapons, and the fact that such systems affect civil users across a border is a consequence of physics rather than a policy choice. Demanding that a state cease defensive measures because they inconvenience neighbors is a demand that would be rejected by any state receiving it, including those making it. This argument does not excuse everything, but it is not frivolous and it is too often waved away.

The fourth strand is that the framing of this activity as coercion may be doing the presumed coercer's work for it. Pressure of this kind functions partly through the target's own interpretation. A society that treats degraded reception as evidence of siege experiences a cost that a society treating it as an operating condition does not. There is a reasonable argument that the most effective response is public equanimity, and that analysis emphasizing the strategic significance of the phenomenon inflates its value at no cost to whoever generates it.

Against all four, the arguments that survive are the tail risk and the warning erosion. Neither is refuted by the absence of direct harm to date, because both concern low-probability or second-order effects that would not appear in a tally of incidents. The counter-case is right that the day-to-day disruption is modest and probably over-covered. It does not reach the two consequences that make the phenomenon worth sustained attention, and an assessment that accepted the counter-case entirely would be discarding the parts of the problem that actually matter.

## What Would Change This Assessment

Naming in advance what would revise a judgment is the discipline that separates assessment from opinion, and five developments would materially change the conclusions above.

A confirmed causal link between degradation and a serious accident would change everything about the political threshold, regardless of what it changed about the analysis. The direct-harm criterion is doing substantial work in the verdict, and a single well-investigated case in which navigation failure was established as a contributing cause of a fatal event would move this subject from nuisance with tail risk to something requiring a governmental response. Whether that response would be well designed is a separate question, and the pressure to act quickly would not favor good design.

A documented shift from loud failure to quiet failure across a meaningful area would change the severity assessment independently of any accident. Confident wrong answers defeat the layered fallback architecture that currently absorbs the disruption, and a pattern characterized by that failure mode would be a materially more dangerous phenomenon than the one described here.

An expansion into timing-dependent civil infrastructure severe enough to produce visible service failures would change the cost assessment and, more importantly, the political one. Aviation and maritime disruption is experienced by professionals who absorb it. Telecommunications or power disruption is experienced by the public directly, and public experience is what moves political thresholds.

Attribution reaching public certainty, whether through admission, technical demonstration, or an incident that removes doubt, would change the response calculus without changing the legal analysis. The threshold argument would still hold. But the political cost of visible inaction rises steeply once deniability collapses, and governments would face a demand for response that they currently do not face.

A change in the relationship between this and other forms of pressure would change the strategic reading. If degradation began reliably preceding or accompanying other gray-zone activity in a way that suggested coordination, it would cease to be a standalone irritant and become a component of a sequenced campaign, which is a substantially more serious characterization. Watching for that coordination is more valuable than watching the interference alone.

## What This Case Teaches About Below-Threshold Competition

The reason this particular problem repays close study is not that navigation matters more than the other domains under pressure. It is that the case is unusually clean. The tool is simple, the effects are observable, the legal position is uncontested, and the response options are visibly constrained. That clarity makes it a useful laboratory for the general logic of competition below the threshold, and four lessons generalize.

The first is that deniability is worth more than effect. A tool that achieves a great deal and is traceable invites a response that may cost its user more than the effect gained. A tool that achieves little and is untraceable can be applied forever. Over long horizons the second tool delivers more total advantage than the first, and any actor thinking in decades rather than in news cycles will prefer it. This inverts the intuition that stronger tools are better tools, and it explains a repertoire that otherwise looks oddly modest for the effort invested in building it.

The second lesson is that thresholds create shelter as well as protection. A defensive framework anchored to a clear standard gives an adversary a precise specification of what to avoid. The clarity that makes collective defense credible against the acts it covers is the same clarity that maps the space beneath it. This is not an argument for vagueness, since a vague commitment deters nothing and invites the miscalculation that clear commitments prevent. It is an argument for recognizing that any well-drawn line will be approached from below, and for building tools that operate in that space rather than complaining that the line is where it is.

The third lesson concerns accounting. Pressure that produces distributed, unattributed, uncounted costs is pressure that never enters the decision process of the state absorbing it. Whoever applies such pressure is exploiting an accounting failure as much as a legal one, and the remedy is administrative rather than military. Building the capacity to measure what below-threshold competition costs is not a preliminary to responding; it is a substantial part of the response, because it converts an invisible drain into a visible number that can compete for attention and funding against alternatives.

The fourth lesson is about warning. Every persistent below-threshold campaign consumes indicators. Activity that would signal something when rare signals nothing when routine, and the process of normalization that makes a society comfortable with an irritant simultaneously blinds the apparatus that would notice an escalation. A defender that measures the success of gray-zone competition only in costs imposed is missing the larger transfer, which is the steady conversion of signals into noise. Preserving the ability to distinguish the two is a strategic task in its own right, and it is treated across the warning cluster of this series rather than resolved here.

None of this argues that below-threshold pressure is decisive. The limits described earlier apply generally: such tools generate friction rather than outcomes, and a state relying on them exclusively will find after a decade that the fundamentals it wanted to change have not changed. What they do is impose cost, gather information, normalize presence, and degrade warning, continuously and at low risk, which is a meaningful return for a modest investment. Understanding that return honestly, without inflating it into an existential challenge or dismissing it as harassment, is the analytical requirement this domain places on anyone trying to think about it clearly.

## The Two Clocks

Two processes run against each other in this problem, and which one runs faster determines the outcome over any period longer than a few years.

The first clock measures adaptation. Every alternative timing source installed, every crew trained to operate in degraded conditions, every procedure written and exercised, every system re-architected to survive loss of the signal reduces what a given level of pressure achieves. This clock runs slowly, because the investments are distributed across thousands of independent operators, justified against a threat that produces no acute crisis, and always competing against expenditures with clearer returns. But it runs in one direction only. Resilience once built is rarely dismantled, and each increment permanently lowers the value of the instrument.

The second clock measures the pressure itself: its geographic extent, its duration, the breadth of user categories it reaches, and above all whether its character shifts from the loud failure mode toward the quiet one. This clock can run fast, because expanding or altering the pressure requires a decision rather than a decade of distributed investment. It can also run backward, since the condition can be reduced or halted at will by whoever sustains it.

The strategic question is which clock leads. If adaptation outpaces pressure, the instrument is being devalued faster than it is being improved, and a defender that keeps investing will eventually find the tool has become a genuine nuisance and nothing more. If pressure outpaces adaptation, the defender is falling behind on a problem it cannot solve by response and can only solve by expenditure it is chronically reluctant to make.

The honest assessment is that neither clock is clearly winning and that the balance is not being measured. Adaptation is happening, unevenly, driven mostly by the operators who feel the effects rather than by any coordinated program. Pressure appears broadly stable in character, which favors the defender, though the absence of a shared regional picture means that judgment rests on impression rather than data.

That is a reason to build the measurement rather than a reason for comfort. A defender who does not know which clock is leading cannot know whether current resilience spending is sufficient, excessive, or badly behind, and will default to the level of spending that current political attention supports, which in a problem this quiet is close to the minimum. Establishing which clock leads is therefore the most decision-relevant analytical product this subject could generate, and it requires no attribution, no accusation, and no new capability. It requires only that somebody count.

## The Verdict

Navigation degradation across the region around Poland is the clearest available demonstration of what an optimized gray-zone instrument looks like, and its analytical value lies as much in what it teaches about below-threshold competition as in what it costs.

The four properties hold. It is cheap to sustain and expensive to defend against, so the cost ratio favors its user indefinitely. It is close to unattributable in the sense that matters politically, since even confident technical geolocation does not deliver the identity and intent that a public accusation requires. It is genuinely disruptive, imposing distributed costs across aviation, shipping, timing-dependent infrastructure, and the unprotected portions of a military force. And it cannot cross the collective-defense threshold no matter how long it continues, because it produces no damage, no injury, and no death, and no accumulation rule exists to convert quantity into qualification. The ideal-instrument rule is satisfied on all four counts, which is why this particular tool will not be abandoned and why a defender should expect the condition to persist.

The verdict on severity is deliberately unexciting. On direct harm, this is a nuisance: a real one, an expensive one, and one that competent systems have absorbed without serious consequence over a long period. Any characterization of it as an attack is wrong on the law, wrong on the effects, and counterproductive in that it commits the speaker to a response that does not exist. Any characterization of it as harmless is equally wrong, and fails on two grounds that the incident tally does not capture.

Those two grounds carry the assessment. The first is tail risk: small probabilities across enormous exposure over long durations produce events, and the political sequence following an incident that coincided with documented degradation would be driven by inference rather than by established causation, with both parties pushed toward positions neither would have chosen. The second is the erosion of warning, which is the quiet strategic cost of normalization. A defender that has taught itself to treat degraded navigation as background weather has lost the ability to read it as an indicator, and that loss is unrecoverable by any measure short of a distinguishable change in the pattern that an adversary controls.

The response that follows from this analysis is unglamorous and correct. Measure the cost properly, because uncounted costs do not enter decisions and the current accounting systematically understates the total. Build resilience deliberately and early, because it works under every reading of intent, requires no attribution, provokes nothing, and devalues the instrument in the only way available. Watch the pattern rather than the episode, along the five dimensions of geography, timing, breadth, character, and response. Resist both the demand to treat a nuisance as an act of war and the temptation to treat a strategic instrument as static. And hold clearly in mind that the tool exists precisely because it presents a defender with no good moment to react, which means that the absence of a good moment is the design rather than the evidence that nothing is happening.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does gray-zone GPS jamming do across the region around Poland?

It degrades the satellite navigation and timing signals that aviation, shipping, emergency services, telecommunications, and military units in the region rely on, producing loss of position accuracy, procedural workarounds, added workload, and occasional operational restrictions. The effect appears and recedes in episodes rather than running continuously, and it reaches several countries at once because electromagnetic propagation ignores borders. Nothing is destroyed and nobody is directly injured. What accumulates instead is a distributed tax on operations across many user communities, paid mostly in delay, redundancy, engineering hours, and training time. The most consequential effect is not any single disruption but the gradual conversion of a hostile electromagnetic condition into an unremarkable feature of the operating environment.

### Q: Why is navigation jamming an ideal gray-zone tool?

Because it satisfies four requirements simultaneously that almost no other coercive instrument satisfies together. It is cheap to sustain and expensive to defend against, so the cost ratio permanently favors whoever applies it. It is close to unattributable in the political sense, since even a confident technical geolocation does not establish identity or intent. It is genuinely disruptive rather than merely symbolic, imposing real costs across civil and military users. And it structurally cannot cross the armed-attack threshold, because it produces no damage, injury, or death. A tool that maximizes disruption and deniability while holding the probability of provoking a response near zero is the theoretical ideal for below-threshold competition, and this one sits closer to that ideal than sabotage, incursions, or influence work.

### Q: How does gray-zone GPS jamming affect civil aviation near Poland?

Aircraft lose satellite position accuracy and revert to inertial systems, ground-based aids, and controller vectors, all of which crews are trained for and which the safety architecture was designed to accommodate. Flights continue. The costs appear in three places instead: increased flight-deck workload at moments when spare capacity matters most, degraded availability of satellite-based approach procedures at particular airfields, which raises weather minima and produces more diversions, and a small but non-zero increase in the probability of a serious event across a very large number of operations. Aviation reports these effects more thoroughly than any other sector because its safety culture requires documentation, which makes aviation the best-instrumented affected community rather than necessarily the most exposed one.

### Q: Why does gray-zone GPS jamming stay below the response threshold?

Collective defense is built around armed attack, and the mainstream test asks whether effects are equivalent to those of a kinetic strike. Signal degradation produces no physical damage, no injuries, and no fatalities, and affected systems keep functioning using alternatives. It therefore fails the effects test comfortably, and no serious legal argument suggests otherwise on the facts as generally reported. Critically, there is no accumulation rule: nothing converts a sufficient quantity of sub-threshold pressure into a qualifying act, however long it continues or however much it costs in total. That absence is not an oversight to be corrected by better drafting. It follows directly from the deliberate decision to anchor collective defense in armed attack rather than in a general concept of harm.

### Q: Is gray-zone GPS jamming a deliberate pressure or incidental spillover?

Both readings fit the evidence, and honest analysis does not pretend otherwise. The deliberate reading points to the geographic distribution, the persistence over years, and the fact that any operator knows the effects on civil users and continues regardless. The spillover reading points to the legitimate defensive rationale for operating such systems, the episodic rather than continuous pattern, and the awkward fact that the emitter degrades its own aviation and shipping too. The most defensible position is that the distinction may be false. Activity can be defensively motivated in origin and coercively tolerated in continuation, with the costs to neighbors treated as a welcome by-product rather than a purpose. For a defender the ambiguity is the operative fact, since responses must work under both readings.

### Q: How does gray-zone navigation jamming degrade military systems?

Far less than popular accounts suggest and in more specific places. Military receivers carry protections civil equipment lacks, inertial systems provide continuity, alternative navigation methods are maintained, and planning assumes the service may be unavailable in any real conflict. A prepared force degrades rather than stops. The genuine military costs sit elsewhere. Unprotected commercial receivers throughout the logistics tail, contracted transport, and cheaper unmanned systems have none of that hardening, so the vulnerability concentrates in movement and support rather than in combat elements. Persistent degradation also functions as a standing intelligence collection opportunity, since every episode reveals how a defender adapts. And routine degradation erodes the warning value that abnormal degradation would otherwise carry.

### Q: Why is gray-zone GPS jamming hard to attribute?

Attribution requires chaining five inferences and each one loses confidence. Detecting degradation is easy. Geolocating a source requires sustained collection from multiple positions and yields a probability region rather than a point. Characterizing the emission requires technical analysis. Identifying who controls the indicated location requires separate information. Establishing intent requires understanding purpose, which technical collection cannot supply at all. A government needs high confidence before publicly accusing another state, because being wrong in public is costly and lasting. Even a solid chain leaves the harder question untouched, which is what response would follow. Emissions leave nothing to recover, unlike a saboteur who can be arrested or a device that can be examined.

### Q: Does gray-zone GPS jamming near Poland demand a response?

Not in the sense of a forceful or retaliatory one, and treating it as though it did would commit a government to action that is neither available nor warranted. It does demand a response in three other senses. It demands measurement, because costs that no institution aggregates never enter political decisions and the current accounting badly understates the total. It demands resilience investment, which works under every reading of intent, requires no attribution, and provokes nothing. And it demands sustained pattern analysis, because the strategically important developments would be changes in character or breadth rather than the present level. The correct posture resembles that of an engineer toward a slowly worsening structural problem: measure, monitor, remediate on a schedule.

### Q: How does gray-zone jamming normalize a hostile presence deniably?

Through repetition that converts anomaly into background. Early episodes are investigated and discussed. As they recur, procedures get written, crews get briefed, advisory notices become routine publications, and analysts stop examining incidents and start describing conditions. Eventually the phenomenon is mentioned in briefings the way weather is mentioned. Three things follow. Hostile electromagnetic presence becomes unremarkable, so the emitter operates without generating friction. The defender's warning apparatus loses a category of signal, because something that happens constantly cannot indicate anything by happening. And the political system learns that this class of pressure produces no reply, which shapes expectations about the next class. Normalization also produces genuine adaptation, so the net balance is contested.

### Q: What civil and military users does gray-zone GPS jamming disrupt?

Eight categories, with severity and visibility that vary sharply. Aviation en route and on approach experiences the most documented disruption. Merchant shipping in confined waters experiences less reported but arguably greater exposure. Small craft and fishing vessels have the fewest fallbacks and report almost never. Emergency and rescue services suffer conditionally, when degradation coincides with an incident. Unprotected military logistics and support systems are affected while hardened combat systems largely are not. Timing-dependent infrastructure, including telecommunications, power coordination, and transaction systems, suffers delayed and obscure effects that usually surface as ordinary technical faults. And public confidence erodes cumulatively. Reporting is heaviest where instrumentation is best, which systematically under-represents the least protected users.

### Q: What separates gray-zone navigation spoofing from simple jamming?

The failure mode, and the difference is the most important technical fact in this subject. Jamming overwhelms a receiver with noise so it loses lock, reports degraded accuracy, and warns the operator. That is loud failure: disruptive but honest, and it triggers the fallback procedures that every well-designed system maintains. Spoofing substitutes a plausible false signal, so the receiver computes a wrong position and reports it with normal confidence indicators and no warning at all. That is quiet failure, and it defeats the assumption on which every fallback rests, namely that the operator will know when to stop trusting the display. A documented shift in the regional pattern from the loud mode toward the quiet one would be the single most significant development this problem could produce.

### Q: Can gray-zone navigation interference near Poland cause an accident?

Directly and by itself, it is very unlikely, and no serious accident on the open record has been attributed to it despite a long period of documented degradation across heavily trafficked airspace and waters. Layered redundancy in aviation and maritime safety has absorbed the disruption as designed. The honest concern is probabilistic rather than direct: degradation thins the margin available to handle the next problem, and thinner margins across enormous exposure over long durations produce events eventually. The greater danger is political rather than physical. An incident coinciding with documented degradation would generate a demand for response driven by inference, since causation would be impossible to establish quickly, and both parties could be pushed toward positions neither selected.

### Q: Does gray-zone navigation interference precede a larger operation?

Not reliably, and treating it as a leading indicator would generate constant false alarms, since the condition persists through long periods in which nothing else happens. The relationship runs the other way and is more troubling. In any real conflict, navigation denial would be immediate and comprehensive from the first hours, combined with every other form of electronic disruption available. Persistent peacetime degradation is therefore a preview of the wartime environment at low intensity, and by making that environment routine it removes the contrast that would otherwise make an abrupt intensification meaningful. What deserves watching is not the disruption alone but whether it begins reliably coordinating with other below-threshold activity, which would indicate a sequenced campaign rather than a standing condition.

### Q: Why does gray-zone interference reward patience over retaliation?

Because the instrument is engineered so that certainty never arrives while frustration accumulates, which makes a premature reaction the most likely defender error and the most useful one for whoever applies the pressure. A response based on wrong attribution, or on treating an incidental effect as deliberate, hands the other party a genuine grievance and a public case. Retaliation also requires selecting something proportionate to a harm that is real but diffuse and unquantified, which is a problem with no good solution. Resilience, by contrast, works under every reading of intent, needs no attribution, provokes nothing, and devalues the tool by reducing what a given level of pressure achieves. Devaluation is the appropriate counter to an instrument built to make retaliation impossible.

### Q: How are gray-zone interference reports verified as genuine?

Through convergence rather than any single confirmation. Individual reports are weak evidence, since equipment faults, local conditions, and operator error all produce similar symptoms. Confidence comes from multiple independent users in the same area reporting comparable effects in the same period, from aviation and maritime reporting systems that require documentation, and from technical monitoring that observes reception quality directly. Even strong convergence establishes only that degradation occurred, not its source or its purpose. Analysts should also correct for a systematic bias: heavily instrumented sectors report thoroughly while fishing vessels, small craft, and timing-dependent infrastructure report rarely or not at all, so any dataset understates exactly the users with the fewest alternatives.
