Two families of problems dominate the applications of derivatives on the AP Calculus AB exam, and both begin life as paragraphs of ordinary English rather than as equations. Related rates problems describe a physical scene in motion, a ladder sliding, a tank filling, a shadow lengthening, and ask how fast some quantity is changing at a particular instant. Optimization problems describe a situation with a goal, a fence to build, a box to fold, a can to manufacture, and ask for the dimensions or the decision that produces the largest or smallest possible value of something. Students often experience these as the hardest questions on the exam, yet the calculus inside them is usually brief. The difficulty lives in the translation, in turning a story about the world into a relationship between variables, and that translation is a learnable, repeatable skill.

This guide treats the two families as siblings. Both reward the same discipline: name every quantity, decide which quantities are variables and which are fixed, write one governing equation, and only then reach for a derivative. A related rates problem differentiates a relationship with respect to time. An optimization problem reduces a relationship to one variable and then differentiates with respect to that variable. Everything else, the chain rule mechanics, the algebra, the arithmetic, is supporting work. Readers who want the broader map of the course, including limits, derivative rules, and integrals, will find it in the [complete guide to AP Calculus AB](/2008/08/10/ap-calculus-ab-complete-guide/), which frames where these applications sit inside the full syllabus.

Worked examples carry much of the teaching here, because setup skill grows from watching setups happen. Each classic scenario below is solved slowly, with the modeling decisions stated out loud before any symbol is differentiated. The guide also names a single study tool, the InsightCrunch applications template, a side-by-side solving frame that places the related rates routine next to the optimization routine so the shared skeleton becomes visible. By the end, the aim is simple to state: given a fresh word problem, a reader should know exactly which first three moves to make, and should make them before touching a derivative.

## Two Problem Families, One Habit of Mind

Related rates and optimization look unrelated at first glance. One involves clocks and motion, the other involves design and extremes. Underneath, both are exercises in building a function or an equation that faithfully represents a situation, then asking the derivative a question about that representation. In a related rates problem, the question is dynamic: if this quantity changes at a known rate, how fast must that quantity change to keep the relationship true? In an optimization problem, the question is comparative: among all configurations the situation allows, which one makes the target quantity largest or smallest? The derivative answers both questions because the derivative is precisely a machine for describing how one quantity responds when another changes.

The shared skeleton explains a shared failure mode. Students who struggle with these problems rarely struggle with differentiation itself. Hand them an equation and they can differentiate it. Hand them a paragraph and they stall, because the paragraph does not announce which equation to write, which quantities deserve letters, or which numbers in the story are constants and which are snapshots of a moving variable. The stall happens before calculus begins. Graders know this, and the rubrics for free-response questions reflect it: a large share of the points attaches to the setup, to the correct governing equation, the correct substitution timing, and the correct interpretation at the end. A flawless differentiation of the wrong equation earns almost nothing.

Working in the opposite order fixes most of the damage. The productive habit is to model first and compute second, treating the derivative as a late step rather than an opening move. Modeling has its own sub-skills. A solver must read the problem twice, once for the story and once for the quantities. A solver must draw the scene, because geometry hides in most of these problems and a labeled diagram exposes relationships the prose conceals. A solver must separate what is known permanently from what is known only at one instant, since confusing those two categories is the single most common technical error in related rates. None of these moves requires calculus. All of them decide whether the calculus that follows will be worth anything.

Optimization adds one more modeling demand: every optimization problem contains two equations wearing one disguise. There is an objective, the quantity to be maximized or minimized, and there is a constraint, the condition that limits the choices. Area is the objective and the fencing budget is the constraint; volume is fixed as a constraint and surface area is the objective. Students who try to differentiate the objective while it still contains two variables are attempting something impossible, and the attempt usually signals that the constraint was never written down. The constraint is not decoration. The constraint is the tool that converts a two-variable wish into a one-variable function that calculus can actually handle.

A useful way to hold both families in mind is to think of each problem as a small contract. The problem promises that certain relationships stay true throughout the scene, the ladder keeps its length, the cone keeps its proportions, the perimeter stays spent. The solver's job is to write the contract in symbols, then ask what the contract forces to happen. Related rates asks what the contract forces about speeds. Optimization asks what the contract permits at its best. Seen this way, the two families are one subject, the study of relationships under change, and the rest of this guide develops that subject in its two exam costumes.

## The Language of Change: Variables, Rates, and Units

Every related rates problem speaks a small dialect, and fluency in that dialect prevents half the errors students make. A rate is a derivative with respect to time, whether or not the problem uses the word derivative at all. When a problem says a balloon is being inflated at 100 cubic centimeters per second, it is handing over dV/dt, the rate of change of volume with respect to time, and the positive sign records that volume is growing. When a problem says water is draining, the corresponding rate will carry a negative sign or will earn one during interpretation. The phrase "how fast" always signals a rate, and the phrase "at the instant when" always signals that some variable has a known snapshot value to be substituted near the end of the work.

Variables deserve a formal introduction in every solution. A strong opening line for any related rates solution names the players: let x be the distance from the wall, let y be the height of the ladder's top, let t be time in seconds. This naming is not ceremony. Unnamed quantities drift, and mid-solution a solver who never declared variables will confuse the rate of the thing changing with the thing itself. Declaring variables also forces a key decision: which letters stand for quantities that genuinely vary, and which numbers in the problem are constants that will never need a derivative. The length of the ladder never changes, so it stays a number. The radius of a cone's water surface changes constantly, so it keeps its letter until similar triangles tie it to the depth.

Units are the quiet grammar of the whole subject. A rate carries compound units, feet per second, cubic meters per minute, radians per second, and those units behave like algebraic fractions that must cancel consistently. If a volume is measured in cubic feet and time in minutes, the answer emerges in cubic feet per minute, and a solver who tracks units can catch setup errors before they metastasize. An answer in square feet per second for a question about a length changing is a confession that somewhere an area was differentiated instead of a length. Exam questions are written so that units are consistent within each problem, and a solver who writes units beside every rate gains a free proofreading pass at the end.

Angles introduce the one unit convention students forget under pressure: calculus with trigonometric functions assumes radians. Any rate of change of an angle on this exam is measured in radians per unit time, and any derivative of a sine or cosine function presumes radian measure. A problem that reports an angle in degrees expects conversion before its rate is used, and a final answer for an angle's rate should be stated in radians per second or radians per minute. This convention comes from the limit that makes the derivative of sine equal cosine, a fact developed alongside the basic derivative rules in the [companion treatment of limits, derivatives, and integrals](/2008/08/05/ap-calculus-ab-limits-derivatives-integrals/), and it applies with full force in every rotating-ladder or sweeping-searchlight problem.

## Related Rates: The Modeling Sequence

A reliable sequence exists for related rates, and it has five beats. First, draw and label the scene, marking each varying quantity with a letter and each constant with its number. Second, list the rates: write down which derivative is given, with its sign and units, and which derivative is being asked for. Third, find a geometric or physical relationship connecting the variables, an equation that is true at every instant of the scene, not merely at the snapshot instant. Fourth, differentiate that relationship with respect to time, which means applying the chain rule to every variable in it, since every variable is secretly a function of time. Fifth, and only fifth, substitute the snapshot values and solve for the unknown rate.

The ordering of the last two beats is the technical heart of the method. Snapshot values must never be substituted before differentiation. If a solver replaces a variable with its instant value early, the variable becomes a constant, its derivative becomes zero, and the resulting equation is nonsense dressed as algebra. The equation must be differentiated while every varying quantity is still a letter, so that each one contributes its chain rule factor, dx/dt or dy/dt or dr/dt. After differentiation, the equation contains the rates, and the snapshot values, the known rates, and the geometry of the instant can all enter at once. This single rule, differentiate first and substitute second, is worth more points across a school year than any other sentence about related rates.

### Why does the snapshot value wait until after differentiation?

Because a quantity known at one instant is still a variable across the whole scene, and differentiation describes that whole scene. Replacing it early freezes the scene, turning a changing length into a constant whose derivative is zero. Substituting after differentiation preserves every chain rule factor, letting the instant values pin down the unknown rate.

### What does a negative answer mean in a related rates problem?

A negative rate means the quantity is decreasing, and the sign is information rather than error. If dy/dt comes out negative for the height of a ladder's top, the algebra reports that the top moves downward as time increases, exactly matching the scene. State the interpretation in words: the height is decreasing at that many units per second.

Consider the sequence in its simplest possible setting before the famous scenarios arrive. A circle's radius grows at 2 centimeters per minute, and the question asks how fast the area grows when the radius is 5 centimeters. The relationship is A = πr², true at every instant. Differentiating with respect to time gives dA/dt = 2πr dr/dt, with the chain rule factor dr/dt appearing because r depends on t. Substituting r = 5 and dr/dt = 2 yields dA/dt = 20π, about 62.8 square centimeters per minute. Nothing in that computation is hard, and that is the point. The method, not the arithmetic, is the skill, and the method never changes no matter how elaborate the scene becomes. Every problem in the next several sections is this circle problem wearing a costume: a relationship, a differentiation with respect to time, a substitution, an interpretation.

## Worked Example: The Sliding Ladder

A ladder 10 feet long leans against a vertical wall. The base is pulled away from the wall at 1 foot per second. How fast is the top sliding down when the base stands 6 feet from the wall? The scene is a right triangle that flattens over time, and the Pythagorean theorem is the contract the triangle must honor: if x is the distance from the wall to the base and y is the height of the top, then x² + y² = 100 at every instant, because the hypotenuse is the ladder itself and the ladder does not stretch. The given rate is dx/dt = 1, positive because x grows. The wanted rate is dy/dt, evaluated at the instant x = 6.

Differentiation comes next, and it comes before any substitution of the number 6. Differentiating x² + y² = 100 with respect to time gives 2x dx/dt + 2y dy/dt = 0, each variable contributing its chain rule factor because each is a function of time. Only now does the snapshot enter. When x = 6, the geometry of the instant gives y from the same theorem: y² = 100 - 36 = 64, so y = 8. Substituting x = 6, y = 8, and dx/dt = 1 into the differentiated equation gives 12 + 16 dy/dt = 0, hence dy/dt = -12/16 = -0.75. The top of the ladder slides down at 0.75 feet per second, the negative sign certifying that y decreases. Notice the division of labor: the theorem supplied both the relationship and the snapshot value of y, and the problem never needed the time at which the scene occurs.

Small variations of this scenario repay close study, because examiners rotate them. Ask instead how fast the angle between the ladder and the ground changes at the same instant. Now the relationship is cos θ = x/10, with θ the angle at the base. Differentiating gives -sin θ · dθ/dt = (1/10) dx/dt. At the instant in question sin θ = 8/10, so dθ/dt = -1/(10 · 0.8) = -0.125 radians per second, the angle shrinking as the ladder flattens. Ask instead how fast the triangle's area changes: A = (1/2)xy differentiates to dA/dt = (1/2)(x dy/dt + y dx/dt) = (1/2)(6(-0.75) + 8(1)) = (1/2)(3.5) = 1.75 square feet per second, still growing because the base's gain outweighs the height's loss at this instant. One scene, three relationships, three rates, and the modeling sequence handles all of them without modification.

The ladder also teaches a physical intuition worth keeping. Push the base farther out, to x = 8 with y = 6, and the same computation gives dy/dt = -8/6, about -1.33 feet per second: the top falls faster as the ladder flattens, even though the base moves at the same steady speed. Push the reasoning to the edge and the relationship explains itself: as y shrinks toward zero, the factor x/y in dy/dt = -(x/y) dx/dt explodes, which matches the real-world violence of a ladder's final collapse. Rates in these problems are not constants. They are instant values of a changing balance, and the snapshot substitution is what makes a general equation answer a specific moment.

## Worked Example: The Filling Cone and Similar Triangles

A conical tank, vertex pointing down, stands 10 feet tall with a top radius of 4 feet. Water is pumped in at 2 cubic feet per minute. At what rate is the water level rising when the depth is 5 feet? The volume formula for a cone, V = (1/3)πr²h, contains two variables that change together, the radius r of the water's surface and the depth h, and differentiating it immediately would drag in dr/dt, a rate nobody gave and nobody wants. The rescue is the cone's fixed shape. At any depth, the water forms a smaller cone similar to the tank, so r/h = 4/10 and r = 0.4h at every instant. That proportion, available from a pair of similar triangles in the diagram, is the most frequently needed move in the whole related rates family.

Substituting r = 0.4h into the volume formula collapses the problem to one variable: V = (1/3)π(0.16h²)h = (0.16π/3)h³. Differentiating with respect to time gives dV/dt = 0.16πh² dh/dt. Now the snapshot and the given rate can enter: with dV/dt = 2 and h = 5, the equation reads 2 = 0.16π(25) dh/dt = 4π dh/dt, so dh/dt = 1/(2π), about 0.159 feet per minute. The depth rises slowly, and the smallness makes sense: at a depth of 5 feet the water's surface is wide, so each cubic foot spreads thin. Near the vertex the same inflow would raise the level far faster, which is why the rate depends on the instant rather than on the inflow alone.

The cone pattern generalizes into a recognition skill. Whenever a container has straight sides meeting at a point, or a shadow stretches from a lamp, or a trough has a triangular cross-section, some length in the scene is proportional to another length, and finding that proportion is the true first step. A trough 10 feet long with an isosceles triangle cross-section, 3 feet across the top and 2 feet deep, holds water whose volume is V = 7.5h² when the depth is h, because the surface width is (3/2)h and the cross-section area is half the product of width and depth. If such a trough fills at 2 cubic feet per minute, then 2 = 15h dh/dt, and at a depth of 1 foot the level rises at 2/15, about 0.133 feet per minute. Different container, same skeleton: proportion first, volume second, derivative third, snapshot last.

Students sometimes ask why the radius could not simply be measured at the snapshot and treated as a constant. The cone punishes that shortcut visibly. Treating r as fixed at its snapshot value of 2 feet and differentiating V = (1/3)πr²h with respect to h alone produces dV/dt = (4π/3) dh/dt and an answer of 3/(2π), roughly triple the correct rate, because it pretends the surface never widens. The error is not arithmetic; it is a modeling lie, a frozen scene. The chain rule exists precisely to charge every changing quantity its share of the change, and similar triangles exist to express those quantities in a single currency before the charging begins.

## Worked Example: Spheres, Ripples, and Rates Inside Rates

Air is pumped into a spherical balloon at 100 cubic centimeters per second. At what rate is the radius growing when the radius is 10 centimeters, and how fast is the surface area growing at that same instant? The volume of a sphere, V = (4/3)πr³, differentiates with respect to time into dV/dt = 4πr² dr/dt, a formula whose right side contains the surface area itself, which is no coincidence: a thin shell of new volume is approximately the surface area times the shell's thickness. Substituting dV/dt = 100 and r = 10 gives 100 = 400π dr/dt, so dr/dt = 1/(4π), about 0.0796 centimeters per second. The surface area, S = 4πr², differentiates into dS/dt = 8πr dr/dt, and feeding in the radius rate just found gives dS/dt = 8π(10)(1/(4π)) = 2 square centimeters per second exactly. Chained questions like this, where the answer to one part becomes the input to the next, are a favorite free-response architecture, and they reward keeping exact forms, the 1/(4π), until the final line.

The balloon carries a second lesson about how rates behave as scenes evolve. Early, when the radius is small, the same inflow stretches the radius quickly; later the inflow spreads over a vast surface and the radius creeps. A solver can see this in the formula dr/dt = 100/(4πr²), which shrinks as r grows. Related rates answers should always survive this kind of plausibility interrogation. If a computed rate says a filling tank's level rises faster when the tank is nearly full and wide, or a shadow shortens while its owner walks away from the lamp, something upstream is wrong, and the most likely suspects are a sign, a swapped variable, or an inverted proportion.

## Worked Example: The Lengthening Shadow and the Two Cars

A person 6 feet tall walks away from a lamppost 15 feet tall at 5 feet per second. How fast is the shadow lengthening? The diagram holds two similar right triangles, the lamp's large triangle and the person's small one, sharing the shadow's tip as a common vertex. Let x be the distance from the post to the person and s the length of the shadow. Matching ratios gives s/(s + x) = 6/15, which simplifies through 15s = 6s + 6x to s = (2/3)x. Differentiation then gives ds/dt = (2/3) dx/dt = 10/3, about 3.33 feet per second. A natural follow-up asks about the shadow's tip, whose position is x + s: the tip moves at dx/dt + ds/dt = 5 + 10/3 = 25/3, about 8.33 feet per second, faster than the walker, as anyone who has watched a sunset shadow race across a field already knows. The similar triangles did all the real work; the derivative at the end was almost an afterthought, which is exactly the balance this problem family aims for.

Distance problems form the other great clan. Two cars leave an intersection, one heading north at 30 miles per hour, the other east at 40 miles per hour. How fast is the distance between them growing after two hours? The relationship is again Pythagorean, z² = x² + y², differentiating to 2z dz/dt = 2x dx/dt + 2y dy/dt. After two hours the cars sit at x = 80 and y = 60, so z = 100, and dz/dt = (80·40 + 60·30)/100 = 5000/100 = 50 miles per hour. The 3-4-5 triangle scaled by twenty makes the arithmetic graceful, but the deeper point is that dz/dt here is constant, since both speeds are constant and the geometry is self-similar. Not every related rate varies with the instant; part of mastery is noticing when it does not, and the ladder, where it emphatically does, is the proper contrast.

Rotating and approaching variants round out the standard repertoire. A plane flies at a constant altitude of 3 miles toward a point directly over an observer, traveling at 400 miles per hour. Set x equal to the remaining horizontal distance to that overhead point, with θ the angle of elevation from the observer, so tan θ = 3/x. Differentiating gives sec²θ · dθ/dt = (-3/x²) dx/dt. When x = 4, the geometry supplies tan θ = 3/4 and therefore sec²θ = 1 + 9/16 = 25/16, while dx/dt = -400 because the remaining distance is shrinking. Substitution gives (25/16) dθ/dt = (-3/16)(-400) = 75, so dθ/dt = 48 radians per hour. The angle of elevation grows ever faster as the plane nears the vertical, a result anyone who has tracked a jet across the zenith has felt in their neck. Sign decisions in angle problems hinge on how variables are defined: x here measures distance remaining, so its rate is negative, and a solver who defines x as distance already covered must rebuild the relationship rather than borrow the signs. A clean diagram with arrows settles what prose leaves ambiguous.

## The InsightCrunch Applications Template

Study tools work best when they compress a method into a shape the eye can hold. The InsightCrunch applications template does that for this unit by placing the two solving routines side by side, stage by stage, so a student can see that a related rates problem and an optimization problem are the same animal at every stage except two: the source of the governing equation and the question asked at the end. In related rates, the equation comes from geometry or physics and the final question is about a rate at an instant. In optimization, the equation comes from an objective paired with a constraint, and the final question is about an extreme value and the configuration that produces it.

| Stage | Related Rates | Optimization |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opening move | Draw the scene; name every varying quantity and fix every constant | Name the objective quantity and the quantity it depends on |
| Given | At least one rate, plus snapshot values for one instant | A constraint: a fixed total, budget, perimeter, volume, or material |
| Governing equation | A geometric or physical law true at every instant: Pythagoras, similar triangles, a volume formula | The objective as written, then rewritten with the constraint until it holds one variable |
| Calculus move | Differentiate with respect to time, chain rule on every variable | Differentiate with respect to the single variable; locate critical points |
| Substitution timing | Snapshot values enter only after differentiation | Candidate values enter the objective after critical points and endpoints are known |
| Decisive test | Sign and units of the computed rate, checked against the scene | First derivative test, second derivative test, or candidates test to certify the extreme |
| Answer form | A rate with units and a direction: rising, shrinking, approaching | A dimension or decision plus the extreme value, stated in the problem's terms |

Reading the template vertically teaches each routine. Reading it horizontally teaches the transfer between them. The opening moves rhyme, because both begin by refusing to compute. The governing equations rhyme, because both must be true of the whole situation rather than of one moment or one candidate design. The calculus moves differ in costume only: differentiating with respect to time and differentiating with respect to a design variable are the same chain rule wearing different letters. Even the final stages rhyme, since both end with a judgment call, an interpretation that translates symbols back into the world the problem described. A student who internalizes the template stops asking "which kind of problem is this" as a trivia question and starts asking it as a routing question, which column of the template governs the next move.

The template also exposes where each family hides its trap. Related rates hides its trap in the substitution row: the snapshot beckons early, and yielding early is fatal. Optimization hides its trap one row higher, in the governing equation: the constraint beckons to be forgotten, and forgetting it leaves an objective with two variables and no legal derivative. Naming the traps by row turns vague anxiety into a checklist. Before differentiating, a related rates solver checks the substitution row and confirms every variable is still a letter. Before differentiating, an optimization solver checks the equation row and confirms exactly one variable remains. Two glances, two disasters avoided.

## Optimization: From Words to a Single Variable

An optimization problem presents a wish and a limit. The wish is an objective function: maximize the area, minimize the surface, maximize the profit, minimize the distance. The limit is a constraint: only so much fence exists, the sheet of cardboard has fixed dimensions, the volume is prescribed, the point must lie on a given curve. The standard solution architecture has five beats that mirror the related rates sequence. Name the variables with a diagram. Write the objective in whatever variables it naturally needs. Write the constraint as an equation. Use the constraint to eliminate all but one variable from the objective. Differentiate the resulting single-variable function, find its critical points, and then prove, with a test rather than a hope, that the leading candidate truly delivers the extreme the problem wants.

The elimination beat deserves emphasis because it is where optimization problems are actually solved. An objective like A = xy is not yet a function calculus can optimize, since it has two independent inputs and no unique rate of change. The constraint, say 2x + 2y = 100 for a fenced rectangle, converts y into an expression in x, y = 50 - x, and the objective becomes A(x) = x(50 - x) = 50x - x², a genuine one-variable function with a derivative, critical points, and a verdict. Every optimization solution in this guide performs that conversion, and every failed student solution this author has ever graded skipped it or performed it halfway. The constraint is not a footnote to the problem; it is the problem itself, wearing work clothes.

### What is the difference between a constraint and an objective?

The objective is the quantity the problem wants pushed to an extreme, the area to maximize or the cost to minimize. The constraint is the condition limiting the choices, the fixed perimeter, volume, or budget every candidate must respect. Optimization works because the constraint ties the variables together, collapsing the objective to a single variable that calculus can test.

Domain awareness rides along with the elimination beat. The single-variable objective only makes sense on the inputs the scene physically allows: lengths are positive, a cut cannot exceed half the sheet, a radius cannot exceed the tank. Writing the domain beside the objective, even informally, prevents two embarrassments. It prevents reporting a critical point that geometry forbids, and it defines the endpoints that the candidates test will later need on a closed interval. Many optimization problems live on open intervals in disguise, where the endpoints correspond to degenerate designs, a box with no height, a fence with no width. There the extreme, if the problem is honest, sits at an interior critical point, and the derivative tests carry the full burden of proof. Knowing which situation one is in, closed interval with real endpoints or open interval with degenerate ones, is part of reading the problem rather than solving it.

## Worked Example: The Open Box

A square sheet of cardboard, 12 inches on a side, becomes an open box by cutting identical squares from the four corners and folding up the sides. How large should the cut be to maximize the volume? Let x be the side of each cut square, in inches. Folding converts the sheet's dimensions: the box's height is x, and its base is a square of side 12 - 2x, since each side loses a cut at both ends. The objective is V(x) = x(12 - 2x)², and the physical domain is 0 < x < 6, since a cut of 6 inches would consume the sheet entirely. This problem's constraint is woven into the geometry rather than stated as a budget, which is typical of folding problems: the fixed sheet is the limit, and the base side's expression, 12 - 2x, is the constraint doing its work silently.

Differentiation uses the product and chain rules together: V′(x) = (12 - 2x)² + x · 2(12 - 2x)(-2), and factoring out (12 - 2x) tidies it into V′(x) = (12 - 2x)(12 - 6x). Setting the derivative to zero gives x = 6 or x = 2. The first root is the degenerate endpoint in disguise, a box of zero base and no volume; the second is the candidate. The first derivative test certifies it: V′ is positive just left of 2, as the test value x = 1 gives V′(1) = 10 · 6 = 60, and negative just right of 2, as x = 3 gives V′(3) = 6 · (-6) = -36. Volume rises, then falls, so x = 2 gives the maximum. The answer completes itself in the problem's own terms: cut squares of 2 inches, producing a box 8 inches by 8 inches by 2 inches with volume 128 cubic inches.

Two habits in this solution generalize. The first is factoring before solving: derivative equations in optimization are built to factor, and a solver who expands everything into a cubic and then wrestles it has chosen the hard road. The second is reading critical points against the domain and the scene. A root that flattens the design into nothing is mathematics reporting a boundary, not a candidate, and recognizing that saves the embarrassment of recommending a box with no inside. Exam versions of this problem change the sheet's proportions, use a rectangle instead of a square, or add a lid, and each change alters the objective's expression while leaving the architecture untouched: geometry into objective, domain into view, derivative into factors, test into verdict, verdict back into dimensions.

## Worked Example: The Fence, the Barn, and the Constraint That Bites

A farmer has 1000 feet of fencing and wants the largest possible rectangular pen, using the long side of an existing barn as one side of the rectangle so fencing is needed on only three sides. Let x be the length of each side perpendicular to the barn, in feet; the side parallel to the barn then measures 1000 - 2x, since two fence runs of length x consume part of the budget. The objective is A(x) = x(1000 - 2x) = 1000x - 2x², on the domain 0 < x < 500. The derivative A′(x) = 1000 - 4x vanishes at x = 250, and the second derivative, A″(x) = -4, is negative everywhere, so the parabola opens downward and the critical point is the maximum without further ceremony. The optimal pen has perpendicular sides of 250 feet, a parallel side of 500 feet, and an area of 125,000 square feet.

Variations on this problem test whether a solver reads constraints or merely memorizes answers. Fence all four sides with the same 1000 feet and the optimum becomes the square, 250 feet on a side, area 62,500 square feet, exactly half the barn-assisted result, because the barn contributed a free side and the budget spread further. Divide the pen with an interior fence parallel to the perpendicular sides and the constraint changes to 3x plus the parallel side equaling 1000, shifting the optimum again. Require the pen to sit in a corner with two existing walls and only two fenced sides, and the constraint stretches further still. Students who solved one version by genuine modeling handle all versions; students who memorized "250" are stranded by the first twist. This is precisely why the exam rotates the costume: the skill being measured is constraint extraction, and costumes are how the exam checks that the skill, rather than a remembered number, is present.

A subtler cousin replaces the fencing budget with a fixed area and asks for the least fencing, flipping objective and constraint. Given 125,000 square feet to enclose against a barn, minimize F(x) = 2x + 125,000/x, where x is again the perpendicular side and the parallel side is the area divided by x. The derivative F′(x) = 2 - 125,000/x² vanishes when x² = 62,500, so x = 250 and the parallel side is 500 feet, the same optimal shape seen from the other direction. The symmetry is not a coincidence; maximizing area for a fixed perimeter and minimizing perimeter for a fixed area are dual problems with the same answer, and noticing the duality is the kind of insight that makes the second problem on an exam feel free.

## Worked Example: The Efficient Can

A cylindrical can must hold 1000 cubic centimeters. Which dimensions use the least material? Let r be the radius and h the height, in centimeters. Here the prescribed volume serves as the constraint, πr²h = 1000, which gives h = 1000/(πr²). The objective is the total surface area, counting both circular ends and the curved wall: S = 2πr² + 2πrh. Eliminating h yields S(r) = 2πr² + 2000/r, a sum of a rising quadratic term and a falling inverse term, and the minimum will sit where the two tendencies balance. The derivative is S′(r) = 4πr - 2000/r². Setting it to zero gives r³ = 500/π ≈ 159.15, so r ≈ 5.42 centimeters. The second derivative, S″(r) = 4π + 4000/r³, is positive for every positive radius, so this critical point is a minimum, and the height is h = 1000/(πr²) = 2r ≈ 10.84 centimeters. The minimum surface area is about 554 square centimeters.

The elegant residue of this computation is the proportion h = 2r: for the most efficient closed can, the height equals the diameter. That proportion is independent of the prescribed volume, a fact a solver can prove by keeping V symbolic through the same derivation, and it explains the squat, cube-hugging proportions of real one-liter cans on a shelf. Real cans deviate from the ideal for reasons outside the model: tops and bottoms are cut from sheet with waste, seams cost material, shelves and hands prefer certain shapes. An optimization answer is always relative to its model, and a strong solution says so. On the exam, the model is the whole world, and within it the answer is exact. In the world outside, the model is a first approximation, and knowing the difference is part of interpreting results like a grown-up rather than a calculator.

The can problem also showcases the domain's quiet role. As r approaches zero, the term 2000/r explodes, because a needle-thin can needs an enormous wall to hold its liter. As r grows huge, the term 2πr² explodes, because a pancake can wastes material on its vast ends. Somewhere between the two explosions the surface area bottoms out, and the derivative merely locates the bottom that the limiting behavior already guaranteed. Checking end behavior like this, even informally, is a powerful sanity habit: if the objective dives at some interior point rather than dipping, or if no interior minimum can exist, the derivative's verdict will look strange, and the strangeness will be visible early to a solver who sketched the tendencies first.

## Critical Points and the Two Derivative Tests

A critical point of a function is an interior point of its domain where the derivative is zero or fails to exist. Both species matter. Points where the derivative vanishes are the familiar flat spots, the tops of hills and bottoms of valleys where optimization answers usually live. Points where the derivative does not exist, a corner, a cusp, a vertical tangent, can also host extremes, as the absolute value function demonstrates at its vertex. In applied optimization, the variables usually live inside smooth formulas, polynomials, roots, and rational expressions, so vanishing derivatives do most of the work. Still, a solver who never asks where the derivative might fail to exist will eventually lose an extreme hiding at a kink, particularly in problems built from distances, where square roots produce exactly such points.

Finding a critical point is only an arrest; a test must still convict it of being a maximum or a minimum. The first derivative test reads the sign of the derivative on either side. If the derivative changes from positive to negative as x passes through the critical point, the function rose and then fell, and the point is a local maximum. If the sign changes from negative to positive, the point is a local minimum. If the sign does not change, the point is neither, a terrace where the function pauses and continues in the same direction. The test is conclusive whenever it applies, because it reports the function's actual behavior rather than a proxy for it. Its cost is labor: signs must be sampled or reasoned on both sides, usually with test values or with a factored derivative whose factors announce their own signs.

### How can a solver tell whether a critical point gives a maximum or a minimum?

Watch the derivative rather than the function. If the derivative changes sign from positive to negative at the point, the function climbed and then fell, so the point is a local maximum. A change from negative to positive marks a local minimum. No sign change means neither, whatever the point's height suggests at a glance.

The second derivative test trades that labor for information about curvature. If the derivative vanishes at a point and the second derivative is positive there, the graph is concave up at the point, cupping upward, and the point is a local minimum. If the second derivative is negative there, the graph is concave down, and the point is a local maximum. The test is quick when the second derivative is easy to compute and easy to evaluate, as it was for the fence problem, where a constant negative second derivative settled everything at once. Applied problems often arrange exactly this kindness, since objectives built from a downward parabola or from a sum of a quadratic and an inverse term have friendly second derivatives.

### When is the second derivative test inconclusive?

The test fails to speak when the second derivative equals zero at the critical point, or when the second derivative does not exist there. Zero curvature at the point means the graph might hold a maximum, a minimum, or an inflection point. Fall back on the first derivative test, which reads the sign change directly and always gives a verdict.

Prudence suggests choosing the test after inspecting the derivative rather than before. A derivative that factors beautifully invites the first derivative test, since each factor's sign is readable at a glance across the number line. A first derivative that is miserable to factor, paired with a painless second derivative, invites the second test. Graders accept either, provided the justification is genuinely present: a named test, applied, with its condition verified. What graders do not accept is the naked assertion that a critical point "is the maximum because it is the only critical point," unless the domain reasoning supports it. On an open interval with degenerate endpoints whose objective values collapse to zero or soar to infinity, uniqueness plus end behavior can indeed certify an absolute extreme, but the certification must be written, not assumed. The written justification is part of the mathematics, and in this unit it is often the difference between full and partial credit.

One more distinction keeps solutions honest: local versus absolute. The derivative tests certify local extremes, peaks relative to nearby points. Exam optimization questions usually ask for absolute extremes, the best design anywhere in the domain. When the domain is an open interval and the objective has exactly one critical point, local and absolute coincide, provided the end behavior cooperates, and the fence, box, and can problems all enjoy that structure. When the domain is a closed interval, local reasoning is insufficient by itself, and the next section's candidates test takes over. Confusing the two levels, reporting a local maximum as the answer to an absolute question, is a conceptual error that no amount of careful arithmetic can rescue.

## Closed Intervals, the Candidates Test, and the Extreme Value Theorem

The Extreme Value Theorem guarantees that a continuous function on a closed interval attains both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum somewhere on that interval. The guarantee sounds abstract until a problem depends on it: a manufacturer choosing among allowed production levels, a driver choosing a speed within legal bounds, a function evaluated on a stated interval in a calculator-active question. On a closed interval, extremes have only three kinds of hiding places: interior points where the derivative is zero, interior points where the derivative does not exist, and the two endpoints. The candidates test simply checks all of them: list every critical point inside the interval, evaluate the function at those points and at both endpoints, and compare the values. The largest value wins the title of absolute maximum, the smallest takes absolute minimum, and no test of signs or curvature is needed, because direct comparison is stronger than any inference.

### Why do endpoints matter when finding absolute extrema?

Absolute extrema on a closed interval can hide at the boundaries, where no derivative condition detects them, because the function need not turn around there. A critical point list alone can crown the wrong value. Evaluating the function at both endpoints and at every critical point, then comparing all values, guarantees the true largest and smallest are found.

A compact example shows why the ceremony matters. Take f(x) = x³ - 3x on the interval from -2 to 2. The derivative, 3x² - 3, vanishes at x = 1 and x = -1, with values f(-1) = 2 and f(1) = -2. A solver stopping there would report a maximum of 2 and a minimum of -2. The endpoints tie those values exactly: f(-2) = -2 and f(2) = 2. The absolute maximum value 2 is attained at two points, x = -1 and x = 2, and a question asking where the maximum occurs expects both. Shift the interval slightly and the tie breaks in favor of an endpoint, which is how exam writers punish candidates-test skippers: the extreme sits quietly at a boundary while the interior critical points stage a convincing decoy.

In applied problems, closed intervals arise whenever a real limit bounds the design: a budget that caps a dimension, a tank that holds only so much, a road segment of fixed length. The endpoints then correspond to extreme designs, all budget spent one way or the other, and sometimes the extreme design genuinely wins, as when profit increases monotonically across the whole allowed range and the best decision is the largest permitted one. Monotonic objectives are the candidates test's favorite trap for derivative-only thinkers, because their derivatives never vanish at all. A solver whose only tool is "set the derivative to zero" finds no candidates and reports nothing, while the answer stands at an endpoint laughing. The complete habit, critical points plus endpoints, always, is cheap insurance against an entire species of trick.

The theorem's hypotheses deserve one sentence of respect. Continuity matters: a function with a jump on the interval can approach a value it never attains. Closedness matters: on an open interval, a function can climb toward a boundary value it never reaches. When either hypothesis fails, the extreme may not exist, and a well-posed exam problem will not ask for it. Recognizing when a problem has quietly supplied both hypotheses, a continuous objective on a closed interval, is recognizing that the candidates test will finish the job completely.

## More Optimization Settings: Windows, Inscriptions, and Profits

The Norman window, a rectangle crowned by a semicircle, is the ornamental classic of the genre. Suppose the perimeter, counting the outer frame, is fixed at 30 feet, and the area is to be maximized. Let r be the radius of the semicircle and h the height of the rectangular part. The perimeter constraint reads 2h + 2r + πr = 30: two vertical sides, the bottom width of 2r, and the semicircular arc of length πr. Solving for h gives h = (30 - 2r - πr)/2. The area objective combines the rectangle and the semicircle: A = 2rh + (1/2)πr². Substituting the constraint and simplifying produces A(r) = 30r - 2r² - (π/2)r², whose derivative, A′(r) = 30 - 4r - πr, vanishes at r = 30/(4 + π) ≈ 4.20 feet. The corresponding height is h ≈ 4.20 feet as well, and the optimal window, like the optimal can, hides a proportion: the rectangular height equals the radius at the optimum, a pattern worth predicting before the algebra confirms it.

Inscription problems place one figure inside another and ask for the largest guest. A rectangle with its base on the x-axis and its upper corners on the parabola y = 12 - x² has, by symmetry, half-width x and height 12 - x², so its area is A(x) = 2x(12 - x²) = 24x - 2x³. The derivative A′(x) = 24 - 6x² vanishes at x = 2, where the height is 8 and the area is 2 · 2 · 8 = 32 square units. The symmetry did quiet work here: assuming the rectangle is centered is justified by the parabola's own symmetry, and failing to exploit symmetry would have doubled the variables for no benefit. Recognizing when a problem's symmetry collapses the variable count is a modeling judgment of the same family as spotting similar triangles, and it belongs in the same toolbox: geometry first, calculus after.

Distance minimization introduces one more standard maneuver, optimizing the square of the distance instead of the distance itself. Consider the point on the curve y = √x nearest to (4, 0). The squared distance is D² = (x - 4)² + x, since the point's height squared is x itself. Its derivative, 2(x - 4) + 1, vanishes at x = 3.5, giving the nearest point (3.5, √3.5), at a distance of √(0.25 + 3.5) = √3.75 ≈ 1.94 units. Squaring away the radical is legal because distance and squared distance attain their minima at the same point, and it converts an ugly chain rule exercise into a polynomial. The endpoints of the domain also deserve their glance: as x grows, the distance grows, and at x = 0 the distance is 4, so the interior critical point beats the boundary and the verdict is consistent from every side.

Economics dresses the same mathematics in prices. Suppose a maker of trail maps can sell x maps per week at a price of 120 - x dollars each, while weekly costs run 500 + 20x dollars. Profit is revenue minus cost: P(x) = x(120 - x) - (500 + 20x) = 100x - x² - 500. The derivative P′(x) = 100 - 2x vanishes at x = 50, the second derivative is negative, and the maximum profit is P(50) = 2000 dollars per week at a price of 70 dollars per map. Marginal analysis, the economist's dialect of this work, simply names the derivatives: marginal profit is P′, and production should expand while marginal profit is positive and stop where it crosses zero. The exam's economics set pieces, like its geometry set pieces, are constraint-extraction tests; the demand curve is the constraint, and the solver who writes profit before revenue and cost separately has usually already lost the thread.

## Translation Drills: Setups Without Solutions

Because setup is the scored skill, the fastest way to train it is to practice setups alone, writing variables, governing equations, and differentiation plans without grinding through arithmetic. Read each scene below and notice how the opening moves repeat. A camera on the ground tracks a rocket rising vertically at a known speed; the governing equation is a tangent ratio between the rocket's height and the camera's angle, and the plan is to differentiate the tangent relationship with respect to time. Grain pours from a chute and forms a conical pile whose height stays a fixed fraction of its diameter; the governing equation is the cone volume with the proportion substituted, exactly the tank problem turned upside down, and the plan is to differentiate volume with respect to time. A ship sails north while another sails east from the same port at different times; the governing equation is the Pythagorean theorem between their positions, with each position a linear function of time, and the snapshot must be computed from the sailing times rather than read directly from the problem.

More drills sharpen different muscles. A melting snowball keeps its spherical shape while its surface area shrinks at a constant rate; the governing equations are the sphere's area and volume formulas, and the plan chains them, extracting dr/dt from the area rate and feeding it into the volume rate, the balloon problem run in reverse with a sign change. A streetlight throws the shadow of a walker who moves toward the lamp instead of away; the similar triangles are identical to the earlier scene, and only the sign of dx/dt changes, which is how the exam tests whether a solver models or memorizes. A ladder slides with its top, rather than its base, driven at a stated rate; the Pythagorean setup is unchanged and the given and unknown simply swap chairs, a two-line variation that reliably panics students who learned the problem as a ritual instead of a relationship.

Optimization has its own drill family, and the setups again matter more than the finishes. A rancher subdivides a rectangular field into two equal pens with a fence down the middle; the constraint must count the divider, and the objective counts area, and the whole problem is won or lost in that counting. A poster has fixed area with margins eating its borders; the printed region's dimensions depend on the outer dimensions through subtraction, and the objective is the inner area, a problem whose trap is optimizing the outer area by reflex. A box with a square base and no top must hold a fixed volume with minimal material; the constraint ties height to base side, and the objective counts one square and four rectangles, and miscounting the faces is the classic own goal. A cylindrical can with no top, an open box with a lid, a pen against a river instead of a barn: each variation tests whether the setup is rebuilt from the scene or recalled from a sibling.

Running these drills at speed builds the recognition layer that exam conditions demand. Under time pressure, nobody derives a method from first principles; a solver sees a filling cone and the similar-triangles move arrives unbidden, or sees a fixed perimeter and the elimination beat starts automatically. Recognition is not cheating and it is not shallow. Recognition is what modeling looks like after deliberate practice, and the drills aim to make the sight of a scene summon its governing equation the way the sight of a product summons the product rule.

## Choosing the Governing Equation: A Field Guide to Setups

A short field guide, held in memory, covers nearly every setup the exam deploys. If the scene contains a right triangle with a fixed hypotenuse or a fixed leg, the Pythagorean theorem governs, and the ladder, the two cars, and the kite string all live there. If the scene contains a point light source or a camera or a cone, similar triangles govern, converting one length into a proportion of another. If the scene involves a container or a pile or a balloon, a volume formula governs, usually with a proportion substituted to reach one variable. If the scene involves a sweeping angle, a tangent or sine ratio governs, and the derivative will carry a squared trigonometric term or a cosine factor that the snapshot geometry must supply. If the scene involves a surface, an area formula governs, and the chain rule will connect it to whatever length is actually changing.

Each family also carries a signature error that a forewarned solver can refuse. Pythagorean scenes tempt solvers to substitute the snapshot lengths before differentiating, freezing the triangle. Similar-triangle scenes tempt solvers to write the proportion backwards, radius over height when height over radius is meant, and the defense is to check the proportion at an easy configuration, the full tank or the empty one. Volume scenes tempt solvers to differentiate a two-variable formula without eliminating, producing an equation with two unknown rates and no path forward. Angle scenes tempt solvers to feed degrees into a calculus that only accepts radians. Area scenes tempt solvers to forget that a growing square's side and a growing circle's radius are not the quantity whose rate is given, and that the chain rule toll must be paid on every link between the given rate and the wanted one.

The field guide has a second use: checking finished work. When a computed answer looks implausible, the family diagnosis localizes the search. A ladder rate that stays constant across instants suggests the substitution happened too early. A cone rate that ignores the inflow's units suggests a formula was misremembered. An angle rate in degrees per second announces itself as wrong by its units alone. Troubleshooting by family turns the sick feeling of "this looks off" into a targeted inspection, and targeted inspections finish in time to matter.

## Common Errors and How They Cost Points

A guided tour of the failure modes is worth as much as another worked example, because most lost points in this unit are repeat offenders. The premature substitution has already been indicted: snapshot numbers slipped into the governing equation before differentiation, converting variables into constants and rates into zeros. Its tell in a written solution is a differentiated equation with numbers already inside it and chain rule factors missing. The chain rule omission is its close cousin: differentiating x² + y² = 100 into 2x + 2y = 0, as though y were the variable of differentiation, drops dy/dt entirely and usually reduces the problem to nonsense. The defense against both is procedural, a written habit: variables stay letters until the differentiated equation is fully formed, and every differentiated variable visibly carries its rate factor.

Sign errors form the third family. A draining tank, a shrinking distance, a falling height: each contributes a negative rate, and problems deliberately include one decreasing quantity to see whether the solver's signs reflect the scene. Two bookkeeping rules prevent most of the damage. First, assign signs when listing the given rates, before any algebra, while the scene is still in view. Second, interpret the final sign in the conclusion sentence rather than silently discarding it, since a computed negative rate for a quantity the problem describes as increasing is an alarm that something upstream inverted. Graders award the interpretation point for the sentence that says what the sign means; students who treat the sentence as decoration donate that point.

Optimization contributes its own catalog. Forgetting the constraint entirely, and differentiating a two-variable objective, produces a partial-derivative muddle the course never taught. Misidentifying the constraint is subtler: in a problem fixing the volume, students periodically maximize the volume and minimize nothing, having swapped the roles of objective and constraint in their notes. Reporting a critical point without a test is the justification failure already discussed. Reporting the critical point's coordinate as the answer when the question asked for the extreme value, or for the other dimension, or for the price that achieves it, is the interpretation failure: the derivative finds x, but the problem asked about the world, and the world speaks in areas, heights, and dollars. A final class of error is domain blindness, recommending dimensions the scene forbids, a negative radius, a cut deeper than the cardboard allows, a pen wider than the property line. Each of these is prevented by the same cheap move, writing the domain beside the objective at setup time.

Arithmetic, surprisingly, is the least of it. Graders read for structure and award most points to correct relationships, correct differentiation of those relationships, and correct interpretation, with exact arithmetic carrying a modest share. A solver with a flawless setup and one slipped digit outscores a solver with flawless arithmetic on a misconceived setup every time. That grading reality should reorder study priorities: drill setups until they are automatic, treat differentiation as a careful transcription exercise, and reserve arithmetic anxiety for the final line, where a rough magnitude check catches most slips anyway.

## Writing the Justification an Answer Needs

Free-response grading in this unit is a reading exercise: the grader hunts the page for specific mathematical claims and pays for each one found. A high-scoring solution therefore writes those claims explicitly. For optimization, the claims are the objective as a function of one variable, the derivative set to zero or the critical points found, the test applied with its name and condition, and the conclusion that translates the winning variable back into the requested dimensions or value. Phrases that carry legal weight include "by the first derivative test," "since the second derivative is negative at this point," and "comparing the values at the critical points and endpoints." Each announces that a theorem was invoked and satisfied, which is exactly what justification means at this level.

For related rates, the payable claims are the governing equation, its differentiation with respect to time with all chain rule factors present, the substitution performed after differentiation, and the conclusion with units and direction. Writing "differentiating with respect to time" above the key step is not filler; it declares the method and helps the grader locate the point-earning line. Showing the snapshot geometry, the small computation that produced y = 8 from x = 6, earns its own credit and protects against the suspicion that the value was guessed. The pattern across both families is a single principle: the page must contain the reasoning, not merely its results, because results without reasoning are unverifiable and unpaid.

Students often ask how much writing is enough. A useful calibration is to imagine the grader as a competent stranger who accepts nothing on faith. Every claim the stranger would challenge needs a sentence or a displayed step. Under that standard, most competition for points is won by solutions that look almost pedantic: variables defined, equations labeled, tests named, conclusions stated in words with units. The pedantry is the product. Elegance, in this unit, is a setup so clear that the calculus looks easy, and the solutions that read as effortless are invariably the ones whose modeling was done in the open.

## Rates Flow Both Ways: From Change to Accumulation

Related rates work deserves a forward glance, because the exam's later units reverse the direction of travel. Here, a relationship between quantities is differentiated to turn positions into rates. In the integration units, a rate function is integrated to recover accumulated change: a filling rate becomes a volume, a velocity becomes a displacement, a flow becomes a total. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus is the formal bridge between those two directions, and the [companion treatment of Riemann sums and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus](/2006/09/12/ap-calculus-ab-riemann-sums-ftc/) develops it in full. A student who has internalized related rates already thinks in the theorem's native language: quantities and their rates are different views of one process, linked by differentiation in one direction and by accumulation in the other.

The interplay shows up inside single exam questions. A free-response problem may give a tank's inflow rate as a function of time and ask for the moment when the water reaches a stated depth, an accumulation question, then ask how fast the level is rising at that moment, a related rates question wearing the same tank. The modeling habits transfer directly: variables declared, the geometric proportion between depth and volume written once and used in both directions, units tracked through every step. Students who learn this unit as a philosophy of relationships, rather than as a bag of scenarios, find the back half of the course strangely familiar. The scenes change; the discipline does not.

## Interpreting the Answer: The Last Ten Percent

Both families end with a translation back into the world, and the translation is graded. A related rates answer is a sentence, not a number: the water level is rising at 1/(2π) feet per minute, the distance between the cars is increasing at 50 miles per hour, the angle is decreasing at 0.125 radians per second. The sentence carries the quantity, the direction, and the units, and each of the three can be the difference between full credit and a shrug. An optimization answer is likewise a sentence in the problem's vocabulary: the largest area is 125,000 square feet, achieved with sides of 250 feet and 500 feet; the least material is used when the radius is about 5.42 centimeters and the height about 10.84 centimeters. Notice that both halves of the claim appear, the extreme value and the configuration that achieves it, because problems ask for the two in varying combinations and partial answers earn partial pay.

Interpretation also includes the sanity audit, a ten-second interrogation of every finished answer. Does the sign match the scene? Does the magnitude sit in a plausible band, a ladder top falling at feet per second rather than at hundreds of feet per second, a cone filling at a gentle pace rather than at a flood? Do the units answer the question that was asked, length rates for lengths, area rates for areas? When the audit fails, the failure is information, and the field guide to setups localizes the likely fault. Building the audit into the workflow, rather than treating it as a luxury for leftover time, converts a share of would-be errors into caught ones, and caught errors are the cheapest points on any exam.

There is a final interpretive skill that separates strong candidates: stating what the answer depends on. The ladder's top speed depended on the instant chosen; the cone's filling rate depended on the depth; the can's proportions did not depend on the volume at all. Naming the dependency shows a grader that the solver understands the result as a relationship rather than a lucky number, and in multi-part questions it frequently anticipates the next part, which so often asks exactly that: what changes, what stays, and why.

## Exam Strategy for the Applications Unit

Preparation for this unit should mirror its grading. Since setup carries the points, practice should be setup-heavy: take problems through the governing equation and the differentiation plan, then check those against a solution before grinding the algebra. Timed mixed sets come after, because the exam's cruelty is adjacency, a related rates problem standing next to an optimization problem next to a graph-analysis problem, each demanding a different opening move. Within a single problem, budget time by beats: a minute or two for the diagram and variable declarations is not overhead but the highest-yield spending in the whole solution, followed by the equation, the differentiation, and only then computation. The free-response craft for this course, scoring architecture, partial credit, and time allocation across multi-part questions, is treated in depth in the [Calculus AB free-response strategy guide](/2008/08/01/ap-calculus-ab-frq-strategy/), and its advice applies with full force here because applications questions are nearly always multi-part.

Multiple-choice questions in this unit reward a different economy. Many can be answered by setting up correctly and recognizing the target expression without completing the arithmetic, since distractors are built from the classic errors: the premature substitution, the dropped chain rule factor, the swapped proportion. Reading the choices as a catalog of mistakes turns them into a checklist. A choice that lacks a dy/dt term, or that plugs the snapshot radius into a derivative, identifies itself as bait. The solver who knows the failure modes can eliminate with confidence and spend the saved minutes on the questions that genuinely require computation.

Resource triage matters too. Worked solutions studied actively, with the setup covered and predicted before being revealed, build the recognition layer far faster than solutions read passively. Problem sets should interleave the families rather than segregating them, because the exam interleaves them. And drill volume should privilege fresh scenarios over repetitions of favorites; the ladder solved for the tenth time teaches comfort, while a trough, a sand pile, or a swinging spotlight teaches transfer. For volume practice with answers at hand, the series hub collects the whole AP landscape in the [complete AP exams guide](/2008/11/08/ap-exams-complete-guide/), and targeted sets of [free AP math and science practice questions](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ap-math-science-practice.html) supply fresh related rates and optimization items to rehearse against. The through-line of every strategy in this section is the thesis of the unit itself: points are won in the setup, so practice, time, and attention should all be spent where the points live.

## Worked Example: The Conical Pile and the Draining Tank

Not every cone holds water; some cones are made of the material itself. Grain pours from a chute at 10 cubic feet per minute and forms a conical pile whose radius is always twice its height, a proportion fixed by the grain's angle of repose. The question is how fast the height is rising when the pile stands 3 feet tall. The volume is V = (1/3)πr²h, and the proportion r = 2h converts it to V = (4π/3)h³ before differentiation, exactly the elimination move the tank problem taught. Differentiating gives dV/dt = 4πh² dh/dt, and substitution of dV/dt = 10 and h = 3 yields 10 = 36π dh/dt, so dh/dt = 5/(18π), about 0.088 feet per minute. The radius grows twice as fast, about 0.177 feet per minute, since r = 2h at every instant. Two details separate this problem from its tank sibling. The proportion runs the other direction, radius in terms of height rather than height in terms of radius, and the pile's surface is the material, so there is no waterline to picture; the geometry is the same and only the story changed. Exam writers rely on that sameness: the setup move is identical, and students who learned a relationship rather than a picture recognize it instantly.

Run the cone backward and a second classic appears. The tank from the earlier worked example, 10 feet tall with top radius 4 feet, now drains at 5 cubic feet per minute. Find how fast the depth is falling when the water is 8 feet deep. The proportion r = 0.4h and the volume V = (0.16π/3)h³ are unchanged, because the tank's shape has not moved. Differentiation gives dV/dt = 0.16πh² dh/dt, and the given rate is now negative, dV/dt = -5, because volume leaves. At h = 8 the equation reads -5 = 0.16π(64) dh/dt = 10.24π dh/dt, so dh/dt = -5/(10.24π), about -0.155 feet per minute. The depth falls at roughly 0.155 feet per minute, and comparing with the filling problem is instructive: filling at 2 cubic feet per minute at depth 5 raised the level at 0.159 feet per minute, while draining at 5 cubic feet per minute at depth 8 lowers it at nearly the same speed, because the deeper water spreads across a wider surface and each cubic foot buys less depth. Rate problems reward this cross-checking between siblings; the numbers should converse with each other, and when they contradict, one of the setups is lying.

A third variation in the same family replaces the snapshot question with a when question. A spherical balloon is inflated at 100 cubic centimeters per second, as before, but now the problem asks for the radius rate at the moment the radius reaches 20 centimeters: dr/dt = 100/(4π · 400) = 1/(16π), about 0.0199 centimeters per second, exactly one quarter of the rate at radius 10. The quartering is the signature of the inverse-square relationship hiding in dr/dt = 100/(4πr²), and noticing it builds the instinct that doubling a linear dimension quarters a surface-driven rate. Instincts of this kind cannot be memorized as rules for every family, but they grow automatically when sibling problems are solved side by side and their answers compared, which is an argument for studying related rates in clusters rather than in isolation.

## Worked Example: The Sweeping Searchlight

A lighthouse stands 2 kilometers from the nearest point of a straight shoreline, and its beam rotates at a constant half radian per minute. How fast does the spot of light race along the shore when the spot is 2 kilometers from that nearest point? Write x for the spot's distance along the shore from the nearest point, and θ for the angle between the beam and the line to that nearest point. The geometry gives tan θ = x/2, the opposite side over the adjacent side of a right triangle whose offshore leg never changes. Differentiating with respect to time gives sec²θ · dθ/dt = (1/2) dx/dt. When x = 2, the angle is π/4 and sec²θ = 2, so 2 · 0.5 = (1/2) dx/dt and dx/dt = 2 kilometers per minute. The spot outruns any boat it might track, and it accelerates without bound as the beam swings toward parallel with the shore, because sec²θ grows without limit while the rotation rate stays constant. That divergence is physical, not a modeling defect: a nearly parallel beam paints a spot that must travel enormous distances to advance the angle slightly.

Searchlight problems are the purest form of angle related rates, and they teach two transfers. First, the constant in the problem may be the angular rate itself, reversing the usual direction in which a linear rate is given and an angular rate is wanted. Second, the snapshot information may arrive as a distance, an angle, or a trigonometric value, and the solver must be ready to supply whichever of sin, cos, or sec² the differentiated equation demands, using the snapshot triangle rather than a calculator's approximation. The ladder angle variant earlier in this guide and the plane's angle of elevation belong to this clan, and a student fluent in one is fluent in all three, because the clan's entire secret is a tangent or cosine ratio, differentiated honestly, with radian measure respected throughout.

A land-based cousin completes the clan. A spotlight mounted on the ground 30 meters from a building shines on a person 1.8 meters tall walking toward the building at 1.5 meters per second; the person's shadow climbs or falls on the wall. Similar triangles relate the shadow's height on the wall to the person's position, and the rate follows by the now-familiar route. The scene differs from the streetlight shadow in one modeling respect: the projection surface is vertical rather than horizontal, so the wanted quantity is a height on the wall rather than a length on the ground. Students who draw the triangles fresh handle the swap without noticing there was one. Students who memorized the streetlight answer as a formula produce a length when the problem asked for a height, which is the modeling-versus-memorization divide in its purest form.

## Worked Example: The Best Seat in the Gallery

Optimization occasionally maximizes an angle rather than a length or an area, and the viewing angle problem is its showcase. A statue 10 feet tall stands on a pedestal 5 feet tall, so the statue's base is 5 feet above eye level and its top is 15 feet above eye level for a viewer standing on level ground. How far from the pedestal should the viewer stand to maximize the viewing angle, the angle between the lines of sight to the statue's base and top? Call x the viewer's distance from the pedestal. The viewing angle is a difference of two elevation angles: θ(x) = arctan(15/x) - arctan(5/x). Differentiating both inverse tangents and setting the result to zero gives 15/(x² + 225) = 5/(x² + 25), because the derivative of arctan(a/x) with respect to x simplifies to -a/(x² + a²), and the two terms must balance. Cross-multiplying: 15(x² + 25) = 5(x² + 225), so 15x² + 375 = 5x² + 1125, so 10x² = 750 and x² = 75. The viewer should stand x = 5√3, about 8.66 feet, from the pedestal.

The solution's shape deserves a look. The equation 15/(x² + 225) = 5/(x² + 25) says that at the best distance, the marginal gain in the top angle from stepping closer exactly offsets the marginal loss in the base angle. Step nearer and the base of the statue rises out of comfortable view faster than the top grows; step farther and both angles shrink toward each other. The optimum sits where the two tendencies trade evenly, which is the same balancing story the can told about its quadratic and inverse terms, translated into angles. A solver who hears that shared rhythm across families, extremes live where competing tendencies balance, has learned something no formula sheet contains. The candidates logic also applies: as x approaches zero the viewing angle collapses because the two sight lines merge overhead, and as x grows without bound both angles dwindle to nothing, so the interior critical point is the global winner by end behavior, exactly the open-interval certification described in the tests section.

Museums, theaters, and cinema designers solve grown-up versions of this problem when they place screens and rake seating. The exam version strips the scene to two arctangents, but the modeling moves are the full set: variables declared, an objective assembled from a geometric reading, a derivative computed with rules borrowed from the derivatives unit, a critical point certified by behavior at the domain's edges. Problems like this one are placed late in a free-response set precisely because they assemble so many parts, and students who have practiced assembly, rather than isolated skills, find them generous rather than cruel.

## Reading Derivatives: Optimization When the Function Arrives as a Graph

Not every optimization problem hands over a formula. A signature exam style presents the graph of a derivative, or a table of derivative values, and asks where the original function attains its extremes. The methods survive the change of costume because the methods were never about formulas. On the graph of f′, the critical points are the crossings and touches of the x-axis. The first derivative test reads the graph directly: where f′ passes from positive to negative, f has a local maximum; from negative to positive, a local minimum; a touch without a crossing is neither. The second derivative test translates into slope: at a critical point where the graph of f′ is rising, f″ is positive and the point is a local minimum; where f′ is falling, a local maximum. A student who can run both tests on a picture owns the tests, rather than renting them from algebra.

Tables demand the same reading with less ink. Given values of f′ at sampled points, a sign change between consecutive entries locates a critical point between them, and the direction of the change classifies it provisionally, with the caveat that sampling can hide wiggles. Absolute extrema on a closed interval with only derivative data available require one more tool: the change in f across a subinterval is the accumulation of f′, so endpoint comparisons become sums of signed areas or of table-driven estimates. That is the accumulation direction of the course reaching back into the optimization unit, and it rewards students who treated the units as one story. The practical habit for graph and table problems is to annotate: mark the sign of the derivative on a number line, arrow the function's rises and falls, and let the sketch, rather than memory, deliver the verdict.

Justification language adapts without strain. "f has a local maximum at x = 3 because f′ changes sign from positive to negative there" is a complete sentence of proof whether the sign change was read from a factored polynomial, a graph, or a table. Graders pay for the stated reason, not for the medium it was read from. Students who practice saying the reason out loud for every candidate, in every medium, stop losing the justification point that this unit so reliably offers.

## Rates from Measurements: Tables, Sensors, and Honest Estimates

A final related rates style works from measured data rather than from a tidy formula. A conical tank's depth is recorded at several times by a sensor, and the problem asks for the volume's rate of change at a stated moment, or for the moment when the inflow matched the outflow. The geometry is unchanged: the proportion ties radius to depth, the volume formula converts depth into volume, and the chain rule converts a depth rate into a volume rate. What changes is the source of the depth rate, which must be estimated from the table, typically by a difference quotient between neighboring readings, the average rate over a short interval standing in for the instantaneous rate at its midpoint or endpoint. The estimate is honest mathematics, not a shortcut, because the derivative is defined as the limit of exactly those quotients.

Units and reasonableness do heavy lifting in data problems. A depth rate read from readings a minute apart arrives in feet per minute and must not be blended with a flow stated in gallons per second without conversion. A volume rate computed from a depth rate should be checked against the tank's cross-section at that depth: the volume rate equals the surface area at that depth times the depth rate, a relationship visible in the differentiated cone formula, where dV/dt = 0.16πh² dh/dt and 0.16πh² is precisely the water surface's area at depth h. That identity, volume rate equals surface area times level rate, holds for any tank shape and gives data problems a built-in audit: if the computed volume change wildly exceeds the surface area times the level change, a decimal point has wandered.

Data problems also train a disposition the formula problems cannot: comfort with answers that are estimates. Free-response rubrics accept a well-labeled estimate built from a stated difference quotient, and they reward the sentence that says which two readings were used and why. The modeling sequence absorbs the variation without complaint. Draw, declare, relate, differentiate, and only the substitution beat changes costume, trading exact values for measured ones. A solver whose method is genuinely a method, rather than a script for one problem type, will not blink.

## Inside the Rubric: How Applications Questions Are Scored

Free-response applications questions are built in parts, and the parts are priced. A typical related rates question opens with a part that pays for the governing equation and its differentiation, continues with a part that pays for substitution and the computed rate, and closes with a part that pays for interpretation or for a second rate chained from the first, like the balloon's surface area following its radius. A typical optimization question pays separately for the single-variable objective, for the derivative work, for the justification by a named test, and for the final interpreted answer. The pricing has a strategic consequence: an error in part one does not bankrupt the later parts when the later work is consistent with the stated setup, because graders follow the student's declared model. Writing the setup explicitly, rather than leaping to computation, therefore does double duty. It earns the setup points, and it establishes the model the grader will use to judge everything downstream.

The pricing also explains the exam's fondness for the word "justify" in this unit. Justify is a billing code: it announces that the next point is paid for reasoning, not for a value. In optimization, justification means a named test with its hypothesis checked, a first derivative sign change, a second derivative sign, or a candidates comparison. In extrema questions on closed intervals, justification means the comparison table itself, values at critical points and endpoints displayed side by side. Students sometimes spend their justification budget on restating the answer in a confident tone. Confidence is not a test. The sign change is the test, and the sentence reporting it is short enough that there is never a time excuse for omitting it.

Calculator-active parts change the arithmetic, not the architecture. A graphing calculator will happily produce a numeric derivative, solve the critical point equation, and evaluate candidates, and the rubrics accept its output when the setup is shown. What the calculator cannot supply is the objective, the constraint, the governing equation, or the interpretation, and those remain the majority of the points. The wise division of labor is to let the machine do arithmetic and root-finding while the human does modeling and verdicts. Students who reverse that division, modeling sloppily and computing heroically, discover that the rubric priced their heroism near zero.

Partial credit philosophy completes the picture. Because each part is priced separately, a stuck solver should always write the setup for a later part even when an earlier part failed, since the parts are graded on their own terms more often than students expect. A blank page earns nothing with certainty; a page with a correct governing equation and a correct differentiation earns its points with equal certainty, even if the final arithmetic never happens. In this unit more than any other in the course, the page should be treated as a ledger where each modeling decision is recorded the moment it is made, because the grader can only pay for decisions the page records.

## Chained Rates: When One Rate Feeds Another

Some scenes chain two relationships, so the output rate of one stage becomes the input rate of the next. An oil spill spreads as a thin circle whose area grows at 500 square meters per hour. When the radius is 100 meters, the area relationship A = πr² differentiates into dA/dt = 2πr dr/dt, giving 500 = 200π dr/dt and dr/dt = 2.5/π, about 0.796 meters per hour. No second stage there yet; the chain appears when the next question asks about the slick's edge sweeping past a fixed marker, or couples the radius to a thickness as the fixed volume of oil spreads thinner. The discipline for chains is to finish each stage completely, writing the intermediate rate as a labeled quantity, before opening the next relationship. Rates carried forward anonymously, as naked numbers, invite unit mix-ups and sign losses; rates carried forward with names and units behave.

A melting snowball supplies the classic two-stage chain with a twist of direction. The snowball keeps its spherical shape while its surface area decreases at 2 square centimeters per minute. When the radius is 5 centimeters, how fast is the volume decreasing? The surface formula S = 4πr² differentiates into dS/dt = 8πr dr/dt, so -2 = 40π dr/dt and dr/dt = -1/(20π), about -0.0159 centimeters per minute. That radius rate then enters the volume relationship: dV/dt = 4πr² dr/dt = 4π(25)(-1/(20π)) = -5 cubic centimeters per minute exactly. The volume melts away at 5 cubic centimeters per minute. The exact cancellation of π is a quiet signature that the chain was assembled correctly, and the two negative signs agree with the shrinking scene at both stages.

Chains also run through geometry twice removed. In the shadow problem, the walker's speed drives the shadow's growth, and the shadow's growth plus the walker's speed drives the tip; each stage is one similar-triangles differentiation. In the ladder family, the base rate drives the height rate, and the two together drive the area rate and the angle rate. Free-response writers love chains because each stage can be priced separately and because a chain exposes, with surgical precision, whether a student's rates are quantities with meanings or noises made by a formula. The defense is bookkeeping: a small table of quantities, rates, and units, maintained as the solution grows, turns a chain from a memory test into a transcription exercise.

## When Materials Have Prices: Cost as the Objective

Real optimization usually minimizes cost rather than area, and cost reshapes the answer because different parts of a design can carry different prices. Take the one-liter can from the earlier example, but now suppose the top and bottom material costs twice as much per square centimeter as the side material. Measuring cost in units of the side material's price, the cost function becomes C = 2πrh + 2 · (2πr²) = 2πrh + 4πr², where the ends are charged double. The volume constraint still gives h = 1000/(πr²), so C(r) = 2000/r + 4πr². The derivative, C′(r) = -2000/r² + 8πr, vanishes when r³ = 250/π, giving r ≈ 4.30 centimeters and h = 4r ≈ 17.19 centimeters. Pricier ends push the design tall and thin, shrinking the expensive disks and stretching the cheap wall. Compare the unpriced optimum, where height equaled diameter at h = 2r: the proportion itself moved in response to the price ratio, which is exactly the kind of qualitative dependence examiners probe when they ask how an answer changes as a condition changes.

The symmetric problem completes the lesson. Fix a closed box's surface area at 150 square inches and maximize its volume, with the base a square of side x and height h. The constraint 2x² + 4xh = 150 gives h = (75 - x²)/(2x), and the volume becomes V(x) = x²h = (75x - x³)/2. The derivative, (75 - 3x²)/2, vanishes at x = 5, where h = (75 - 25)/10 = 5. The optimal box is a cube, 5 inches on every side, with volume 125 cubic inches. Among all closed boxes with a given surface area, the cube holds the most, a result with the same flavor as the square's victory among rectangles of fixed perimeter. Symmetry keeps winning these contests because extremes dislike imbalance: any lopsided design can trade a little of its excess dimension for its deficient one and improve, until balance makes every trade a loss. That single intuition predicts the square pen, the cube box, and the balanced can before any derivative is computed, and it gives solvers a forecast to check their algebra against.

## Building the Skill: A Practice Progression

The unit yields to a deliberate order of attack. Begin with pure setups: twenty scenes, no solutions, just diagrams, declared variables, governing equations, and differentiation plans, checked against worked keys. This stage feels easy and is in fact the whole game, repeated until the governing equation for any standard scene arrives within a minute. Move next to full solutions of the canonical five related rates scenes and the canonical five optimization scenes, the ladder, cone, sphere, shadow, and distance problems on one side, the box, fence, can, window, and inscription problems on the other, solving each until the arithmetic is boring. Boredom at the canonical level is the goal; it frees attention for the variations.

Variations come third, and they should be hunted deliberately: the barn removed, the divider added, the top priced double, the tank draining instead of filling, the angle wanted instead of the length. Each variation is a small exam in whether the method or the memory is doing the work. Fourth comes the justification layer: rewrite earlier solutions adding the named tests, the interpretation sentences, and the endpoint ceremonies, because those sentences are points and they are the first thing fatigue erases. Fifth, timed mixed sets, with the families interleaved and a strict rule that the first two minutes of every problem are spent on the diagram and declarations. An error log closes the loop. Every mistake gets classified by family: premature substitution, dropped chain factor, sign slip, missed constraint, missing test, wrong target. The log's categories, reviewed before each new set, convert generic carelessness into a short personal checklist, and personal checklists are what separate a prepared candidate from a merely knowledgeable one.

One more habit belongs in the progression, borrowed from how the graders work. After solving, reread the problem statement once, slowly, hunting for the sentence the solution never used. Problems in this unit are written tight; an unused fact almost always marks a modeling omission, a second rate never entered, a margin never subtracted, a lid never counted. The reread takes thirty seconds and catches the class of error that no amount of internal checking can find, because the error is not in the work. The error is between the work and the world the problem described, and only the statement can reveal it.

## Worked Example: The Rocket, the Camera, and the Two Ships

A tracking camera sits 1200 meters from a launch pad, filming a rocket that rises vertically at 200 meters per second. Two questions organize the scene. First, how fast is the camera's elevation angle changing when the rocket is 1600 meters up? The angle θ satisfies tan θ = h/1200, where h is the height. Differentiation gives sec²θ · dθ/dt = (1/1200) dh/dt. At h = 1600 the triangle is a 3-4-5 scaled by 400, so tan θ = 4/3 and sec²θ = 25/9. Substitution yields (25/9) dθ/dt = 200/1200 = 1/6, hence dθ/dt = 0.06 radians per second. Second, how fast is the distance from camera to rocket growing at that moment? The distance s satisfies s² = h² + 1200², so s = 2000 meters at the snapshot, and differentiating gives 2s ds/dt = 2h dh/dt, hence ds/dt = (1600/2000)(200) = 160 meters per second. The rocket's distance from the camera grows more slowly than the rocket climbs, because part of the climb runs across the line of sight rather than along it, and the ratio h/s is precisely the cosine of the elevation angle, quietly converting vertical speed into radial speed. Scenes with two natural questions reward the solver who answers them from one diagram and one declaration of variables, and punish the solver who rebuilds the world for each part.

Ships supply the time-offset variation. Two ships leave the same port at the same time, one sailing east at 20 kilometers per hour, the other north at 21 kilometers per hour. The distance between them obeys z² = x² + y², and after one hour x = 20, y = 21, and z = 29, the 20-21-29 right triangle. Differentiating and substituting gives dz/dt = (20 · 20 + 21 · 21)/29 = 841/29 = 29 kilometers per hour. The answer's coincidence with z itself is an accident of the chosen hour, and the deeper regularity is that with both speeds constant, dz/dt is constant in time for any pair of straight courses from a common point, because the whole configuration scales uniformly. Now stagger the departures, the second ship leaving an hour after the first, and the constancy survives but the setup hardens: each ship's position must be written as speed times its own elapsed time, and the snapshot positions must be computed from two different clocks. That bookkeeping, positions as functions of time with offsets respected, is the entire difficulty, and it is why the exam includes staggered variants. The governing equation never changes. Only the honesty of the position functions is tested.

## Worked Examples Revisited: The Poster and the Divided Field

A poster has a total area of 1000 square centimeters, with margins of 5 centimeters at the top and bottom and 4 centimeters at each side. What outer dimensions maximize the printed area? Let the poster's width be x and its height y, so the constraint is xy = 1000. The printed region loses the margins on every side: its width is x - 8 and its height is y - 10. The objective is P = (x - 8)(y - 10), and eliminating y gives P(x) = (x - 8)(1000/x - 10) = 1080 - 10x - 8000/x. The derivative, P′(x) = -10 + 8000/x², vanishes when x² = 800, so x = 20√2 ≈ 28.28 centimeters and y ≈ 35.36 centimeters. The second derivative, -16000/x³, is negative, certifying the maximum, and the printed area at the optimum is about 514 square centimeters. The poster's trap is reflexive: students who maximize total area have answered a question nobody asked, since the total is fixed by the constraint. The objective hides inside the constraint's shadow, inset by the margins, and only a solver who asks "what exactly is being maximized" before writing anything survives.

A rancher with 1200 meters of fencing builds a rectangular field and divides it into two equal pens with a fence parallel to one pair of sides. Let the rectangle have horizontal side a and vertical side b, with the divider running vertically, parallel to the sides of length b. The fencing count is 2a + 3b = 1200: two horizontal runs, two outer vertical runs, and the divider. Area A = ab becomes A(a) = a(1200 - 2a)/3 after eliminating b. The derivative vanishes when 1200 - 4a = 0, so a = 300 meters, b = 200 meters, and the field encloses 60,000 square meters. Miscounting the divider, using 2a + 2b, silently solves a different ranch. The example belongs beside the barn problem in every student's memory, since both are won entirely at the counting stage, before a single derivative exists. Counting what the fence actually does, where the margins actually fall, which faces the box actually has: this is the unglamorous core of optimization, and the exam returns to it relentlessly because it cannot be faked by fluency with derivatives.

## The Symbolic Habit: Keep Letters Longer

Strong solvers postpone numbers. Working symbolically, with the given values kept as letters until the final line, produces answers that explain themselves and survive changes in the problem's data. The ladder's general solution is a formula, dy/dt = -(x/y) · dx/dt, which says the top's speed is the base's speed scaled by the triangle's aspect ratio; every numerical ladder answer is that sentence evaluated. The cone's general solution says the depth rate equals the inflow divided by the water's surface area at that depth, dh/dt = (dV/dt)/(πr²), which is why wide moments are slow moments, for any tank of any shape. The can's general solution is the most famous: with volume V kept symbolic, surface area becomes S(r) = 2πr² + 2V/r, the derivative condition gives r³ = V/(2π), and the height-to-radius ratio falls out as h/r = V/(πr³) = 2, proving the height-equals-diameter proportion for every can size at once.

Symbolic work pays three dividends on the exam. It exposes structure, the ratios and cancellations that numeric work hides, and structure is what the follow-up parts of a question usually ask about. It localizes arithmetic to the final substitution, so an arithmetic slip contaminates one line rather than five. And it makes plausibility checks possible, since a symbolic answer can be interrogated dimensionally: if a formula for a rate of length growth contains an area in the numerator without a compensating length, the formula is wrong before any number touches it. The habit costs a little comfort at first, because letters feel less concrete than digits. The comfort returns quickly, and with interest, the first time a part (b) asks for the same quantity with a different inflow and the symbolic answer simply re-evaluates while the numeric solver starts over.

## A Failure Gallery: When the Standard Moves Mislead

The standard routine assumes an interior critical point exists, lies inside the domain, and can be certified. Three gallery cases show what happens when each assumption fails, and how the complete method absorbs the shock. First, the monotonic objective: a firm's profit on a regulated product is P(x) = 40x - 1000 dollars for x units, with production capped at 100 units by license. The derivative is 40, never zero, so a derivative-only search finds no candidate and stalls. The candidates test on the closed interval from 0 to 100 compares P(0) = -1000 with P(100) = 3000 and crowns the cap: produce all 100 units. Objectives that only rise or only fall across their allowed range are common in applied settings precisely because real constraints bite, and the endpoint ceremony exists for them.

Second, the exiled critical point. Return to the farmer and the barn, with 1000 feet of fencing, but now the barn's wall is only 300 feet long, capping the pen's parallel side at 300 feet. The unconstrained optimum wanted a parallel side of 500 feet, which the wall forbids. The constraint 1000 - 2x ≤ 300 forces x ≥ 350, exiling the critical point x = 250 from the feasible domain. Since the area function A(x) = x(1000 - 2x) decreases throughout the feasible region beyond its peak at 250, the best feasible design presses against the new boundary: x = 350, parallel side exactly 300, area 105,000 square feet. The derivative did not fail; the domain moved, and the maximum moved to the domain's edge. Problems with secondary constraints, a wall too short, a sheet too narrow, a budget with a floor as well as a ceiling, are testing exactly this alertness: feasibility first, critical points second, boundaries always.

Third, the silent second derivative. The function f(x) = x⁴ has a critical point at zero where the second derivative is also zero, and the point is a genuine minimum, as the first derivative test confirms by its sign change from negative to positive. Its cousin f(x) = x³ shares the same derivative data at zero, zero first derivative and zero second derivative, yet the point is neither maximum nor minimum. Identical test data, opposite verdicts: the second derivative test is not being coy, it is being honest about its limits, and the gallery's moral is procedural. When the quick test speaks, listen. When it is silent, the first derivative test always has jurisdiction, and the candidates test has jurisdiction over every closed interval regardless. A complete method is not a favorite trick; it is the full bench of tests with rules for which one presides.

## Two More Scenes: The Dock Rope and the Inscribed Cone

A boat is pulled toward a dock by a rope passed through a ring on the dock 6 feet above the boat's bow. The rope is hauled in at 2 feet per second. At what rate is the boat approaching when it is 8 feet from the dock? Let x be the boat's distance from the dock and z the length of rope from ring to bow. The right triangle gives z² = x² + 36, and the snapshot is a 6-8-10 triangle, z = 10 when x = 8. Differentiating, 2z dz/dt = 2x dx/dt, and the haul rate is dz/dt = -2, negative because the rope shortens. Substitution gives dx/dt = (z/x) dz/dt = (10/8)(-2) = -2.5 feet per second: the boat approaches at 2.5 feet per second, faster than the rope is hauled, and the advantage grows as the boat nears, since z/x swells as x shrinks. Dockhands learn this in their shoulders; the mathematics merely explains the lesson. The problem also polishes a sign habit: the given rate belongs to a shrinking quantity and must enter with its negative sign attached, or the answer will report the boat retreating from a rope that is pulling it in.

An inscribed cone reverses the direction of containment: the largest cone that fits inside a sphere of radius 9. Place the cone's apex at the sphere's bottom and its base as a horizontal slice at height h from the apex, with base radius r. The sphere's cross-section ties the variables: r² = h(18 - h), because a point on the base circle lies on the sphere. The volume objective becomes V(h) = (1/3)π r² h = (1/3)π h² (18 - h). Differentiating, V′(h) = (1/3)π (36h - 3h²), which vanishes at h = 12. Then r² = 12 · 6 = 72 and the maximum volume is (1/3)π · 72 · 12 = 288π, about 904.8 cubic units. The optimal height, 12, is exactly four-thirds of the sphere's radius, another of this unit's portable proportions, and the second derivative or a sign check confirms the maximum. Inscription problems generalize widely, cones in spheres, cylinders in cones, boxes in pyramids, and every one of them is the same two moves: a containment relation from the host figure's geometry, then an objective reduced to one variable. The host changes; the hospitality rules do not.

## Method Before Machinery

Across every section of this guide, the same wager has been placed repeatedly: the derivative is the easy part. Related rates and optimization are won in the minutes before differentiation, in the drawing of the scene, the declaring of variables, the separating of constants from snapshots, the writing of the governing equation, and the elimination that leaves one variable standing. They are secured in the minutes after differentiation, in the named test, the compared candidates, the interpreted sentence with its units and direction. The machinery in the middle, chain rule, product rule, a factored derivative, is reliable precisely because the machinery is mechanical. Students who invert that order of respect, drilling computation and improvising models, find exams mysteriously unfair. Students who honor the order find the same exams strangely repetitive, one contract after another, each asking to be written down and then asked a question. That repetition is not a flaw in the exam. It is the exam's thesis, and mastering it is the real content of the unit.

Carry the same order of respect into the examination room itself. When a fresh problem appears, resist the derivative for the first full minute. Spend that minute the way this guide has spent its pages: on the picture, the players, the contract between them. The minute feels expensive and is in fact the highest-yielding sixty seconds of the solution, because every later line inherits its correctness from those first decisions. Machinery serves method, and method, practiced until it is reflex, is what the score ultimately reports.

## Sensitivity: Answers as Functions of the Givens

A mature way to close the study of any worked problem is to ask how its answer would move if the givens moved. The barn fence generalizes cleanly: with a fencing budget of B feet against a barn, the area A(x) = x(B - 2x) is maximized at x = B/4, the parallel side is B/2, and the greatest area is B²/8. The 1000-foot answer, 125,000 square feet, is the formula at B = 1000. Doubling the budget quadruples the best area, because area scales with the square of every linear dimension in a self-similar design. A student who sees the B² in the answer understands the scaling without computing anything, and scaling arguments let a solver audit numerical answers instantly: a fencing answer that merely doubles when the budget doubles has failed its audit.

The box generalizes the same way. For a square sheet of side s, the volume V(x) = x(s - 2x)² has derivative (s - 2x)(s - 6x), so the optimal cut is x = s/6 regardless of the sheet, and the best volume is 2s³/27, which at s = 12 returns the familiar 128 cubic inches. The cut is always one sixth of the side, and the height of the best box is always one quarter of its base width, since the base measures s - 2x = 2s/3 against a height of s/6. Proportions like these are the portable residue of the unit: numbers are forgotten, proportions are carried into the next problem, the next course, and the next design meeting. Examiners know this, which is why so many follow-up parts ask for a ratio, a comparison, or a prediction under changed conditions rather than a second bare number.

Related rates answers show their dependencies just as plainly. In the filling cone, the depth rate at a fixed depth is directly proportional to the inflow rate, because the differentiated equation, dV/dt = (surface area) · dh/dt, is linear in both rates: double the inflow and the level rises twice as fast at that depth. In the ladder, the top's speed is the base's speed multiplied by x/y, so it depends on position but not on the ladder's length except through the position ratio. In the balloon, the radius rate falls with the square of the radius, so equal inflows produce ever gentler growth. Reading an answer for its dependencies converts a solved problem into a small theory of its scene, and theories transfer. The next unfamiliar problem is usually a familiar theory in a new coat, and the solver who collected theories recognizes the coat's cut.

## A Second Route: Differentiating Before Eliminating

The cone problem was solved by eliminating the radius before differentiating, but a second route exists and confirms the first. Keep both variables and differentiate V = (1/3)πr²h with the product rule, obtaining dV/dt = (1/3)π(2rh dr/dt + r² dh/dt). The proportion r = 0.4h differentiates into dr/dt = 0.4 dh/dt, a rate relation rather than a length relation. At the snapshot h = 5 and r = 2, substitution gives dV/dt = (1/3)π(2 · 2 · 5 · 0.4 dh/dt + 4 dh/dt) = (1/3)π(12 dh/dt) = 4π dh/dt, exactly the coefficient the elimination route produced, and the same answer follows. Two algebraically distinct paths landing on one coefficient is the strongest verification a solver can perform without a key, and the habit of re-deriving by a second route, when time permits, converts suspicion into certainty.

The second route also clarifies when elimination is strategy rather than necessity. Elimination before differentiation shrinks the algebra and is the right default under time pressure. Differentiation before elimination keeps the scene's variables visible, which helps when a problem asks for two rates at once, since the un-eliminated equation relates them both. Optimization forbids the second route, because an objective must stand in one variable before its critical points mean anything, but related rates permits either, and fluency is choosing deliberately. Graders accept both routes without preference. What they reward, in both, is the same skeleton: relationship stated, differentiation performed with every chain factor present, snapshot admitted only after its moment has come.

## Precision in Language

The unit's vocabulary is small and each term carries a technical load that loose usage collapses. A rate without qualification means an instantaneous rate, a derivative, not an average over an interval; the two can differ wildly, as the ladder's top accelerates through its fall. A maximum without qualification is ambiguous between local and absolute, and careful writers always say which they mean. A critical point is a location, an x-value, while a maximum is a value or a point depending on the sentence, and confusing the coordinate with the height is the interpretation error already flagged. Constraint and objective name roles in a model, not kinds of equations, since the same equation can serve as either in dual problems. Even the humble word "when" does technical work in related rates, marking the snapshot whose values wait politely outside the differentiation until called. Solutions written in this precise dialect grade well because they can be followed without charity. They also think well: a solver who cannot say whether a point is local or absolute usually has not decided, and the vocabulary forces the decision into the open where it can be made correctly.

Two more distinctions deserve the same care. Optimize is the genus and maximize and minimize are its species; a problem that says optimize expects the solver to infer the direction from context, and misreading the direction wastes a flawless computation. Related is the load-bearing word in related rates: the rates are not merely listed together, they are bound by an equation, and any solution that treats them as independent has missed the subject entirely. Even the phrase "with respect to time," repeated until it feels like boilerplate, is a genuine instruction, the reminder that every variable in the scene is a function riding on the clock, and that the chain rule is the fare each one pays for the ride.

One last piece of language deserves protection, because students surrender it cheaply: the conclusion sentence. Every solution in this unit should end by answering the question that was actually asked, in the vocabulary that question used, with the direction and the units the scene supplied. The sentence feels redundant after pages of correct work, and that feeling is a trap. The work earns its points in pieces, but the conclusion is where the pieces are assembled into a claim about the world, and graders read for the claim. A ladder rate reported as a bare decimal is arithmetic; reported as the top of the ladder sliding down at three quarters of a foot per second, it is mathematics finishing its sentence. The habit of writing that final sentence, every time, without exception, is the cheapest insurance in the entire unit, and it doubles as a final audit, since a conclusion that cannot be written sensibly exposes a solution that went wrong somewhere upstream, while there is still time to find out where.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What are related rates on AP Calculus AB?

Related rates are problems in which two or more quantities change with time and are linked by an equation, so the rate of change of one quantity can be found from the rates of the others. A ladder sliding down a wall, a tank filling with water, and a shadow lengthening are standard scenes. The linking equation comes from geometry or physics, the Pythagorean theorem, similar triangles, or a volume formula. Differentiation with respect to time, with the chain rule applied to every variable, converts that equation into a relationship among rates. One known rate and a snapshot of the scene then determine the unknown rate. These problems test modeling first and computation second, and they sit in the applications of derivatives unit of the course.

### Q: How do you solve a related rates problem on AP Calculus AB?

Draw the scene and assign a letter to every quantity that changes, keeping constants as numbers. Write down the given rate and the rate being sought, with signs and units. Find an equation connecting the variables that holds at every instant, using geometry or a physical formula. Differentiate the equation with respect to time, attaching the chain rule factor to each variable. Only after differentiating, substitute the snapshot values and the known rate, using the original equation to supply any missing snapshot value. Solve for the unknown rate and state it in a sentence with units and direction, checking that the sign agrees with the scene.

### Q: What is optimization on AP Calculus AB?

Optimization is the use of derivatives to find the largest or smallest possible value of a quantity under a stated limitation. The quantity is the objective, area, volume, cost, distance, or profit, and the limitation is the constraint, a fixed perimeter, a prescribed volume, a budget, or a curve the design must lie on. The constraint ties the variables together so the objective becomes a function of one variable. Its critical points, examined with a derivative test or compared against endpoints, reveal where the extreme occurs and what it equals. Optimization problems reward the same modeling discipline as related rates, and their answers are always interpretations: a dimension, a decision, or a best value stated in context.

### Q: How do you solve an optimization problem on AP Calculus AB?

Name the variables with a diagram and state the quantity to be maximized or minimized as the objective. Write the constraint as an equation, then solve it for one variable and substitute, reducing the objective to a function of a single variable with a stated domain. Differentiate and find the critical points inside the domain. Certify the winner with the first derivative test, the second derivative test, or the candidates test when the interval is closed, evaluating endpoints as required. Finish by answering the exact question asked, reporting the extreme value and the dimensions or decision that produce it, with units attached.

### Q: How do you set up a related rates equation on AP Calculus AB?

Begin from the scene rather than from a formula sheet. Sketch the situation, label each changing quantity with a variable, and mark constants with their values. Ask which geometric or physical law stays true throughout the scene: a right triangle suggests the Pythagorean theorem, a light source or a cone suggests similar triangles, a container suggests its volume formula, a sweeping angle suggests a trigonometric ratio. Write that law using only variables for changing quantities. If the law contains more variables than the given rates can support, hunt for a proportion that links the extra variables, as the cone's radius links to its depth. The setup is complete when differentiating the equation will produce exactly one unknown rate.

### Q: How do you find a maximum or minimum on AP Calculus AB?

Reduce the objective to a function of one variable using the constraint, then differentiate. Critical points, where the derivative is zero or does not exist, are the only interior candidates. On a closed interval, evaluate the function at every critical point and at both endpoints and compare the values directly; the largest and smallest values win. On an open interval, apply the first derivative test or the second derivative test to certify that the candidate is the extreme sought. Whichever route the problem takes, the final answer must be interpreted: give the extreme value and the variable value that produces it, in the units and terms of the original situation.

### Q: How do you use critical points for optimization on AP Calculus AB?

Critical points organize the search for extremes. After the objective is written in one variable, set its derivative equal to zero and solve, also noting any point in the domain where the derivative fails to exist. Each critical point is a candidate, not a conclusion. Test each candidate: a sign change in the derivative from positive to negative certifies a local maximum, negative to positive a local minimum, and the second derivative's sign can confirm the verdict when it is nonzero. On closed intervals, add the endpoints to the candidate list and compare function values. Critical points outside the physical domain, such as a negative length, are discarded as modeling artifacts rather than reported.

### Q: How do you handle a related rates word problem on AP Calculus AB?

Read it twice, once for the story and once for the quantities. Translate each phrase carefully: "at a rate of" introduces a derivative with respect to time, "when" introduces snapshot values reserved for late substitution, and "how fast" names the unknown rate. Draw and label the scene, declaring every variable in writing. Then follow the standard sequence: governing equation, differentiation with respect to time, substitution, solution, interpretation. Word problems are engineered so that each sentence contributes exactly one fact, so a sentence left unused is a warning that the setup missed something. If the scene involves containers, lights, or cones, expect a proportion from similar triangles to be the hidden hinge of the problem.

### Q: What is the first derivative test on AP Calculus AB?

The first derivative test classifies a critical point by the sign of the derivative around it. If the derivative changes from positive to negative as the variable increases through the critical point, the function increases then decreases, and the point is a local maximum. If the derivative changes from negative to positive, the point is a local minimum. If the sign stays the same on both sides, the point is neither. The test reads actual function behavior, so it never gives a false verdict, and it remains available when the second derivative test is inconclusive. In optimization solutions, naming the test and reporting the sign change constitutes the justification that the candidate is truly the extreme.

### Q: What is the second derivative test on AP Calculus AB?

The second derivative test classifies a critical point using curvature. When the derivative is zero at a point and the second derivative is positive there, the graph is concave up and the point is a local minimum. When the second derivative is negative there, the graph is concave down and the point is a local maximum. When the second derivative is zero or does not exist at the critical point, the test is inconclusive, and the first derivative test must decide. The test is fast when the second derivative is simple, as with quadratic objectives, and it doubles as a concavity check on the whole model, since a negative second derivative throughout confirms the critical point is the global peak of a downward-opening objective.

### Q: How do you identify the constraint in an optimization problem on AP Calculus AB?

Look for the quantity the problem holds fixed or the condition every design must obey: a total length of fencing, a prescribed volume, a fixed amount of material, a budget, or a curve on which a point must lie. The constraint is usually an equation waiting to be written, and signal phrases include "has 1000 feet of fencing," "must hold 1000 cubic centimeters," and "with a perimeter of 30." The objective, by contrast, is the quantity preceded by maximize or minimize. If a draft solution has an objective with two variables and no equation connecting them, the constraint has not yet been found. Re-read for the fixed quantity; it is always present, because without it the objective could grow without bound.

### Q: How do you interpret the answer to an optimization problem on AP Calculus AB?

State the result in the problem's own terms, answering exactly what was asked. If the question asks for dimensions, give the dimensions; if it asks for the greatest area, give the area; if it asks for both, give both, each with units. Connect the variable back to the scene: a critical value of x = 250 becomes sides of 250 feet and 500 feet once the constraint supplies the other dimension. Confirm the configuration is physically possible within the domain, and confirm that the certified extreme is the kind requested, a maximum rather than a minimum. A bare number with no units, no dimension, and no verdict earns only part of the credit the work deserved.

### Q: How do you relate variables over time in a related rates problem on AP Calculus AB?

Treat every changing quantity as a function of time, even when the problem never writes t explicitly. The variables are related by an equation that the scene keeps true, and because each variable depends on time, differentiating the equation with respect to t links their rates through the chain rule: a term x² contributes 2x dx/dt, a term involving y contributes its derivative times dy/dt, and constants contribute nothing. This is why implicit differentiation is the engine of the method. The rates are not independent; change one quantity and the equation forces compensating changes in the others, at speeds the differentiated equation computes exactly for the instant described.

### Q: What are the common related rates setups on AP Calculus AB?

Five setups recur. The sliding ladder uses the Pythagorean theorem between the wall distance and the height. The filling cone uses similar triangles to tie the water's radius to its depth, then the cone volume formula. The inflating balloon or expanding ripple uses the area and volume formulas for circles and spheres. The lengthening shadow uses similar triangles between a light source, a person, and the shadow's tip. Approaching or departing objects, cars, ships, and planes, use the Pythagorean theorem or a tangent ratio for an angle of elevation. Each setup pairs one governing equation with snapshot substitution after differentiation, so recognizing the family is most of the battle.

### Q: How do you avoid mistakes in optimization problems on AP Calculus AB?

Write the constraint before differentiating, and confirm the objective holds exactly one variable. Declare the domain beside the objective so impossible dimensions never survive to the answer. Factor the derivative rather than expanding it, since critical points are built to be found. Never report a critical point without a named test or an endpoint comparison backing it. Distinguish the question's target carefully: the value of the variable, the other dimension from the constraint, and the extreme value are three different answers, and problems choose among them deliberately. Finally, interrogate the finished design for physical sense. A negative radius, a cut deeper than half the sheet, or a fence longer than the budget each indicts the setup, not the arithmetic.

### Q: How does implicit differentiation power related rates on AP Calculus AB?

Related rates equations, such as x² + y² = 100, define variables implicitly in terms of one another, and every variable is itself a function of time. Implicit differentiation with respect to t respects both facts at once: it differentiates each term as written, then multiplies by the derivative of the inner variable, producing dx/dt and dy/dt factors through the chain rule. Without that step, there would be no rates in the equation at all, only lengths. Implicit differentiation is therefore not a technique used inside related rates; it is the mechanism that makes related rates possible, turning a static geometric truth into a dynamic statement about how the scene's speeds must balance at every instant.

### Q: What is the candidates test for absolute extrema on AP Calculus AB?

The candidates test finds absolute extrema on a closed interval by direct comparison. Collect every candidate: the points inside the interval where the derivative is zero, the points where the derivative does not exist, and the two endpoints. Evaluate the function at all of them. The largest value is the absolute maximum and the smallest is the absolute minimum, with the winning points reported as their locations. The test works because the Extreme Value Theorem guarantees the extremes exist and calculus guarantees they can only hide in that candidate list. It needs no sign charts and no curvature, and it catches boundary extremes that derivative tests alone would miss entirely.

### Q: How do units guide a related rates setup on AP Calculus AB?

Units act as a running proofread. Every rate carries compound units, and the differentiated equation must balance dimensionally: differentiating an area with respect to time must yield square units per time unit, so if the algebra produces a rate in feet per second for an area, a length was differentiated where an area belonged. Units also dictate conversions, since a rate given per hour cannot mingle with one given per minute. Writing units beside each given rate at setup time exposes mismatches while they are still cheap. At the end, the answer's units certify the work: radians per second for an angle, cubic feet per minute for a volume, each matching the question asked.

### Q: How do you check the sign of an answer in a related rates problem on AP Calculus AB?

Compare the computed sign with the scene's direction of motion. A quantity that grows must carry a positive rate and a quantity that shrinks a negative one, at the instant in question. If the algebra returns a negative rate for a height the problem describes as rising, inspect the given rates first, since a decreasing input, like a draining flow or a closing distance, often legitimately carries a negative sign that was dropped during setup. Then inspect the relationship for an inverted proportion. The final sentence should report direction in words, "decreasing at 0.75 feet per second," converting the sign into meaning rather than hiding it, and a mismatch between words and scene is the last available alarm.

### Q: What is the Extreme Value Theorem on AP Calculus AB?

The Extreme Value Theorem states that a function continuous on a closed interval attains an absolute maximum value and an absolute minimum value somewhere on that interval. Both hypotheses carry weight: a discontinuity can let a function approach a height it never reaches, and an open interval can let it climb toward an endpoint value it never attains. The theorem powers the candidates test, since it promises the extremes exist and the derivative analysis promises they occur only at critical points or endpoints. In optimization, the theorem explains why closed-interval problems always have an answer, while open-interval designs require a critical point and a test to guarantee the extreme is real rather than merely approached.
