The verdict on the Latin American life of Romeo and Juliet can be stated in one sentence: the tragedy did not arrive in the region as a finished European possession and get politely received; it was seized, translated by poets, laughed into new shapes by comedians, carried into streets and circus rings, and fed into the serial machinery of radio and television, and at every stage of that journey the price of admission was the same, a local feud and a local form. What Latin America did to the play is the strongest evidence in the whole global record for a proposition the rest of the series has tested country by country: the play survives travel not by being protected from its new home but by being surrendered to it. The surrender, though, was never free. Each host culture paid for the play in its own currency, and the exchange rate can be read off the endings. Where a region's forms prize reconciliation, the lovers tend to live. Where its forms can hold a corpse in public view and call the sight necessary, the lovers die on schedule. Tracing that exchange rate, from the first Spanish rewritings that reached American stages to a Brazilian football comedy and an Argentine television serial, is the work of the pages that follow.

The documentary spine of the account is the scholarship gathered in Latin American Shakespeares (2005), the collection edited by Bernice W. Kliman and Rick J. Santos for Fairleigh Dickinson University Press, still the only book in English devoted to the subject, and the archive work of projects such as the MIT Global Shakespeares and its Brazilian partner site. Santos names the phenomenon in his introduction with the region's own master word: a mestizo Shakespeare, mixed as the peoples of the continent are mixed, native, colonial, and immigrant at once. Mestizaje is not a compliment in that sentence. It is a method. It says the play in Latin America is never found pure, because nothing in Latin America is found pure, and the mixture is where the meaning gets made.

## Romeo and Juliet in Latin America: The Map of the Argument

Three claims organize everything documented below. The first concerns translation: in Spanish and Portuguese America the play's text was, for most of its history, an import twice removed, filtered through France into Spain and carried across the Atlantic in peninsular editions, until the region's own poets and scholars began producing versions that answered to local ears. The second concerns the feud: production after production relocated the "ancient grudge" of the Prologue (Prologue, line 3) onto the divisions a given audience already lived inside, class in Mexico, race and class in Cuba's radio melodrama, football allegiance in São Paulo, dictatorship and its aftermath in the Southern Cone. The third concerns form, and it is the claim with teeth: Latin America possesses, in melodrama, a narrative technology older than its television and stronger than its imported classicism, and melodrama's deepest law is not exaggeration but recognition, the lost child found, the secret birth revealed, the enemy house reconciled. A tragedy whose Prologue promises that nothing but the children's deaths can end the parents' rage collides with that law head-on. The region's adaptations divide, with unusual neatness, into those that obeyed the Prologue and those that obeyed melodrama.

The textual anchor for the whole inquiry is the Prologue itself, quoted here from the Folger Shakespeare edition of Barbara A. Mowat and Paul Werstine. "Two households, both alike in dignity, / In fair Verona, where we lay our scene, / From ancient grudge break to new mutiny, / Where civil blood makes civil hands unclean" (Prologue, lines 1-4). Four features of those lines predict the Latin American record with eerie precision. The households are alike in dignity: the feud is a civil war inside one class, one city, one people, which is why it maps so readily onto divisions within a single society rather than wars between nations. The grudge is ancient and unexplained: no Latin American adapter needed to invent a reason, since every audience supplied its own. The blood is civil: the violence is intimate, neighbourly, domestic. And the resolution is priced in advance: "the continuance of their parents' rage, / Which, but their children's end, naught could remove" (Prologue, lines 10-11). Every adaptation examined below can be graded against that price tag. The ones that kept it made tragedies. The ones that refused it made something else, often something beloved, but the refusal is always the most informative event in the record.

## Before the Text Arrived: The Tale in the Spanish World

The strangest fact in the play's Latin American prehistory is that the story circulated in Spanish before Shakespeare's version of it existed in Spanish at all, and indeed before most Spaniards could have named its author. Lope de Vega, the towering dramatist of Spain's Golden Age, wrote Castelvines y Monteses in the years around 1606 to 1612, a tragicomedy of the feuding Castelvines and Monteses of Verona drawn from the Italian novella tradition that Shakespeare also used, the line of tales running through Luigi da Porto and Matteo Bandello. Lope worked from the story's Italian sources, not from the English play, and his treatment is instructive precisely because it is independent: given the same feuding houses and the same young lovers, the Spanish stage's instincts ran toward the tragicomedy's mixed ending rather than the double tomb. The tale, in other words, entered the Spanish-speaking world already bent toward the solution Latin American melodrama would later perfect, survival through complication rather than extinction through error. Shakespeare's text would spend centuries competing, in Spanish, with a native habit of ending the story differently.

When the English play finally did approach the Spanish stage, it came disguised as a Frenchman. Jean-François Ducis, who read no English, had produced his Roméo et Juliette in 1772 by rebuilding the tragedy to French neoclassical measure, and it was Ducis, not Shakespeare, who stood behind the first Spanish versions: Dionisio Solís's Julia y Romeo of 1803 and Manuel Bernardino García Suelto's Romeo y Julieta of 1817. Jennifer Ruiz-Morgan's research on the early Spanish reception, published in the Alicante Journal of English Studies in 2022 and extended in her University of Murcia dissertation on Juliet's Spanish representations, gives these versions their proper name: refundiciones, re-foundings, the period's licensed practice of melting a foreign play down and recasting it to local rules of decorum, structure, and taste. García Suelto, as the translation historian Ángel-Luis Pujante notes, in all likelihood never read Shakespeare's play at all. His Romeo y Julieta is a translation of a translation of a synopsis, three removes from Verona, and Ruiz-Morgan shows that even so the versions played, were revived, and held the stage in Madrid into the 1830s.

### Did the first Spanish Romeo and Juliet come from Shakespeare's text?

No. The first Spanish treatments of the lovers, Solís's Julia y Romeo (1803) and García Suelto's Romeo y Julieta (1817), were refundiciones built on Jean-François Ducis's French Roméo et Juliette of 1772, and Ducis worked without English, from earlier French prose digests. Spain met the plot a generation before it met the language.

That sequence shaped the whole Hispanic reception.

The consequences of that sequence reach further than chronology. A culture that meets a plot before it meets a text treats the plot as common property and the text as one treatment among others, which is exactly the attitude visible across the whole Latin American record: Mexican cinema feels free to hand the lovers to a clown, Brazilian street theatre to cut and sing the verse, television to stretch five days into 150 episodes. Nor was the French filter a brief embarrassment. Shakespeare entered Spain in the luggage of the Bourbon century, and the first direct Spanish translation of any play, Leandro Fernández de Moratín's Hamlet of 1798, remained an isolated feat for decades while French-mediated versions dominated the stage. Víctor Balaguer's Julieta y Romeo of 1849 and his Catalan Las esposallas de la morta of 1878 extended the re-founding habit into the century's second half, in the hands of a major Catalan man of letters. Ruiz-Morgan's dissertation adds a finding that matters for everything after: Spanish adapters consistently moved Juliet to the centre, making the heroine the protagonist of versions nominally about a pair. The region's Juliets, from 1803 forward, were protagonists before they were victims.

For Latin America itself, the implication is foundational. The Spanish American republics inherited their Shakespeare through the same peninsular channel, in refundiciones, in French-derived acting versions, and in printed editions shipped from Madrid, at the very moment when the new nations were also inheriting Spain's theatrical institutions, its touring companies, and its repertory habits. The play's American career thus begins under a double sign. It is a colonial import, arriving through the empire's cultural plumbing, and it is simultaneously a story the receiving cultures had every historical excuse to treat as raw material rather than relic. Both signs stay legible in the record for two hundred years. The argument of the closing sections is that the second sign won, and that the evidence of its victory is the region's endings.

The mechanism of the catastrophe deserves the same precision, because melodrama will later be defined against it. The lovers do not die of the feud directly; they die of a failed delivery. Friar John, quarantined by plague suspicion in Mantua, reports the errand's collapse to Friar Laurence: "Unhappy fortune! By my brotherhood, / The letter was not nice, but full charge / Of dear import, and the neglecting it / May do much danger" (5.2.17-20). The tragedy is engineered as a recognition that misses its hour by minutes: Romeo hears of death but not of the feigned death, Juliet wakes after the poison has done its work, and the tomb scene becomes a sequence of arrivals each a breath too late. Shakespeare's Verona runs on messages, potions, and timings, a machinery of near misses. Hold that machinery in mind when the radionovela arrives, because the serial form is the same machinery run in reverse, an engine for making the message arrive, late, theatrical, and in time.

A note on evidence governs everything that follows, and the run of global-reception articles it closes has observed it throughout: a production, translation, or broadcast enters this account only with a title, a date, and a documentary source behind it, in scholarship, in a production archive, or, for the cigar rollers' story, in the consistent testimony of the trade's own histories, flagged as such where it is used. Latin American performance history is a field of lost promptbooks and unrecorded tours, and the temptation to fill its silences with plausible colour is the one temptation a reception history must refuse, since a single invented staging devalues every verified row around it. What survives that discipline is still, as the record will show, an abundance.

## The Nineteenth-Century Crossing: Opera, Companies, and a Havana Factory Floor

Between the refundiciones and the modern translations stretches the century in which the story became American common property, and its carriers were institutions rather than books. Italian opera companies toured the capitals of the new republics with a repertory in which the lovers already sang: Bellini's I Capuleti e i Montecchi (1830) and, from 1867, Gounod's Roméo et Juliette carried the tale into the opera houses of Mexico City, Havana, Buenos Aires, and Rio de Janeiro in a language of pure feeling that needed no translator. The spoken drama travelled in the same trunks, in the luggage of Spanish and Italian touring companies whose seasons in the great port theatres set the repertory habits of local stages. A public formed by these channels knew the story the way a public knows a rumour of its own family, intimately and without documentation, and that undocumented intimacy is the soil every later Latin American adaptation grows in. When the twentieth century's makers reached for the play, they reached for something their audiences had absorbed through the ear, in Italian, in French-derived Spanish, in prose reprints, long before a reliable text in a local language existed to be faithful to.

The century's end supplied the story with a passport of a peculiarly Hispanic kind. Spanish letters had long paired Shakespeare with Cervantes, the pairing that Ángel-Luis Pujante and Juan F. Cerdá's bibliography of Shakespeare in Spain traces to an essay of 1880 and finds confirmed in 1916, the tercentenary of both writers' deaths in the same year, 1616. The coincidence was celebrated across the Spanish-speaking world with editions, lectures, and commemorations, and its cultural work was significant for the colonies-turned-republics: if Shakespeare died Cervantes's contemporary and shared his anniversary, then Shakespeare could be claimed as a kind of co-national, a classic of the Spanish language's own golden age rather than a purely English possession. Spanish America inherited that claim with the rest of the inheritance. The play's American makers of the next century would act, again and again, as if the lovers were partly theirs by right of the calendar, and the tercentenary's rhetoric is the earliest respectable warrant for the feeling.

Cuba preserves the crossing's most beautiful document, and it is not a playbill. Havana's cigar factories maintained, from the 1860s, the institution of the lector, a reader elevated on a platform who read aloud to the torcedores, the cigar rollers, through the working day: newspapers in the morning, and in the hours the workers themselves chose, the great books. Trade histories of the Habanos industry record that Romeo and Juliet stood among the rollers' favourite texts, requested again and again from the lector's chair, and that the enthusiasm left a permanent mark on the island's material culture: when Inocencio Álvarez and Manin García founded their cigar brand in Havana in 1875, they named it Romeo y Julieta, and the lovers' balcony has looked out from its boxes ever since. A later owner, José "Pepín" Rodríguez, built the marca into a world marque and named its flagship cigar for its most famous smoker, Winston Churchill. The episode deserves its weight in any honest reception history. Before the play had a proper Cuban stage tradition, it had a Cuban workplace tradition: hundreds of workers who knew the lovers by ear, at the speed of a reader's voice, as part of the furniture of labour. The Montecristo brand, named in 1935 for Dumas's novel by the same lector route, proves the mechanism was general, but the Shakespeare case came first, and it means that in Cuba the play's naturalization was achieved not by theatres or translators but by collective listening. The continent's reception repeatedly shows this pattern, high culture arriving through popular channels and staying because the channels were loved.

## Translating the Play into Spanish: Astrana Marín and the Prose Problem

The modern Spanish text of the play begins with an act of sheer stamina. Luis Astrana Marín, a tireless Spanish man of letters, translated the complete works of Shakespeare directly from the English and published them with the Madrid house of Aguilar in 1929 as the Obras completas, an edition whose title page announced its claim: the first integral version from the English. Nothing on that scale had existed in Spanish before, and the edition's reach matched its ambition. Translation historians describe Astrana Marín's Works as the most popular and widely circulated Shakespeare in the language, reprinted for decades and read on both shores of the Atlantic, which means that for generations of Latin American readers, students, and actors, La tragedia de Romeo y Julieta was Astrana Marín's prose. The region's default Shakespeare was a single Spaniard's marathon.

Prose is the operative word, and it carries a cost the record keeps noting. Astrana Marín aimed at semantic fidelity and paid for it, as the scholarship on his method observes, by surrendering the verse: his Shakespeare speaks accurate, expanded, explanatory prose, in which the lovers' shared sonnet at their first meeting (1.5) loses the click of its interlocking rhymes and Juliet's "What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other name would smell as sweet" (2.2.43-44) arrives as information rather than music. For a reading public, the bargain was defensible and even noble; it gave the Spanish-speaking world the whole canon, reliably, in one man's lifetime. For a stage, it was a standing temptation to treat the text as a scenario, since prose that has already given up the verse invites directors to give up more. The later history of Spanish translation can be written as a rebellion against precisely that invitation. José María Valverde's Teatro completo for Planeta (1967-68) stayed in prose but restored a poet's ear to the phrasing. Ángel-Luis Pujante's annotated editions re-established the distinction between verse and prose that Shakespeare's own text observes, and the Instituto Shakespeare at the University of Valencia, the team project associated with Manuel Ángel Conejero, built its translations for Cátedra's Letras Universales line on the same principle: verse as verse, prose as prose, with the scholarship printed alongside. Latin American companies and classrooms inherited this whole shelf, peninsular prose and peninsular verse alike, and the inheritance explains a rhythm visible across the region's record: long stretches in which the play is staged from imported Spanish texts, punctuated by eruptions in which a local writer decides that the continent needs a version of its own.

Those eruptions are the subject of the next section, but the imported-text era deserves its due first, because it built the audience the eruptions addressed. Nineteenth-century Spanish American stages received the lovers through touring companies and peninsular repertory; the story's familiarity across the region, the quality that later lets a Cuban radionovela assume it and a Mexican film parody presume it, was manufactured by those unglamorous decades of refundiciones, opera-house seasons, and Aguilar reprints. By the time the region's own translators took up the play, they were not introducing a stranger. They were contesting the custody of a family possession.

The prose problem can be made concrete at the play's most famous hinge of form. When the lovers meet at Capulet's feast, Shakespeare writes them a shared sonnet, fourteen lines passed between two voices, pilgrim and saint, hands and lips, until the form itself performs the mutuality it describes: "If I profane with my unworthiest hand / This holy shrine, the gentle sin is this: / My lips, two blushing pilgrims, ready stand / To smooth that rough touch with a tender kiss" (1.5.92-95). A prose rendering can carry every fact in those lines, the metaphor, the daring, the negotiation, and lose the event, because the event is two people discovering that they speak in matching verse, that the rhyme scheme has paired them before the plot dares to. Translation scholarship on the Spanish tradition makes the general point against Astrana Marín's method: semantic fidelity purchased by expansion, the original's metre surrendered, its puns and line-endings flattened into explanation. The defense is equally real and should be recorded: a prose complete works is a library before it is a script, and the Spanish-speaking world's library, for the first time in 1929, held the whole Shakespeare. The stage's revenge came gradually. Valverde's verse-aware prose for Planeta in 1967-68 restored a poet's cadence to the reading text; Pujante's annotated editions and the Valencia Instituto Shakespeare's versions for Cátedra rebuilt the text on the principle that the play's own alternation of verse and prose is meaning, not decoration, so that a Spanish Romeo of the late twentieth century could again hear the sonnet click shut. Latin American production inherited both shelves and the choice between them, and the choice mattered most where the region's own stages were weakest institutionally and strongest poetically, which is the paradox the next sections keep meeting.

## The Poets Seize the Play: Neruda in Chile, León Felipe in Mexico

The single most consequential Latin American Romeo and Juliet of the twentieth century was made by a poet who treated translation as a form of authorship. Pablo Neruda, Chile's Nobel-bound Communist bard, turned to the play in the quatercentenary year of Shakespeare's birth and produced the Spanish version that bears his name. The documentary record is unusually firm for the premiere: the Instituto de Teatro de la Universidad de Chile staged Neruda's Romeo and Juliet in Santiago on 18 October 1964, and the standard Neruda bibliography lists the production among his works for the stage, describing the text, tellingly, as adapted from Shakespeare's play. The word matters. From the first night, the Chilean record classified Neruda's version not as a crib but as a poet's re-creation, a Romeo y Julieta whose Spanish would sound like Neruda because Neruda had written it.

### Why did Neruda translate Romeo and Juliet for the quatercentenary?

Because the play staged his lifelong subject in miniature: young, life-giving love against an old public order organized by inherited hatred. Gregary J. Racz argues that Neruda treated the tragedy as an exemplar of his views about society, the feuding houses standing for the entrenched powers that crush the future, so the version reads as poetic politics.

The 1964 version, on Racz's showing, was never offered as a neutral rendering, and its first audience was not asked to receive it as one.

Racz's reading, published in the Kliman and Santos volume, gives the adjudication this article owes its readers, because the volume prints a counterweight a few chapters away. Juan J. Zaro's essay examines León Felipe, the Spanish poet who spent his exile in Mexico producing free Spanish versions of Shakespeare, and finds in them the opposite pole: a spiritual rather than political approach, the plays read as stations of the soul rather than diagrams of society. Two poet-translators, two exiles' and militants' Shakespeares, one political and one spiritual. The honest verdict is that the disagreement dissolves at the level of method and survives only at the level of creed. Neruda and León Felipe perform the identical operation: each subordinates Shakespeare's text to a fully formed personal vision and expects the play to be grateful. What differs is the vision doing the subordinating, Neruda's historical materialism against León Felipe's religious inwardness. Latin American translation of the play, at its highest level, is therefore not a service industry. It is a succession of strong poets annexing Verona to their own countries of the mind, and the annexation is the region's characteristic contribution to the play's world history.

Chile supplies a second exhibit that confirms the pattern by extending it to another play. Nicanor Parra, Neruda's great antipode and the inventor of antipoetry, translated King Lear in the years after the Pinochet dictatorship fell, and Catherine Boyle's essay in the same volume reads that Lear as a creature of the transition, its language deliberately dismantling the monumental register in which Chilean culture had been required to speak. Parra's Lear stands at passage in an article on the lovers, but the family resemblance is the point: in Chile, translating Shakespeare became a way for the country's defining poets to argue about the country's condition under the cover of a classic. Neruda's Romeo and Juliet belongs to that national habit as much as to the play's bibliography. And the version travelled: Spanish-language companies beyond Chile took it up, and the Latinx Shakespeares digital archive tracks later productions of Neruda's text in the United States, evidence that a translation made for Santiago in 1964 had become, within the Spanish-speaking theatre, the region's exportable Romeo.

A caution belongs in the record, and the accuracy of this article requires it plainly: Neruda's version has also been handled roughly by the standards of literal fidelity, and no honest account should present it as a scholar's crib. It is a poet's version, loose where its maker's voice wanted room, and its authority in the Spanish-language theatre rests on theatrical life rather than philological exactness. That division of authority, stage over page, turns out to be the regional rule in Portuguese as well.

Two further features of the Chilean case locate it in the region's deeper currents. The Instituto de Teatro de la Universidad de Chile was the institution that had professionalized Chilean drama, a university theatre with a national brief, so Neruda's version arrived not as a commercial venture but as an act of the republic of letters at its most official: the country's greatest living poet handing its national university stage a classic remade in the country's own Spanish. The quatercentenary supplied the occasion, and occasions of that kind, centenaries and quatercentenaries, recur throughout the region's record as the respectable pretexts under which annexations are performed, from the 1916 commemorations that paired Shakespeare with Cervantes across the Hispanic world to Brazil's complete works timed for 2016. Anniversaries license poets to seize classics. The second feature is the version's durability as repertory rather than as print: the Latinx Shakespeares archive tracks Neruda's text travelling to Spanish-language stages in the United States in 1965 and 1979, within a year and within fifteen years of the Santiago premiere, carried by the same diasporic circuits that move the region's music and its exiles. A translation made to argue with Chile's own order proved exportable wherever Spanish-speaking audiences recognized the argument's shape, an old order organized by inherited hatred and a young love arraigned before it. Racz's political reading, arrived at from the text, is confirmed by the itinerary.

## Brazil in Portuguese: Pennafort's Verse, Heliodora's Complete Works, the Cordel String

Brazil received the play through the same French-filtered nineteenth century as its Spanish-speaking neighbours, with Portuguese versions trailing French models and Italian opera seasons carrying the story to Rio de Janeiro and São Paulo before Brazilian translators made it theirs. The impresario system that carried it has a name and a scholarly question attached. José Roberto O'Shea's essay in the Kliman and Santos volume asks whether the father of Brazilian theatre can be an impresario who performed Shakespeare rather than encouraging native writers, a pointed reference to João Caetano dos Santos, the actor-manager whose companies dominated the Rio stage in the middle decades of the nineteenth century and whose repertory brought the European classics, Shakespeare among them, to Brazilian audiences as commercial theatre. The question matters because it states the Brazilian starting condition exactly: the national stage was built by a man whose genius was importation, and the long Brazilian argument about whether Shakespeare was a foundation or a foreign occupying force begins with him. The lovers reached Rio, on the evidence of the repertory system O'Shea describes, as part of that imported stock, in the prose-and-translation traffic of the opera-house century, and they stayed in the stock while the century turned. Brazil's Shakespeare is therefore, from its first documents, a theatre of managers and translators rather than of authors, which may be why the country's mature contributions to the play's history are precisely a translator's verse, a company's staging, and a chapbook's retelling: the forms of the people who carry plays, not of the people who first write them.

The Brazilian difference emerges in the twentieth century, and it is a difference of verse. Where the Spanish world's standard text long arrived in prose, Brazil's classic Romeo and Juliet is a poem: the verse translation by Onestaldo de Pennafort, the rendering that generations of Brazilian actors learned and that, decades after its making, a street-theatre company from Minas Gerais would choose as the verbal bedrock for the region's most famous production. Pennafort's text gave Brazilian Portuguese a Romeo that could be sung as well as spoken, and the Brazilian record is unintelligible without that fact, because the country's two great popularizations of the play, the cordel chapbook and the street production, both run on rhythm.

The scholarly summit of Brazilian translation came later and wore a critic's hat. Bárbara Heliodora (1923-2015), for half a century Brazil's leading Shakespeare authority and its most feared theatre critic, devoted her life's late work to translating all thirty-eight plays; the complete Teatro Completo appeared from Nova Aguilar in 2016, the quatercentenary of Shakespeare's death, in three volumes revised and completed for publication by the scholar Liana de Camargo Leão. Heliodora stated her method with a musician's clarity: to search for a fluent Brazilian Portuguese music and rhythm that might stand as the nearest equivalent to the original's. Set the sentence beside Astrana Marín's programme and the two Iberian traditions divide cleanly. The Spanish classic promises fidelity of sense in prose; the Brazilian classic promises fidelity of music in verse. Audiences formed by Heliodora's standard hear the play as scored language, which is one reason Brazilian adapters reach so naturally for song when they remake it.

Below the summit and the stage text runs the third Brazilian channel, the cheapest and in some ways the most revealing: literatura de cordel, the rhymed chapbooks of the Northeast sold hanging from strings at market stalls, from which the form takes its name. Aimara da Cunha Resende's essay in the Kliman and Santos volume traces the play's absorption into the native forms of cordel and the telenovela, and printed cordel versions of Romeu e Julieta circulate in the Brazilian record as catalogued fact: the lovers' story retold in the sextilha stanzas and woodcut covers of the fairground press, priced for buyers who would never open Nova Aguilar. The cordel matters analytically because it proves the play's plot had completed the journey from imported classic to folk property. A story hangs on a market string only when the seller can assume the buyer already half-knows it. Machado de Assis, Brazil's greatest novelist, had absorbed Shakespeare into the bloodstream of Portuguese prose a century earlier, as José Luiz Passos's essay in the same volume demonstrates, but cordel is the absorption at street level, and street level is where this article's Brazilian story culminates.

The cordel's mechanics reward a closer look, because they explain what kind of object the play became in Brazilian hands. A cordel pamphlet is a small, cheap, woodcut-covered booklet of rhymed narrative verse, composed in the stanza forms of Northeastern oral poetry, printed on rough paper, and sold for pennies at fairs and markets, where the poet-seller might sing the opening stanzas to gather a crowd. To enter cordel, a story had to survive reduction to its narrative skeleton and rebirth in rhyme, stripped of everything a listener could not hold by ear. The lovers passed that test completely: houses, hatred, secret marriage, potion, tomb, each element a stanza's worth of fate. Rocha's 2017 study of the play's cordel afterlife in Brazilian classrooms shows the chapbooks still working pedagogically, teachers using the rhymed Romeu e Julieta to carry literacy and literature together in the Northeast's schools, which means the cordel is not a quaint survival but a functioning distribution network that the Nova Aguilar volumes never reached. A reception history that counts only editions and productions will miss the play's largest Brazilian readership entirely, the fairground audience for whom Shakespeare's name mattered less than the story's rhymes. The map at this article's centre records the cordel as a case in its own right for exactly that reason: it is the point where the region's reception becomes indistinguishable from its folklore, and where the mestizo thesis stops being a metaphor.

## Mexico Laughs First: Cantinflas and the Verse-Speaking Clown

Mexican cinema's encounter with the play is the stuff of national legend, and for once the legend is fully documented. Romeo y Julieta, produced by Posa Films and directed by Miguel M. Delgado, opened on 3 September 1943 with Mario Moreno, Cantinflas, as its Romeo and María Elena Marqués as its Juliet, from a script by Jaime Salvador, and with its dialogue written in verse. The film is a parody, and it is also, in the judgement of the scholar who has studied Mexican Shakespeare most closely, something more consequential. Alfredo Michel Modenessi of the National University of Mexico, in his essay on the ghostly presence of Shakespeare in Mexican cinema and in the Kliman and Santos volume, establishes the stark census on which any account must rest: in the whole history of Mexican film, exactly two pictures derive integrally from a Shakespeare play, Cantinflas's Romeo y Julieta of 1943 and Iván Lipkies's Huapango of 2003, an Othello transposed to the world of huapango music and dance. The lovers' film stood alone for sixty years as Mexican cinema's one full-length act of Shakespearean appropriation, and the form the appropriation took was a clown in verse.

### Is the Cantinflas film a parody or an adaptation?

Both, in sequence: parody is its method and adaptation its achievement. Modenessi calls the film an unabashed adaptation, Mexican cinema's sole integral Shakespeare derivation for six decades, and its comedy works by collision, the verse of a courtly tragedy placed in the mouth of Cantinflas's pelado, the penniless urban trickster whose verbal floods turn elevated lines into negotiations with poverty.

The cantinfleo, his famous torrential evasion-speech, does the rest of the work, and hunger and the police are never far from the negotiation.

The class content is not an interpreter's embroidery; it is the film's documented scholarly reading. Luis Nitrihual Valdebenito and José Galera, analysing the picture in Arte, Individuo y Sociedad in 2011, argue that Cantinflas's Romeo y Julieta builds its symbolic world on the class division of early twentieth-century Mexico, using satire and parody to restage the impossible-love plot as a story about who may speak well, who may marry up, and who must improvise survival in the gaps of other people's rhetoric. The feud of the houses becomes, in Mexican dress, the distance between the moneyed world of the Marqués Juliet and the street world of the pelado Romeo, and the verse dialogue sharpens the joke into a thesis: give the underclass hero the aristocracy's own metre and the metre itself starts telling the truth about class. Nor should the ending surprise any reader who has followed this article's argument. A Cantinflas vehicle ends in Cantinflas's element, comic resolution; the tomb is not in the clown's contract. Mexico's foundational Romeo is therefore the region's purest early specimen of the melodrama rule stated at the outset: the popular form that carries the play also sets its price, and in 1943 the price was the tragedy itself, exchanged for the most durable comic performance in Spanish-language cinema.

The casting completes the argument the verse begins. María Elena Marqués was no comic foil but one of Mexican cinema's leading dramatic actresses, and the film plays her Juliet with a straightness that lets the tragedy's register survive inside the parody as a kind of tuning fork: her scenes hold the pitch of the original while Cantinflas, around her, demonstrates what the popular stage does to pitch. The result is a double exposure that Mexican audiences were uniquely prepared to read, since the carpa tradition had always worked by exactly this layering, a straight romance on the surface and a running commentary of hunger and insolence underneath. Analysts of the film return to this structure because it explains the picture's durability better than its jokes do. Nitrihual and Galera's reading, in which the film cements symbolic universes about class division in the Mexico of the early twentieth century, depends on the contrast: the moneyed world speaks its verse as property, the pelado speaks the same verse as theft, and the audience's laughter registers the difference as knowledge. A Romeo who must steal the language he courts in is the feud rewritten as a fact about who owns eloquence, and no production in the region states the class reading of the play more economically.

The film's afterlife confirms its standing. Delgado and Salvador made it as one of a run of literary parodies for Posa Films, following Los tres mosqueteros of 1942, and it circulated across the Spanish-speaking world with the rest of the Cantinflas catalogue, so that for millions of viewers from Buenos Aires to Madrid the first Romeo they ever met was a Mexican tramp in doublet and hose talking his way out of a duel. That is mestizo Shakespeare at industrial scale, a generation before the term was coined for it.

The figure doing the appropriating matters as much as the act. Modenessi's work stresses that Cantinflas came out of the carpa, the travelling tent theatre of Mexico City's poor districts, and carried that popular stage's licence with him into the cinema: the carpa's whole art was the irreverent reprocessing of respectable material, zarzuela, opera, and classic drama alike, for audiences who owned none of it and were therefore free to enjoy all of it without reverence. The 1943 film is the carpa's method at studio scale, and it helps explain the peculiar shape Modenessi finds across Mexican culture, where Shakespeare's presence is, in his essay's governing image, ghostly: everywhere as allusion, joke, and schoolroom spectre, almost nowhere as integral staging or filming. A culture whose foundational encounter with the tragedies is a beloved parody has been given permanent permission to treat the canon as raw material, and Mexico's later makers accepted the permission largely in the fragmentary modes the census records. Even the title of his study, with its hunt for a "Mr GoodWill" in the Mexican western Rancho Grande, makes the point by inventory: Shakespeare haunts Mexican screens in traces and quotations, while the single time he was embodied whole for sixty years, he was embodied by a clown, in verse, to general and lasting delight. Delgado's film is thus both an adaptation and an explanation of why there were no others like it: after Cantinflas, the play in Mexico was already national property, and national property is quoted, not re-imported.

## Brazil Takes the Play Outdoors: Grupo Galpão's Circus Verona

If Mexico's emblematic Romeo belongs to the cinema, Brazil's belongs to the street. Grupo Galpão, founded in Belo Horizonte in 1982 and rooted in the travelling-player traditions of Minas Gerais, began work on Romeu e Julieta in 1991 and premiered the production in 1992 under the direction of Gabriel Villela, with dramaturgy by Cacá Brandão. The MIT Global Shakespeares archive and its Brazilian sister site, whose production notes come from the scholar Anna Stegh Camati, document the recipe precisely. The company took Pennafort's verse translation and reworked it extensively, cutting heavily, transmuting speeches into songs and rhythms of the Brazilian backlands, the sertão, and mixing Shakespeare's syntax with the verbal world of João Guimarães Rosa, the modernist master of the Minas Gerais interior. Around the text they built the apparatus of popular festival: street theatre, circus performance, commedia dell'arte, live music, and a Verona conjured from the cultural imaginary of Minas Gerais rather than from any Italian postcard.

### Why does Grupo Galpão perform the play on stilts?

Because Villela's company took from Peter Brook the reading of the tragedy as a tragedy of precipitation, of bodies and decisions moving too fast to be recalled, and stilts make precipitation visible: lovers and fighters perched where every step is a small fall arrested and every embrace a balance kept by luck.

The archive notes the borrowing directly. The device is a thesis about the play's mechanics performed by the actors' ankles, haste with the risk left in.

Two decisions define the production's place in this article's argument. The first is subtraction: as Junia C. M. Alves's early scholarly account records, the staging keeps Verona and Mantua and nearly the whole dramatis personae, but Paris is cut entire, the county whose survival in the text embodies the marriage market's respectable alternative. A Verona without Paris is a Verona where Juliet's choice is stripped of its social alibi; the folk frame sharpens rather than softens the play's economics of marriage. The second decision is the ending. Galpão's production, for all its carnival surface, plays the catastrophe: the lovers die, in circus idiom, before festival audiences, in public squares, and the record shows audiences accepting the blow inside the laughter rather than instead of it. Here, then, is the counter-case to Mexico's: a popular form, as popular as any in the hemisphere, that refuses melodrama's consolation and keeps the Prologue's price. The difference between the two cases is not national character. It is the difference between a star vehicle, which must return its star to the audience intact, and an ensemble street rite, which can afford to bury its lovers because the company, not the couple, is the continuing institution.

The production's itinerary made it the hemisphere's ambassador. David S. George, reviewing the staging at the Festival de Curitiba in 1993, praised it for resurrecting the play from the dust of accumulated romantic clichés. In 2000 Galpão brought Romeu e Julieta to Shakespeare's Globe in London, where the festive street effect survived translation into the wooden O, and in 2012 the company returned for the Globe to Globe festival, the season of thirty-seven plays in thirty-seven languages mounted for the World Shakespeare Festival, performing in Brazilian Portuguese as the festival's only returning company. The London theatre press of the festival called it, in the season's own promotional phrase, perhaps the Americas' most famous production of the play. The claim can stand, because no rival from the hemisphere matches the combination of a single staging, three decades of touring, and a homecoming to the play's first address.

Honesty about the record requires the dissenting voice, and the 2012 London revival supplies it. The arts desk reviewer of the festival staging was left cold, complaining that the carnival register never yielded, that Juliet's death was performed as a comic dying swan, and that a production without shifts of tone had flattened the tragedy it carried. Jacquelyn Bessell's chapter on the production, written from attendance at both the 2000 and 2012 Globe performances, is the advocate's answer, a critic's pilgrimage record that nonetheless concedes the terms of the quarrel. The quarrel itself is the analytical gold. Even the production that keeps the deaths cannot fully protect them from its own festive grammar; the melodramatic and carnivalesque pressure this article has tracked does not disappear when the ending is preserved, it migrates into the tone, and the feud between the play's catastrophe and the region's performance joy is fought, in the Galpão case, inside a single death scene. The verdict on Galpão is therefore double and must stay double: the most complete Latin American Romeo on record, and the clearest demonstration that completeness costs a fight the production visibly does not always win.

Alves's account, written when the production was still young, supplies the analytic vocabulary its later fame would confirm. Her title calls Galpão's staging an inverted circus, and the inversion is precise: the circus normally promises that skill defeats gravity and nobody really falls, while Galpão's circus keeps the skill and restores the falling, so that acrobatic delight and mortal risk occupy the same bodies at the same time. She records the method behind the effect, a company working by addition, deletion, substitution, and inversion on Pennafort's text until the play that emerged was, in her judgement, a new play that nonetheless perpetuated the original's qualities. The regional songs she documents are not decoration pasted over the verse; they mark the beginnings and ends of scenes, carry transitions, and set the festive temperature inside which the catastrophe must land, the way a chorus of neighbours might carry a funeral through a town that refuses to stop singing. Read against the melodrama argument, Alves's analysis shows the production's real mechanism: Galpão did not resist the region's festive and melodramatic energies, it conscripted them, and then made them carry a weight, two corpses in the square, that those energies usually exist to lift. The strain the London dissent heard in the death scene is the sound of that conscription at its limit.

The production's sheer longevity is part of its evidence. Bessell's chapter traces a staging in and out of the company's repertoire across two decades, revived for anniversaries and festivals, performed in squares, circuses, and proscenium houses, and still mounted in the company's home city in 2017, when Galpão played it in Belo Horizonte's Praça do Papa. Longevity of that order changes what a production is: not an interpretation offered to a season's audience but a piece of civic furniture, a version of the play that Brazilian audiences of different generations have grown up attending, the way earlier cultures attended a saint's day. The London notices across the two visits even trace the shift, the 2000 appearance received as a revelation and the 2012 return reviewed, by the disappointed critic quoted above, with the familiarity owed to an institution. Institutions get audited rather than discovered. That the audit could find the death scene wanting while conceding everything else is the normal fate of a classic, and Galpão's Romeu e Julieta had, by 2012, achieved precisely the status its makers' method predicted: it had stopped being a Brazilian adaptation of an English play and become a Brazilian classic with an English ancestor, judged by the standards audiences apply to their own possessions.

## Screen Feuds with Local Names: A Diminutive in Buenos Aires, a Derby in São Paulo

Between Cantinflas and the football comedy stands a small Argentine witness that the regional pattern was continental, not local to two big industries. Romeo y Julita, directed by Enrique Carreras and released on 16 March 1954, in the classical era of Argentine cinema, retells the play with Alfredo Barbieri, Amelia Vargas, Esteban Serrador, and Susana Campos, from a screenplay by Rafael Beltrán on a plot by Miguel de Calasanz. The title alone files the film's genre report: Julita, the diminutive, Juliet shrunk to an affectionate household size, in a national cinema whose golden age ran on musical comedy and neighbourhood melodrama. Argentina's classical screen received the tragedy the way its industry received everything, by domesticating the scale. The lovers of Verona became porteño sweethearts, and the feud became the kind of family opposition an Argentine audience of 1954 would have recognized from its own comedies of manners, sharp enough to structure a plot, soluble enough to end a film.

The diminutive deserves a slower reading, because it connects the film to the oldest pattern in this article. Ruiz-Morgan's research on the Spanish stage found that adapter after adapter moved Juliet to the centre of the story, making the heroine the protagonist of versions that carry both names; Carreras's title performs the same operation in miniature and in the opposite emotional direction. Shakespeare's Juliet is enlarged by the Spanish tradition into a protagonist; the Argentine screen's Julita is shrunk by the diminutive into a sweetheart, and the two gestures are the same gesture, the local culture seizing the right to resize the heroine to its own scale of feeling. A diminutive is not disrespect in Spanish; it is possession, the grammatical form of endearment a family applies to what it has adopted. The 1954 film thus announces, before its first scene, that the tragedy has been adopted into an Argentine household and will be treated with a household's rights, including the right to be spared. Between García Suelto's re-founded Juliet of 1817 and Carreras's diminished Julita of 1954 runs a single line: the Spanish-speaking stage and screen never stopped regarding the heroine as theirs to re-found, enlarge, or tuck in, and the play's survival in the region is inseparable from that proprietary tenderness.

Brazil's screen industry ran the same operation in the same decades, in the native form Thais Flores N. Diniz's essay in the Kliman and Santos volume takes as its subject: the chanchada, the cheap, exuberant musical comedies produced in Rio de Janeiro, above all by the Atlântida studio from the 1940s, starring the great popular clowns Oscarito and Grande Otelo. The chanchada's stock in trade was parody of prestige culture, Hollywood genres and literary classics alike, processed for carnival-season audiences, and Diniz champions the political function of that parody inside the native form: the chanchada let Brazil's poor laugh the imported canon down to their own size, which is the Cantinflas operation in a Brazilian accent. No integral chanchada Romeo stands in the record at the strength of the Mexican film, and the map does not invent one; the form's importance here is atmospheric and structural. It proves that by mid-century the region possessed, in both its great film industries, a standing popular machinery for digesting classics into local comedy, and that the play's passage into that machinery was a continental habit rather than a Mexican accident. The tragedy's dignity survived the century in Latin America not because the region's popular forms spared it, but because its story was strong enough to remain legible after the forms had finished with it.

Half a century later and a continent's width away, the same operation produced the region's most analytically perfect localization. O Casamento de Romeu e Julieta, released by Globo Filmes on 18 March 2005 and directed by Bruno Barreto, adapts Mário Prata's novel Palmeiras, um caso de amor (2002), with Luana Piovani as Julieta and Marco Ricca as Romeu, and its premise deserves slow statement because every element earns its place. São Paulo's football rivalry between Palmeiras and Corinthians is among the most totalizing allegiances in Brazilian life, inherited, territorial, and policed by families. Julieta is the daughter of a Palmeiras director and herself a women's football coach; Romeu is an ophthalmologist and a leader of the Corinthians' organized supporters. The film's feud work is exact: where Shakespeare's houses are "both alike in dignity" (Prologue, line 1), the clubs are alike in everything, two mass institutions of the same city dividing households from within. Romeu, to court Julieta, feigns conversion to Palmeiras, a lie about allegiance that functions precisely as the play's disguises and secret marriage do, a private truth maintained inside a public falsehood, at the price of eventual exposure.

And the ending announces itself in the title: O Casamento, the marriage. Barreto's film is a romantic comedy, generously made and popular, and it resolves the derby feud in a wedding, with the families reconciled across the aisle. Set beside Galpão's street tragedy, the film completes a controlled experiment that no scholar could have designed better. Same country, same play, same folk energies, two forms: the ensemble street rite keeps the tomb, the industrial screen comedy keeps the couple. The variable is not Brazil. The variable is the form's contract with its audience, and in the commercial cinema of the region that contract, from Carreras's diminutive onward, has been melodrama's: intensity in the middle, recognition and repair at the end. Shakespeare film scholarship has noticed the film on other grounds too, reading its Julieta as a strong adult agent rather than a thirteen-year-old victim and its Lady Capulet figure as a fount of maternal wisdom who finally shames the men into sense, but for this article's ledger the salient line is structural. Brazil's screen Romeo is the play with the Prologue's price tag torn off, and the tear is clean, deliberate, and announced on the poster.

The film also redistributes the play's moral authority in a way the map's columns cannot show and the analysis should not omit. Shakespeare's Verona gives the work of reconciliation to corpses and to a prince; nobody living persuades the houses to stop. Barreto's São Paulo gives it to the women. His Julieta is an adult professional, a coach in the men's game, whose competence the film treats as ordinary fact, and the reconciliation is driven through the Capulet mother, Isabella in the film's naming, whose knowledge and nerve shame the men of both camps into recognizing what their hatred costs. The pattern rhymes with the Spanish tradition's heroine-centred Juliets and with Caignet's Mamá Dolores, the servant whose moral intelligence carries El derecho de nacer: across the region's popular forms, the work the tragedy assigns to death is reassigned to women's knowledge. That reassignment is melodrama's deepest revision of the play, deeper than the saved ending it produces, because it changes the tragedy's anthropology. In Shakespeare, the young die because the old cannot learn in time. In the region's melodramatic line, the old can be taught, by mothers, servants, and daughters, and a world in which the old can learn is a world that does not need the tomb. The wedding in the title is the consequence; the teachable fathers are the cause.

## The Telenovela Machine: Caignet's Cuba, Martín-Barbero's Matrix, 150 Episodes in Buenos Aires

To understand why the region's dominant narrative industry treats the lovers as it does, leave the play for one section and examine the machine. Latin American melodrama's founding text is a Cuban radio serial: El derecho de nacer, written by Félix B. Caignet and first broadcast by CMQ in Havana on 1 April 1948. Its plot is a machine for generating the region's master emotions: a Havana heiress bears a child outside marriage; her authoritarian father orders the infant destroyed; the family's Black servant, Mamá Dolores, saves the boy and raises him in poverty; the child grows into a brilliant doctor who, in the fullness of the serial's coincidences, saves the life of the very grandfather who condemned him, and the revelation of his birth reconciles the house. Caignet, a pioneer of Cuban radio who said without embarrassment that he wrote to make audiences cry, had built the template from which the telenovela would industrialize itself: the hidden birth, the class and race divide inside one family, the long deferral of recognition, and an ending in which the secret, once spoken, repairs the world. The serial was remade across the region for decades, filmed in Mexico in 1952 under Zacarías Gómez Urquiza and produced by Televisa as a landmark telenovela in 1981, and its dramaturgy, not any imported model, is the grammar into which the play's story would eventually be fed.

The theorist who explains the machine is the Colombian philosopher Jesús Martín-Barbero, whose De los medios a las mediaciones (1987) founded the modern study of Latin American popular narrative. Martín-Barbero reads melodrama as the region's cultural matrix, the narrative operating system through which popular audiences process the shocks of modernization, and he formalizes its cast with a memorable economy: four master feelings, fear, enthusiasm, pain, and laughter, embodied in four figures, the Traitor, the Just One, the Victim, and the Fool, whose collisions mix the romance, the epic, the tragedy, and the comedy in a single serial body. His sharpest sentence for the purposes of this article holds that melodrama pursues an intensity achievable only at the cost of complexity. A matrix that trades complexity for intensity, that runs on hidden births and climactic recognitions, and that belongs to the audience as a birthright rather than to a canon as a loan: set the Prologue's bargain beside it and the collision is exact. Shakespeare's play locates its catastrophe in a failed recognition, the letter that never arrives, the awakening that comes minutes late. Caignet's matrix locates its climax in a successful one. The tomb scene and the recognition scene are the same room with the door opening in opposite directions.

Brooks's theory, which the verdict section will take up in full, already casts light here: melodrama for Brooks is the mode a culture uses when the sacred guarantees have withdrawn and moral meanings must be made legible by other means, by gesture, music, tableau, and excess, a "moral occult" operating beneath the surfaces of ordinary life. Martín-Barbero's contribution was to give that mode a sociology and an address in the Americas: the matrix is what the popular classes of a modernizing continent actually think with, the narrative form in which migration, money, race, and the new city become emotionally processable. Mexican cinema's golden age ran on the same fuel in the very years of the Cantinflas film, its great melodramas of mothers, fallen women, and neighbourhood honour building a national audience in parallel with the comedies, and the chronicler Carlos Monsiváis spent a career insisting that these popular forms, melodrama and the pelado's verbal art alike, were Mexico's real national literature, the place where the country told itself the truth in the only registers it fully trusted. The play's Mexican career sits exactly at that intersection: parodied by the tent theatre's heir in 1943, surrounded by a cinema whose serious wing was melodrama, and absorbed by audiences trained by both to read heightened feeling as information rather than as decoration. When the radionovela and then the telenovela industrialized that training, the lovers' story was always going to be among the materials the industry reached for, and the industry's terms were set long before the story arrived.

The template's portability is documented in the region's other great television culture. Brazil's TV Tupi turned Caignet's serial into O Direito de Nascer, which premiered on 7 December 1964 and ran for 160 episodes, adapted by Teixeira Filho and Thalma de Oliveira, and is remembered in Brazilian television history as the young medium's first major telenovela success, remade again in 1978. The Cuban matrix thus became the Brazilian industry's foundation stone in the very years when Brazilian theatre was preparing its own Romeo in the streets, and the two facts belong in one paragraph: the same culture, in the same decades, received the lovers twice, once through a form that would teach its screens to save couples and once through a company that would insist on burying them. Brazil's Shakespeare record is unintelligible without holding both receptions together, and the telenovela grammar consolidated by the Caignet adaptations is the background against which every later Brazilian screen Romeo, football comedy included, strikes its bargain with the tomb.

The experiment has been run, and the result is on the Argentine record. Romeo y Julieta, a telenovela produced for Canal 9 in Buenos Aires and premiered on 14 March 2007, adapted the play directly for the serial form: 150 episodes, a modern-dress setting that keeps Verona as a picturesque backdrop, Brenda Gandini as Julieta Caporale, aged sixteen, and Elías Viñoles as Romeo Montero, aged seventeen, the surnames domesticated into near-neighbours of the originals. The production drew modest ratings, a fact the record should keep, because it refines rather than weakens the analysis: even the industry's own Romeo could not make the play's five days fill 150 episodes without becoming something else, a teen serial about young love under family pressure, in which the title does the tragedy's work and the episodes do the telenovela's. What the serial form kept from the play was the brand and the geometry, two houses, two teenagers, one prohibition. What it could not keep, because the form's economics and its matrix both forbade it, was the tomb. A telenovela may kill secondary figures and torment its leads for months, but its leads are its capital, and its final chapter owes the audience the recognition scene Caignet patented, the secret spoken, the houses reconciled, the couple ratified.

### Why must the telenovela's lovers survive?

Because the form's two engines both run on deferral and discharge. Martín-Barbero's melodramatic matrix climaxes in recognition, the hidden truth spoken at last, which requires the lovers alive to hear it; and serial economics require the couple whose courtship fills 150 episodes to remain available for the promised finale. Death ends deferral.

The telenovela therefore inherits the play's geometry and reverses its ending as a condition of manufacture.

Aimara da Cunha Resende's essay on the play in cordel and telenovela forms, cited earlier for the chapbooks, makes the complementary point from the audience's side: the story survives in these forms because its emotional architecture, young love against the assembled prohibitions of family and rank, is the very architecture the region's popular narrative had already built for itself. The play did not teach Latin America that architecture. It arrived to find the architecture standing, in the radionovela, in the chanchada film comedies whose political uses of parody Thais Flores N. Diniz's essay in the same volume examines, in the fairground chapbook, and it was welcomed as a distinguished foreign relative of a native family. That welcome, warm and total, is the deepest reason the region's Romeos so often live: the house they marry into has rules, and the first rule is that the story owes its hearers a recognition, not a corpse.

## Prospero's Shadow: The Caliban Objection and the Appropriation Answer

Every account of Shakespeare in Latin America must pass one checkpoint, and the checkpoint is an essay about a different play. In 1971 the Cuban poet and critic Roberto Fernández Retamar published Calibán, the most influential Latin American reading of Shakespeare ever written, in which he seized on the enslaved islander of The Tempest and declared him the symbol of "our America": colonized, robbed of his island, taught the colonizer's language, and left with one profit from the lesson, the knowledge of how to curse. Retamar was inverting a famous predecessor: José Enrique Rodó's Ariel (1900) had cast Ariel as the spirit of Latin America's high culture against a materialist North figured as Caliban, and Retamar reversed the charges, claiming the monster as the region's true emblem and Prospero as the colonizing power whose books and language arrived as instruments of rule. The essay's frame governs the whole field's conscience. If Shakespeare enters Latin America as Prospero's cargo, then every local Romeo is a suspect object, its popularity evidence of cultural occupation, its translators collaborators or resisters in a drama of empire.

The argument Retamar inverted had been running for seventy years, and the inversion only makes sense against its prehistory. In 1898, in the shadow of the Spanish-American War, the Nicaraguan poet Rubén Darío published "El triunfo de Calibán," casting Caliban as the triumphant material power of the United States and Ariel as the imperilled spirit of Latin culture; two years later the Uruguayan José Enrique Rodó's Ariel (1900) systematized the casting into the region's most influential essay, Ariel for Latin America's spiritual vocation, Caliban for North American utilitarianism. For seven decades the region's intellectuals therefore read Shakespeare's island as a map on which Latin America was the airy spirit and the monster was somebody else. Retamar's 1971 essay seized the map and turned it over: the monster, he argued, is the region itself, colonized and cursing in the colonizer's tongue, and the famous sentence he built the claim on is Caliban's own, "You taught me language, and my profit on't / Is, I know how to curse" (The Tempest, 1.2). The essay founded what later Cuban scholarship calls a Calibanology, extended by Retamar himself in Todo Calibán (2003) and carried onto the Havana stage by Raquel Carrió and Flora Lauten's Teatro Buendía in Otra tempestad (1997), where Shakespeare's cast is made to negotiate with Afro-Cuban gods. All of that belongs to The Tempest's article rather than this one, but the frame it built is the weather in which the region's Romeos must be assessed, because it poses, in its strongest form, the question of whether a Latin American Romeo can be anything but Prospero's ventriloquism.

### Does Retamar's Caliban argument convict the region's Romeo and Juliet?

Not on the record assembled here. The Caliban frame explains the channel of arrival, colonial languages, peninsular editions, imported repertory, but cannot explain the outcomes: a Communist poet's translation as social argument, a clown's verse parody turned national property, a street production invited back to the Globe. Occupied goods are rarely remade this thoroughly.

The frame stands as a warning about channels, not as a verdict on uses.

The appropriation answer rests on documents, not sentiment. Santos's mestizo Shakespeare names the process without apologizing for it: mixture as method, the foreign text ground down and recombined with native forms until its provenance stops mattering. Modenessi's Mexican work supplies the mechanism in action, parody as an assault that domesticates, Cantinflas's pelado colonizing the colonizer's metre from inside. The Brazilian record supplies the limiting case that keeps the answer honest, since the Kliman and Santos volume itself prints Ferreira da Rocha's account of a staging conceived as a political statement against a military dictatorship whose statement failed to reach its audience: appropriation is a capacity, not a guarantee, and local makers can fumble the weapon they seize. Rocha's failed statement deserves its full weight in the adjudication, because it disciplines both sides of the argument. Against the imposition theorist, the episode shows a Latin American company treating Shakespeare as available weaponry, the least colonial relation imaginable; against any romantic version of the appropriation thesis, it shows the weapon misfiring in local hands for local reasons, a statement conceived clearly and lost in transmission to the audience it was meant to move. The lesson generalizes across the map. The region's successful remakings succeed through form, through the clown's verse, the street rite's stilts, the chapbook's stanzas, the serial's recognition machinery, and not through declared intention, while its failures fail the way Rocha's production failed, at the joint between intention and form. Appropriation, graded on the full record, is therefore neither submission nor magic. It is a craft, with masters and apprentices, and the masters are the ones who rebuilt the play inside a form their audience already dreamed in.

The region's literature supplies the appropriation thesis with its most elegant single witness, and he belongs in the record before the verdict. Jorge Luis Borges, the Argentine writer whose work Grace Tiffany's essay in the Kliman and Santos volume surveys for its Shakespearean saturation, spent a career treating the English playwright not as a monument of empire but as a riddle about identity that happened to rhyme with his own. His short fiction "Everything and Nothing," published in El hacedor in 1960, imagines Shakespeare at death's threshold hearing God's voice tell him that he, like God, has been many men and no one, a nobody swollen with everybody. The story is the opposite of a colonial complex: it is the region's supreme literary intelligence calmly annexing the colonizer's greatest writer into an Argentine metaphysics of selfhood, on terms set entirely in Buenos Aires. When a culture's Borges treats Shakespeare as raw material for its own deepest questions, the imposition thesis has lost the literary register, whatever it retains about trade routes. Borges never wrote a Romeo, but his example licensed generations who did: the classics, in the dispensation he modelled, belong to whoever can dream them hardest.

The adjudication this article reaches is therefore two-tiered. As a theory of how the play got to Latin America, the imposition account is correct and indispensable; the cargo was Prospero's, the languages were the empire's, the editions were Madrid's and Lisbon's. As a theory of what Latin America did with the play, the imposition account fails its own evidence, because the region's signature acts, Neruda's annexation, Cantinflas's parody, Galpão's street rite, the cordel chapbook, are acts of manufacture, not consumption. The curse Caliban learned to speak became, in the region's Romeos, something Retamar's frame does not predict: a blessing pronounced in local accents over a foreign couple the continent had decided, on the evidence of two centuries, to adopt.

## What the Record Cannot Show: Thin Countries and Lost Productions

A map drawn only from documented cases has blank spaces, and the blanks deserve a section rather than an apology, because they are evidence about the archive rather than about the play. The Kliman and Santos volume, for all its continental ambition, builds its case from Mexico, Brazil, Chile, Cuba, and Argentina, with Borges's Argentina and the Spanish exile community in Mexico at its edges; the Shakespearean International Yearbook's Latin American section of 2019, the field's next collective pulse-taking, works the same thick ground, Brazil, Chile, Cuba, and the Caribbean, adding Colombia in a key that itself testifies to archival difficulty, an essay on anti-Shakespeare rhetoric in Colombian culture. Peru, Venezuela, Ecuador, and the smaller republics have Shakespeare histories, of school texts, touring stops, and isolated productions, but no Romeo and Juliet from those stages stands in the international record at the strength this article requires, and inventing their entries would corrupt every verified row around them. The honest finding is about preservation: the region's theatres have historically kept promptbooks badly, toured without archives, and lost companies' papers to fires, bankruptcies, and regimes, so the map's thin places mark where the paper trail failed, not where the play did. Somewhere in the lost record there are certainly Romeos of Lima and Caracas that no scholar will ever grade. The discipline this article has kept, title, date, and source or silence, is the only respectful response to their absence: the map shows the documented continent and labels the rest as ocean.

The thinness has one more analytical use. The countries with thick records are the countries with strong mediating institutions, a national university theatre in Chile, a great film industry in Mexico, a street company with an archive in Brazil, a radio empire in Cuba, and the correlation states the article's quietest thesis: the play naturalizes where an institution exists that can hold it long enough to remake it. Where the institutions were thin or were broken, by dictatorship, by economic collapse, by the simple absence of a company that outlives its founder, the play remained what it had been in the nineteenth century, a visiting story in other people's repertories. Latin America's Romeo is therefore also an index of Latin America's cultural infrastructure, and the map's blanks and densities, read together, chart the region's capacity to keep what it loves.

## The Verdict for the Region: Grammar Gained, Waste Endangered

The closing judgement must do two things: grade the melodrama fit as the evidence grades it, and state the pattern for this region alone, leaving the other continents to their own articles. On melodrama, the scholarly frame is Peter Brooks's account of the mode in The Melodramatic Imagination (1976): a theatre of excess, of gesture and feeling pitched beyond realism, in the service of a moral legibility that a post-sacred world could no longer take from religion. Martín-Barbero's matrix is Brooks's mode naturalized and given an address, and the Latin American record supports the naturalization at every point where the play meets the region's popular forms. The play fits melodrama because the play is already built from melodrama's materials: a love legible at first sight, a prohibition legible at first hearing, a secret marriage, a hidden potion, a message that fails, a tomb that fills. Heightened feeling is the tragedy's native register; "My only love sprung from my only hate" (1.5) is a melodramatic sentence before the word existed. The telenovela did not corrupt the play's emotional grammar. It recognized a relative.

The threat is equally specific, and the map names it: melodrama consoles, and this play is the great refusal of consolation in the repertoire it joined. The Prologue's contract is that the rage ends only because the children do; the Prince's closing audit gives the survivors not comfort but a bill, "See what a scourge is laid upon your hate, / That heaven finds means to kill your joys with love" (5.3). A form whose climactic sacrament is the recognition scene, the lost child embraced and the house made whole, cannot stage that audit without breaking its own sacrament, and the region's industrial forms, honestly graded, chose their sacrament every time. The critical tradition that dismisses telenovela melodrama as escapism nonetheless fails the record in the opposite direction, because the matrix carried the region's hardest content inside its most popular vessels: Caignet's foundational serial is an argument about race, illegitimacy, and a patriarch's power, delivered to an audience that stopped work to hear it, and Vassallo de Lopes's scholarship on the Brazilian telenovela as a communicative resource documents the form's long second career as a national forum. Melodrama in Latin America is not where social reality goes to be avoided. It is where social reality goes to be felt at full volume, and the price is exacted elsewhere, in the complexity Martín-Barbero conceded and in endings that must repair what the tragedy insists on leaving broken.

The regional pattern, stated for Latin America alone as this closing article of the global sequence is charged to state it, has three clauses. First, translation as annexation: the play became regional when its strong poets and verse-makers, Neruda in Spanish, Pennafort and Heliodora in Portuguese, stopped serving the text and started naturalizing it, and the schoolroom afterlife of those versions, traced in the series' survey of [the play in global classrooms](/2030/06/28/play-in-global-classrooms/), is their monument. Second, the feud by census: every durable adaptation renamed the grudge from the local register of divisions, and the renamings are the region's true criticism of the play, a two-century demonstration that the feud's content is whichever enmity a society cannot stop rehearsing. Third, the ending by form: the tragedy survives intact where a form can hold public grief, the state stage, the street rite; it is commuted where a form must return its audience consoled, the star film, the screen comedy, the serial. Set beside the play's other continental lives, in [the African stagings and adaptations](/2030/05/31/african-stagings-adaptations/) that mapped the feud onto clan and colony, or in [the Arabic adaptations](/2030/05/24/arabic-adaptations/) that read the lovers beside Layla and Majnun, the Latin American signature is distinct: no other region industrialized the lovers' survival. The continent that invented the radionovela and perfected the telenovela looked at the West's most famous dead teenagers and answered, in its factories of feeling, that the story was too useful to end in a tomb, while in its streets and on its stages it proved, in the same decades, that it could bury them as unsparingly as Verona ever did. Both answers are the region's verdict, and the play is larger in the world for having been given both.

Naturalization has a linguistic depth gauge, and Brazil registers the play at the bottom of the language, in its food: Brazilian Portuguese calls the classic pairing of cheese with guava paste Romeu e Julieta, the lovers' names doing duty as the idiom for two unlike things that belong together. A text has finished travelling when a language starts spending its names on lunch. Set beside the Havana cigar rollers naming their brand for the same couple in 1875, the Brazilian dessert completes a hemispheric pattern in which the play's deepest Latin American life runs through workplaces, markets, and tables, the institutions of people who met the lovers by ear and by appetite long before any of them owned the book. The scholarly record this article has followed, with its editions, premieres, and festivals, is the visible ridge of a reception whose mass lies in that vernacular depth, and the verdict's confidence rests on the depth as much as on the ridge.

One conduit remains to be named, because it will decide which of the two answers the future inherits: the classroom. Across the region the play is a school text before it is anything else, encountered in Astrana Marín's prose or in verse translations and local editions by students who will later buy the cordel, stream the serial, or sit in the square for Galpão, and the schoolroom's Romeo is characteristically the tragedy's, since examinations reward the catastrophe the industries commute. The region's double verdict is therefore also a division of labour between its institutions, the school keeping the tomb in trust while the screen sells the wedding, the street company alone refusing the division and insisting that a festival crowd can hold both. Whether the serial industries' descendants, the streaming platforms that have inherited the telenovela's economics and its global reach, will keep commuting the sentence is the question the record hands forward. The evidence of two centuries suggests the play will survive the answer either way, because Latin America never merely kept the lovers. It rebuilt them, in its own languages, feuds, and forms, and the rebuilding is the reception. The continent's two centuries with the play amount to a single demonstration: a classic survives where it is used, and it is used where it is remade.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How did Romeo and Juliet first reach Latin American audiences?

Through Spain, and through France before Spain. The Spanish-speaking republics inherited the repertory of the peninsular stage, where the lovers had been known since the first years of the nineteenth century in refundiciones, licensed Spanish re-foundings of foreign plays. Those versions, Solís's Julia y Romeo of 1803 and García Suelto's Romeo y Julieta of 1817, were built on Jean-François Ducis's French Roméo et Juliette of 1772 rather than on Shakespeare's English text. Touring companies, printed editions from Madrid, and the Italian opera seasons that carried the story in musical form spread the tale across the Atlantic world, so Latin American audiences generally met the plot, the names, and the feud long before they met the play's language.

### Q: Which Spanish translation of the play served the region's readers for most of the twentieth century?

The Romeo y Julieta inside Luis Astrana Marín's Obras completas, the complete works of Shakespeare published by Aguilar in Madrid in 1929 and billed as the first integral Spanish version translated directly from the English. Astrana Marín rendered the whole canon in prose, aiming at accuracy of sense, and his edition became the most popular and widely circulated Shakespeare in the language, reprinted for decades and read throughout Spain and Latin America alike. The consequence for the region was double: generations of readers and students met the play entire and reliable, and generations of actors met it without its verse, a deprivation later Spanish translators such as José María Valverde, Ángel-Luis Pujante, and the Instituto Shakespeare team at Valencia set out to repair.

### Q: What is a refundición, and why did the play reach the Spanish world in that form?

A refundición, in English a re-founding or re-casting, was the eighteenth and nineteenth-century Spanish theatre's licensed method of naturalizing a foreign play: the source was melted down and recast to local rules of structure, decorum, and taste, with scenes reordered, matter cut, and endings adjusted. The practice explains the play's Spanish entry because Spain received Shakespeare through Bourbon-era France, where Jean-François Ducis had already re-founded the tragedy to neoclassical measure without reading English. Solís and García Suelto refunded Ducis in turn. Jennifer Ruiz-Morgan's research gives these versions their due as documents rather than embarrassments: they played, were revived into the 1830s, and taught Spanish and Spanish American audiences the story on which all later, textually faithful receptions were built.

### Q: When and where was Neruda's version of the play first performed?

In Santiago de Chile, on 18 October 1964, by the Instituto de Teatro de la Universidad de Chile. Chile's Pablo Neruda, the Communist poet who would receive the Nobel Prize in 1971, produced his Spanish Romeo y Julieta for the quatercentenary of Shakespeare's birth that year, and the Instituto's premiere is the version's documentary anchor: Neruda's standard bibliography lists the staging among his works for the theatre. The translation became the Spanish-language stage's most famous poet-made Romeo, taken up beyond Chile by Spanish-language companies tracked in the Latinx Shakespeares archive. Its authority has always been theatrical rather than philological, valued for the voice it gives the lovers in Spanish rather than for literal fidelity.

### Q: Why does the Chilean record call Neruda's version an adaptation?

Because its own makers classified it that way. The Instituto de Teatro's 1964 production, and the Neruda bibliography that preserves it, describe the text as adapted from Shakespeare's play, and the description is precise about the kind of object a poet-translator produces. Neruda approached the tragedy as material for a Spanish poem-play of his own, reshaping language and emphasis to carry his reading of the story, rather than as a document to be reproduced with scholarly neutrality. The classification also protects the record's honesty: measured against the original, the version is free; measured against the Spanish-language stage's needs in 1964, it was the strongest available instrument. Calling it an adaptation states both facts at once and assigns the version to the category in which its maker worked.

### Q: How did Gregary Racz read Neruda's Romeo and Juliet?

As politics by other means. In his essay for the Kliman and Santos collection Latin American Shakespeares (2005), the translation scholar Gregary J. Racz argues that Neruda acted on an understanding of the play as an exemplar of his views about society: the feuding houses stand for the entrenched, inherited order, and the lovers for the life that order crushes, so that translating the tragedy became a way of restating the poet's lifelong argument in a classic's voice. The reading gains force from its neighbour in the same volume, Juan J. Zaro's account of the exiled Spanish poet León Felipe, whose Mexican Shakespeare versions Zaro finds spiritual rather than political. The two essays together map the poles between which Latin America's poet-translators worked.

### Q: What is the mestizo Shakespeare argument?

The argument, named by Rick J. Santos in his introduction to Latin American Shakespeares (2005), holds that Shakespeare in the region is mestizo in the strict sense: mixed, as the continent's peoples are mixed, of native, colonial, and immigrant elements, and that the mixture is the meaning rather than a falling away from some pure original. On this account a Mexican clown's verse parody, a Brazilian street production sung in backland rhythms, and a Cuban radionovela's dramaturgy are not dilutions of a European property but the region's characteristic cultural operation, the same operation that produced its languages, musics, and cuisines. The thesis reframes fidelity itself: the question stops being how closely a version hugs the source and becomes what the mixture makes that the source alone could not.

### Q: Why is Cantinflas's Romeo y Julieta spoken in verse?

Because the verse is the joke's engine and its thesis. Miguel M. Delgado's film (Posa Films, 1943), scripted by Jaime Salvador, casts Mario Moreno, Cantinflas, as Romeo opposite María Elena Marqués and writes the dialogue in verse, so that the elevated measure of a courtly tragedy issues from Mexican cinema's supreme pelado, the penniless urban trickster whose torrential, evasive speech, the cantinfleo, was already a national institution. The collision does analytical work: as Luis Nitrihual Valdebenito and José Galera argue, the film builds its symbolic world on Mexico's class division, and handing the underclass hero the aristocracy's own metre lets the metre expose the class order that owns it. Verse in the clown's mouth is parody on the surface and criticism underneath.

### Q: How many Mexican films derive from a whole Shakespeare play?

Two, on the census established by Alfredo Michel Modenessi, the National University of Mexico scholar who has mapped Shakespeare's presence in Mexican cinema. The first is Cantinflas's Romeo y Julieta of 1943, which stood alone for sixty years as the one unabashed, integral Shakespeare adaptation in the national cinema. The second is Iván Lipkies's Huapango of 2003, which transposes Othello into the world of huapango music and dance. Modenessi's point in drawing the census so tightly is that Mexican cinema otherwise admits Shakespeare in fragments, allusions, and hauntings rather than whole derivations, which makes the two exceptions legible as events: a clown's parody in the industry's golden age and a regional music film six decades later, each fully local in form.

### Q: What makes Grupo Galpão's Romeu e Julieta Brazilian rather than imported?

Its materials and its address. The Belo Horizonte company, founded in 1982, built the 1992 production on Onestaldo de Pennafort's Portuguese verse translation and then reworked that text extensively, cutting passages and transmuting speeches into songs and rhythms of the sertão, the Brazilian backlands, while blending Shakespeare's syntax with the verbal world of Guimarães Rosa. Around the words stands the apparatus of Brazilian popular festival: street theatre in public squares, circus skills, commedia dell'arte, live regional music, and a Verona drawn from the cultural imaginary of Minas Gerais. Nothing in the evening asks the audience to visit England or Italy. Verona has been moved, entire, into the audience's own festive traditions, and the foreign play is received as a neighbour's story told in the town square.

### Q: How was Grupo Galpão's production received in London?

With a distinction no other company matched and with one loud dissent. Galpão first brought Romeu e Julieta to Shakespeare's Globe in 2000, where the street production's festive effect survived the wooden O, and returned in 2012 for the Globe to Globe festival, the thirty-seven-play, thirty-seven-language season of the World Shakespeare Festival, as the festival's only returning company; the season's billing called it perhaps the Americas' most famous production of the play. Jacquelyn Bessell's chapter on the two visits records an advocate's pilgrimage across a dozen years. The dissent came from a festival reviewer for The Arts Desk, who found the 2012 performance tonally unvarying and its Juliet's death a comic dying swan. The split verdict is itself documentary evidence of the production's wager between carnival and catastrophe.

### Q: What do Palmeiras and Corinthians have to do with Romeo and Juliet?

They supply the feud in Brazil's most exact screen localization of the play. Bruno Barreto's romantic comedy O Casamento de Romeu e Julieta (Globo Filmes, 2005), adapted from Mário Prata's novel Palmeiras, um caso de amor (2002), casts Luana Piovani as Julieta, daughter of a Palmeiras director and a women's football coach, and Marco Ricca as Romeu, an ophthalmologist who leads the Corinthians' organized supporters. São Paulo's derby divides the city the way Verona's houses divide theirs, by inherited, policed, household allegiance, so the clubs give the film a feud its audience already lives. Romeu's feigned conversion to Palmeiras replays the play's machinery of disguise and secret allegiance, and the title announces the ending the form requires: a marriage, not a tomb.

### Q: Why does the Brazilian film end in a wedding instead of a tomb?

Because its form's contract requires repair. O Casamento de Romeu e Julieta announces its destination in its title and belongs to the romantic comedy line of the region's commercial cinema, whose governing matrix is melodrama: intensity through the middle, recognition and reconciliation at the end. Within that contract the lovers' deaths are not a sad option but a breach, the one outcome the audience has not paid for. The contrast inside Brazil itself proves the variable is form rather than national temperament. Grupo Galpão's street production, working in the ensemble rite of popular festival theatre, buries its lovers before cheering crowds, while Globo Filmes' industrial comedy marries its pair. Same country, same play, same folk energies: the ending follows the institution that stages it.

### Q: What is literatura de cordel, and how did the play enter it?

Literatura de cordel is the rhymed chapbook literature of Brazil's Northeast, cheap pamphlets of verse narrative sold hanging from strings, cordel, at market stalls and fairs, and sung or read aloud to buyers who often owned no other books. The play entered it the way beloved stories enter any folk press: by retelling. Printed cordel versions of Romeu e Julieta circulate in the Brazilian record, the lovers' tale recast in sextilha stanzas under woodcut covers, and Aimara da Cunha Resende's scholarship traces the absorption of the story into cordel and the telenovela as twin native forms. The chapbook matters as evidence of completed naturalization. A story is sold on a market string only when the seller can assume the crowd already half-knows it and will pay to hear it rhymed.

### Q: Who was Félix B. Caignet, and why does a radionovela matter to the play's Latin American life?

Félix B. Caignet (1892-1976) was the Cuban radio writer who effectively founded the Latin American serial melodrama, famous for declaring that he wrote to make audiences cry and for engineering that result with a craftsman's deliberation. His El derecho de nacer, first broadcast by Havana's CMQ on 1 April 1948, fixed the form's template: a child born outside marriage and condemned by a patriarch, saved by the family's Black servant, and revealed in adulthood as the doctor who saves his own grandfather, reconciling the house. The radionovela matters to the play because it built the narrative machine, and the audience, into which the lovers' story was later fed: any Latin American Romeo produced for popular serial media meets Caignet's grammar first and Shakespeare's second.

### Q: What does Jesús Martín-Barbero mean by the melodramatic matrix?

In De los medios a las mediaciones (1987), the Colombian philosopher Jesús Martín-Barbero names melodrama the cultural matrix of Latin American popular narrative, the operating system through which mass audiences process modernization's shocks of migration, class mobility, and changing family order. He formalizes the matrix as four master feelings, fear, enthusiasm, pain, and laughter, embodied in four figures, the Traitor, the Just One, the Victim, and the Fool, whose collisions blend romance, epic, tragedy, and comedy inside one serial body. His governing judgement is that melodrama achieves an intensity available only at the cost of complexity. The concept matters beyond media studies because it explains the region's adaptations of the play: a matrix tuned to recognition and moral legibility will keep the lovers' geometry and renegotiate their ending.

### Q: Is there a telenovela of Romeo and Juliet?

Yes, one direct specimen stands in the Argentine record. Canal 9 of Buenos Aires premiered its Romeo y Julieta on 14 March 2007, a teen telenovela of 150 episodes in a modern-dress setting that retains a picturesque Verona as backdrop. Brenda Gandini played Julieta Caporale, sixteen years old, opposite Elías Viñoles as Romeo Montero, seventeen, the famous surnames softened into plausible Argentine neighbours. The production's ratings were modest, a detail worth keeping because it measures the strain of the fit: the play's five days of action cannot fill 150 episodes without becoming a general serial about young love under family prohibition, in which the title contributes the tragedy and the episodes contribute everything else. The form kept the brand and the geometry and declined the ending.

### Q: How does a telenovela change the play's ending?

By structural necessity rather than by anyone's decision. The telenovela inherits the melodramatic matrix whose climax is recognition, the hidden truth spoken in time, and recognition requires the principals alive to hear it; a tomb produces silence, not recognition. The form's economics point the same way, since the couple whose courtship fills 150 episodes is the production's capital and its finale owes the audience the ratification that courtship promised. The result, visible in the Argentine Romeo y Julieta of 2007 and in the region's screen comedies from Carreras's Romeo y Julita of 1954 onward, is a consistent substitution: the feud is settled by exposure, negotiation, and marriage, and the Prologue's price, that nothing but the children's end can remove the parents' rage, is quietly torn off the contract.

Its ten rows run from the Spanish gateway cases, the Ducis-derived refundiciones of 1803 and 1817 and Astrana Marín's prose Obras completas of 1929, through Caignet's radionovela matrix of 1948, the Mexican and Argentine screen comedies, Neruda's Santiago premiere of 1964, Galpão's street production, the Brazilian cordel chapbooks and football film, and the Argentine telenovela of 2007. ### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch Latin-America map to argue that the region remade the play rather than merely received it?

The feud column shows that no adapter of record left the grudge as an unexplained Italian inheritance: each renamed it from the local census of divisions, class in Mexico, class and race in Cuba, club allegiance in São Paulo, family prohibition in the Argentine serial. An essay that reads the three columns together can show reception becoming authorship, case by documented case.
