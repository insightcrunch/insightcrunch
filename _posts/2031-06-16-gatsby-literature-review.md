Most students who search for how to write a literature review on gatsby begin by reading a stack of scholarly essays and then writing down what each one says, in order, one paragraph per critic. The result reads like a receiving line at a formal event. Each scholar steps forward, bows, and is introduced, and then the next steps forward. Nothing touches anything else. The reader learns that Marius Bewley wrote about the novel in 1954 and that Judith Fetterley wrote about it in 1978 and that the materialist readings came later, but learns nothing about whether those writers agree, what they disagree about, or why any of it matters to the project the review is supposed to introduce. This is the complication this guide confronts head on. The string of summaries feels like diligence and is in fact evasion. It reports the existence of scholarship without entering the conversation the scholarship constitutes, and a literature review that does not enter the conversation is not a literature review at all.

The claim this guide defends is simple to state and demanding to practice. A literature review synthesizes the scholarship into a critical map that identifies gaps and positions a contribution, beyond summarizing sources. The map metaphor does real work here. A map does not list every building in a city. It selects the features that let a traveler orient, it draws the relationships between them, and it marks the uncharted territory. A Gatsby literature review does the same for a century of criticism. It shows which critics belong to which debates, where the debates stand, what evidence each side leans on, and where the map goes blank. That blank space is the point. The review exists so that the writer's own project can be positioned inside it: here is what the field has settled, here is what remains contested, and here is the opening where the new argument fits.

## How to Write a Literature Review on Gatsby: The Synthesis Method

The method has four movements, and naming them at the outset makes the rest of this guide navigable. First, the writer surveys the field widely enough to see its shape, gathering sources that span the major positions rather than the first results that confirm a hunch. Second, the writer groups those sources by the debates they join, because scholarship is organized by disagreement and a review organized by anything else misses the structure of the field. Third, the writer synthesizes, which means writing paragraphs where critics appear in relation to one another, not in sequence, so that the unit of the review is the debate and not the source. Fourth, the writer identifies the gap and positions the contribution, stating plainly what the review has shown the field does not yet know and what the new project will add. Each movement depends on the one before it. A review that skips the debate mapping cannot synthesize, and a review that never synthesizes cannot find a gap worth filling, because gaps only appear against a mapped field.

This method answers a question students often ask sideways rather than directly, which is why the review is hard. A summary asks the writer to understand one source. A synthesis asks the writer to understand several sources well enough to say how they relate, which requires a second order of comprehension: not just what each critic argued, but what each argument does to the others. That is why the string of summaries is the default failure mode. It demands only first order understanding, and it can be produced without ever deciding anything. The synthesis method refuses that comfort. It asks the writer to decide, on the evidence, where each critic stands in each debate, and those decisions are the review's real content.

The summary habit persists for reasons that deserve a fair hearing, because understanding its causes helps the writer defeat it. The first cause is pedagogical history. Most students first meet academic writing through assignments that reward accurate reporting: the book report, the article summary, the annotated list. These are genuine skills, and the student who mastered them earned real praise for them. The literature review then arrives looking like a larger version of the same task, more sources, more summaries, and nothing in the assignment's surface signals that the underlying operation has changed. The student applies the old skill to the new task with diligence, and the diligence is real. What is missing is not effort but the recognition that the review asks a different question: not what did each scholar say, but what does the scholarship, taken together, show.

The second cause is psychological, and it is the more stubborn of the two. Synthesis requires the writer to take a position about the field before feeling fully qualified to have one. The summary, by contrast, can be written from a posture of deference: the critic said it, I report it, my judgment is not involved. For a student writing about a novel that has generated a century of professional criticism, the deference feels like honesty. Who am I, the student thinks, to say where Trilling stands or whether a debate is settled. The answer this guide offers is that the review does not ask the student to outrank the critics. It asks the student to describe the arrangement of the critics, which is a task of observation rather than of authority. Saying that the Daisy scholarship divides into two lines with a historicist complication is not a claim to exceed Fetterley. It is a claim to have read the field carefully, and careful reading is exactly what the student's training has prepared.

The third cause is structural, and it lives in the research process itself. Students typically read sources one at a time, over days or weeks, and the serial nature of the reading produces a serial structure in the notes: one entry per source, each entry a summary. The draft then follows the notes, because the notes are the only record of the reading. The grid method this guide prescribes is the structural antidote. It forces the second reading, the comparative pass in which the writer goes back through the notes asking the against question of each source and filling the debate rows. That pass is where the summary notes become position notes, and the review written from position notes cannot easily collapse back into the list. The practical moral is blunt: if the notes are summaries, the review will be a list no matter how firmly the writer intends otherwise. The battle against the string of summaries is won or lost at the note-taking stage, weeks before the drafting begins.

## Reading Scholarship for Position: The Research Phase

The research phase of a literature review differs from ordinary research in its objective. Ordinary research seeks information: what happened, what the text says, what the context was. Review research seeks positions: who holds what, against whom, on what evidence. The difference changes how every source is read. A reader seeking information reads an essay and extracts its claims. A reader seeking position reads the same essay and extracts its location: which debate it joins, which side it takes, what it concedes to the other side, and which earlier work it answers. Both readers understand the essay. Only the second has material a review can use.

### How do you read a scholarly essay for its position rather than its content?

You read for position by asking what the essay argues against, what it concedes, and what evidence it leans on hardest, and by tracing its footnotes to find the conversation it joined, because content tells you what the essay says and position tells you where it stands.

The position reading proceeds in three passes, and the passes are worth distinguishing because each catches what the others miss. The first pass is the thesis pass, a fast read of the introduction and conclusion to extract the essay's central claim and, more importantly, the claim's target. Scholarly essays almost always name their target, sometimes explicitly and sometimes by implication: a reading that opens by noting that critics have treated Daisy as a symbol is announcing that the symbol reading is its target. The target is the debate membership card. An essay whose target is the symbol reading of Daisy belongs in the Daisy debate on the feminist side, whatever else the essay contains. Writers should practice extracting the target from the first two pages before reading further, because the habit trains the eye to see debates rather than essays.

The second pass is the argument pass, a slower reading that maps how the essay supports its claim and, crucially, where it concedes ground. Concessions are the most informative sentences in any scholarly essay for the reviewer's purposes. When a critic writes that an opposing reading has force on a particular point, or grants a qualification before pressing the main claim, the concession marks the boundary of the critic's position, the place where the position ends and the neighboring position begins. Those boundaries are what the synthesis needs. A review that knows only what each critic affirms will draw the debates as caricatures, with each side occupying the extreme. A review that knows what each critic concedes can draw the debates at their actual shape, with the real points of contact and the real points of friction. Students should underline concessions the way they underline theses, because the concessions are where the map gets its precision.

The third pass is the footnote pass, and it is the most neglected and the most productive. The footnotes of a scholarly essay are a map of the conversation the essay joined: whom it cites with approval, whom it cites to refute, which earlier work it treats as the state of the question. A reviewer who follows the footnote trail from three starting essays will quickly find the debate's core participants, because scholars in the same debate cite each other, and the citation network is the field's own diagram of itself. The footnote pass also reveals the debate's history, showing which positions are responses to which, and it surfaces the sources the reviewer's initial searching missed. An hour spent tracing footnotes from a good starting essay often yields more usable sources than an hour of database searching, because the footnotes are already filtered by relevance: they are the sources the debate itself considers worth answering.

A concrete illustration will show the three passes working together. Take Trilling's 1945 essay, the piece that helped establish the novel's serious critical standing. The thesis pass finds the central claim about the novel's moral seriousness and its target, the then current dismissal of the book as a glittering period piece. The argument pass finds the structure of the case and its concessions, the places where Trilling grants the novel's limitations before insisting on its achievement. The footnote pass, or in this case the essay's explicit engagement with its moment, shows the conversation it joined: the critical reassessment of the 1920s novelists then underway. A reviewer who has done the three passes does not merely know that Trilling praised the novel. The reviewer knows where Trilling stands in the stature debate, what position he argued against, what he conceded, and which later critics took up his terms. That is position knowledge, and it converts directly into a grid row.

The position reading also disciplines the reviewer's relationship with difficulty. Some essays will be hard, and the difficulty will tempt the reviewer to file them under a vague summary and move on. The three passes give the reviewer a way to extract value from a hard essay without fully mastering it: even when the argument's details resist, the target is usually visible, the concessions are usually findable, and the footnotes are always traceable. A reviewer who can place a difficult essay in the grid, even tentatively, has done more for the review than a reviewer who writes a careful summary of it. The review needs the placement. It does not need the paraphrase.

### What happens to a Gatsby research project when its literature review is weak?

A weak literature review quietly sabotages the project it introduces: the thesis arrives without warrant, the method looks arbitrary, and readers cannot tell what the new argument adds because the review never established what already exists, and the examiners who know the field measure the writer by the distance between the claim and its warrant.

The sabotage works through three mechanisms, and each is worth naming because each produces a distinctive failure in the finished work. The first mechanism is the orphan thesis. A thesis that follows a summary list has no visible relationship to the field, because the review never drew the map against which the thesis could be located. The reader cannot tell whether the claim about Nick's narration is original or whether it repeats what the reliability debate settled decades earlier, and a claim whose originality is uncertain reads as a claim without authority. The second mechanism is the defenseless method. When the review never shows which questions are live, the choice of method looks like preference rather than strategy. A student who writes about Daisy through a psychoanalytic lens, without mapping the feminist reassessment of the character that Judith Fetterley opened in 1978 and the debates it generated, cannot explain why that lens was chosen over the available alternatives, and the examiner's unspoken question, why this approach and not the others, goes unanswered. The third mechanism is the invisible gap. A project cannot contribute to a field it has not described, and the review that lists sources without synthesizing them never describes the field at all. The new argument may in fact be original, but the review gives no reader any way to see the originality, and in academic writing, unseen originality might as well not exist.

Consider the concrete difference in two versions of the same project. In the weak version, a student writes that Tom Buchanan's racism matters to the novel, summarizes three essays that mention race in Gatsby, and proceeds to analyze Tom. In the strong version, the student maps the race scholarship: the long period when Tom's racism was treated as period decoration, the later scholarship that made it a structural question about whiteness and anxiety in the 1920s, the debate over whether the novel endorses or exposes Tom's views, and the scholars on each side with their evidence. The analysis of Tom that follows is not a different analysis in its readings of the novel. It is the same analysis, now warranted, because the review has shown the reader exactly which conversation the analysis joins and what it adds. Nothing about the student's own insight changed between the two versions. Only the review changed, and the review changed everything about how the insight reads.

This is why the literature review is often the part of a thesis that examiners read most carefully. They are not reading it to learn about Gatsby scholarship, which they know. They are reading it to test the writer. A reviewer who has synthesized the field can be trusted with an original claim, because the synthesis proves the writer knows what originality would require. A reviewer who has only listed sources has proved only that they can read, and the original claim that follows is read with corresponding skepticism. The review is thus not a preface to the real work. It is the evidence that the writer is qualified to do the work, and the weak review fails exactly this test.

### How do you find the debates inside Gatsby scholarship before you start writing?

You find the debates by reading for disagreement rather than for information: track which critics cite each other to argue, which phrases recur as terms of dispute, and which positions are always described in relation to an opposing view, then record each find in the debate grid.

The practical procedure starts with a wide but bounded reading pass. Gather thirty to fifty scholarly sources on the novel, spanning the major positions, and read each one with a single question in mind: what is this writer arguing against. The against matters more than the for, because the for is the critic's own position and the against is the critic's location in the field. Lionel Trilling's 1945 essay, the piece that helped begin the novel's serious critical life, argued against the then common dismissal of the book as a period curiosity; knowing what Trilling opposed tells a reader where he stood in the conversation of his moment. Marius Bewley's 1954 reading argued for the novel's spiritual seriousness against readings that found it merely a story of rich people's manners. Judith Fetterley's 1978 feminist reading argued against a critical tradition that had treated Daisy as a symbol rather than a person, and against readings of the novel's women as decorative. Each of these positions becomes visible as a debate position only when the reader asks the against question, and the habit of asking it turns a reading list into a field map.

The second move is to watch for the vocabulary of contestation. Scholarship signals its debates with recurring terms that function as flags. When several critics use the phrase reliability about Nick's narration, they are not independently choosing a word; they are joining a debate whose name is already established. When critics argue about whether the novel's ending is elegiac or ironic, the paired adjectives mark the axis of disagreement. When one writer calls another's reading reductive and the other returns the compliment, the exchange itself is the map. A student who reads ten essays on the green light and notices that half treat it as hope and half treat it as the novel's central irony has found a debate without needing anyone to label it. The labels help, but the pattern of disagreement is primary, and it is visible to any reader who reads comparatively rather than serially.

The third move is the most mechanical and the most productive: build a working grid as you read, with debates as rows and critics as entries. One row for the American Dream debate, one for the narrator's reliability, one for Daisy's characterization, one for the role of race, one for the materialist readings of wealth, one for the compositional questions about revision and intention. Each critic you read goes into every row where the critic takes a position, with a note on what the position is. After twenty sources, the grid has a shape. Some rows are crowded, which means the debate is central. Some rows are empty on one side, which means the field has reached a working consensus there, and a consensus is itself a finding worth reporting. Some rows hold only old sources, which means the debate may be dormant and available for reopening. The grid is the raw material of the synthesis, and the review's structure will follow it: one section per live debate, each section showing the positions, the evidence, and the state of play.

This is the stage where the field's breadth should be absorbed before the writing narrows, and the [series' survey of Gatsby scholarship](/2030/12/02/gatsby-scholarship-survey/) serves that purpose, laying out the major schools, the landmark studies, and the live debates so that the reviewer's map starts from the field's actual shape rather than from the first handful of sources encountered. The survey is the wide view; the review is the argument built from it. A reviewer who has internalized the survey's shape will not mistake a minor skirmish for the central conflict of the field, and will not build a review around three conveniently available sources when the debate they join involves thirty.

## From Summary to Synthesis: The Core Move

Synthesis is the verb the whole guide keeps returning to, so it deserves a precise definition before the examples begin. To summarize a source is to report what one critic said. To synthesize sources is to report what the relationship between critics' positions is, using the sources as evidence for a claim about the field. The grammatical difference is visible in the topic sentence. A summary paragraph opens with a critic's name: "Matthew Bruccoli argues." A synthesis paragraph opens with a field claim: "The scholarship divides on whether." Everything after that opening follows from it. The summary paragraph stays inside one source for its whole length. The synthesis paragraph moves between sources, because its subject is the pattern they form, and the individual critics appear as instances of positions rather than as the paragraph's organizing principle.

The distinction has a test that any writer can apply to a draft. Cover the critics' names in a paragraph and read what remains. If what remains is still an argument about the field, the paragraph synthesizes. If what remains is a hole, because the paragraph had no argument apart from the sequence of names, the paragraph summarizes. The test is brutal and reliable, and writers should apply it to every paragraph of a review before considering the section done. The string of summaries fails the test in every paragraph, because each paragraph is a hole shaped like a critic.

### What does a synthesis paragraph do that a summary paragraph cannot?

A synthesis paragraph makes a claim about the field that no single source contains, showing how positions relate, where they clash, and what the pattern means, which is the only thing a literature review can say that a bibliography cannot.

The difference is easiest to see in a worked pair, using the debate over Daisy's characterization, where the scholarship genuinely divides. First the summary version, the kind of paragraph the complication of this guide warns against:

Summary version. "Judith Fetterley, writing in 1978, argues that the critical tradition has read Daisy as a symbol of the dream rather than as a person, and that this reading repeats the novel's own objectification of her. Earlier critics had treated Daisy primarily as an emblem of wealth and desire. Later feminist scholarship developed Fetterley's position, examining the constraints on women's agency in the novel's world. Some critics maintain that Daisy's choices are her own and that she should be judged for them."

The paragraph reports accurately. Every sentence is true. And the paragraph says nothing. The reader learns that several things were said, but not how they relate, not which positions conflict, not what the state of the debate is, and not what any of it has to do with the project this review introduces. The sentences could be reordered without loss, because they have no argumentative order. The final sentence gestures at disagreement and then stops, as if noting the disagreement discharged the reviewer's obligation. This is the pile of summaries in miniature, and it fails the cover-the-names test completely: remove the names and nothing remains.

Now the synthesis version of the same material:

Synthesis version. "The Daisy scholarship divides on whether her characterization is the novel's indictment of its world or its repetition of that world's judgments. The feminist line, opened by Judith Fetterley's 1978 reading, holds that the critical tradition's Daisy, the careless emblem of wealth and desire, is an artifact of the same objectification the novel performs, and that reading her as a person rather than a symbol requires reading against both Nick's narration and the criticism that followed it. Against this stands the agency line, which insists that Daisy's choices are hers and that the novel judges her for them, a position that treats the text's moral language as evidence rather than as a symptom to be diagnosed. What is contested is not the facts of Daisy's behavior, on which both lines agree, but the status of the novel's own judgments: whether the text exposes the world's treatment of women or participates in it. The debate remains open because the evidence cuts both ways, with Nick's retrospective narration supplying both the feminist line's suspicion of the narrator and the agency line's record of Daisy's decisions."

The second paragraph does what the first cannot. It names the two positions, states what each holds, identifies the precise point of contestation, which is not Daisy's behavior but the status of the novel's judgments, and reports the state of play, open, with the reason for the openness. Remove the one proper name and the argument survives, because the argument is about the field's structure. The paragraph also does something the first paragraph never attempts: it gives the writer's own project a place to stand. A thesis about Daisy can now position itself, agreeing with the feminist line on the narrator while dissenting on the novel's complicity, or siding with the agency line while borrowing the feminist line's attention to constraint. That positioning is the review's payoff, and only the synthesis version earns it.

Writers should study the mechanics of the second paragraph the way a student of prose studies any model. Notice the topic sentence makes a field claim, not a critic claim. Notice the positions are named as lines, which lets multiple scholars occupy each side without a roll call. Notice the pivot sentence, "What is contested is not," which is the synthesis move in its purest form: it tells the reader where the disagreement actually lives, and it does so by excluding the things the disagreement is not about. Notice the closing sentence reports the state of play and its cause. These four moves, field claim, position naming, contestation pinpointing, state of play, are the paragraph architecture of every strong review, and they can be practiced until they become habit.

### How do you choose which scholars to put in conversation with each other?

You choose by debate membership, not by fame or convenience: the scholars who belong together are the ones who take positions on the same contested question, and the review's credibility rests on whether those groupings match the field's real alignments.

The most common error at this stage is the prestige roll call, where the review assembles the most famous names and treats their co-presence as synthesis. A paragraph that mentions Trilling, Bewley, and Fetterley in sequence has not put them in conversation, because those three critics are not arguing about the same thing in the same terms; they belong to different moments and different debates. Real conversation requires a shared question. Trilling belongs with the critics who argued about the novel's seriousness and stature in the 1940s and 1950s. Bewley belongs with the readers who debated the spiritual and moral dimensions of Gatsby's dream. Fetterley belongs with the feminist reassessments of the novel's women. Putting Trilling and Fetterley in the same paragraph is possible, but only if the paragraph's question genuinely engages both, which it rarely does, and the forced pairing reads as exactly what it is: name-dropping disguised as coverage.

The second error is the convenience sample, where the review's cast is determined by which sources were easiest to find. This is the error that produces the review of five sources, three of them saying the same thing, and it is the error the source-finding discipline exists to prevent. The [guide to finding peer-reviewed Gatsby sources](/2031/01/27/gatsby-peer-reviewed-sources/) lays out where the real scholarship lives and how to recognize it, and the reviewer's obligation is to use that discipline before writing, so that the conversation staged in the review is the field's conversation and not the reviewer's bookshelf. A debate section on the American Dream that omits the materialist line because those sources were harder to locate is not a partial map; it is a false map, and the gap the review later claims will be equally false.

The positive method is the grid described earlier, now used as a casting tool. For each debate row, the reviewer lists the critics who take positions there and checks the list against three questions. First, are both sides represented, because a debate section that presents only one side is a summary wearing a debate's clothes. Second, are the landmark positions present, because a review of the Daisy debate that omits Fetterley has missed the position that organized the field, and a review of the novel's critical stature that omits Trilling's 1945 essay has missed the founding move. Third, is there at least one position that complicates the binary, because real debates are rarely two-sided and the review that forces every critic into one of two camps is smoothing the field for its own convenience. The Daisy debate, for instance, holds not only the feminist line and the agency line but the historicist position that reads Daisy through the constraints of the 1920s marriage market, which complicates both. The reliability debate holds not only the reliable and unreliable positions but the selectively unreliable reading, which grants Nick local honesty and global distortion. These third positions are where the best reviews do their most valuable work, because they show the field's actual texture rather than a debating-society cartoon.

A final consideration governs the casting: proportion. The review's space should track the field's weight, not the reviewer's enthusiasm. If the American Dream debate occupies a third of the scholarship, it should occupy roughly a third of the review. If the race scholarship is a growing but still smaller strand, it should be present in proportion, noted as growing, and given its real weight rather than inflated to match the reviewer's project or shrunk to fit the reviewer's reading. Proportion is a form of honesty. The map misleads when it enlarges the reviewer's neighborhood and shrinks the distant provinces, and examiners who know the field notice the distortion immediately.

## Organizing the Review by Debate

The organization of the review is where the synthesis method becomes structure. The weak review organizes by source: one section per critic, or one paragraph per critic, in an order that is usually chronological and sometimes alphabetical, which is to say an order with no argumentative logic. The strong review organizes by debate: one section per contested question, with the critics appearing inside each section at the positions they occupy. The difference is not cosmetic. The by-source organization makes synthesis structurally impossible, because each critic is quarantined in a separate container and the containers never touch. The by-debate organization makes synthesis structurally inevitable, because each section's subject is a disagreement and the only way to write about a disagreement is to relate the positions.

The transition from the grid to the outline is mechanical. Each live debate row becomes a section. The sections are ordered by the logic of the field and the needs of the project: the most central debates first, the debates most relevant to the project given enough space to do their work, the settled questions reported briefly rather than staged as live contests. A typical Gatsby review might run five sections: the American Dream debate, the narrator's reliability, the characterization of Daisy, the readings of wealth and class, and the race scholarship, with the compositional and intentionalist questions folded in where the project requires them. Each section follows the paragraph architecture from the worked example: field claim, positions, point of contestation, state of play. The review's own thesis, the gap and the contribution, then follows naturally, because the state-of-play sentences have been accumulating the evidence for it all along.

The review's opening paragraph deserves its own discipline, because it is the one paragraph every reader reads and the one most writers waste. The wasted opening rehearses the novel's plot, or the author's biography, or the history of the book's reception, none of which is the review's subject. The review's subject is the scholarship, and the opening paragraph should say what the scholarship, taken together, looks like. A strong opening does three things in about one hundred and fifty words. It states the field's shape in a sentence: that Gatsby scholarship is organized around a handful of durable debates, naming the central ones. It states the review's organizing logic in a sentence: that the review maps those debates in an order the project requires, and why. And it states the review's destination in a sentence: that the mapping will reveal a specific opening, which the project's contribution will fill. The three sentences are a contract with the reader. Everything that follows either fulfills the contract or breaks it, and the writer who drafts the opening last, after the map is drawn, will find the contract easy to write honestly.

Writers should also decide, before drafting, what the review will not cover, and the opening is the place to say so. No review covers everything, and the honest delimitation strengthens the review rather than weakening it. A review organized around the novel's major interpretive debates might exclude the textual scholarship on editions and variants, or the reception history of the films, with a sentence explaining that those territories belong to other inquiries. The exclusion must be principled, not convenient: it should follow from the project's needs, and the review should not exclude a debate the project actually depends on. But the delimited review is more trustworthy than the review that pretends to comprehensiveness while quietly ignoring half the field. The map that marks its own edges is the map the reader can use.

Writers often worry that the debate organization will produce repetition, with the same critic appearing in several sections. The worry is misplaced, and the repetition, when it happens, is a feature. A major critic does take positions in several debates, and appearing in several sections shows the critic's range rather than repeating the critic's summary. What must not repeat is the summary of the critic's whole argument. Each appearance should present only the position relevant to that section's debate. Fetterley appears in the Daisy section for the feminist reading of the character and does not need to appear in the American Dream section unless the review's argument requires her there. The discipline is the same as the paragraph discipline: the unit is the position, not the person.

### How does the Daisy debate become a worked example of synthesis?

The Daisy debate works as the model because its positions are clear, its evidence is shared, and its point of contestation is precise, which lets a reviewer practice every synthesis move on material the field has already organized into a teachable shape.

The debate's clarity comes from its structure. Both sides work from the same textual record: Daisy's voice, her marriage to Tom, the affair with Gatsby, the hit-and-run, the withdrawal at the end. Nobody disputes what happens. The dispute is entirely about how to read it, which makes the debate a pure case of interpretive disagreement, the kind a literature review is built to map. The feminist line, from Fetterley's 1978 intervention through its developments, reads the critical tradition's Daisy as a construction, arguing that the scholarship reproduced the novel's objectification by treating her as a symbol of the dream rather than as a person making constrained choices. The agency line reads the same record as a moral account, arguing that Daisy's choices are intelligible as choices and that the novel holds her responsible for them. The historicist complication reads her through the institutional facts of marriage, divorce, and money in the 1920s, arguing that both the feminist and the agency readings understate how narrow her options were.

A reviewer mapping this debate has three jobs, and each is a synthesis move in action. First, state the positions in their strongest form, which means resisting the temptation to make the side the reviewer disagrees with sound foolish. The agency line is not the naive reading; it is a reading with textual evidence, and the review must present that evidence fairly or the map is propaganda. Second, pinpoint the contestation, which here is the status of the novel's own judgments: does the text expose the world's treatment of women, or does it participate in that treatment, or does it do both at once. Third, report the state of play honestly, which is that the debate remains open, and say why it remains open: because Nick's narration is the evidence for both sides, supplying both the suspicion that the narrator objectifies Daisy and the record of her decisions. That final move is the one students most often skip, and it is the most valuable, because a debate whose openness is explained is a debate the reviewer's own project can enter with a clear sense of what would count as a contribution.

The Daisy debate also teaches the reviewer how to handle the relationship between the debates, because no debate in Gatsby scholarship stands entirely alone. The Daisy positions connect to the reliability debate: the feminist line's suspicion of Nick's narration is continuous with the unreliable-Nick position, and the agency line's trust in the novel's moral language is continuous with the reliable-Nick position. A strong review notes these connections without collapsing the debates into one. The connections are reported as what they are, alignments between positions in different debates, and they give the review its depth: the map is not five separate sketches but one terrain, with the debates as regions and the alignments as the roads between them. The reviewer who sees these roads can write the review's most sophisticated sentences, the ones that show how a position in one debate constrains the positions available in another.

This worked example generalizes. The reliability debate can be mapped the same way, with its positions, its shared evidence in Nick's retrospective narration, and its point of contestation in whether the text signals Nick's distortions or endorses his judgments. The American Dream debate can be mapped with its positions, from Bewley's spiritual reading through the materialist line's insistence that the dream in the novel is a historically specific ideology of wealth, with the contestation located in whether the novel mourns the dream or exposes it. Each debate gets the same three moves, and the review's authority accumulates with each repetition, because the reader watches the reviewer perform the same disciplined operation on different material and learns to trust the operator.

### How does the reliability debate supply a second worked example?

The reliability debate works as a second model because its evidence is a single narrative voice, which forces the reviewer to show how one text supports opposing positions, the hardest synthesis task a Gatsby review faces and therefore the best training ground.

The debate's positions are well established. The reliable-Nick line holds that the novel endorses its narrator's judgments, treating his retrospective moral accounting as the book's own. The unreliable-Nick line holds that the text signals Nick's distortions, his selective memory, his class loyalties, his self-exculpation, and that the novel's meaning emerges in the gap between what Nick says and what the narrative shows. The complicating position holds that Nick is selectively unreliable: locally honest about facts and globally distorting in judgment, a witness the reader can trust for what happened but not for what it meant. All three lines work from the same evidence, which is Nick's narration itself, and that shared ground is what makes the debate such good practice.

The summary version of this debate writes itself, and every reviewer should recognize it as the failure mode. "Some critics argue that Nick is a reliable narrator whose judgments the novel endorses. Others argue that Nick is unreliable, pointing to his biases and self-interest. A third group takes a middle position, suggesting Nick is reliable about facts but not about judgments." The paragraph is accurate and empty. It reports that positions exist without stating what any position holds in enough detail to matter, without identifying what is contested, and without giving the reviewer's project any place to stand. The sentences are interchangeable in order. The cover-the-names test leaves nothing.

The synthesis version does the work. "The reliability debate turns on a single question: whether the novel endorses Nick's judgments or exposes them. The reliable-Nick line treats the retrospective narration as the book's moral instrument, reading Nick's famous judgments as Fitzgerald's own verdicts on the characters. The unreliable-Nick line treats the same narration as evidence against itself, arguing that Nick's selectivity, his class allegiances, and his need to exonerate his own passivity are visible in the text he produces, so that the novel's meaning lies in the distance between the tale and the teller. The selective position splits the difference with precision: Nick can be trusted for the facts of the narrative, which the novel needs a witness to establish, while his evaluations of those facts are the novel's real subject, and the most distorted thing in the book. What is contested, then, is not whether Nick shapes the story, both lines grant that, but whether the novel signals the shaping or shares it. The debate remains open because the text supplies both readings with their best evidence: the passages where Nick's judgment seems authorially endorsed and the passages where his blind spots are unmistakable."

The paragraph earns its length. It states the positions with their logic, not just their labels. It identifies the exact point of contestation, endorsement versus exposure, and excludes the false contestation, whether Nick shapes the story, which nobody disputes. It explains the openness by pointing to the text's double supply of evidence. And it gives the reviewer's project its footing: a thesis about any character can now be positioned against this debate, because the debate determines what any character reading is allowed to assume about the narration it reads through. A student writing about Gatsby's dream who has not positioned the reading against the reliability debate is building on unexamined ground, and the review that maps this debate is what makes the ground examinable.

Notice, finally, how the two worked examples connect, because the connection is itself a synthesis move the review should make explicit. The Daisy debate's feminist line depends on suspicion of Nick's narration, which aligns it with the unreliable-Nick position. The Daisy debate's agency line depends on trusting the novel's moral language, which aligns it with the reliable-Nick position. These alignments do not collapse the two debates into one; Daisy and Nick remain distinct questions. But they show the field's structure, the way a position taken in one debate constrains the positions available in another, and the review that reports these alignments gives its reader the field's real topography. The map has regions and roads. The debates are the regions. The alignments are the roads.

| Weak review habit | Strong synthesizing move, organized by debate |
|---|---|
| One paragraph per critic, in chronological order | One section per debate, with critics placed at their positions inside each section |
| Topic sentences that name a scholar | Topic sentences that state a field claim the paragraph will evidence |
| Reporting what each source says | Reporting how positions relate, where they clash, and what is at stake |
| Treating all sources as equal weight | Giving space in proportion to each debate's weight in the field |
| Noting disagreement and moving on | Pinpointing the exact point of contestation and the evidence each side uses |
| Presenting the opposing view weakly | Stating each position in its strongest form before evaluating it |
| Ending sections with a list of names | Ending sections with the state of play and why the debate stands there |
| Claiming a gap from thin reading | Deriving the gap from the mapped field, stated against the debates |
| Keeping the novel out of the review | Testing every mapped position against the text it claims to read |
| Writing the review from five convenient sources | Building the cast from the field's real alignments, both sides represented |

The table above is the guide's artifact, and it is worth reading as a diagnostic instrument rather than as decoration. A writer can run a draft against each row and mark where the draft falls. The left column describes the habits that produce the pile of summaries; the right column describes the moves that produce the map. Most drafts will show a mixture, with some sections synthesizing and others listing, and the revision task is to move every section to the right column. The table also serves the guide's anti-cannibalization boundary: it does not teach source-finding, which is covered elsewhere in the cluster, and it does not teach annotation, which belongs to the [annotated bibliography guide](/2031/01/20/gatsby-annotated-bibliography/). Those are the groundwork. This guide teaches what to do with the groundwork once it exists.

That groundwork relationship deserves emphasis, because the three articles form a sequence and the sequence is the research-method cluster's logic. The annotated bibliography is the instrument for understanding sources one by one, with each entry recording what a source argues and how it might be used. The literature review is the instrument for understanding the field the sources compose, with each section showing how the sources' arguments relate. The bibliography answers the question of what the reviewer has read; the review answers the question of what the reviewer has understood about the conversation. A student who has built a real annotated bibliography, with evaluative annotations rather than summaries, has already done half the review's work, because the annotations contain the against question's answers. The review is the assembly of those answers into the map.

## Finding the Gap and Positioning the Contribution

The gap is the most misunderstood element of the literature review, and the misunderstanding takes two common forms. The first is the empty-shelf fallacy: the belief that a gap is simply a topic nobody has written about. A topic nobody has written about is usually nobody's loss. The scholarship has not produced a study of the novel's minor party guests because the minor party guests do not repay study, and a review that claims this absence as its gap has mistaken the field's good judgment for its oversight. The second is the asserted gap: the claim that something is missing, offered without the mapped field that would show the missing thing's shape. A gap is not a hole the writer declares. It is a hole the map reveals, and the map must be drawn first.

A real gap has three properties, and the reviewer should test any candidate against all three. First, it is adjacent to live debates, not floating in unclaimed territory. The gap that matters is the question the mapped debates raise but do not answer, the position the field's logic implies but nobody has taken, the evidence the debate needs but nobody has examined. Second, it is researchable, which means there is a method for addressing it and evidence that could bear on it. A gap that can only be filled by documents that do not exist is not a gap but a wish. Third, it is significant within the field's terms, which means filling it would change something the field cares about: it would resolve or reframe a live debate, complicate a consensus, or open a debate the field should be having. The review earns its gap claim by showing all three properties against the map, and the showing is what distinguishes the strong review's final section from the weak review's hopeful last paragraph.

The positioning of the contribution follows the gap, and it should be stated with the same discipline. The contribution is not the thesis restated. It is the thesis located: given this map of the field, with these debates and this gap, the project will do this specific thing, using this method, on this evidence, and the result will matter to the field in this specific way. The location metaphor keeps the statement honest. A contribution that cannot be located on the map is either not a contribution or not to this field, and the review that cannot locate its project has not finished its work.

This is where the [guide to research questions](/2031/02/10/gatsby-research-questions/) connects, because the gap and the research question are two descriptions of the same opening. The research question is the gap phrased as an inquiry: what the field does not yet know, stated as a question the project will answer. The review's final section and the project's opening question should align exactly, and the alignment is a check on both. If the review's gap and the project's question describe different openings, one of them is wrong, and the writer should revise until they match. The cluster's design is deliberate here: the questions article teaches how to frame the inquiry, this article teaches how to warrant it against the field, and the two together produce a project whose question is both well formed and well placed.

Consider a concrete gap claim, built from the Daisy debate mapped above. The mapped field shows the feminist line, the agency line, and the historicist complication, with the contestation located in the status of the novel's own judgments. A reviewer working this map might notice that the debate's evidence is drawn almost entirely from the novel's middle chapters, the affair and its collapse, while the final chapter's treatment of Daisy, her absence from the funeral and Nick's judgment of her, is cited by all sides but analyzed by none as a distinct problem. That is a gap with the three properties: it is adjacent to the live debate, it is researchable through close reading of a specific passage, and it is significant because the passage both sides cite may not support what either side assumes. The contribution is then locatable: a reading of the final chapter's Daisy that tests both lines against the passage they share. Whether the reading succeeds is a matter for the project. That the review has made the project's place in the field visible is a matter for the review, and it is the whole of the review's final duty.

A second gap example, drawn from the American Dream debate, will show the method working on different material and will illustrate a further point about where gaps hide. The mapped field shows Bewley's 1954 spiritual reading, which found in Gatsby's dream a genuine if misdirected aspiration, and the materialist line, which reads the dream as the novel's exposure of a historically specific ideology of wealth. The contestation lies in whether the novel mourns the dream or exposes it, and the debate has the settled-unsettled shape common in mature fields: the broad positions are established, but their implications are not exhausted. A reviewer working this map might notice that both lines argue almost entirely from the novel's language of aspiration, its green light, its parties, its talk of wonder, while the novel's language of work, the actual economic activity that produces the wealth being dreamed about, is cited by neither side as primary evidence. Gatsby's business, Wolfsheim's enterprises, the drug stores and the bonds: the material base of the dream is present in the text and oddly absent from the debate about the dream. That is a gap with the three properties. It is adjacent to the live debate, it is researchable through the passages both sides under-read, and it is significant because the debate's central question, what the novel thinks of the dream, may look different when the dream's economic machinery is brought into view. The contribution locates itself: a reading of the novel's economic language that tests both the mourning and the exposure positions against the text they share.

The example also illustrates where gaps most often hide, which is not in the unmapped wilderness but in the middle of the mapped debates. Beginners imagine gaps as distant territories nobody has visited. Experienced reviewers find them in the passages everybody cites and nobody examines, the evidence both sides need and neither side has fully worked through. The reason is structural: a live debate consumes the participants' attention at the point of contestation, and the shared ground beneath the contestation goes unexamined precisely because it is shared. The reviewer who reads for the shared ground, asking what both sides assume rather than what they dispute, will find gaps that the participants cannot see from inside the argument. This is one more thing the synthesis discipline yields that the summary list cannot: the list never shows the shared ground, because it never relates the positions, and the gaps hiding there remain invisible.

There remains the case the reviewer fears most, which is the field that looks settled everywhere the review looks. The fear is usually misplaced, and the reviewer's response should be diagnostic rather than despairing. First, check whether the settlement is real or an artifact of narrow reading. A debate looks settled when the reviewer has read only one side's sources, and the remedy is the grid's completeness check: for each debate, are both sides represented by their strongest statements. Second, distinguish settlement of positions from settlement of implications. A debate can be settled in its broad lines while its consequences remain unexplored, which is the American Dream case above: nobody disputes the two lines, but the economic language both lines under-read is still open. Third, consider the scale shift. A question settled at the level of the whole novel may be open at the level of a single passage, a single image, a single chapter, and the review that descends a level often finds the opening the wide view concealed. Only after these three diagnostics should the reviewer conclude that the immediate territory is genuinely settled, and even then the conclusion is a finding worth reporting: a review that establishes what the field has settled has done real work, and the project's contribution can be positioned as extending the settled ground rather than contesting it.

Notice what the gap claim does not do. It does not claim that nobody has ever discussed the final chapter. It does not claim that the debate is exhausted. It claims something precise and checkable: that a specific passage, central to a specific debate, has not received the specific analysis the debate's logic requires. Precision is what makes a gap claim credible, and precision is only possible against a mapped field. The reviewer who has not mapped the debate cannot be precise, and the imprecision is how examiners recognize the asserted gap. The discipline is simple to state and hard to practice: never claim a gap you cannot draw on the map.

## Drafting, Length, and Revision

The drafting of the review follows the outline the grid produced, and the first drafting rule is the one the worked example already taught: every section opens with a field claim, and every paragraph earns its place by advancing the map. The second drafting rule concerns the reviewer's voice, which is the element students handle most anxiously. The anxiety takes the form of a false choice between disappearing entirely, writing as if the map drew itself, and intruding constantly, announcing opinions about every critic. The reviewer's voice belongs in three places and should be restrained elsewhere. It belongs in the field claims, which are the reviewer's judgments about the scholarship. It belongs in the evaluations, where the reviewer says which positions the evidence supports and which it does not, because a review that never evaluates is a survey, and the survey is a different article. And it belongs in the gap and contribution statements, which are the reviewer's own argument about the field. Everywhere else, the voice should recede behind the mapping, letting the positions speak in their strongest form. The reviewer is the mapmaker, not a combatant in every debate, and the map's authority comes from its fairness.

The third drafting rule concerns quotation, which is the review's most abused resource. A quotation from a critic should do work the reviewer's paraphrase cannot do: capture a formulation so precise that paraphrase would blunt it, or document a claim the review is about to evaluate, so the reader can check the fairness of the evaluation. A quotation should never do the arguing for the reviewer. The paragraph that strings three quotations together with minimal connective tissue is the string of summaries in another costume, and it fails for the same reason: it reports positions without relating them. The discipline is to paraphrase the positions, quote only the formulations that earn it, and keep the argument in the reviewer's own sentences. A useful ratio to internalize is that the reviewer's synthesis sentences should outnumber quoted sentences by a wide margin in every section. When the balance reverses, the review is letting its sources write it.

The fourth drafting rule concerns the hinges between debate sections, which are the paragraphs most reviews omit and the paragraphs readers need most. A review organized by debate faces a structural problem the summary list never had: its sections are about different questions, and the reader needs to know why they appear in this order and how they relate. The hinge paragraph, placed at the end of one debate section or the opening of the next, does this connective work. It states the relationship between the debates: that the reliability debate constrains the Daisy debate, because what the reviewer may assume about Nick determines what the reviewer may claim about Daisy; that the American Dream debate and the materialist readings of wealth overlap but are not identical, the first about aspiration and the second about class position; that the race scholarship, long treated as a separate strand, bears directly on the novel's language of civilization and decline that the Dream debate also handles. These are the roads between the regions, and the review must draw them or the map reads as separate sketches.

The hinge paragraph has its own architecture, simpler than the synthesis paragraph but equally disciplined. It opens with the connection stated as a field observation, not as the reviewer's invention: the scholarship itself shows these debates informing each other, and the hinge reports that fact. It then specifies the direction of the constraint, saying which debate's positions limit which other's, because vague gestures at interconnection teach nothing. It closes by stating what the connection means for the review's organization, why this debate follows that one, which lets the reader trust the sequence as reasoned rather than arbitrary. A review of five debates needs four hinges, and the writer who drafts them will discover that the exercise tests the map: if two debates cannot be connected by an honest hinge, the reviewer should reconsider whether both belong in this review, or whether the map has a region the field does not recognize. The hinge is thus both a writing task and a diagnostic, and the review with strong hinges reads as one argument rather than five essays stapled together.

A related drafting matter is the handling of the review's own evaluations across sections, because the evaluations accumulate into the reviewer's overall judgment of the field and that judgment must stay consistent. The reviewer who finds for the unreliable-Nick position in the reliability section cannot then write the Daisy section as if Nick's judgments were transparent, unless the shift is acknowledged and justified. Consistency does not mean the reviewer must take the same side in every debate; the field's debates are independent enough that a reviewer can hold mixed positions. It means the positions taken must be compatible, and the reviewer should check them against each other before drafting the gap section. The practical method is a one-page position sheet, completed after the grid and before the drafting: for each debate, the reviewer's own evaluation in a sentence. The sheet becomes the review's conscience, consulted whenever a section's evaluation is written, and it prevents the common embarrassment of a review that argues against itself across its own sections.

### How do you keep the novel itself present inside a review of scholarship?

You keep the novel present by testing every mapped position against the text it claims to read, citing the passages the debate turns on, so the review never becomes a conversation about criticism detached from the book rather than about the novel.

The detachment is a real hazard, and it has a recognizable symptom: a review in which the novel appears only in the first and last paragraphs, while the middle is pure historiography of who said what. Such a review may map the field accurately and still fail, because the field it maps is Gatsby scholarship, and Gatsby scholarship is scholarship about a novel. The positions the review maps are readings of passages, and the passages are the evidence the positions stand or fall on. A review that never touches the passages cannot evaluate the positions, and a review that cannot evaluate is a survey.

The remedy is built into the section structure. Each debate section should name the textual ground the debate stands on. The reliability debate turns on Nick's retrospective narration, on the famous passages where his judgments are most exposed, and the review should cite them when reporting what each side claims about them. The Daisy debate turns on the scenes both sides share, and the review should keep those scenes in view. The American Dream debate turns on the novel's language of aspiration and its imagery of wealth, and the review should anchor each position to the passages its proponents cite. This does not mean the review becomes a close reading of the novel; the close reading belongs to the project. It means the review stays honest about what the scholarship is scholarship of, and it gives the reader the material to judge whether the reviewer's evaluations are fair.

There is a second reason to keep the novel present, and it concerns the gap. The most persuasive gaps are textual gaps, openings visible in the relationship between the scholarship and the book. The reviewer who knows the novel well will notice the passages the scholarship under-reads, the scenes every debate cites but none examines, the details the positions assume rather than argue. These observations are the raw material of the gap claims that meet the three properties, and they are available only to the reviewer who keeps the text in view while mapping the field. The annotated text, where the novel can be examined passage by passage with the scholarship's questions in mind, is the instrument for this double vision, and the Study section below describes how to use it.

### How do you repair a draft that reads as a string of summaries?

You repair it by converting each source container into a debate section: extract every critic's position from its isolated paragraph, regroup the positions by the question they answer, and rewrite with field claims as topic sentences until every section passes the cover-the-names test.

The repair procedure is mechanical enough to be followed like a recipe, and writers with a summary draft in hand should work through it section by section rather than trying to fix the whole review at once. Step one is extraction. Go through the draft and, for each paragraph, write down the position the paragraph's critic takes, stated as an answer to a question: not what the critic said in general, but what the critic holds on each contested point the paragraph touches. A paragraph on Fetterley yields the Daisy position; a paragraph on Trilling yields the stature position; a paragraph on a materialist critic yields the wealth position. Step two is regrouping. Collect all the positions that answer the same question and lay them side by side. This is the moment the draft's hidden structure appears, because positions that were quarantined in separate paragraphs now stand next to each other, and their relationships, agreement, disagreement, partial overlap, become visible. Step three is rewriting. For each group, write the section fresh, opening with the field claim the group supports, naming the positions, pinpointing the contestation, and closing with the state of play. Do not try to salvage the old paragraphs by stitching them together. The old paragraphs were built on the wrong unit, and stitching preserves the unit. Rewrite from the positions.

Writers attempting this repair often discover that their draft is missing a side of a debate, because the summary draft never required both sides to be present. The repair then demands more reading, and this is not a flaw in the procedure but its value: the procedure reveals what the draft does not know. A Daisy section built from the repair that contains only the feminist line is visibly incomplete in a way the summary draft's Fetterley paragraph was not, because the section's subject is the debate and a debate with one side is a contradiction in terms. The writer must go find the agency line, read it fairly, and give it its strongest form. The review gets longer, but it gets longer in the right place, by becoming more complete rather than more padded.

The final step of the repair is the evaluation pass, where the reviewer says what the mapping has shown. The summary draft never evaluated, because it never related. The repaired draft must evaluate each debate: which positions the evidence supports, where the field has reached working consensus, which questions remain genuinely open. The evaluations are the reviewer's voice doing its proper work, and they are what transform the map from a reference work into an argument. A reader should finish each section knowing not only what the field holds but what the reviewer, having mapped it, thinks the field has established. That judgment is the bridge to the gap claim, and the gap claim is the bridge to the project.

### How long should a Gatsby literature review be, and how is the space divided?

The length follows the project, but the proportions are stable: the debate sections carry the bulk, the gap and contribution close the review, and no section should be so short that it lists or so long that it becomes a separate essay.

For a standard undergraduate research essay, the review might run eight hundred to fifteen hundred words, which allows three debates to be mapped with real synthesis and no more. The writer must choose the three debates most relevant to the project and map them properly rather than covering six thinly. Thin coverage is the summary list returning through the back door, and three synthesized debates serve the project better than six listed ones. For a thesis chapter, the review might run three thousand to six thousand words, which allows five or six debates, the connections between them, and a fully developed gap claim. For a dissertation's dedicated review chapter, the length can run much longer, but the discipline does not change: the unit remains the debate, the paragraph architecture remains field claim and positions and contestation and state of play, and the review must still close with the gap and the contribution rather than trailing off into comprehensiveness for its own sake.

The space division within the review follows the field's weight and the project's needs together. The debates most central to the project get the most space, but centrality to the field sets the floor: a Gatsby review that gives the American Dream debate a single paragraph has misjudged the field no matter what the project is about, because that debate is too large to be reported so thinly without distortion. A practical rule is that no live debate the review covers should get fewer than two full synthesis paragraphs, because one paragraph cannot hold positions, contestation, and state of play without collapsing into listing. The gap and contribution section should be substantial enough to state the gap's three properties and locate the project precisely; a two-sentence gesture at the end is the asserted gap, and the review should not end that way. The opening of the review, finally, should be brief: a paragraph stating the field's shape and the review's organizing logic, and then the map begins. The review is not the place for a history of the novel's reception, which is a different article's work. It is the place for the conversation the project joins, drawn at the scale the project needs.

## Verdict

The verdict this guide has been defending since its first paragraph is the claim stated there and practiced in every section since: a literature review synthesizes the scholarship into a critical map that identifies gaps and positions a contribution, beyond summarizing sources. The map is not a metaphor for decoration. It is the review's job description. It selects the debates that organize the field, draws the positions within each debate at their strongest, pinpoints where the disagreement actually lives, reports the state of play and why it stands there, and marks the blank space where the new project fits. The string of summaries fails because it performs none of these operations. It lists buildings without drawing the city.

The guide takes this position without hedging because the alternative, that a competent list of sources suffices, fails against the purposes the review serves. The review warrants the thesis, and a list cannot warrant. The review defends the method, and a list cannot defend. The review establishes the contribution, and a list cannot establish what it never describes. Every downstream element of a research project depends on the review having done the mapping work, and the project built on an unmapped field is a project whose foundations the reader cannot inspect. The synthesis standard is therefore not a stylistic preference. It is the condition of the review's function, and the writer who meets it has done the hardest intellectual work the project requires.

There is a further consideration, and it concerns the review as a credential. Examiners, supervisors, and peer reviewers read the literature review as a test of the writer's membership in the scholarly conversation, and they apply that test with an experienced eye for its specific failure modes. They notice when a debate is presented with only one side, because they know the other side. They notice when a landmark position is missing, because the landmark organized their own training. They notice when the gap is asserted rather than derived, because the assertion has a recognizable texture, the vague gesture toward what has not been done, unsupported by the map that would make the gesture precise. And they notice, with particular sharpness, when the opposing positions are stated weakly, because the weak statement of the other side is the oldest tell in academic writing. The strong review passes all of these inspections, not by flattering the examiner's views, but by showing the field at its actual shape with a fairness the examiner can verify. That verifiability is the review's real defense. Every field claim the review makes can be checked against the scholarship, and the review that survives checking has proved the writer's qualification in the only way that counts, by doing the work in public.

There is a final consideration, and it concerns what the discipline of synthesis does to the writer. The student who has mapped the Daisy debate, or the reliability debate, or the American Dream debate, with positions stated fairly and contestation pinpointed, has learned something no summary could teach: how a field thinks. The debates are the field's thinking made visible, and the reviewer who can draw them can also, eventually, move them. That is the deeper promise of the literature-review skill, and the reason this guide treats it as the capstone of the research-method cluster. The annotated bibliography teaches the writer to understand sources. The survey teaches the writer to see the field. The literature review teaches the writer to enter the conversation, and the writer who can enter the conversation is ready to change it.

## Study

The study of the literature-review skill begins where every Gatsby study should begin, with the novel itself read closely and read whole, because the review maps scholarship about a book and the map is only as good as the reviewer's knowledge of the terrain. The place to ground the review in the text is [VaultBook's annotated text of The Great Gatsby](https://vaultbook.net/tools/great-gatsby-annotated-text.html), where the passages the debates turn on can be examined line by line: the scenes the Daisy scholarship shares, the narration the reliability debate contests, the imagery the American Dream readings invoke. Read each mapped position against its passages before writing the section, and mark where the position's evidence is strong and where it strains. That testing is what lets the review evaluate rather than merely report, and the annotated text is the bench on which the testing is done.

The second instrument is the scholarship itself, gathered systematically rather than conveniently, and the place to find the scholarship to synthesize is [ReportMedic's research resources page](https://reportmedic.org/tools/great-gatsby-essay-practice.html), where the field's critical resources are gathered for the kind of wide, debate-tracking reading this guide prescribes. Use it to build the source base before the grid: collect across the debates, across the decades, across both sides of every live question, and read each source with the against question in mind. The practice tasks that follow enforce the guide's method in order. First, take five sources from the resources and fill one row of the debate grid, writing each critic's position as an answer to the debate's question. Second, write one synthesis paragraph from that row, using the four-move architecture of field claim, positions, contestation, and state of play, then apply the cover-the-names test. Third, take a summary paragraph from an old draft, or write one deliberately, and run the repair procedure on it: extract, regroup, rewrite, evaluate. Fourth, draft the gap claim for a real or imagined project, testing it against the three properties of adjacency, researchability, and significance, and locate the contribution on the map in two sentences. Each task isolates one movement of the method, and the four together assemble the complete skill. Repeat the cycle with a new debate each week, and the movements fuse into a single habit: the reviewer who can map any debate can map the field, and the field, once mapped, is where original work begins.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you write a literature review on The Great Gatsby?

A literature review on The Great Gatsby is written by mapping the scholarship rather than listing it. Begin by gathering sources across the major debates, including the American Dream readings, the narrator reliability question, the Daisy characterization debate, the materialist accounts of wealth, and the race scholarship, so the review starts from the field's real shape. Group the sources by the debates they join, using a working grid with debates as rows and critics' positions as entries. Write each section around one debate, opening with a claim about the field, naming the positions in their strongest form, pinpointing where the disagreement actually lives, and closing with the state of play. End by identifying the gap the map reveals and positioning the new contribution inside it. The review succeeds when a reader can see the shape of the field and where the project fits, which is the synthesis standard this guide defends throughout.

### Q: How do you synthesize the scholarship into a map?

Synthesis into a map happens when the unit of writing changes from the source to the debate. Instead of devoting one paragraph to each critic, devote one section to each contested question and place the critics at their positions inside it. The paragraph architecture carries the method: open with a field claim such as the scholarship divides on a stated question, name the positions with their evidence, state precisely what is contested and what is agreed, and report whether the debate is settled or open and why. Apply the cover-the-names test to every paragraph, checking that an argument about the field remains when the proper names are hidden. Build the map from a debate grid assembled during reading, give each debate space in proportion to its weight in the field, and note the alignments between debates so the map reads as one terrain rather than separate sketches.

### Q: How do you identify gaps and position a contribution?

A gap is identified against the mapped field, never asserted without it, and it must show three properties: adjacency to live debates, researchability through available evidence and method, and significance in the field's own terms. Look for the questions the mapped debates raise but do not answer, the positions the field's logic implies but nobody has taken, and the passages every side cites but none examines closely. Test each candidate against the three properties and discard the empty-shelf topics nobody has written about for good reason. Positioning the contribution means stating the project as the gap's answer: given this map, with these debates and this opening, the project will do this specific thing, and the result will matter to the field in this specific way. The gap and the project's research question should describe the same opening, and their alignment checks both.

### Q: How do you organize a review by debate?

Organizing by debate means each section takes one contested question as its subject and the critics appear inside the section at the positions they occupy. Convert the reading grid into an outline mechanically, with each live debate row becoming a section ordered by the field's weight and the project's needs. Open each section with a field claim, present each position in its strongest form with its evidence, pinpoint the exact point of contestation, and close with the state of play and its cause. Let major critics appear in several sections where they hold several positions, presenting only the relevant position each time rather than repeating a whole-argument summary. Report settled questions briefly instead of staging them as live contests, keep each debate section to at least two full synthesis paragraphs, and reserve the closing section for the gap claim and the positioned contribution.

### Q: Why is a string of summaries weak?

A string of summaries is weak because it reports the existence of scholarship without entering the conversation the scholarship constitutes. Each paragraph stays inside one source, so no paragraph can say how positions relate, where they clash, or what is at stake, which are the only things a literature review can say that a bibliography cannot. The summary sequence has no argumentative order, its sentences can be rearranged without loss, and it fails the cover-the-names test because removing the critics' names leaves a hole where the argument should be. Downstream, the weakness sabotages the project: the thesis arrives as an orphan without warrant, the method looks arbitrary because the live questions were never shown, and the contribution is invisible because the field was never described. The string of summaries feels like diligence and is in fact evasion, demanding only first-order understanding of single sources while the review requires second-order understanding of how sources relate.

### Q: How does a review show the shape of the field?

A review shows the field's shape by making its structure visible: which debates are central and which are minor, where consensus has formed and where contest remains, how positions align across debates, and where the map goes blank. Centrality appears in the review's proportions, with the largest debates receiving the most space and the settled questions reported briefly. Consensus appears in sections that report what the field has established and on what evidence, distinguishing the genuinely settled from the merely unexamined. Alignments appear in sentences that connect positions across debates, such as the continuity between the feminist reading of Daisy and the unreliable-Nick position, which turn separate sections into one terrain. The blank spaces appear in the gap section, where the review states precisely what the mapped field does not yet know. A reader who finishes such a review can orient in the scholarship the way a traveler orients with a map.

### Q: What is a synthesis matrix, and how does it help a Gatsby review?

A synthesis matrix is a working table the reviewer builds during reading, with the debates or themes as rows and the sources as columns, and each cell recording the position a given source takes on a given debate. It helps a Gatsby review by forcing the against question during reading, since filling a cell requires stating what each critic holds on each contested point rather than summarizing the critic's essay as a whole. The matrix makes the field's shape visible before any drafting begins: crowded rows mark the central debates, one-sided rows mark working consensus, and empty cells mark the combinations nobody has tried. When drafting starts, each row converts directly into a review section, and the cells supply the positions the section must relate. The matrix is scaffolding, not part of the finished review, but the review built without it usually collapses back into the source-by-source sequence the matrix exists to prevent.

### Q: How do you write a topic sentence that synthesizes two critics?

A topic sentence synthesizes two critics when it states the relationship between their positions rather than introducing either critic by name. The weak form opens with a name and a reporting verb, which commits the paragraph to staying inside one source. The strong form opens with a field claim that requires both critics to evidence it, for example that the scholarship divides on whether a stated question should be answered one way or the other. With the relationship stated up front, the sentences that follow have a job: show how the first critic's position and the second critic's position instantiate the two sides, using the critics as evidence for the claim rather than as the paragraph's subject. The test is reversibility of emphasis: if the paragraph still makes sense when the critics' names are covered, the topic sentence did its work. Practice this move on the Daisy debate, where the two lines are clear and the shared evidence makes the relationship easy to state precisely.

### Q: How do you quote a scholar without letting the quotation do your arguing?

A quotation serves the review when it does work paraphrase cannot do, and it harms the review when it substitutes for the reviewer's own synthesis. Quote a critic to capture a formulation so precise that paraphrase would blunt it, or to document a claim the review is about to evaluate, so the reader can verify the fairness of the evaluation. Do not quote to establish a position that could be stated in the reviewer's own sentence, and never string quotations together as the paragraph's substance, because a paragraph of quotations is the string of summaries in another costume. Keep the reviewer's synthesis sentences in a wide majority in every section, paraphrase positions as the default, and reserve quotation marks for the formulations that earn them. When a draft shows more quoted sentences than synthesis sentences in a section, the review is letting its sources write it, and the repair is to convert the quotations to paraphrase and rewrite the argument in the reviewer's voice.

### Q: Where should your own voice appear in a Gatsby literature review?

Your own voice belongs in exactly three places and should recede elsewhere. It belongs in the field claims that open sections and paragraphs, because those claims are your judgments about the scholarship's shape. It belongs in the evaluations, where you say which positions the evidence supports and which debates remain open, because a review that never evaluates is a survey rather than a review. And it belongs in the gap and contribution statements, which are your argument about what the field lacks and what your project adds. Everywhere else, the voice should step back and let the positions speak in their strongest form, including the positions you disagree with. The reviewer is the mapmaker, not a combatant in every debate, and the map's authority comes from its fairness. Students who fear disappearing entirely should remember that the field claims and evaluations are unmistakably theirs; students who overwrite every paragraph with opinion should remember that constant intrusion destroys the trust the map requires.

### Q: How do you balance foundational scholarship against later scholarship?

Foundational and later scholarship are balanced by giving each the role the field actually assigns it, rather than by splitting space evenly or by favoring one era. Foundational work, such as Trilling's 1945 essay or Bewley's 1954 reading, appears where it organized a debate or set the terms later critics argued with, and the review should show that organizing function explicitly rather than treating the early essay as one more opinion. Later scholarship appears where it confirmed, revised, or overturned the foundations, and the review should trace those operations so the reader sees the debate move through time. Neither era should be quarantined in its own section; the debate sections mix them, because a debate is defined by disagreement across time as well as within it. The balance to avoid is presentism in reverse, the assumption that newer always supersedes, which misreads how criticism works: some foundational positions still stand, and the review says so where the evidence supports it.

### Q: What is the difference between a gap and a topic nobody has written about?

A gap is an opening the mapped field reveals and the field's logic requires; a topic nobody has written about is usually an absence the field's judgment explains. The difference is tested by the three properties: a real gap sits adjacent to live debates, can be researched with available evidence and method, and matters in the field's own terms, so that filling it would move something the field cares about. A topic nobody has treated typically fails at least one property, most often significance: the scholarship has not produced the study because the subject does not repay it. The empty-shelf fallacy mistakes this good judgment for oversight and builds a project on a foundation the field will not recognize. Reviewers should also watch for the subtler case, the topic that looks unaddressed only because the reviewer has not read widely enough, which is why the gap claim must be stated precisely enough to be checked against the map rather than asserted as a feeling about the literature.

### Q: How do you write a literature review for a short undergraduate essay on Gatsby?

A short undergraduate review, typically eight hundred to fifteen hundred words, succeeds by mapping few debates well rather than many debates thinly. Choose the two or three debates most relevant to the essay's thesis, the ones the thesis must be warranted against, and give each a genuine synthesis section with positions, contestation, and state of play. Resist the urge to mention every debate the field contains; thin coverage is the summary list returning through the back door, and three synthesized debates serve the essay better than six listed ones. Keep the opening to a single paragraph stating the field's shape and the review's logic, and keep the gap claim tight: one precise opening, tested against the three properties, with the essay's thesis located inside it. The standard does not lower with the word count. The synthesis paragraph architecture, the fairness to opposing positions, and the cover-the-names test all apply at eight hundred words exactly as they apply at six thousand.

### Q: How do you write a literature review for a dissertation chapter on Gatsby?

A dissertation-level review, typically several thousand words, carries the same method at larger scale: more debates mapped, the connections between debates developed, and the gap claim fully evidenced. Cover five or six debates, give the central ones the space the field's weight demands, and use the extra length for the operations a short review must compress: tracing how debates evolved, reporting the complicating third positions that keep binaries honest, and showing the alignments between debates that make the field one terrain. The gap section should be substantial, stating the opening's adjacency, researchability, and significance with the map as evidence, and the contribution should be located precisely enough that a reader could state it back. The chief danger at this scale is comprehensiveness for its own sake, the review that keeps going because there is more to list. Length must come from deeper mapping, not wider listing, and any section that cannot pass the cover-the-names test should be rewritten no matter how long the chapter already is.

### Q: How do you cite the same scholar more than once without repeating yourself?

Repeated citation of one scholar is handled by citing positions rather than persons, so each appearance presents only what the section's debate requires. A major critic takes positions in several debates, and the review may need the same name in three sections; the repetition becomes a problem only when each appearance re-summarizes the critic's whole argument. The discipline is to write each appearance from the position outward: in the Daisy section, Fetterley's feminist reading of the character and nothing else; in a section on the novel's women more broadly, whatever that debate requires, stated fresh. Vary the reference form so the prose does not chant the name: the feminist line, the 1978 reading, the position that treats Daisy as constructed, each pointing to the same scholar without restating the biography. Never introduce a critic twice with the full scholarly apparatus; the first appearance establishes who they are, and later appearances can move straight to the position. The reader experiences range rather than repetition when every appearance earns its place in a different debate.

### Q: What does the closing paragraph of a literature review have to do?

The closing paragraph must state the gap and locate the contribution, doing in final form what the whole review has been building toward. It names the opening the map revealed, stated precisely enough to be checked: the question the debates raise but do not answer, the position the field's logic implies, the passage every side cites but none examines. It tests that opening against the three properties in compressed form, showing adjacency, researchability, and significance rather than merely claiming them. And it positions the project as the gap's answer, saying what the project will do, on what evidence, and why the result will matter to the field. What the closing paragraph must not do is summarize the review, introduce new debates, or gesture vaguely at future research. The review's last movement is a commitment, not a recap, and the reader should finish it knowing exactly where the project stands on the map the review has drawn.

### Q: How do you handle two scholars who say nearly the same thing?

Two scholars who say nearly the same thing are handled by grouping them as one position and spending the review's energy on the difference, however small, because the difference is where the field's movement lives. Name the shared position once, attribute it to both, and then state precisely where they diverge: in the evidence cited, in the scope of the claim, in the conclusions drawn from the shared premises. The divergence matters because scholarship advances through such refinements, and a review that treats near-identical positions as simply identical misses the texture of the field. If no meaningful difference exists after honest comparison, say so and treat them as a single instance of the position, giving the space instead to the positions that genuinely contest it. What the review must never do is devote a full paragraph to each as if they were independent contributions, because that is the source-by-source organization smuggled back in, and it teaches the reader nothing the first paragraph had not already taught.

### Q: How do you write about scholarship that contradicts your own thesis?

Scholarship that contradicts your thesis is presented in its strongest form, engaged directly, and answered with evidence rather than minimized or omitted. The temptation to soften the opposing position, to make it sound foolish so the thesis looks strong by comparison, destroys the review's authority with any reader who knows the field, and examiners always know the field. State the contradiction plainly, give the opposing position its best evidence, and then show where your reading diverges and why: a different weighting of the shared evidence, a passage the opposing reading under-reads, a consequence of the position its proponents have not faced. This direct engagement is what makes the review an argument rather than a brief for one side, and it is also strategically sound, because a thesis that has survived its strongest objection in the review arrives at the project's own chapters already tested. The review that hides the contradiction does not protect the thesis; it leaves the thesis defenseless at the first serious reading.

### Q: What notes should you take while reading scholarship for the review?

Notes taken for a literature review record positions, not summaries, because the review is built from the answers to the against question. For each source, log the debate or debates it joins, the position it takes in each stated as an answer to the debate's question, the evidence it leans on most heavily, and what it argues against, naming the opposed position or the critic it answers. Add a line on how the source relates to what you have already read: does it confirm a position, refine one, or open a new front. Keep the novel in the notes by logging the passages each position relies on, since those passages become the review's textual anchors and the raw material of gap claims. This note format converts directly into the synthesis matrix, with each note supplying cells, and it prevents the most common note-taking failure, the page of summary that cannot be turned into a paragraph of synthesis. Twenty sources noted this way give the reviewer a field; twenty sources summarized give the reviewer a pile.

### Q: How do you represent a scholar's argument fairly when you disagree with it?

Fair representation of a scholar you disagree with is a discipline with three rules: state the position in terms its proponent would accept, lead with its strongest evidence, and separate the position from your evaluation of it. The first rule is tested by imagination: would the scholar recognize this description as their view, or only as a caricature. The second rule prevents the quiet burial, where the opposing view appears but its best reasons do not. The third rule keeps the review's structure clean, with the mapping done fairly before the evaluation begins, so the reader can see that the evaluation was earned rather than smuggled in. This fairness is not generosity for its own sake. It is the source of the review's authority, because a reviewer who has stated the opposing case at its strongest and still finds for the other side has done the intellectual work the review exists to display. The unfair review persuades nobody who matters and discredits the reviewer with everybody who does.
