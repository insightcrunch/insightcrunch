Rosa Luxemburg presents a problem that no other major Marxist thinker presents in quite the same form. Four traditions claim her, and they claim her with documents in hand. Social democrats quote her prison manuscript against the Bolshevik suppression of political freedom. Communists point to the party she co-founded in the last fortnight of her life and to the manner of her death at the hands of counter-revolutionary troops. Feminists recover a woman who conquered the most masculine intellectual world in Europe on its own terms and left thousands of pages of correspondence that no male contemporary matched for range. Green movements take up her account of a capitalism that must perpetually consume what lies outside itself. Each claim rests on real texts and real episodes. Each is also partial, and the partiality is not accidental. What none of the claimants holds is the combination she herself held, all of it, at once, for most of her adult life.

![Rosa Luxemburg, an intellectual biography, spontaneity, accumulation and four claiming traditions - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

That combination deserves a name, because naming it is what converts a familiar quarrel over her memory into an intelligible fact about her thought. Call it the unheld combination. She was revolutionary against reformism at a time when her own party was quietly becoming reformist in practice while keeping revolutionary language for festivals. She was democratic against Bolshevik practice at the very moment when she was defending the Bolshevik seizure of power against its social democratic critics, and she wrote the criticism from prison as a supporter, not as an opponent looking for an exit. She was an organiser against every version of spontaneism, a builder and leader of parties across two countries, although posterity persistently remembers her as the theorist of the unled crowd. And she was opposed to national self-determination against almost the whole of her movement, including Lenin, on a point where her position has been vindicated by almost nobody and abandoned even by most of her admirers. Hold all four positions together and you have Luxemburg. Drop any one of them and you have one of her claimants, quoting accurately and representing falsely.

An intellectual biography is the right instrument for this subject because the life and the positions explain each other in both directions. The Polish beginning explains the national stance, since she formed it inside a movement where the national question was not an abstraction but the daily grammar of politics. The Zurich doctorate explains the economics, because she entered Marxist theory through a research problem about industrial development in a partitioned, largely agrarian country, and the realisation problem that occupied her masterpiece was already latent in that research. The German party school explains the organiser, since a woman who spent seven years teaching political economy to the cadres of the largest socialist party in the world was not waiting for history to happen without institutions. The prisons explain the democratic insistence, because she wrote her most searching pages on freedom while locked inside fortresses by one state and watching another state, born of a revolution she supported, narrow the freedoms she believed revolutions exist to widen. Biography organised around the combination, rather than around the famous death, is what the following pages attempt.

Two disciplines govern the account. First, claims about her views are attributed to her texts, with the text named, because Luxemburg more than most thinkers suffers from quotation detached from occasion, and several of her most repeated sentences reverse their meaning when the occasion is restored. Second, the history of her reception is kept in a separate account from the life itself. What later movements made of her is a large and instructive subject, and it receives its own sections, but it is not allowed to leak backwards into the record of what she argued, built, and decided between her birth in a Polish provincial town in 1871 and her murder in Berlin in January 1919. The death itself is reported plainly in its place, with the units involved named and the contested questions marked as contested. It shaped her reception more than any single text she wrote. A biography that made it the climax would be repeating the distortion it exists to correct.

## Rosa Luxemburg and the shape of a life in theory

The bare chronology is quickly told, and telling it plainly first clears the ground for everything interpretive that follows. She was born Rozalia Luksenburg on 5 March 1871 in Zamość, a garrison town in the Polish provinces of the Russian Empire, the youngest of five children in a Jewish family of the commercial middle class. Her father traded in timber; her mother, from a family with rabbinical learning, kept a household in which German and Polish books circulated and education was treated as the one form of property that could not be confiscated. A disorder of the hip, present from early childhood and never corrected, left her with a pronounced limp and slightly unequal legs, a physical fact her enemies would later mine for caricature and her friends recorded as irrelevant to the force of her presence. In 1873 the family moved to Warsaw, where she attended a girls' gymnasium, an institution under Russification pressure in which Polish pupils studied inside an imperial curriculum, and she completed her schooling with distinction while the city around her was absorbing the aftershocks of defeated insurrection and early industrial growth.

Warsaw in the eighteen eighties was a city where every political question arrived doubled. The class question came wrapped in the national question, because the factory owners might be German or Jewish or Polish while the state that policed the factories was Russian, and a strike could be read as an economic act, a national act, or both depending on who was describing it. The first Polish socialist organisation, the Proletariat party, had been broken by executions and Siberian sentences in the middle of the decade, but its literature and its memory circulated among gymnasium pupils, and the young Luxemburg moved in those circles. By 1889 her involvement had made arrest a realistic prospect, and at eighteen she crossed into Austrian territory, by the standard accounts hidden in a hay cart through the border village, and continued to Switzerland. The detail of the hay cart has the polish of legend, and biographers repeat it with varying confidence; the underlying fact is not in doubt, which is that she left Russian Poland as a political fugitive before she was nineteen and never lived in her native country again except as a visitor, a defendant, and a prisoner.

Zurich was the standard refuge of the Polish and Russian emigration for reasons that were practical before they were romantic. The university admitted women to degrees when most European universities did not, the canton was comparatively tolerant of exiles, and the city held a dense colony of Polish socialists among whom a brilliant eighteen year old with gymnasium mathematics and three languages found both comrades and an audience. She enrolled first across philosophy, mathematics, and the natural sciences, a spread that later readers flatten when they cast her as a purely literary revolutionary, and then settled on law and political economy. The settlement matters. Luxemburg came to Marx through the seminar room and the statistical table as much as through the pamphlet, and her mature economics, whatever its contested conclusions, was written by someone trained to handle national accounts, trade returns, and the Polish industrial data that her dissertation organised.

### What did Luxemburg study for her doctorate?

Her doctorate, taken at Zurich in 1897, rested on a study of the industrial development of Poland, examining how Congress Poland had been drawn into the Russian imperial market, how its textile and mining growth depended on eastern demand, and why that integration made an economically self-sufficient Polish state a doubtful proposition.

The dissertation, published in 1898 as The Industrial Development of Poland, is the seedbed of positions that readers who meet her late work first often mistake for improvisations. She argued, against the grain of Polish patriotic economics, that the industrialisation of Congress Poland was not a national achievement waiting for a national state to crown it. It was an integration effect. Łódź textiles sold into the Russian interior; Warsaw manufactures grew behind imperial tariffs; the whole structure presupposed the empire-sized market that political independence would have dismantled. A restored Poland would therefore face an economic logic pulling against its political premise, and the Polish working class would pay for the contradiction. Whatever one makes of the argument's fate, notice its form, because the form recurs everywhere in her work. A cherished political slogan is tested against the material integration of an economy, and the slogan loses. She would apply exactly that procedure to Polish independence for twenty more years, to German reformism in her first famous pamphlet, to the reproduction schemas in her economic masterpiece, and to Bolshevik decrees in her prison manuscript. The doctorate also made her, in the plain institutional sense, one of the earliest women in Europe to hold a doctoral degree in economics, a credential she carried into rooms where no other woman was present and where it functioned, as her correspondence shows she well understood, as armour.

The Polish party came before the German career, a sequence that capsule biographies routinely invert. In 1893, from exile, she joined with Leo Jogiches, Julian Marchlewski, and Adolf Warszawski in founding the Social Democracy of the Kingdom of Poland, a party built against two opponents at once. Against the Polish Socialist Party, founded in Paris the year before under Józef Piłsudski's influence, the new party held that the restoration of Polish independence was a utopian and reactionary diversion from the common struggle of Polish, Russian, and German workers against their respective states. Against orthodox patriots it held that socialism, not statehood, was the answer to Polish misery. The party's programme was internationalist in a sense stricter than the word usually carries. It did not merely add international solidarity to a national programme; it denied that the national programme was a stage on the way, and it organised Polish workers for a revolution expected to arrive across the whole empire at once. When the organisation later extended into Lithuania it became the SDKPiL, the initials under which she conducted Polish politics for the rest of her life, editing its press, drafting its resolutions, and quarrelling with its rivals in a polemical register that Polish opponents remembered with respect and resentment in roughly equal measure.

Jogiches requires a sentence of his own, because the standard narrative mishandles the partnership in two opposite ways, first by making her his pupil and later, in corrective enthusiasm, by making him her secretary. He was a formidable organiser and conspirator, the party's grey eminence, and for years her partner in life as well as in politics; the intellectual work, the books and the theory, was hers, a distribution the surviving correspondence makes plain and which Nettl's biography documented exhaustively. Their relationship cooled into a demanding political alliance, and he was murdered in a Berlin jail in March 1919, weeks after her own killing, while investigating it. The Polish party they built remained small, as any party must that refuses the most popular slogan in its country, but its smallness taught her something the mass German party would later confirm from the opposite direction: that a programme's truth and a programme's popularity are variables with no reliable correlation, and that she was constitutionally willing to pay for the first with the second.

## The German move and the making of a party intellectual

German citizenship, the practical key to a career in the largest socialist movement in the world, was obtained by the method the emigration used when it had to: a marriage of convenience, contracted in 1898 with Gustav Lübeck, the son of an activist family, a legal instrument she treated with dry humour in her letters and never confused with a marriage in any other sense. In May 1898 she settled in Berlin, twenty seven years old, a doctor of economics with one book, a small Polish party behind her, and an immediate appetite for the central argument of German social democracy. The appetite was well timed. The Social Democratic Party of Germany, the SPD, had emerged from the years of Bismarck's antisocialist laws with a mass membership, a daily press, a trade union federation aligned with it, and a theoretical inheritance it administered through Karl Kautsky's journal Die Neue Zeit. It was also, though its language denied the fact, drifting toward a practice of patient parliamentary and union accumulation in which revolution survived as a rhetorical horizon. Eduard Bernstein, lately returned from London exile, was preparing to say the quiet part in print.

Her apprenticeship was journalistic and fast. She wrote for the party press in Dresden and Leipzig, briefly holding an editorship at the Sächsische Arbeiterzeitung that ended in friction with local notables who found her brilliance insubordinate, and she discovered the lecture platform, the other great medium of the movement, where her speaking, precise, ironic, and structurally relentless, began to draw the audiences that followed her for twenty years. Contemporaries who disliked her politics rarely denied the platform gift. She could hold a hall of metalworkers through an hour on the tariff question, and she treated audiences as intelligences to be convinced rather than congregations to be warmed, a respect that working class listeners recognised and returned. Within a few years she was a leading figure of the party's left wing, a contributor to its theoretical journal, a polemicist its right wing read with care, and, in the eyes of the Prussian police who opened files on her movements, the most dangerous woman in the empire. The phrase attached itself to her in the press. Like most of her labels, it tells more about the labellers than about her, but it registers the speed of the ascent accurately enough.

The party school in Berlin completes the picture of the organiser that the spontaneity legend later erased. From 1907 to 1914 she taught political economy and economic history at the SPD's central school, an institution founded to train the movement's future officials, journalists, and deputies, where her colleagues included Franz Mehring, the party's great historian, and where her students included men who would later lead German communism and others who would administer its suppression from social democratic ministries. Teaching was not a refuge from politics for her; her letters treat the classroom as cadre formation in the strictest sense, and the lecture manuscripts that became her Introduction to Political Economy show a teacher building a complete course from crisis theory to the history of communal property for students she expected to run a movement. A theorist of leaderless spontaneity does not spend seven years as the economics instructor of a party school. The institution she served in those years, and the patience the service required, belong near the centre of any honest account of her political character, and they complicate every later attempt to recruit her as a patron of movements that dispense with durable organisation.

### How did the party school shape her influence?

The Berlin school gave Luxemburg a cadre of students trained in her economics, a standing inside the party that no pamphlet alone could have built, and seven years of practice in explaining Marxist theory to working officials, which disciplined her writing toward the clarity that distinguishes even her most technical books.

The school also fixes her coordinates inside the party's internal geography before the war. She belonged to the left, with Mehring, Clara Zetkin, and the young Karl Liebknecht, but her leftism was of a particular kind that later taxonomies keep misplacing. She was against Bernstein's revisionism root and branch, yet her anti-revisionism was never the orthodoxy defence that Kautsky practised, because she held that the party's danger came less from wrong doctrines than from the widening gap between its revolutionary theory and its daily practice, a gap the leadership preferred to administer rather than to close. When the mass strike debates arrived after 1905 she pressed the logic of action against Kautsky's logic of attrition, and the friendship with Kautsky, once close enough that their families holidayed in each other's company, broke in 1910 over the Prussian suffrage campaign, when she demanded that demonstrations be escalated toward a political mass strike and he counselled restraint in print. The break matters beyond biography. It dates her estrangement from the party centre years before the war credits vote, and it shows the estrangement arising from strategy, from the question of what a mass party is for, rather than from temperament, which is how hostile memory preferred to file it.

## The revisionist challenge and her first famous answer

Bernstein's challenge deserves to be stated in its strongest form before her answer is described, because her answer is unintelligible as the mere defence of a catechism it is sometimes mistaken for. Bernstein argued, from the statistics of the eighteen nineties, that capitalism was not moving toward the collapse the party's programme expected. Credit, cartels, and improved communications were damping the crisis cycle; the middle classes were not vanishing; real wages and union strength were rising; democracy was extending. If the facts ran that way, then the movement should revise its theory to match its practice, drop the revolutionary goal as a piece of inherited rhetoric, and become openly what it already was in the unions and the municipalities, a democratic party of social reform. The argument was empirical in method, candid in intention, and aimed at honesty. It also threatened, as she immediately saw, to dissolve the one proposition that made the SPD more than a pressure group: the proposition that its reforms were steps within a transformation of the whole order, not substitutes for that transformation.

Her reply, Social Reform or Revolution, appeared as a series of articles in 1898 and 1899 and in book form in 1899, written when she was twenty eight and new to the German movement, which makes its assurance the more striking. The pamphlet's architecture is worth noticing because she reused it for twenty years. She concedes the opponent's facts wherever they are facts, then contests the interpretation at the level of the system's tendencies, then shows that the opponent's practical conclusion does not follow even from his own evidence. Credit and cartels, she argued, do not abolish the anarchy of production; they displace and concentrate it, sharpening the eventual reckoning and socialising production in forms that make its private appropriation more, not less, anomalous. Trade unions and parliamentary reforms are indispensable, and she never wrote a word against fighting for them; the question is what they are for. A movement that treats the daily struggle as the whole of socialism has not moderated its goal but exchanged it, and the exchange, once theory blesses it, cannot be reversed by resolutions at congresses. The final goal is not a decorative addition to the movement. It is the difference between a movement that transforms society and a movement that administers it. Readers who want the pamphlet's argument unpacked passage by passage will find it in the [reform or revolution readers guide](/2014/08/15/reform-or-revolution-readers-guide/), and the wider quarrel it opened, which ran through the movement for decades, is mapped in the [reform or revolution debate](/2013/02/01/reform-or-revolution-debate/).

Two features of the episode fixed her reputation and her method together. The first is that she won the formal battle and lost the practical war, a pattern her career repeats. Congress after congress condemned revisionism in the resolutions she helped to inspire, Dresden in 1903 most thunderously, while the party's practice continued its drift undisturbed, voting the budgets, minding the union funds, and treating the condemnations as liturgy. She learned from this, earlier than most of her generation, that a Marxist party could hold correct doctrine as a possession while ceasing to act on it, and the lesson is the hidden premise of her later insistence that theory lives only in the movement of the class itself. The second feature is the texture of the polemic. She did not excommunicate Bernstein; she dismantled a position while treating its holder as a serious mind driven by real evidence, and she reserved her contempt for the evasions of the centre, for Kautsky's postponements and the leadership's comfort. Posterity, which prefers its revolutionaries shrill, has never quite known what to do with the courtesy of her strongest polemics, but the courtesy was strategic as well as temperamental: arguments answered at full strength stay answered, while opponents merely denounced return at the next congress with their followings intact.

The revisionism fight also positioned her, though nobody used the vocabulary then, on the question that organises this whole biography. Reform without revolution was one amputation later claimants would perform. Revolution without democracy was another. In 1899 only the first amputation was on the table, and she opposed it completely. The significance of the pamphlet for the unheld combination is that its author was never, from that point forward, available for recruitment by any politics of gradual accommodation, however democratic its manners. When social democratic memory later claimed her as a witness for democracy against communism, it had to forget that the same writer had spent her first German decade arguing that social democracy without a revolutionary goal was a contradiction consuming itself, and that she died having founded a communist party rather than seek shelter in the party of her youth. The forgetting was convenient. It was not textual.

## A revolution in the east and the theory of the mass strike

The Russian revolution of 1905 changed her from a leading polemicist into something rarer, a theorist with an event to think with. Strikes had rolled across the empire from January, soviets had appeared in St Petersburg, and in Congress Poland the movement fused with school strikes and national feeling in ways her party had to navigate daily. She followed the events with the attention of a participant manqué through the autumn, writing analyses for the German press that treated the Russian upheaval as the school in which the European working class would learn its next period. In late December she travelled to Warsaw, illegally, under a false name, to work inside the revolution she had been interpreting, and for two months she edited, organised, and wrote there until the police arrested her in March 1906. She spent the spring in the Warsaw Citadel and the Pawiak prison, in conditions that wrecked her health for a season, and she left Poland in the summer under bail arranged on medical grounds, travelling through Finland, where at Kuokkala she met Lenin, the two of them guests of a movement that still expected to argue inside one international.

Out of that experience came The Mass Strike, the Political Party and the Trade Unions, published in 1906, the pamphlet on which her reputation as a theorist of spontaneity was built and systematically misbuilt. The argument, recovered from the text rather than from the legend, runs as follows. The mass strike is not a device. It cannot be manufactured by a committee's decision, propagated by leaflets, or scheduled for a convenient date, because it is not a tactic alongside others but the characteristic form of movement of the working class in a revolutionary period, the way the class appears on the historical stage when the stage is genuinely its own. In Russia the mass strike had not been decreed; it had matured through a decade of economic struggles, local stoppages, and political shocks, each wave teaching organisation to workers whom no party had reached, until economic and political strikes interpenetrated and the distinction between a wage demand and a demand on the state dissolved in practice. The party's task in such a period is real but specific. It gives political direction, slogans, and clarity about the enemy; it accelerates and generalises; it does not, and cannot, substitute its own calendar for the movement's rhythm. Against the German trade union leaders, who feared mass action as a threat to their funds and their legality, she argued that the unions themselves had grown from earlier waves of struggle and would grow again from new ones, that organisation is as much the product of struggle as its precondition. Against anarchist enthusiasts she argued with equal firmness that the mass strike was no miraculous substitute for politics, no single convulsion after which power lies in the street; it was a period, a process, a school.

### What did Luxemburg mean by the mass strike?

She meant the form of movement of a class in a revolutionary period: strikes that begin over wages or hours, widen as repression politicises them, and merge economic with political demands, arising from accumulated struggle rather than from any committee decision, and giving parties material to direct rather than an event to command.

### Was Luxemburg a spontaneist?

No, in any sense the word usefully carries. She led parties, edited their papers, taught their cadres, and drafted their programmes across twenty five years. Her argument concerned the origin of mass action, which she held arises from struggle itself and cannot be decreed, not the necessity of organisation, which she never questioned.

The misreading has a traceable history, and tracing it is part of correcting it. The label served the German union bureaucracy first, since a Luxemburg who trusted the elemental crowd was easier to dismiss than a Luxemburg who had analysed why the crowd moved and found the bureaucracy's caution theoretically empty. It served later orthodoxies differently, supplying a foil against which a doctrine of party command could define itself, and Lenin's own polemical remarks about spontaneity, aimed at Russian Economism in 1902, were retrospectively borrowed to frame her, although her 1904 exchange with Lenin concerned the structure of a party under illegality and not the worship of the unorganised. It served, finally, some of her admirers, who preferred a prophetess of the streets to a party builder with a doctorate. Each use requires the same excision, of the party school, the Polish organisation, the editorial desks, and the programmes. Restoring those institutions to the portrait does not make her a centralist of the opposite school. Her 1904 essay on the organisational questions of Russian social democracy, written against the ultracentralism she detected in Lenin's plan for a party of professional revolutionaries, warned that a central committee substituted for the class would strangle the initiative on which revolution depends, and the full anatomy of that quarrel belongs to the [luxemburg vs lenin debate](/2014/10/15/luxemburg-vs-lenin-debate/). The point for the biography is the combination again: she held the party necessary and its substitution for the class fatal, in the same years, in print, without experiencing the pair as a contradiction, because for her the party was the class organised at its most conscious, not an external machine applied to it.

The mass strike analysis also travelled, and its travels belong to the life because they show her theory working as an instrument rather than a monument. She deployed it in the Prussian suffrage campaign of 1910, arguing that the movement for equal voting rights in Prussia, where the three class franchise weighted a rich man's ballot above a worker's many times over, should be driven beyond demonstration toward the political mass strike, and that the party's fear of its own weapon was the true obstacle. The campaign subsided under the leadership's management, and her breach with Kautsky followed, as narrated earlier. She deployed the analysis again in judging the wartime strikes that spread through German war industry from 1916, reading them from prison reports as the class reconstituting itself under conditions the official movement had declared impossible. A theory that its author keeps using, on new material, under changing conditions, for a decade, is not a youthful effusion. It is her characteristic contribution to the theory of revolution, the place where her name belongs beside the names the textbooks already canonise for the theory of the party and the theory of the state.

## The accumulation argument and the world outside capital

Her largest book grew from a teacher's difficulty. Preparing her party school lectures and rereading the second volume of Capital, she confronted the reproduction schemas, the numerical models in which Marx traces how the total social capital replaces itself and grows when the economy is divided into a department producing means of production and a department producing means of consumption. Marx used the schemas to display the proportions a growing capitalist economy must maintain. Luxemburg fastened on a question she believed the schemas, as usually read, did not answer. In a society composed exclusively of capitalists and workers, who buys the portion of the surplus product in which accumulated surplus value is embodied? Workers cannot, since their wages cover only the variable capital advanced to them. Capitalists cannot, in their capacity as consumers, since the point of accumulation is not personal consumption but expanded production, and expanded production in one cycle requires buyers for a larger product in the next, in a regress that merely enlarges the question. The demand that closes the circuit, she concluded, must come from outside the purely capitalist relation: from peasants, artisans, colonial populations, and states, from the non-capitalist strata and territories that capitalism penetrates, trades with, taxes, and in the end destroys.

The Accumulation of Capital, published in 1913 with the subtitle A Contribution to the Economic Explanation of Imperialism, works the argument through three great movements. The first restates the realisation problem and audits the history of economic thought for earlier encounters with it, treating the debates between Sismondi, Malthus, Say, and their successors as evidence that the difficulty is real and recurrent rather than an artefact of her reading. The second examines Marx's schemas directly, including the schemes of expanded reproduction and the monetary circulation they presuppose, and argues that they describe the conditions of production with precision while leaving the effective demand for the accumulated residue unaccounted for, a gap Marx himself, in her reading, half acknowledged in his remarks on foreign trade. The third movement turns historical. Capitalism, on her account, has always lived in symbiosis with non-capitalist surroundings: it draws labour, markets, and raw materials from peasant agriculture, handicraft production, and communal land systems, and it transforms those surroundings as it feeds on them, by taxation that forces money incomes, by loans that mortgage states, by land seizure, by the deliberate ruin of native crafts, and by war. Imperialism is the name for the phase in which the remaining non-capitalist world becomes the object of open competition among capitalist states, each racing to secure the shrinking outside on which all depend. When the outside is exhausted, the system's condition of existence is exhausted with it. The economic argument and the political catastrophe are, in the book's design, one argument.

### What problem did The Accumulation of Capital set out to solve?

The book asks who, in a society of only capitalists and workers, can purchase the surplus product in which new accumulation is embodied, since wages cover labour power and capitalist consumption is not the purpose of accumulation. Her answer located the missing demand in non-capitalist buyers outside the system.

The realisation problem has a longer pedigree inside Marxist economics than her book, and the companion treatment of [overproduction and underconsumption](/2013/11/01/overproduction-and-underconsumption/) sets out the rival answers, from the disproportionality accounts to the demand side theories with which hers is often confused. The distinctions matter for placing her. She was not a simple underconsumptionist. She did not argue that crises erupt because workers are paid too little to buy back the product, a claim she regarded as a truism about distribution that misses the mechanism of accumulation itself, since rising accumulation can proceed for long stretches with an expanding wage bill. Her claim concerned the destination of the accumulated portion specifically, the part of surplus value destined to become new capital, and it was structural rather than cyclical: not a theory of why crises break out in one year rather than another, but of what capitalism requires from its environment in order to be capitalism at all. That is why the book, whatever its technical fate, changed the questions her tradition asked about colonies, militarism, and the peasantry. After 1913 it was no longer possible, inside Marxist discussion, to treat imperialism as a mere policy preference that wiser governments might drop. On her account the policy had a function rooted in the circuit of capital, and the afterlife of that relocation, through the theories of imperialism written during and after the war, is traced in the [marxism and imperialism guide](/2017/02/01/marxism-and-imperialism-guide/).

Militarism occupies a chapter that readers of summaries rarely reach and that her green and anti-militarist claimants are right to prize, though they usually prize it without its mechanism. Arms expenditure, financed by indirect taxation falling on workers and peasants, constitutes on her analysis a field of accumulation in its own right: the state guarantees demand, pays from revenues extracted largely from consumption, and thereby realises surplus value through a channel that appears to stand outside the market while resting on the fiscal extraction of the non-capitalist and working population. The analysis is cold and specific, innocent of pacifist rhetoric, and it explains what moral denunciation cannot, why war economies are so readily embraced by capitals that have no ideological need of war. It also completes her portrait of a system whose expansion is simultaneously an expansion of violence, not through the wickedness of its agents but through the structure of its requirements. The young economist who had audited Polish industrial integration in the eighteen nineties had become, by 1913, the theorist of an integration enforced on the whole planet, and the continuity between the dissertation and the masterpiece is the soundest reason for treating her economics and her politics as one career rather than two.

The book's reception inside her own movement was, to put it at its mildest, hostile, and the hostility shaped the rest of her life in ways the biography must register. Reviewers in the party press, including Otto Bauer in Vienna and Gustav Eckstein in Kautsky's journal, treated the work as a blunder to be corrected; Anton Pannekoek, from the left, reviewed it more seriously and still dissented; the party school's star teacher found her major work received by her own movement as an embarrassment, and her private correspondence from 1913 carries the wound plainly. The episode deepened her estrangement from the Kautsky centre before the war supplied a larger cause, and it left the book to be rescued by unlikelier readers: by Nikolai Bukharin, who wrote a book length critique from the Bolshevik side in 1924 that at least took the argument's scale seriously; by Henryk Grossmann, who defended and extended it in 1929; by Paul Sweezy, who gave it a respectful hearing in his 1942 survey of Marxian theory; and by Joan Robinson, whose introduction to the English translation of 1951 praised the question while disputing the answer, an epitaph the book has worn ever since. That pattern, hostile movement, serious enemies, posterity divided between the question and the answer, is the exact shape of the objection that must now be stated at full strength.

## The strongest objection to her economics, stated fairly

The objection is not that she was a poor economist; nobody who worked through the schemas as she did could be dismissed that way. The objection is that her central argument rests on a misreading of the very models it claims to expose. Marx's schemes of expanded reproduction, the critics argued already in 1913 and have argued since, do demonstrate how accumulation proceeds within a closed capitalist economy, because capitalists themselves purchase the additional means of production and the additional consumption goods for the additional workers their investment sets in motion; Department I sells to Department II and to itself in proportions the schemas themselves specify, and the regress she feared dissolves once investment demand is recognised as demand. Otto Bauer constructed a four year counter-scheme, published in Die Neue Zeit within months of her book, in which population growth and accumulation proceed together without any external buyer appearing. On this reading her problem was a pseudo-problem, generated by treating the schemas as descriptions of monetary realisation when they were constructions for displaying proportion, and her solution, the necessary outside, was an answer to a question the theory had never posed. Bukharin added the political sting: a theory that makes capitalism dependent on a non-capitalist environment implies that capitalism collapses mechanically when the environment runs out, replacing the class struggle with an automatic breakdown her own politics elsewhere refused. If the objection stands entire, then the largest theoretical work of her life is an elaborate error, and her reputation must rest on the political writings and on the moral force of her death, which is precisely the distribution of weight her martyrological image assumes.

Answering at the same level of strength begins with a concession, and the concession should not be grudged: the technical objection has substantial force. As a refutation of the schemas, her argument does not survive the counter-schemes, and the consensus of economists who have worked the models, including sympathetic ones, grants the point. What survives is everything the objection does not aim at. She established, with a mass of historical material no reviewer had assembled before her, that capitalism in its actual career had never existed in the closed form the schemas idealise, and that its expansion into non-capitalist strata and territories was not an ornament of its youth but a continuous, violent, and economically functional process: the tax noose around peasant production, the loan as an instrument of state subordination, the destruction of communal tenure, the seizure of land and labour by administrative violence. A theory of imperialism as the competitive partition of the non-capitalist world entered Marxist thought through her book and stayed, even for thinkers, Lenin among them, who built on different economic mechanisms and said so. The relationship between capital and its outside, which she made the central object, remains the question that dependency theory, world systems analysis, and the literature on dispossession keep re-asking in new vocabularies. The fair verdict is the double one her best critics reached: the demonstration fails where she aimed it at the schemas, and the investigation succeeds where she aimed it at history, and a biography that suppressed either half of that verdict would be recruiting her for a claimant's brochure rather than describing her work.

There is a last point in this section that belongs to intellectual character rather than to economics. She answered the critics herself, in a manuscript written in prison during the war and known as the Anti-Critique, published after her death in 1921, and the answer is combative, funny in places, and unyielding on the core claim while conceding details of presentation. Prison manuscripts written under fortress conditions by an author who cannot consult a library are not where economists do their most careful work, and she knew the circumstances perfectly well. That she spent part of her imprisonment defending the book rather than beginning something safer measures the place the argument held in her own estimate of herself. She did not regard the accumulation theory as a footnote to her politics. Neither should her biographers, even, especially, when they find its central proof defective.

## War, prison, and the Junius argument

The fourth of August 1914 is the hinge of her life, as it was of her century. The SPD fraction in the Reichstag voted the Kaiser's war credits, unanimously in the public vote, and the International whose congresses had pledged resistance to imperialist war dissolved into its national parts within days. Her private reaction is recorded by friends as physical despair; her public reaction was organisational. Within days she had gathered the small circle, Liebknecht, Mehring, Zetkin, and a handful of others, that became the Gruppe Internationale and in time the Spartacus League, named for the leader of the Roman slave revolt and signing its illegal letters with the pseudonym Spartacus. Karl Liebknecht's lone Reichstag vote against further credits in December 1914 made him the public symbol of the opposition; she was its principal theorist and, through the Spartacus letters that circulated illegally to soldiers and workers, a principal voice. The state understood the distribution of danger accurately. She had already been convicted in February 1914 for a Frankfurt speech in which she told an audience that soldiers ordered to fire on their fathers and brothers should consider what obedience meant, a prosecution that turned her trial into a platform; in February 1915 she began serving that sentence in a Berlin women's prison, and from July 1916, after a period of precarious liberty, the military authorities held her in preventive detention without further sentence, first at the fortress of Wronke and then at Breslau, until the revolution opened the gates in November 1918. Of the war's fifty one months she spent roughly forty behind walls of one kind or another.

From the first of those imprisonments came the Junius Pamphlet, written in her Berlin cell in the spring of 1915 and published clandestinely in 1916 under the pseudonym Junius, formally titled The Crisis of German Social Democracy. The pamphlet is her most complete political testament from the war years and the document on which the green and anti-militarist claims to her largely rest, though its argument is not pacifist. She analysed the war as an imperialist contest on every side, refused the defence of fatherland in each belligerent camp, and turned her fiercest pages not on the governments but on her own party, whose collapse she treated as the war's most consequential event, the bankruptcy of the institution to which European socialism had entrusted its future. Against the ruin she posed the alternative that has since travelled the world attached to her name.

### Where did the phrase socialism or barbarism come from?

She posed the alternative in the Junius Pamphlet and attributed it to Engels, writing that bourgeois society stood at a crossroads, either transition to socialism or regression into barbarism, and crediting Engels with having framed that choice a generation earlier. The coinage is his by her own testimony.

### What was the Junius Pamphlet?

The Junius Pamphlet, issued illegally under a Roman pseudonym while its author sat in a Berlin prison, surveyed the imperialist war, indicted the German party's collapse as the decisive catastrophe, and argued that the working class must rebuild an international opposition or watch civilisation regress toward the barbarism she named.

The prison years were not an interruption of her intellectual life but one of its most productive seasons, a paradox her correspondence embodies so fully that the letters have become a separate door into her reputation. From Wronke and Breslau she wrote the letters that readers ever since have taken as the revelation of a private Luxemburg behind the polemicist: letters about birdsong identified by ear, about the geology of her walks in the fortress yard, about a buffalo hide she had seen beaten in a prison yard and whose pain she recorded with a moral attention that crossed species without sentimentality, letters to Sophie Liebknecht and to the young Hans Diefenbach, killed at the front in 1917, that move between botany, literature, and politics without changing register. The letters matter to the biography for a disciplined reason. They show the democratic insistence and the economic theory growing from the same root of attention, a mind that registered particulars, the actual bird, the actual peasant commune, the actual wording of a decree, and distrusted every abstraction that had stopped answering to them. The feminist and green recoveries of her work begin in these letters more than in any programme, and they are entitled to the material. What they are not entitled to do, and mostly do not, is set the letters against the treatises as the warm truth against the cold doctrine. She wrote the Anti-Critique of her economics in the same confinement as the letters about the buffalo. The prisoner was one person.

It was also in prison, at Breslau in the autumn of 1918, that she wrote the manuscript on the Russian Revolution which has organised her posthumous quarrels ever since. News reached her censored and late; the manuscript, unfinished and unrevised, breaks off in mid-course; it was published in 1922 by Paul Levi, the executed party's second leader in all but name, against the wishes of the German communist leadership, and its afterlife as a weapon in other people's struggles should not obscure what it is: a supporter's audit. She hailed the Bolsheviks as the one party that had understood the revolution's logic and dared to act, credited them with an immortal place in history for the October seizure itself, and then audited the regime's emergency measures as a theorist of the thing they were jointly trying to build. The dispersal of the Constituent Assembly, the restriction of the suffrage, the suppression of press and assembly freedom, the land settlement that multiplied small proprietors, the endorsement of national self-determination, and the reliance on terror each drew criticism whose premise was agreement about the goal.

### Did Luxemburg criticise the Bolsheviks?

Yes, from a position of support. Writing in prison in 1918, she defended the revolution's legitimacy while criticising the Constituent Assembly's dissolution, the narrowing of the franchise, the curbs on press and assembly, and the land and nationality policies, arguing that socialist rule starved of open contest would see public life wither.

The manuscript's central sentence has been quoted so often, and so often severed from its argument, that restoring the argument is a biographical duty. Her claim was not that freedom is a liberal ornament socialism should keep for its respectability. Her claim was functional and severe: that without general elections, unrestricted freedom of the press and of assembly, and a free struggle of opinion, life dies out in every public institution, the soviets themselves become a façade, and rule passes by default to a bureaucracy, because the working class can learn to govern only by governing, errors included, in the open. The famous formulation that freedom is always the freedom of those who think differently belongs inside that institutional argument, and quote collections that lift it as an aphorism about tolerance shrink it into the liberal commonplace she was explicitly going beyond. The companion discussion of [marxism and democracy critique](/2013/03/15/marxism-and-democracy-critique/) places the manuscript in the longer argument about Marxism and political freedom; the biography's narrower point is about the combination. In the same autumn she defended October against Kautsky's attack in her pamphlet's surviving fragments and criticised October's methods in her manuscript. Both documents are hers. Claimants who quote one are describing half a mind at the moment it was thinking hardest.

## The national question, her most distinctive stance

Every element of the unheld combination has its defenders somewhere in her posterity except one, and the exception is diagnostic. Her position on national self-determination, formed in the Polish struggles of the eighteen nineties and held without retreat for twenty five years, is the stance her claimants of every school quietly set aside, because it embarrasses all of them. She held that in the epoch of imperialism the slogan of national self-determination, and with it the project of a restored independent Poland, was a reactionary utopia: reactionary because it directed Polish workers toward an alliance with their own possessing classes for a state that economic integration had already made unviable, utopian because the partitioning empires would yield Polish territory only in a general European catastrophe whose outcome no national programme could steer. The positive side of the position is as important as the negative and is usually dropped from summaries. She fought for Polish culture, Polish language schooling, and the organisational autonomy of Polish socialism with a ferocity her Polish opponents acknowledged, and her party's press defended Polish workers against Russification as a matter of course. What she refused was the conversion of cultural and democratic demands into a state project. Nation, for her analysis, was a category that divided the only subject she believed history had equipped to transform society, and every national banner raised over a mixed working class was paid for in the currency of that division.

The quarrel with Lenin on this point is the deepest theoretical disagreement between them, deeper than the organisational exchange of 1904 with which it is often confused. Lenin held self-determination, up to and including secession, to be a democratic right whose recognition was the precondition of voluntary unity between the workers of oppressor and oppressed nations, and he wrote against her position directly, naming the Polish comrades' error with respect and asperity in 1914 and again in 1916. She held the right to be an empty abstraction in an imperialist world, a formula that in practice handed the national movement to the bourgeoisie of the small nation and the diplomacy of the great powers. The exchange continued into her prison manuscript, where the Bolsheviks' nationalities policy, the recognition of Finnish, Ukrainian, and other separations, drew her charge that the revolution was disarming itself by fragmenting the economic and military space it needed to survive. Historians have usually scored the argument to Lenin on the politics of the century that followed, in which national movements proved the most durable mass force on earth and communist parties that ignored them perished of the ignorance. Her side of the argument survives less as a programme than as a standing cross-examination of every politics that treats the nation as a natural home: her questions about who pays for the banner, and in what currency, have not stopped being pertinent merely because her answer stopped being available. The dedicated anatomy of her disagreements with Lenin, organisational and national together, is held in the [luxemburg vs lenin debate](/2014/10/15/luxemburg-vs-lenin-debate/), and this biography deliberately leaves the fine grain there.

Two biographical roots of the stance deserve record, since caricature prefers to explain it as doctrine hardened into dogma. The first root is empirical and Polish: she had watched the independence slogan, in the hands of the Polish Socialist Party, subordinate strikes to diplomacy and class demands to the calendar of a future state's necessities, and she had built a rival party on the refusal. The second root is the dissertation's: her economics told her that Polish industry lived by the imperial market, so that independence promised the Polish worker a smaller economy guarded by a native bourgeoisie, an exchange she described once, in the Polish polemics, as trading the substance for the flag. Readers may judge the analysis wrong; the century gave them material. What they cannot coherently do is hold her as an authority on the freedom of opinion, the mass strike, and the critique of imperialism while treating the national position as an inexplicable blind spot pasted onto the system from outside. It is load bearing in the system. It follows from the same refusal to let any intermediate banner, national, parliamentary, or bureaucratic, stand between the working class and its own unified action, and it is precisely this load bearing quality that makes the position the combination's least claimable part.

## The organiser restored: parties she built and led

Because the spontaneity legend does so much work in her posthumous image, the organisational record deserves to be laid out as a record, in sequence, rather than asserted as a corrective. In 1893 she co-founded a Polish party and for a quarter of a century she wrote its programme polemics, edited and contributed to its press, represented it in the International, and sustained its organisation through illegality, the 1905 revolution, and the war. From 1898 she worked inside the German party as journalist, editor, and agitator, accepting its discipline when she lost votes at its congresses and contesting its leadership from within its own institutions for twenty years rather than splitting them. From 1907 she taught at its school. In 1914, when the institution she had served capitulated to the war, she did not retire into individual protest; she founded a group, then a league, then, in the last days of December 1918, a party, and she drafted for that party's founding congress the programme, What Does the Spartacus League Want, which set out its aims in the transitional language of soviet power, disarmament of the counter-revolution, and socialisation, a document adopted by a congress whose other decisions she partly lost. On the final question that congress decided against her, whether the new party should contest the elections to the National Assembly or boycott them, she argued for participation, for using the election as a tribune while denying the assembly's legitimacy as the revolution's destination, and was outvoted by sixty two to twenty three. The episode is sometimes quoted to show her moderation and sometimes her irrelevance. It shows something better: a founder submitting her judgement to her own organisation's vote on the eve of catastrophe, because the discipline she preached against ultracentralism was one she practised on herself.

Her organisational theory, such as she stated it in the 1904 essay and across the mass strike writings, is therefore best read as the reflection of this practice rather than as its opposite. A party, in her handling, is neither a general staff issuing the class its orders nor a debating club registering the class's moods. It is the organised form of the class's most conscious elements, bound to the mass movement by a two way traffic: the party brings clarity, memory, and slogans distilled from previous struggles; the movement brings the energy, invention, and scale that no committee possesses, and periodically corrects the party by outrunning it. Errors by the class in motion were, for her, pedagogically precious, a position she stated in 1904 with a confidence that still startles: the mistakes of a genuinely revolutionary movement are historically more fruitful than the infallibility of the cleverest central committee. Readers who find the sentence romantic should set it beside her conduct in January 1919, when she judged an insurrection premature, said so to its leaders, and then, the decision taken by others and the fighting begun, stayed at her editorial post in the rising's newspaper rather than preserve her correctness at a distance. Theory and biography lock together at that point, and the lock is the strongest single evidence against the spontaneist caricature: she believed movements teach, and she paid tuition.

### Did Luxemburg want a party or did she oppose parties?

She founded, led, and taught in parties for twenty five years, and her objection was never to parties as such. Her target was substitution, a committee governing in the class's name and stifling the initiative on which, in her theory, every revolution depends for its energy and its correction.

The Polish and German halves of this record are usually narrated as a career change, Warsaw yielding to Berlin, when the texture of the work suggests something more instructive: two different political materials worked by one method. In Poland she operated inside a small illegal and semi-legal movement where the party was a cadre organisation by necessity, where the press was smuggled, where the national question saturated every demand, and where the enemy state was an empire ruling her people as foreigners. In Germany she operated inside the largest legal mass party in the socialist world, with its unions, cooperatives, insurance funds, and parliamentary fraction, where the enemy was less often the policeman than the institution's own accumulated comfort, and where the fight for the party's soul was conducted in congress resolutions and theoretical journals. The method that travelled between the two settings was the dissertation's method: test the slogan against the material structure, refuse the intermediate banner, and insist that the class's own movement is the only engine that has ever moved the structure. The audiences differed so completely that her tone had to differ, Polish polemics being notoriously the sharper, yet readers who place the Warsaw and Berlin writings side by side find the same mind at the same work, and the comparison is among the surest antidotes to the habit of treating her German fame as the whole of her.

## The four claimants table

The claims can now be mapped, because the life has been laid out in enough detail for the mapping to be audited against it. The four claimants table below sets out, for each tradition that claims her, what it cites, what it omits, and what she actually held on the point in dispute. The table is the article's findable artifact, and its discipline is symmetry: every claimant is credited with real evidence and charged with a real omission, because a map that flattered one claimant would be a fifth claim in disguise.

| Claimant | What it cites | What it omits | What she held |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Social democracy | The prison manuscript's defence of elections, press freedom, and open contest against Bolshevik practice | Her founding of a communist party, her verdict on the party of 1914, and her lifelong refusal of reform without revolution | That democracy is the element in which socialism lives, and that reform divorced from the revolutionary goal administers capitalism rather than transforming it |
| Communism | Her co-founding of the German communist party, her martyrdom under a social democratic government, and her defence of October against its liberal and social democratic critics | Her criticism of the dispersal of the assembly, the restricted suffrage, the muzzling of newspapers and meetings, and her warning that rule without contest breeds bureaucracy | That the seizure of power was justified and necessary, and that its survival required the widest political freedom for the working population, opponents included |
| Feminism | Her conquest of a male intellectual world, her suffrage agitation, and the letters, which display a woman's inner life of unmatched range inside the movement | Her refusal of a separate women's politics, her subordination of feminist demands to the class struggle, and her polemics against bourgeois feminism | That women's emancipation was inseparable from the transformation of society as a whole, to be won by working women in struggle alongside their class rather than by a movement of women as women |
| Green movements | The accumulation theory's picture of a capital that must consume a world outside itself, the militarism analysis, and the letters' attention to living nature | Her commitment to industrial development, her productivism, and her belief that the system would be replaced by planning rather than by restraint | That capitalism's expansion devours non-capitalist life as a structural necessity, an argument about accumulation's mechanics that she never converted into an ethic of limits |

Reading down the third column is the quickest way to feel the unheld combination as a fact rather than a slogan. Each omitted item is the item another claimant cites. Social democracy omits the communist founder; communism omits the democratic critic; feminism omits the class first refusal of separate organisation; the greens omit the industrialiser who expected abundance from a rationally planned economy and would have found a politics of less alien to every instinct her work displays. The omissions are not random gaps in reading. They are structural necessities of the claims, because the combination, held whole, indicts every claimant on the point of its own self image. The social democrat must explain why his tradition's founder figure died founding the rival party under his own government's emergency. The communist must explain why his founder figure's most considered judgement on his tradition's founding regime is a warning against its methods. The feminist must explain the refusal of feminism as a separate banner. The green must explain the absence of limits in a thinker who welcomed the fullest development of the productive forces. Each explanation is possible; movements are ingenious. But the necessity of explaining is the signature of the combination, and it is why she is quotable by all of them and representative of none.

Two subordinate claims, visible inside the table's larger ones, deserve separate mention because the brief for this biography insists on precision about her constituency. Council communists of the Dutch and German left took the mass strike writings and the anti-substitution argument as charters for a politics of workers' councils against all parties, including hers, a reading that keeps the anti-bureaucratic half of her thought and discards the party builder whole, and democratic socialists of the postwar decades took the prison manuscript as a founding text for a socialism of free institutions, keeping the democratic half and discarding the revolutionary who insisted, in the same season, that the councils and not the National Assembly were the revolution's legitimate organs. Leninism, finally, in its own self understanding, has usually handled her by the method of Lenin's 1922 marginalia, which called her an eagle while cataloguing her errors on accumulation, on the national question, and on the Mensheviks, a verdict of genius with deviations that conveniently left the deviating half to be administered by others. The eagle judgement and the deviation catalogue are both genuine parts of the record. What neither captures is that the deviations and the genius were one position, and that the position, the unheld combination, is the only Luxemburg there is.

### What is the unheld combination?

The unheld combination is the set of positions Luxemburg held together: revolutionary against reformism, democratic against bureaucratic substitution, organised against mere spontaneity, and internationalist against national self-determination. No later current has kept all four intact, which is why each quotes her selectively and none can represent her whole.

A word about Luxemburgism belongs here, since the word circulated for decades as if it named a doctrine with adherents. As a label it was forged chiefly by her opponents inside the communist movement of the nineteen twenties, who needed a name for the deviation they were disciplining in the German party, and it was worn proudly by almost nobody at the time, her closest associates preferring to call themselves Spartacists or simply communists. Trotsky, defending her memory against Stalin's attack of 1931, treated the label as a fabrication concealing an attempt to set the dead Luxemburg against the living movement she had helped to found. Later scholarship has sometimes revived Luxemburgism as a convenient name for the combination itself, and used so, descriptively, the word does no harm. But the biography's discipline suggests caution: a thinker whose essence was a combination held against the grain of every organised successor is ill served by an ism, since the ism invites exactly the extraction of a portable doctrine that her whole resists.

## Release, revolution, and the final weeks

The gates of Breslau prison opened on 8 November 1918, in the general collapse of the imperial regime, and she travelled to Berlin through a country in which the old authority had dissolved with a speed nobody had rehearsed. Kiel's sailors had mutinied at the month's start; councils of workers and soldiers were forming in the cities; on 9 November the republic was proclaimed twice in one afternoon, by Philipp Scheidemann from the Reichstag to pre-empt the other proclamation Karl Liebknecht delivered hours later from the palace. She arrived into a revolution whose leadership she did not control, whose tempo she judged dangerously faster than its organisation, and whose enemies were arming with government blessing. Her instrument was the newspaper. With Liebknecht she edited Die Rote Fahne, the Red Flag, writing daily leaders of extraordinary compression in which the programme of the Spartacus League was argued against the government of Friedrich Ebert and the Majority Social Democrats: arm the workers, disarm the counter-revolution, vest power in the councils, convene the revolution's own congress, socialise the decisive industries. The government she opposed was led by the party she had served for twenty years, and it was negotiating, as the Ebert Groener arrangement of 10 November embodied, the support of the old officer corps against the revolution's radical wing. The alignment that would kill her was visible in print, in her own sentences, weeks before it acted.

At the turn of the year the Spartacus League, with the Revolutionary Stewards and the left of the Independent Social Democrats who had broken from the majority party in 1917, convened the founding congress of the Communist Party of Germany. She gave the programme address. She also lost, as recorded earlier, the vote on the National Assembly elections, and the loss deserves a second look in this setting because it illuminates her judgement of the revolution's balance of forces in the last days of December. She believed the masses were not yet won, that the councils were immature, that an attempted seizure of power in Berlin would be crushed and would drown the young party in the defeat, and that the party's task was the slower conquest of the majority inside the council movement. The congress, younger and more impatient, voted the other way on the elections while adopting her programme. A founder who cannot carry her own founding congress on the tactical question of the hour is in a weak position to restrain a rising she has not called, which is the position she occupied when, in the first days of January, the dismissal of Berlin's leftist police president Emil Eichhorn brought the Revolutionary Stewards, the Independents, and the crowds into the streets and the government brought its Freikorps toward the capital.

Her conduct in the January rising is among the most falsified passages of her life, falsified in opposite directions by the two traditions that needed her as respectively a reckless putschist and a pure martyr of other men's errors. The documentary position, established by the standard biographies from the correspondence and the newspaper record, is narrower and more honourable. She judged the rising premature and counselled against the attempt to overthrow the government at that moment, believing the provincial councils and the Berlin masses alike unready; she was overruled within the improvised revolutionary committee in which Liebknecht sat and she did not. When the fighting began she refused the counsels of flight and of editorial silence, remained in Berlin, and wrote for Die Rote Fahne through the suppression, including, on 14 January, the editorial published the day before her death under the title Order Reigns in Berlin, a savage audit of the government's restored order and its price, which closed with the declaration that the revolution would raise itself again and proclaim, I was, I am, I shall be. She did not support the rising as her own decision. She supported it, once begun, as her own side. The distinction cost her life and should not be blurred by anyone summarising the week, and the full narrative of the German revolution, in which her fortnight is one strand, belongs to the [german revolution marxist analysis](/2014/11/01/german-revolution-marxist-analysis/).

## The murder, reported plainly

On the evening of 15 January 1919, Luxemburg and Liebknecht were arrested at the Wilmersdorf apartment where they had been hidden by friends after the rising's collapse, betrayed to the authorities, and taken to the Eden Hotel, the headquarters of the Guards Cavalry Rifle Division, a Freikorps formation under the command of Captain Waldemar Pabst which operated in Berlin under the authority of the social democratic government and its people's deputy for military affairs, Gustav Noske. Liebknecht was driven to the Tiergarten and shot, the official fiction being an escape attempt. Luxemburg, leaving the hotel under escort, was struck on the head with a rifle butt by a soldier named Otto Runge and then shot in the vehicle, by the standard account by Lieutenant Kurt Vogel, and her body was thrown from a bridge into the Landwehr Canal. It was recovered at a canal lock on 31 May 1919, and she was buried at Friedrichsfelde cemetery on 13 June beside Liebknecht, whose grave had waited for hers. The military proceedings that followed were notoriously lenient: Runge and Vogel received short sentences, Vogel was assisted to escape abroad, and Pabst was never tried. These are the facts the record supports, and an intellectual biography has no need to decorate them.

### How should her murder be described accurately?

As the killing of a prisoner by soldiers of a Freikorps division operating under government authority, followed by the disposal of her body in a canal and by lenient military justice, with the degree of complicity of the governing party's leadership presented as the contested question historians still treat it as.

The contested question should be stated precisely so that its contestedness is understood rather than merely flagged. That the government authorised and depended upon the Freikorps, that Noske accepted political responsibility for the suppression in terms that made him the revolution's most hated social democrat, and that the division's officers understood themselves to be executing the government's will against the Spartacus leaders, are matters of record. Whether Ebert, Noske, or any member of the government ordered, desired in advance, or welcomed after the fact the murder of Luxemburg and Liebknecht specifically has been argued for a century on evidence that includes Pabst's late and self serving claims, made decades afterward, that he had obtained political approval, claims other historians treat as a murderer distributing his guilt upward. The standard scholarly position, represented by Nettl and by the modern German literature, is that the murders were the division's work, committed in an atmosphere the government had created and from which it drew the benefit, with direct governmental instruction unproven. A biography reports that distribution of certainty and stops there. Her murder is the fact that did most to shape her afterlife, and treating it soberly is the precondition of treating the afterlife itself as an object of study rather than a continuation of the crime by other means.

## The afterlife: how a death reorganised a reading

The reception history of Luxemburg is the history of her texts being read through the canal water, and separating the two is the last large task of the biography. In the first posthumous decade the communist movement she had co-founded could not decide whether she was its co-founder or its internal critic, and it resolved the tension by period. Under the German party leadership of the mid nineteen twenties, Luxemburgism was named a deviation and her prison manuscript's publication was treated as an indiscretion, while in Moscow the Comintern's historians filed her under genius with errors. Georg Lukács, writing in 1923, had already taken the opposite path, treating her as the truest continuator of Marx's method, the thinker of totality, in essays that the same communist orthodoxy condemned. Trotsky's defence of her against Stalin's 1931 attack, when the Soviet leader's letter on party history had sneered at her legacy and Trotsky answered with an article demanding that hands be kept off her memory, showed the exiled opposition claiming her for its own genealogy against the ruling apparatus, an early proof that the combination would be divided among heirs like an estate among litigants. Paul Frölich, comrade and executor of her papers, gave the first serious biography in 1928 and its fuller version in exile, the foundation on which J. P. Nettl's two volume study of 1966 built the modern scholarly Luxemburg: documented, unsentimental, and restored to her economics.

Against that restoration worked the contrary pressure of the martyr cult, which every tradition found useful and none found resistible for long. The annual demonstrations to her grave in Friedrichsfelde, the streets and squares named for her in the eastern state after 1949, the official veneration of a Luxemburg whose democratic manuscript the same state did not circulate, all converted the thinker into an icon whose function was to be mourned on schedule. The conversion reached its strangest point in January 1988, when East German dissidents marched in the official commemoration carrying her sentence about the freedom of those who think differently on their banners and were arrested by the state that claimed to be her heir, an episode that condenses her whole reception into one image: the heirs policing the quotation. In the west the feminist recovery of the nineteen seventies performed a different and largely honourable restoration, publishing the letters, insisting on the significance of a woman's life inside the movement's history, and correcting the male tradition's habit of treating her as an honorary exception; the correction sometimes slid toward claiming the life against the politics, as though the theorist of accumulation were an interruption of the letter writer rather than the same person at another desk. Green readings arrived with the ecology movements and fastened, as the table records, on genuine material, the devouring outside of the accumulation theory and the letters' creaturely attention, while passing over her expectations of industrial abundance in a planned economy, expectations as Promethean as any in her century.

The scholarly moral of the reception is not that all readings are false. It is that the combination functions as a test each reading fails at a different joint, and that the failures are therefore information: a reception map of Luxemburg is a map of what each successor movement could not afford to think. The evidence that would most change the standard picture, were it to appear, is worth naming in a biography that prizes falsifiability. A Luxemburg text endorsing a separate women's organisation, or blessing national independence, or repudiating party organisation, would each break the combination at a load bearing joint, and none has appeared in a century of archival publication that has instead filled the joint with fresh documentation: the complete correspondence, the Polish writings in scholarly edition, the collected works in German and in English translation, volume by volume, have enlarged every element of the combination and dissolved none. That stability under archival pressure is the closest thing reception history offers to a verdict, and it supports the biography's organising decision. The centre of her life is not the night at the Eden Hotel. It is the four positions, held together, in the open, against the whole of her movement at one point or another, for twenty five years.

## The economics lectures and the unfinished introduction

The treatise was not her only economic book, and the other one, though unfinished and posthumous, may be the better evidence of how her mind worked when it was building rather than demolishing. The Introduction to Political Economy grew from her party school teaching and occupied her, in drafts and redrafts, across the prewar years and into her imprisonment; the manuscript as published after her death breaks off in the midst of its design, but the design is complete enough to read. She intended a political economy that began not with the isolated commodity but with the historical variety of social forms: primitive communism in its ethnographic record, slavery, feudalism, the peasant commune in its Russian and Indian and Germanic survivals, and only then the capitalist order, whose categories she wanted the student to meet as the temporary forms of a late arrival rather than as the furniture of nature. The method is the accumulation argument in embryo. Capital appears throughout as a relation that lives among other relations and feeds on them, and the student who had mastered the introduction was being prepared, whether the party school knew it or not, for the thesis of 1913.

The lectures also fix something about her intellectual temperament that the polemics can obscure. She was, by the testimony of the manuscript, an exceptionally patient expositor, willing to spend chapters on the Mark commune or the Inca storehouse because she believed the abstractions of value theory would mean nothing to students who had not first seen economies organised on opposite principles. Her erudition in the ethnographic and historical literature of her day was real and wide, drawn from the same Zurich formation that had begun in mathematics and natural history. Opponents within the movement sometimes filed her as a journalist of genius, a phrase meant to demote; the lecture manuscripts refute the filing. So does the scholarly apparatus of the accumulation book, whose first movement is a critical history of political economy from the physiocrats forward, executed with a command of the French and German literature that professional reviewers, even hostile ones, did not dispute. The biography's claim for her intellectual standing does not require exaggerating this record into infallibility; the strongest objection section has already conceded the central proof of her masterpiece. It requires only that the record be admitted into the portrait, because a claimant's Luxemburg built from the letters and the last fortnight has no room in it for the scholar who spent a decade teaching the reproduction schemas to the officials of a mass party.

One further economic work belongs to the wartime prison and to completeness: her study of the Russian agrarian question and of Slav economic history, pursued at Wronke and Breslau, including a long engagement with the dissolution of the peasant commune and translations and notes toward an autobiography of her own intellectual formation in political economy. The material remained fragmentary, and she did not live to shape it. Its interest here is directional. In the very years when she was supposedly becoming the lyric poet of revolution, her desk held agrarian statistics and the history of communal property, the oldest subjects of her formation, being worked with the same patience as in 1897. Intellectual biography exists to notice such continuities, because they are what make a thinker's positions a system rather than a sequence of responses to events.

## Her Russia: the empire she knew from inside

No account of her formation is complete without the empire that formed the negative pole of all her thinking about states. She was born a subject of the Tsar, educated under Russification, exiled from his Polish provinces, imprisoned in his Warsaw citadel, and formed as a revolutionary inside a movement whose horizon was the empire's overthrow by the joint action of its subject peoples' workers. Russia was never, for her, the abstraction it could be for German comrades who knew it through congress delegations. She read its press in the original, corresponded with its factions, knew the Mensheviks and Bolsheviks as persons and programmes rather than as positions in a German debate, and in 1907 sat in the London congress of the Russian party as a Polish delegate arguing the agrarian programme. This intimacy explains features of her later judgements that puzzle readers who meet her through German sources alone. Her confidence that the Russian revolution of 1905 was the prologue to a European period, not an eastern peculiarity, came from a formation that treated the empire's working class as the most politically educated audience she had ever addressed. Her severity toward the Bolsheviks' decrees in 1918 came from the same place: she knew exactly what the Constituent Assembly, the land settlement, and the nationalities recognised on paper meant on the ground, having contested those very questions inside the Russian movement for twenty years.

The intimacy also disciplines the famous quarrel with Lenin into its proper proportions, which both traditions have inflated. They disagreed, seriously and in print, about the structure of an illegal party in 1904 and about the national right across a decade; they also collaborated, voted together at international congresses against the revisionists and the liquidators, shared a platform at Stuttgart in 1907 where she and Lenin jointly amended the war resolution in the form that later became the Bolsheviks' charter, and recognised each other across every polemic as belonging to the same revolutionary family quarrelling over its inheritance. Lenin's posthumous note on her prison manuscript, the eagle passage, performs the whole relationship in miniature: admiration for the bird's flight, a catalogue of its errors, and the prediction that her work would serve as a lesson to many communists, a prediction the century fulfilled in ways its author did not intend. The biography records the relationship at this temperature because the combination requires it. Her criticism of Bolshevik practice has the weight it has precisely because it was not the criticism of an outsider, a liberal, or a Menshevik; it was the audit of a co-founder of the revolutionary camp, delivered from a cell, in the revolution's defence as she understood the term.

### In what ways did her Warsaw formation differ from her Berlin career?

In Poland she built a small illegal cadre party against the national slogan, editing smuggled papers under an empire that jailed her; in Germany she worked a legal mass party from inside, teaching its school and fighting its leadership in its own journals. The settings differed utterly; the method, testing slogans against material structure, did not.

That H3 answer compresses a comparison the body of this biography has distributed across its sections, and compressing it exposes the answer's limit, which is worth a paragraph of prose. The difference was not only scale and legality. It was a difference in the direction of her dissent. In Poland she stood to the internationalist left of a movement tempted by nation; in Germany she stood to the revolutionary left of a movement tempted by respectability; and in both cases the temptation she fought was the temptation of the institution she belonged to, never of an institution she observed from outside. She was a dissenter of the interior, a type movements find harder to forgive than enemies, since the interior dissenter cannot be answered by the boundary. Her parties responded as institutions do: the Polish rivals anathematised her as a traitor to the nation, the German leadership managed her as a brilliant embarrassment, and the communist successors disciplined her as a deviation. The continuity of that response, across three organisations and two countries, is itself biographical evidence about the combination she carried. It was indigestible everywhere for the same reason.

## Women, suffrage, and the refusal of a separate banner

The feminist claim on Luxemburg is the most sympathetic of the four and the one requiring the most delicate handling, because the evidence genuinely runs in both directions and a biography loses credit if it pretends otherwise. Begin with what the claim rightly cites. She was the most prominent woman in the European socialist movement of her generation alongside Clara Zetkin, her friend and ally of thirty years, and she achieved the position without any of the protections of dynasty, salon, or separate women's organisation, by the direct route of scholarship and polemic in institutions that admitted her as an anomaly and resisted her as a precedent. She agitated for women's suffrage as a socialist demand of the first rank, notably in her 1912 speech on women's suffrage and class struggle, in which she argued that the vote for working women was not a favour to be granted by enlightened men but a weapon the class needed, since a proletariat that left its women voteless fought with half its army unarmed. She lived a life that broke the conventions of her time without theorising the breakage: the legal fiction of her marriage, the partnership with Jogiches conducted outside every form, the later bond with Kostja Zetkin, the refusal of domesticity as a destiny. And she left the letters, in which a woman's consciousness of the natural world, of literature, of her own body and its limits, is recorded with a fullness no male leader of the movement approached.

Against this stands the record the claim omits, and the omission is not of marginalia. She wrote almost nothing on the woman question as a separate theoretical subject, leaving the field to Zetkin and to the women's movement inside the party whose journal, Die Gleichheit, Zetkin edited; she treated the demand for women's emancipation as wholly derivative of the class struggle, to be realised through it and never against it or beside it; and she polemicised, when the occasion arose, against the feminism of the bourgeois women's movement with the same procedure she applied to every intermediate banner, asking which class of women a given demand armed and which it disarmed. In the taxonomy of her thought the parallel with the national question is exact and was visible to her contemporaries: as she refused the nation a separate road, she refused sex a separate road, and for the same reason, that the intermediate identity divided the only collective subject she trusted. A feminism that claims her must therefore claim her against her own stated architecture, which does not make the claim absurd (thinkers are often claimed most fruitfully at the points where later ages know more than they did) but does make it a claim of inheritance disputed by the will. The honest formulation is the one the claimants table gives: she is a founder figure for the history of women in the socialist movement and a theorist of almost everything except feminism, and the two facts have to be carried together.

There is a biographical undercurrent here that the record permits to be noticed without being decorated. The hip condition that marked her gait, the stature her caricaturists exaggerated, the foreignness that German anti-Semites and Polish nationalists alike converted into epithets (the hate literature of her lifetime attacked her as woman, Jew, Pole, and cripple in rotating combinations) formed the material environment in which her insistence on being judged by argument alone was forged. She never wrote about this environment as a grievance. She answered it by out-arguing it for twenty five years, and the temperature of her contempt for identity as a political warrant, any identity, including her own, is better explained by that answer than by doctrine alone. The explanation is offered as the biography's inference from the record, flagged as inference; the record itself is not in dispute.

## The antimilitarist cases and the politics of the platform

Between the suffrage campaign and the war stands a sequence of episodes that capsule lives skip and that matter here because they show her politics in its public, bodily form before the prison years enclosed it. From 1913 she campaigned against the coming war with an explicitness that the party leadership, committed to legality, found reckless: at Frankfurt in September 1913 she told a meeting that if those in power expected working men to raise murderous weapons against their brothers, they would cry, we shall not do it, and the state obliged her with a prosecution for inciting soldiers to disobedience. The trial in February 1914 she converted, as the great defendants of that era knew how to do, into an indictment of military brutality, calling witnesses to the barracks cruelties until the prosecution's case drowned in the testimony it had summoned; the one year sentence, deferred and then served from 1915, made her the movement's most famous political prisoner before the war had even begun. A second case, arising from the same campaign season, hung over her when war broke out. The significance is not merely heroic colour. Her antimilitarism was a politics of the platform and the witness box, conducted at maximum personal exposure inside the law of a state she expected to imprison her, and it drew on the mass strike theory's central wager: that the refusal of an order, generalised, is a material force, and that someone must model the refusal before it can generalise. The woman later misremembered as a theorist of other people's spontaneity spent 1913 and 1914 supplying, in her own person, the example she believed movements require.

The platform deserves its own paragraph in an intellectual biography, because for Luxemburg the spoken word was a theoretical instrument and not a vulgarisation of one. Her speeches were constructed like her pamphlets, concession, mechanism, consequence, and delivered with a command of rhythm that hostile reporters recorded even while deploring the content; she could reduce a heckler with an irony that the minutes preserved and could lift a hall of the uncommitted by an appeal to their own experience of the workshop and the rent book. In an age when the movement's intellectuals increasingly wrote for each other, she remained an agitator in the strict sense, a mover of audiences, and she regarded the capacity as a duty of her theory rather than a gift beside it. The mass strike, after all, is a doctrine about what crowds do; its author had spent twenty years learning crowds from the rostrum, in three languages, under two empires and a republic, and the doctrine's texture, its feel for the crowd's intelligence and its contempt for the crowd's manipulators, is unreadable without that apprenticeship.

## The quotation problem: a thinker in extracts

Her posthumous fate has a mechanism, and naming it protects the reader of this biography from repeating it. Luxemburg is, by the accident of her corpus, supremely extractable. She wrote in aphorisms inside arguments; her sentences were built to be carried away, and a century of movements has carried them away, sentence by sentence, from the arguments that gave the sentences their direction. The freedom sentence is the type case: lifted from an institutional argument about soviets and bureaucracy, it circulates as a liberal maxim, available for inscription on the monuments of states and parties she opposed in every particular. The barbarism alternative is the second case: attributed by her to Engels as an act of scholarly honesty, it circulates as her coinage, her honesty itself extracted away. The third case is the mass strike, extracted from its pamphlet as a romance of the crowd by readers who skip the pamphlet's organisational chapters. A series pattern recognised elsewhere in this project applies to her with unique force: the thinker claimed by rival traditions is best served not by adjudicating the claims but by restoring the extracts to the wholes they were cut from, which is the operation this biography has attempted section by section. The operation has a simple test for the reader who wishes to continue it. Whenever a sentence of hers is quoted, ask what it was written against, in what year, from what confinement or platform, and at whose expense. The sentence will survive the questions. The claimant's use of it frequently will not.

### What would overturn the spontaneity reading of her work?

A text in which she opposed party organisation as such, or counselled reliance on unorganised action, would overturn it; none exists in the published corpus, while party programmes, school lectures, and two decades of editorial and organisational work document the opposite commitment across her whole career.

That H3 states the falsification condition for one misreading; the same discipline can be applied to the others, and the exercise is a fair summary of the whole biography's method. The martyr reading would be changed by evidence that the murder was incidental to her significance, and the evidence is in a sense already available, since the texts that organise the claims were nearly all written before 1914 and would sustain the claims, differently weighted, had she died in her bed in 1930. The economist reading would be changed by a demonstration that the schemas settled the environment question too, and no such demonstration has survived the historical chapters of her own book. The spontaneist reading would be changed by the text described in the H3, and its nonexistence, across a corpus that grows with every archival edition, is as close to a settled negative as biography can offer.

## The combination tested: four hard cases

An organising claim earns its place in a biography only if it survives the episodes most likely to break it, and the combination has four such episodes, one for each of its elements under maximum strain. Take first the revolutionary element against the charge, pressed by every moderate heir, that her anti-reformism was a youthful position she outgrew into democratic good sense. The test case is December 1918. A Luxemburg mellowing toward parliamentary gradualism had, in those weeks, the easiest opportunity of her life to act on the mellow doctrine: the old party was shattered, the councils were dissolving themselves into a National Assembly, and a democratic republic of a recognisably social tint was on offer with her prestige at its height. She responded by co-founding a communist party, writing its programme around the council system, and editing a daily paper against the assembly's legitimacy. Whatever else December shows, it shows the anti-reformism of 1899 intact at forty seven, in action, at the cost of her safety. The element holds.

Take next the democratic element against the opposite charge, that her prison criticisms were the tactical regrets of a revolutionary who would have governed as the Bolsheviks governed once power was hers. The test case is the same December, read from the other side. In power's antechamber she did not promise her own side a dictatorship of the enlightened. She lost the internal vote on the assembly elections because she wanted the young party to face the electorate rather than boycott it; she wrote into the Spartacus programme the demand that the councils themselves be built from below by recallable election; she treated the majority of the working class, still voting for her opponents, as a constituency to be convinced rather than a mass to be commanded around. Her manuscript's argument (that the class learns to rule only by ruling, in the open, mistakes included) was not a prison literary exercise. It was the doctrine she practised in the six weeks when she held the largest audience of her life. The element holds, and holds in the direction her democratic claimants want, though they rarely cite December as the proof, preferring the manuscript, because December also proves the first element they omit.

Take third the organised element against the spontaneist legend, under the strain of January 1919. A prophetess of the elemental crowd, handed a crowd in actual insurrection, blesses it and rides it. The documentary Luxemburg did the opposite at every decision point the record preserves: she argued against the attempt inside the councils of her own camp, predicted its defeat on grounds of the provinces' unreadiness, and then disciplined her dissent the moment the decision went against her, because the organisation's solidarity outranked her forecast. The sequence is unintelligible on the legend and completely natural on the record of a party builder of twenty five years' standing. The element holds. Take finally the internationalist element against the strain of her own murder's aftermath, when a Polish state did in fact arise from the imperial collapse she had declared unviable as a national project, and her SDKPiL's heirs had to operate inside it. The sequel is genuinely mixed, and the biography scores it honestly: the Polish state's existence refuted the strongest form of her prediction, while the interwar republic's economic subordination, its dependence on foreign capital and western markets, and its eventual destruction between the two empires she had analysed gave her warnings a longer echo than her opponents expected. A position can be historically defeated and analytically durable at once; the combination's fourth element is the one on which that double verdict falls, and recording the defeat is part of the combination's discipline rather than an exception to it.

### How does the unheld combination explain the four claimants?

Mechanically, by subtraction. Each claimant keeps the elements of her position that its own practice can house and discards the element that indicts it, so social democracy drops the revolution, communism drops the democratic audit, feminism drops the class first refusal, and green politics drops the industrial expectation.

The test section permits one general conclusion about her intellectual character, and it is the conclusion the whole biography has been building toward. Consistency is a cheap virtue in thinkers whose circumstances change slowly; hers changed at maximum speed, across two empires, three parties, a world war, and two revolutions, and the positions did not merely survive the changes rhetorically. They were re-derived, under fire, in the mass strike pamphlet from 1905, in the accumulation book from the teaching years, in the Junius argument from a cell, in the Russian manuscript from a fortress, and in the December programme from the middle of a revolution dissolving under her. A reader who finds the combination wrong on any of its four points is in the best of company, since Lenin, Kautsky, Bauer, and Zetkin each found it wrong on at least one. A reader who finds it incoherent has not yet reckoned with the record of its re-derivation, and the distinction between wrong and incoherent is the distinction between arguing with Luxemburg and extracting her.

## The December programme: what the Spartacus League wanted

Because the founding programme is the least read of her major texts and the most consequential for the communist claim, its content belongs in the biography in some detail. What Does the Spartacus League Want, adopted in substance by the founding congress of the German communist party, demanded the disarming of the police, the military, and the possessing classes and the arming of the working population; the annulment of state debts; the confiscation of great landed estates and the socialisation of large and medium industry, banking, and transport; the vesting of political power in councils of workers and soldiers elected across the Reich, with a central council answerable to a council congress; the abolition of the separate German states in favour of a unitary republic of councils; and international fraternity with the revolutionary workers of Russia and of every country, expressed in the call for a new International. Two features distinguish the document from the caricatures pinned to it. The power it imagines is relentlessly institutional and elective, a pyramid of councils with recall, not a committee's ukase; and its economic demands proceed by decisive industries and banks rather than by the expropriation of the small producer, the peasant included, whose land the programme proposed to socialise only where large estates were concerned, a moderation that reflected her agrarian studies and her Polish schooling in peasant politics. Historians of the German revolution treat the programme as the road not taken, since the council congress itself voted for the National Assembly within days; the biography treats it more narrowly, as the final statement of the combination in programme form, revolutionary in its destination, democratic in its machinery, organisational in its construction, and international in its horizon. All four elements, in one short document, eleven days before the Eden Hotel. The claimants' divisions of her begin, precisely, where that document ends.

## The papers, the executors, and the recovery of the whole corpus

An intellectual biography should say a word about the material survival of its subject's mind, because Luxemburg's afterlife is partly an archival story. She wrote incessantly and kept little; papers were seized in police raids in two countries, manuscripts were carried out of prisons by visitors, and the Nachlass that reached posterity was assembled by loyalty and luck. Paul Frölich, entrusted with her literary estate, edited the collected works in the nineteen twenties until the communist party's internal wars and then Hitler scattered the project; the Russian Revolution manuscript survived because Levi had printed it; the prison letters survived because their recipients kept them through exile and war; the Polish writings, a third of her output by any honest reckoning, remained the least accessible part for decades, locked behind a language barrier that German and English scholarship used as an excuse for a Polish Luxemburg amputated from the portrait. The collected works projects of the later twentieth century, in German and then in English translation, have been repairing that amputation volume by volume, and the repair is the material basis of the modern revival: scholars can now set the Warsaw editorials beside the Berlin pamphlets and watch the combination operate in both. Biographers of the next generation will inherit a fuller Luxemburg than Nettl commanded, and the trajectory of the archive, every recovery so far having thickened rather than thinned the combination, is the empirical ground for this biography's confidence in its organising claim.

Her own country's memory of her deserves its separate paragraph, because it is the strangest of all her receptions and the least exported. In the Poland restored after 1918 she was the PPS's great heretic, the woman who had denied the state's right to exist while its founders were building it, and interwar Polish socialism honoured her genius, when it honoured it, as a German phenomenon. The communist Poland after 1945 faced the embarrassment of a patron whose party had opposed the state's founding premise; it named streets for her, printed her German works, and handled the Polish writings with tongs, while the opposition that eventually gathered under Solidarity showed no more appetite for an internationalist who would have dissolved their national banner in a European class movement. Independent Poland's memory institutions have since staged exhibitions and editions that treat her as a Polish thinker in full, a recovery still in progress and complicated by every party to it. The pattern is the combination's, once again: a nation claiming its daughter must set aside the daughter's verdict on the nation. That Poland increasingly chooses to argue with the verdict rather than to bury it may be the most hopeful episode in the whole reception history, because argument, unlike veneration, is a relationship she would have recognised as hers.

## Her place among the Marxists of her generation

Assessment, when it finally comes, should be comparative, since a thinker's stature is a relation and not a solo property. Her generation of Marxist theorists was unusually rich: Kautsky the systematiser, Lenin the strategist of power, Trotsky the theorist of uneven development, Gramsci, a decade younger, the analyst of hegemony, Lukács the philosopher of reification. Within that company her distinctiveness is not a single doctrine but a distribution of weight no other member shares. She is the only one of them who wrote a major work of economic theory, a major work on the theory of revolution, and a major work on the political conditions of socialism, and who also founded parties, taught cadres, edited dailies, and faced a firing squad's civilian equivalent at the end of the sequence. Lenin's economics is a pamphlet beside hers; Kautsky's practice is a study beside hers; Trotsky's prison audit of a revolution he served does not exist, because when his turn came to audit from inside he audited from exile. The combination, in other words, is not only a set of positions. It is a shape of career, in which theory and organisation and the audit of power remained in one pair of hands, and the shape itself explains why no successor movement, each of which specialises in one of the three activities, has been able to inherit her entire.

The failures stand in the same ledger and the biography has not hidden them. The central proof of her economic masterpiece is defective, on the consensus of those who have worked its models. Her judgement that national movements were a spent force was the largest predictive error made by any major Marxist of her generation, and it was not a slip but a theorem of her system. Her tactical reading of December 1918, prudent as it was, left her party's impatient majority to a rising she could neither lead nor prevent, and her authority, real as it was, proved insufficient to the organisation she had founded a fortnight before, a limit on the organiser element that the record forbids us to varnish. Her assessment of the Bolsheviks' emergency, finally, however prescient its institutional logic, was written in ignorance of the civil war's full ferocity and from a Germany whose revolution she expected to relieve Russia's isolation within months; when that expectation failed, the manuscript's prescriptions lost the condition on which several of them silently depended. A biography that has steelmanned her claimants and her critics throughout owes the reader this paragraph unsoftened. The combination survived its author's errors of prediction and proof. It did so because its four elements are disciplines rather than forecasts: test the slogan against the structure; refuse the intermediate banner; audit your own side's power while it is still yours to audit; and hold the organisation answerable to the movement that made it. Disciplines can be inherited by people who reject every forecast their author made, which is the precise mechanism by which she continues to be read by movements that agree with her about nothing else.

## The International: Stuttgart, Basel, and the collapse she predicted

Her internationalism was institutional before it was rhetorical, and the institutions were the congresses of the Second International, where she sat as a Polish delegate among the movement's notables and learned its fault lines at first hand. At Stuttgart in 1907 the congress debated the militarism resolution, and the episode that historians single out captures her working method inside an institution. The draft resolution's language on war was, in the French and German versions, evasive at the decisive point; she, with Lenin and the Russian delegate Martov, carried an amendment obliging the parties, should war break out nonetheless, to exploit the crisis to hasten the downfall of capitalist rule. The amendment passed and entered the resolution, and its wording later served the Bolsheviks as their charter and served her, in 1914, as the standard by which she measured the German leadership's treason in the Junius argument. The Basel congress of 1912, convened in emergency session as the Balkan wars threatened to generalise, adopted a manifesto of ringing antiwar pledges to which she contributed the drafting energy of the left. She did not mistake the pledges for guarantees. Her prewar writings return obsessively to the gap between the International's resolutions and its parties' national integrations, the same gap she had diagnosed inside the SPD between doctrine and practice, and she treated Basel not as a triumph but as a debt the movement would be called on to pay. August 1914 presented the bill. The International's failure, when it came, was therefore not a surprise that shattered her framework; it was the framework's prediction, and the Spartacus opposition she built from that month was an attempt to found the practice the resolutions had described.

From prison she followed the wartime attempts to reassemble an international opposition, at Zimmerwald in 1915 and Kienthal in 1916, conferences she could not attend and whose centrist majorities she judged, in smuggled comments and later writings, too tender toward the social patriots who had voted the credits. Her standard for a rebuilt International was the combination's again: revolutionary clarity about the war's imperialist character, organisational rupture with the parties that had capitulated, and no reunion on the basis of amnesia. The founding of the Communist International in March 1919, weeks after her death, proceeded by other hands and under the shadow of the Russian party's prestige; Paul Levi, her closest political heir in the German party, carried into its early congresses the argument she would have made, for a western movement built by the conquest of majorities rather than by the export of insurrection, and was expelled for making it in public after the March Action of 1921. The counterfactual (what she would have said at those congresses, whether the eagle's audit would have been heeded by the state that published her manuscript as a deviation) is one of the durable temptations of her biography and should be registered as a temptation rather than indulged as an argument. The record ends at the Landwehr Canal. What the record supports is narrower and sufficient: the International she wanted is describable from her texts, and no International that has existed since has matched the description, which is one more way of stating the unheld combination at the scale of institutions.

## The agrarian question and the peasantry in her economics

Political economy in her hands never confined itself to the factory, and the peasantry, the largest class of the world she was born into, runs through her work from the dissertation to the prison studies like a bass line under the melody. The dissertation had already established her characteristic finding: Polish peasant agriculture was being dissolved and preserved simultaneously by the imperial market, drawn into money relations by taxation and conscription while held in communal and smallholding forms that industry found convenient as a reservoir of cheap labour. The accumulation book generalised the finding into a law of the system's metabolism, since the non-capitalist strata whose demand realises surplus value are, in their overwhelming mass, peasants, and the methods by which capital prises them open (the tax in money, the mortgage, the land grab, the destruction of the commune's collective insurance) constitute for her the real history of so called primitive accumulation continued into the present tense. Her wartime studies of the Russian commune and of the ancient Slav economies, pursued in fortress libraries, were the scholarly deepening of that bass line, an attempt to understand from inside the forms capital was devouring. And the prison manuscript's criticism of the Bolshevik land settlement, the distribution of the great estates into small proprietorships, followed from the same formation: she read the decree not as a peasant policy to be graded for its popularity but as the deliberate multiplication of the very stratum her economics identified as capitalism's necessary outside and socialism's most stubborn internal other, and she predicted that a socialist economy would spend generations contending with the smallholder ocean the decree had created. Readers who know the later history of Soviet collectivisation will find the prediction uncomfortable reading in any political direction. It is included here because it demonstrates, better than any summary could, that her prison audit of the Bolsheviks was not a liberal's reflex. It was her economics, her agrarian scholarship, and her Polish schooling applied to decrees, by the one European socialist who had been studying peasant communes since before some of the decrees' authors left school.

## Three opponents: Bernstein, Kautsky, Lenin

A thinker is partly the sum of the opponents she chose, and Luxemburg's three defining polemical relationships map the combination's coordinates with unusual precision, because each opponent held one of her elements in an exaggerated form she had to refuse. Bernstein held democracy and the daily movement, and amputated the revolution; against him she learned to argue that a means severed from its end becomes a different means, and the lesson organised her first book. Kautsky held the doctrine and the organisation, and amputated the act, administering Marxism as a science of patience while the class's own movement knocked at the door in 1905 and again in 1910; against him she learned that orthodoxy can be a method of postponement, and the lesson organised her breach with the centre and her theory of the mass strike as the movement's self education. Lenin held the revolution and the organisation, and, in her reading of 1904 and 1918, risked amputating the democracy that makes the other two socialist; against him she learned to state the audit from inside the camp, at the price of being quoted forever by the camp's enemies, a price she paid knowingly, since the manuscript was written for publication in a Germany she expected to influence rather than as a private diary. Three opponents, three amputations refused. It is not too much to say that the unheld combination is the negative space these three polemics carve out between them, and a reader who masters the three exchanges possesses a working map of her whole position, which is why the dedicated treatments of the exchanges, several of them linked from this biography, repay the detour.

The personal textures beneath the polemics belong to biography as well, because she conducted all three relationships at a level of intellectual honour that her century's party warfare rarely sustained. Bernstein she answered and went on answering, without personal rancour, into the war years when his half hearted opposition to the credits brought them into tactical proximity she never confused with reconciliation. Kautsky she had loved as a friend and mentor in the literal household sense, and the 1910 break, conducted in print over the suffrage campaign, carried a private grief her letters do not hide; she nonetheless treated his wartime centrism in the Junius argument with a contempt she spared the open revisionists, on the principle she stated elsewhere that the evasive middle does more damage than the declared opponent. Lenin she never met socially after the emigration years and never sentimentalised; the relationship remained one of congresses, resolutions, and texts, and its warmth, such as the posthumous eagle note reveals, was the warmth of professional recognition between two people who had spent twenty years testing each other's arguments and finding them, on the largest questions, load bearing. Party history written as melodrama cannot accommodate such relationships, which is one more reason the melodramatic Luxemburg of the claimant literatures keeps dissolving, on inspection, into this more interesting figure: a controversialist who believed her opponents were worth the strongest version of their case.

## The strike wave of the war years and the January days

Her theory of the mass strike received, in the last two years of the war, the largest field test she was permitted to witness, and her prison correspondence and the Spartacus letters show her reading the test with a professional's attention. From 1916 the German war economy generated the stoppages her 1906 pamphlet had modelled: food protests that became strikes, strikes that acquired political demands under repression, the great April 1917 wave in Berlin and Leipzig touched off by a cut in the bread ration, and the January 1918 strike movement that spread from Austria across the German industrial cities demanding peace without annexations and bringing hundreds of thousands out under wartime law. She analysed the January movement from Breslau as the German class reconstituting itself after the August collapse, noted its council forms with the satisfaction of a theorist watching her categories walk, and warned her comrades outside against both of the errors her combination existed to refuse: the error of the officials, who feared the movement as disorder, and the error of the impatient, who would spend it in a premature bid for power before the provinces and the soldiers had moved. The warning describes, almost clause for clause, her conduct eleven months later in the greater January crisis of the republic, which is the strongest available evidence that her final fortnight was not an improvisation or a death wish but the application, under terminal conditions, of the doctrine she had been refining since 1905. The doctrine's last application failed to save her or the rising. Doctrines are not bodyguards, and a biography that implied otherwise would be selling the martyrology this one exists to retire.

## Imperialism among its rival theories

Placing her masterpiece among the other Marxist theories of imperialism written in the same years clarifies both its originality and its isolation, and the placement is a standard examination demand that capsule treatments fumble by treating the theories as interchangeable. Rudolf Hilferding's Finance Capital, published in 1910, explained the new stage through the fusion of bank and industrial capital and the export of capital, an analysis of the system's internal reorganisation that required no outside in her sense. Karl Kautsky projected an ultra-imperialism in which the great capitals might cartelize the planet and divide it peacefully, a speculation she regarded as the economics of the party centre's politics, the wish for a capitalism that had outgrown its violence. Lenin's pamphlet of 1916, written in Zurich with her book on his desk among the others, built on Hilferding and on the liberal Hobson to define imperialism by monopoly, finance capital, and capital export, and treated the partition of the world as completed and due for redivision by war; his mechanism was rivalry within the system, hers was the system's metabolism with its outside. The differences are not pedantic. On her account imperialism ends when the outside is consumed, a terminus internal to the theory; on his, it ends when the weakest link of the chain of states breaks under revolution, a terminus internal to politics. Bukharin's world economy study, written with Lenin's blessing, mediated between them and criticised her realisation argument by name. A candidate who can state these four positions and their mechanisms possesses the comparative grid the examiners reward; a reader who can state them possesses something better, the knowledge of what was at stake when she insisted, against the whole field, that the outside was not a detail of the system's geography but the secret of its motion. The word insisted is chosen carefully. She knew the counterarguments, answered them in the Anti-Critique, and died unconverted, and the biography records the insistence as a fact about her intellectual character as much as about her economics: where she believed a proof had been sighted, she did not trade it for collegial peace, a trait that cost her the party school's tranquillity, the centre's friendship, and, in the accounting of some historians, a quieter life.

## The milieu: Zetkin, Mehring, Liebknecht, Levi

No thinker of her kind works alone, and the circle that sustained her deserves the biographer's attention because posterity, isolating her as a solitary heroine, repeats the extraction error in social form. Clara Zetkin, a decade older, editor of the women's paper and organiser of the socialist women's movement internationally, was her closest political friendship, an alliance of two formidable intelligences that survived disagreements over the women's work itself and gave each woman, in a movement of men, one witness who required no explanation. Franz Mehring, the party's historian and its conscience in matters of scholarship, was her ally on the left and her model for the union of erudition with journalism; his death in late January 1919, days after hers, closed the old left's era in a single month. Karl Liebknecht, whose name the centuries have fused with hers into a single commemorative unit, was in life the alliance's third term, less a theorist than a tribune, the lawyer of soldiers and the Reichstag's lone voice of December 1914, whose courage she relied on and whose impetuosity, in the January days, she could not govern; the fusion of their names in death is historically intelligible and biographically misleading, since their gifts and their judgements were distinct and occasionally opposed. Paul Levi, the lawyer who had defended her in the Frankfurt trial and who led the young communist party after the double murder, was her political heir in the strict sense that he had learned the combination at her side and attempted, in 1919 and 1920, to govern a mass communist party by it: legality and illegality combined, majorities conquered rather than minorities hurled, the Russian alliance held without the Russian tutelage. His expulsion in 1921 and his long afterlife as a social democratic deputy mark the point at which the combination lost its last institutional carrier in Germany. The milieu, taken whole, explains a feature of her work that solitary genius cannot: its institutional intelligence. She wrote as she did because she had spent twenty years inside rooms where Zetkins and Mehrings read the drafts, and a biography restores those rooms to the portrait in the same spirit in which it restores the party school and the editorial desk.

## What she read: the formation behind the formation

An intellectual biography is entitled to one speculative luxury, provided it is flagged as such: reconstructing the library inside the arguments. Hers is unusually well documented, because the letters name her reading with a reader's specificity, and the reconstruction explains capacities that the political record alone leaves mysterious. She read across four literatures as a native: Polish, with Mickiewicz and the romantic tradition her nationalist opponents claimed and she refused to surrender to them; German, with Goethe as a lifelong standard of what prose and a life could be; Russian, with Tolstoy, on whom she wrote a searching essay treating him as a social thinker trapped inside a prophet's temperament, and the novelists whose Russia she knew better than her German comrades knew their own provinces; and French, the language of her earliest political economy. The natural sciences of her Zurich beginnings never left the reading: geology, botany, and ornithology recur in the prison letters not as decoration but as disciplines of observation, and her famous attentiveness to the particular (the bird identified by call, the communal form identified by its tenure rules) is a scientist's habit carried into politics. When her opponents in the movement reached for the word doctrinaire, they were reaching for the wrong shelf. The mind that produced the combination was encyclopaedic by the standards of its own party, and its characteristic failures, the overextended theorem, the prediction held past its evidence, are the failures of a system builder, not of a pamphleteer. Systems builders are out of fashion in an age of extracts. The recovery of her system, in the collected works and in studies that read her whole, is the scholarly event of her afterlife, and it is still in progress.

### Why is reading her whole unusually consequential?

Because her corpus is organised as a system in which each famous text presupposes the others: the pamphlet on reform assumes the dissertation's economics, the mass strike assumes the party she built, the prison audit assumes the revolution she defended. Extraction breaks the presuppositions and manufactures the partial Luxemburgs her claimants circulate.

## Reading her whole: an order for the works

The biography closes its argumentative part with the practical question every reader of an intellectual life eventually asks: in what order should the works be read so that the combination assembles itself instead of arriving as disconnected classics? Begin with Social Reform or Revolution, because it is short, complete, and displays the method in its purest form, the concession of the opponent's facts followed by the contest over their meaning. Proceed to The Mass Strike, read beside a narrative of the 1905 revolution, and notice how much of the pamphlet is reportage raised to theory. Take the dissertation's argument next, in summary if the German or Polish defeats you, because the accumulation book will otherwise appear to begin in midair. The Accumulation of Capital itself should be read with its critics open alongside, Bauer's counter-scheme for the technical objection and Bukharin's critique for the political one, since the book's meaning includes the quarrel it started. The Junius Pamphlet follows as the war's testament, and the letters from prison should be read in the same season, not as relief from the theory but as its author's register of the world the theory was about. The Russian Revolution manuscript comes last among the major works, read entire and in context, with the December programme and the final editorials as its postscript. A reader who follows that order will have performed the biography's thesis in the first person: the positions will have accumulated into the combination, and the claimants' partial editions will thereafter read as what they are, selections from a whole that resists them. Students preparing for assessment will find the examination applications gathered in the [luxemburg trotsky exam answers](/2014/12/15/luxemburg-trotsky-exam-answers/), and the note making method that turns this reading order into a durable study set is the business of the persona sections below.

## The colonial question at the congresses

One arena in which her economics and her politics met in public, before the accumulation book gave the meeting its theory, was the colonial debate inside the International. At Stuttgart in 1907 the Dutch delegate Henri van Kol, supported by Bernstein and by David in the German party, proposed that the congress decline to condemn colonial policy in principle, arguing that colonies under socialist administration might be developed for the benefit of their peoples and of civilisation. The proposal forced the question her dissertation had already posed in Polish form: whether there exists a progressive version of the penetration of non-capitalist peoples by capital, administered with better intentions. She fought the draft in commission and on the floor as a leading voice of the majority that defeated it, holding that colonial policy in the epoch of capital was inseparable from conquest, forced labour, and the destruction of communal forms, and that a socialist movement which accepted the premise in the colonies would find the premise waiting for it at home, since the methods of rule practised abroad had a documented habit of travelling. The congress resolution condemned colonial policy in principle, and the vote stands as one of the International's few unambiguous anti-imperialist acts. For the biography the episode matters on three counts. It dates her anti-imperialism as practice before it was theory, rooted in the colonial wars of the new century, the Herero war in German South West Africa among them, whose conduct the German party's left had campaigned against in the Reichstag elections of 1907, the so called Hottentot election fought by the government on a colonial scare. It shows her, once again, refusing the intermediate banner, in this case the banner of a humane colonialism, on the structural ground she never abandoned: that the institution, not the administrator, generates the conduct. And it supplies the green and anti-imperialist claimants with their strongest documentary material, which the claimants table has already credited to them, while noting the productivism that sits beside it in her expectations for a postcapitalist economy, a tension the honest claimant carries rather than resolves.

Her Reichstag era colleagues remembered the colonial fights as among her finest parliamentary adjacent performances, although she never sat in the chamber herself, lacking both the citizenship of birth and, as a woman, the franchise and eligibility that the empire denied her sex entirely. The fact deserves the emphasis this sentence gives it. The most formidable controversialist of German social democracy conducted her entire German career without a vote, in a state that would not have permitted her to cast one, addressing electors who possessed what she was denied and legislatures she could enter only as a defendant. Her 1912 suffrage speech, arguing that working women required the ballot as a weapon of their class, was written by a woman to whom the ballot was refused on two grounds at once, and the speech's controlled anger at the arrangement has sometimes been mistaken for its subject. It is the biographical ground note beneath her democratic insistence, and it rings through the prison manuscript's passion for general elections: she had spent a lifetime outside the franchise, and she knew in her person what its absence makes of public life.

## The councils: the institution she championed and what became of it

The council, or soviet, form occupies in her final politics the place the mass strike occupied in her theory of 1906: it is the form the class generates when it moves, and her December programme's pyramid of elected, recallable councils was the institutional translation of everything she had argued about spontaneity and organisation for twenty years. The form's genealogy in her thought is traceable and worth tracing, because claimants of the council tradition have sometimes presented the councils as her discovery of 1918, an improvisation of the revolution's last weeks. In fact she had watched the soviets of 1905 at close range and written them into the mass strike analysis as the revolution's own organ, the body through which a striking class becomes a governing one; she had followed their reappearance in Russia in 1917 as the dual power that made October possible; and she arrived in Berlin in November 1918 with the council idea fully formed, to find German councils of workers and soldiers already sitting in every garrison and factory city, the revolution's spontaneous constitution. Her politics in December was the attempt to give that constitution a future against the two forces dissolving it: the government, which preferred the National Assembly, and the councils' own majority, which in the December congress of councils voted, by a large margin, for the assembly and early elections, choosing the parliamentary road with open eyes. She accepted the terrain and fought on it, arguing in the press and at her party's congress for the councils' supremacy while conceding the elections battle she lost inside her own organisation. The sequence is the combination in institutional form one final time: she would not impose the council republic by a minority's coup, because her democracy forbade it, and she would not bless the assembly as the revolution's fulfilment, because her revolution forbade that. Between the two forbiddances there was, in the Germany of that winter, no road that led anywhere but the one she walked, and the biography leaves the judgement of her December there, where the evidence leaves it: the position was coherent, the forces were insufficient, and coherence has never been a substitute for forces.

## The National Question and Autonomy: the essays behind the stance

The stance on self-determination that embarrasses all her claimants rests on a body of writing most summaries never open, the long series of essays on the national question and autonomy published in the Polish theoretical press in 1908 and 1909, and the biography owes those essays a section, since a position's weight is the weight of its argument and not of its slogan. The essays work through the concept of the nation with a patience her opponents rarely matched. A nation, she argued, is not a natural unit with rights inhering in it like a person's; it is a historical formation of classes whose interests diverge, and the phrase national interest performs the trick of smuggling the possessing class's programme into the whole people's mouth. The right of nations to self-determination, examined as a formula, dissolves under that analysis into a metaphysical abstraction, because the subject of the right cannot be specified without specifying a class, and once the class is specified the formula's work is done by the class demand underneath it. What can be demanded concretely, and what she demanded throughout, was autonomy: the widest regional and cultural self government within a democratic state, Polish language schooling, Polish courts and administration, the cultural life of the nation freed from imperial tutelage, all of it as democratic content inside a common revolutionary struggle rather than as a state frontier drawn through mixed populations. The distinction between autonomy and independence is the hinge of the whole essay series and the single most commonly dropped distinction in the secondary literature. She was not an enemy of Polish national existence. She was an enemy of the conversion of that existence into a programme of statehood, and she spent two years of essays, in the middle of her German career's busiest decade, saying so in Polish to the audience the distinction concerned.

The essays also contain her most systematic treatment of a thinker her tradition usually handles as a precursor to be corrected in passing: she audited Marx and Engels on the national question directly, crediting their Polish enthusiasms to the democratic revolutions of 1848, when the restoration of Poland figured as a blow against the Tsarist gendarme of Europe, and arguing that the capitalism of 1908 had dissolved the conditions that made the older judgement rational. Historical materialism applied to historical materialism's founders, the master's conclusions revised by the master's method, is the signature operation of her maturity, and it is the same operation she would perform on Lenin's party in 1918 and on Marx's schemas in 1913. Noticing the recurrence matters for the biography's thesis, because it shows the combination's first element at work inside her scholarship: reverence for the tradition expressed as refusal to let any sentence of it stand untested against the structure it claims to describe. The national essays, long untranslated and still the least accessible of her major works in English, are the missing weight in most accounts of the stance, and their gradual appearance in scholarly editions is changing the stance's reputation from dogma to argument, which is where this biography has insisted it belongs.

## The mass strike debates inside the German party before the war

Between the pamphlet of 1906 and the war, the mass strike was the German movement's recurring internal battlefield, and her conduct of that campaign fills the last unexamined stretch of her German career. The trade union leadership, burned by the cost of the strikes of 1905 and 1906 and committed to the accumulation of funds and members, secured at the Mannheim congress of 1906 an effective union veto over any political mass strike, an arrangement the party left never forgave. She answered in print and on platforms for eight years: in the suffrage struggles of 1908 to 1910, when the Prussian three class franchise became the symbol of the empire's political backwardness and mass demonstrations filled Berlin's streets in defiance of police bans; in the theoretical duel with Kautsky in 1910, where her demand to raise the mass strike slogan openly was answered by his doctrine of attrition, the strategy of wearing the enemy down by legality until the final struggle could be risked, a strategy she diagnosed as the conversion of postponement into a principle; and at the Jena congress of 1913, where the party, facing the war danger and the armaments taxes, at last adopted a resolution contemplating the mass strike as a weapon of defence, with her and Liebknecht pressing from the left and the leadership accepting the words while reserving the deed. Read in sequence, the campaign shows her characteristic patience inside institutions she believed were failing: she did not split the party over Mannheim, did not found a faction with its own apparatus, argued the same proposition at congress after congress for eight years while the international situation darkened, and kept the argument at the level of strategy rather than schism. The campaign failed in its object, since the party that had resolved at Jena to contemplate the mass strike against war voted the war credits eleven months later without one. It succeeded in its byproduct, which was the formation of the cadre (the Spartacus nucleus of editors, deputies' staff, union dissidents, and youth organisers) who had learned politics in her school of the mass strike years and who constituted the only organised antiwar force the empire contained when the test arrived. Biography should record both the failure and the byproduct, because her kind of politics habitually produced the second while aiming at the first, and her judges have generally counted only the aim.

## The nine weeks of freedom: the journalism of the last winter

Between the prison gate and the Eden Hotel she lived nine weeks, and the journalism of those weeks, written at the pace of a daily paper in a collapsing capital, is the final corpus an intellectual biography must weigh, because it is the only body of her writing produced in the presence of the power her theory had always anticipated. The editorials of Die Rote Fahne are occasional pieces and should not be inflated into treatises, but their consistency with the twenty years behind them is remarkable under the conditions: no rhetorical descent into demonology of persons, no inflation of the young party's strength, a refusal, sustained against the temper of her own camp, to confuse the government's wickedness with the revolution's readiness. She audited the Ebert government's arrangement with the industrialists, the Stinnes Legien agreement that traded union recognition for the containment of socialisation, as an economist reads a contract, clause by clause, identifying exactly which of the revolution's demands the signatures had buried. She audited the council congress's vote for the National Assembly as a political educator reads a defeat, without contempt for the delegates who had voted their inexperience. She kept the paper's columns open to the argument she was losing inside her own movement. And on the last day but one she wrote the editorial on the restored order in Berlin whose closing words have been quoted at the head of this biography's account of the murder. The nine weeks matter beyond their pathos because they are the combination's final field test, conducted in freedom, at speed, with everything at stake: the revolutionary who would not trim the destination, the democrat who would not seize what the majority had not granted, the organiser who built a party while its founding vote went against her, the internationalist who read Berlin's winter as one front of a European reckoning. Examiners of her life sometimes ask what she would have become had she lived. The nine weeks are the only evidence admissible in that court, and they point one way: she would have become what she already was, at greater length.

## How she is studied and examined

Her place in the curricula of the subjects this series serves is distinctive enough to deserve a closing map for the reader who meets her first as a syllabus entry. In political theory courses she appears under democratic Marxism, paired against Lenin on organisation and against Kautsky on reform, which is to say she is examined as the middle term her combination defines, and the strongest scripts are those that resist the pairing's suggestion that the middle is a compromise rather than a position with its own proofs. In political economy she appears under imperialism and crisis, where her accumulation argument is set, as this biography has set it, between the reproduction schemas it attacked and the theories of Hilferding and Lenin it provoked, and where the assessment rewards the double verdict, the failed demonstration and the durable question, over either simple dismissal or simple defence. In modern European history she appears under the German revolution, where the documentary discipline about the January rising and the murder (what she decided, what was decided around her, what remains contested about the Eden Hotel) is the difference between the examined answer and the commemorative one. In women's history and in the history of political thought she appears under recovery, the case study of a corpus restored by archival labour from the extracts its transmitters preferred. The common thread across the four syllabuses is the skill this biography has practised throughout: the separation of the life, the texts, and the reception into three accounts that are allowed to inform one another only after each has been rendered accurately. That separation is not pedantry. It is the only known antidote to the claimant mechanism, and a student who has learned it on Luxemburg can apply it to every contested thinker the syllabus holds in reserve.

## Watching a revolution from a fortress: her responses as events unfolded

The prison manuscript of 1918 reads differently once its prehistory is restored, because she had been following the Russian revolution from her cells since its first week with an attention her censors could obstruct but not prevent. The February revolution that destroyed the Tsardom reached Breslau as rumour and then as newspapers, and her letters from those months register an excitement she did not trouble to disguise: the empire of her birth, the prison state of her youth, had been overthrown by the class in whose name she had fled it at eighteen, and the mass strike theory of 1906 had become, in Petrograd, a government forming fact. Through the Spartacus letters smuggled out and printed illegally, she argued the German movement's duty toward the Russian example with the combination already assembled: the Russian workers had shown that war could be answered by revolution, the German party's officials who dismissed the example as eastern chaos were repeating August 1914 in miniature, and the soviets deserved study as the form, not admiration as a spectacle. October she greeted, when the news reached her in full, as the revolution's necessary deepening, the Bolsheviks' seizure being in her judgement the act that saved the revolution from dissolving into a constituent talking shop while the old order rearmed; the defence she wrote of October against Kautsky's assault, in the fragments published with the manuscript, is as unqualified as anything in her corpus. The audit that accompanied the defence was therefore the work of the revolution's partisan, and its specific targets (the assembly, the franchise, the land, the nationalities, the terror) were chosen by a reader who believed each decree would be studied in Germany within the year by revolutionaries preparing their own. She was writing, in the most literal sense, for the next revolution's leadership, which happened to be herself and her comrades, and the manuscript's tone of fraternal severity is the tone of a founder addressing founders. Posterity's habit of reading it as a liberal protest against revolution inverts the document's whole pragmatics, and the inversion is the single most consequential misreading in her reception, since it supplies the social democratic claim with its founding text at the price of the text's meaning. Restoration of the pragmatics is this biography's settled position, argued from the manuscript's occasion, its defence of October, and its author's December conduct, and readers who dissent are invited to the documents rather than to the extracts.

## The question she declined: Jewishness, antisemitism, and the universalist refusal

One further element of her formation requires the biographer's care, both because the hate literature of her lifetime made it central and because her own handling of it was among the most absolute of her refusals. She was born into a Jewish family in a province where Jewishness was a legal and social condition before it was anything else; the Warsaw of her schooling rationed Jewish futures by quota and custom; the German and Polish movements she served contained antisemites as well as philosemites, and the right wing press of the empire attacked her habitually in the vocabulary of race, the Jewish woman from Poland instructing Germans in their politics being a figure the nationalist imagination could not leave alone. Her response, consistent from the Polish years to the end, was the refusal the combination predicts: the intermediate banner declined in its ethnic form as in its national and feminist forms. In a wartime letter to Mathilde Wurm, who had written to her in distress about the sufferings of the Jews under the war's eastern campaigns, she answered with a famous severity, asking what her correspondent wanted with the special pains of the Jews when the victims of empire from Armenia to the Congo cried out in the same register, and declaring the whole suffering world her concern in a passage that readers have alternately honoured as universalism's purest statement and indicted as a blindness to the specificity of antisemitic violence. The biography records both readings and adjudicates neither, because the passage is the combination applied to identity itself, with all the strength and all the cost the combination carried everywhere else: the strength of a solidarity that recognised no frontier of blood, the cost of a framework that had no separate account for a persecution aimed at a people as a people. Her Polish party's record on the ground was better than the letter's abstraction might suggest; the SDKPiL fought the pogroms and the nationalist antisemitism of the Polish right as matters of course, and she had written against the Kishinev pogrom's logic in the Russian movement's debates as a young exile. But the theoretical refusal stands as she left it, the fourth wall of the combination, and claimants who build on her from the standpoint of any identity, including the one she was born into, build against the grain she cut deepest.

## Stone and procession: the memorial culture and its ironies

Her grave became a political instrument within days, and the instruments' history condenses the reception into objects a biography can point at. The funeral of 13 June 1919, when her recovered body was carried to Friedrichsfelde through a Berlin held by the troops of the government her murderers served, drew the revolutionary working class of the capital in a procession the police reports counted in the hundreds of thousands; the cemetery became the German left's pantheon, and in 1926 the communist party raised there the memorial designed by Ludwig Mies van der Rohe, a brick monument of constructivist severity bearing the names of the murdered of January 1919, among the most famous works of political architecture of the century. The Nazis demolished it in 1935 and levelled the graves, an act of counter-memory that confirms what the memorial had achieved. After 1949 the eastern state rebuilt a cult at the site whose official choreography the dissidents of January 1988 interrupted with her own sentence, and since reunification the annual January demonstration to Friedrichsfelde has persisted as a ritual of the German radical left, smaller, argumentative, and increasingly historical in its own self understanding. A biography organised against martyrology must handle this material with precision. The memorial culture is not false consciousness about her; grief for a murdered teacher and comrade is among the most intelligible of political emotions, and the procession of June 1919 was a fact of the revolution's history, not a distortion of it. The distortion begins where the procession replaces the library, where the annual wreath discharges the obligation the books impose, and where the name on the brick monument is invoked by organisations whose practices her combination exists to audit. The monument's architect, who built for the movement she founded and then for the century's other masters, left a structure that has outlasted every organisation that claimed it. The books are doing the same, more slowly, and the biography's wager is that the books are the monument that matters, because they alone contain the combination entire.

## A figure restored from her extracts

The series thread that this biography was commissioned to carry can now be stated in its finished form. A figure restored from her extracts: that is the work, and the restoration has a determinate content. It returns the freedom sentence to the institutional argument about soviets and bureaucracy from which the extractors cut it. It returns the barbarism alternative to Engels, to whom she returned it herself. It returns the mass strike to the party school, the editorial desk, and the eight year campaign inside a mass organisation. It returns the accumulation theory to the dissertation on Polish industry and to the reproduction schemas, flawed proof and durable question together. It returns the national stance to the two years of Polish essays that argued it, and the prison audit to the revolution it defended. It returns the murder to the plain record of a Freikorps headquarters, a canal, and a lenient court, and it returns the afterlife to its own separate account, where the claimants can be studied as evidence about their centuries rather than mistaken for evidence about her. What stands when the restoration is complete is not a heroine and not a cautionary tale. It is a thinker of the first rank whose positions form the unheld combination, whose errors are as instructive as her insights because both were systematic, and whose corpus, read whole, remains the most demanding training ground her tradition offers for the skill it most needs in every generation: the skill of holding a revolutionary destination, a democratic method, an organisational patience, and an international horizon in one mind at one time, without surrendering any of the four to the convenience of the moment. She managed it for twenty five years, in two languages of politics and four of literature, under two empires, in classrooms, congresses, fortresses, and one revolutionary winter. The claimants divide the estate because the estate, entire, is too large for any of them. The reader of this biography is not asked to become the combination's fifth claimant. The reader is asked to become its witness, which is the only relation to her that the record, read whole, finally permits.

## Peace utopias and the economics of disarmament

Before the war made the question practical, she had settled her account with the peace politics of her movement in a pair of essays from 1911 that the green and antimilitarist claimants are entitled to cite and rarely do, preferring the Junius pathos to the colder analysis that preceded it. The essays addressed the proposals then circulating for international arbitration, for staged disarmament, and for a United States of Europe as the institutional shell of a pacified continent, and her treatment applied the dissertation's method without mercy. Disarmament, she argued, is not a policy that capitalist states can adopt by agreement, because militarism is not an excrescence on the system but one of its accumulation fields and its instrument of competition for the non-capitalist world; a conference of such states can regulate armaments at the margin, as it regulates tariffs, but it cannot abolish the function the armaments perform, and a socialist movement that campaigns for disarmament as a reform teaches its followers to expect from diplomacy what only a transformation of the order can deliver. The European federation slogan received the same audit: a customs and military union of the continent's capitalist states would be directed, in the conditions of 1911, against the workers of Europe and the peoples of the colonies, a cartel of the armed rather than a peace of the peoples. The essays are sometimes summarised as her refusal of peace politics, which inverts them. Their whole construction distinguishes between the peace the movement should want, which she never qualified, and the mechanisms by which peace was being promised, which she tested against the material structure and found to be advertisements. When the war arrived, the essays' framework told her exactly what had happened and why no resolution at Basel could have prevented it: the states had armed because arming was their function in the competition her economics described, and the parties that had promised to stop them had administered the promise as liturgy. The Junius Pamphlet's ferocity toward her own side is unintelligible without the 1911 essays behind it, and the combination's antimilitarist element, which the claimants table records, rests on this analysis rather than on any horror of violence in the abstract, a horror her revolutionary commitments in any case forbade her to profess. She opposed the war as a theorist of what the war was for. That is a harder and more durable ground than revulsion, and it is hers.

## The state she never systematised and everywhere implied

She left no treatise on the state, and the absence is itself a datum, since every other major figure of her generation felt obliged to produce one or to be measured against Marx's unfinished remarks. Yet a theory of the state is everywhere implied in her work, recoverable from the places where her arguments touch power, and reconstructing it completes the biography's map of her system. Its first proposition comes from the colonial debates and the accumulation book together: the state is the indispensable instrument by which capital opens and holds its non-capitalist outside, the tax collector, concession granter, and punitive expeditionary force of the realisation process, so that state violence is not an atavism within her economics but a function of it. Its second proposition comes from the mass strike writings: the state's coercive apparatus is formidable against a population in its everyday dispersion and strikingly brittle before a class in general movement, since soldiers and officials are recruited from the same society the strike politicises, a brittleness she watched confirmed in Petrograd and half confirmed in Berlin. Its third proposition comes from the prison manuscript and is the one her democratic claimants prize, rightly, though they usually detach it from the other two: a workers' state that suppresses the political life of its own class destroys the only medium in which its administrators can be corrected, and therefore decays into bureaucracy not by betrayal but by structure. Its fourth proposition comes from December: the old state is not to be taken over but replaced in its functions by the council pyramid, while the new order's legitimacy is measured by the breadth of the suffrage and the freedom of the press it sustains. Assembled, the four propositions describe a state theory as coherent as any her generation produced, hostile to the existing state as capital's instrument, contemptuous of the coup that mistakes the ministry for the society, and severe toward the revolutionary state that silences its own constituency. That she never assembled it herself is a loss the reconstruction only partly repairs. It also explains, one final time, the combination's afterlife: a thinker whose state theory must be assembled from a colonial debate, a strike pamphlet, a prison audit, and a programme leaves each claimant an honest fragment and no claimant the whole, and the fragments have been circulating as wholes ever since.

Her silence on the state had a biographical root worth recording beside the reconstruction. She was a citizen of nowhere in the ordinary sense: born under an empire that exiled her, naturalised in another by a marriage of legal convenience, voteless in the state whose politics she dominated, imprisoned by that state for a third of her last five years, and murdered under the authority of its successor government. Theorists who have held office, or expected to, write about the state as an instrument awaiting hands. She knew it, across a lifetime, as the power that stamps the passport, opens the file, locks the fortress, and signs the Freikorps order, and her implicit theory's insistence that the state be judged by what it does to the political life beneath it may be the most autobiographical proposition in her entire system. The point is offered as the biography's closing inference, flagged like the others, and the reader now has the whole record from which to judge it.

## Names and masks: Luksenburg, Junius, and the Rosa of the legend

Even her name arrives in the record doubled. She was born Rozalia Luksenburg, in the Polish spelling of a family name the German documents render Luxemburg, and the two spellings trail her through the archives as a reminder that the record itself is bilingual before any interpreter touches it. The movement added its own names. Admirers and enemies converged, unusually, on the same epithet, Red Rosa, which the right wing press wielded as a warning and the left's songbooks later wore as a decoration; she herself showed no fondness for being a colour and a Christian name, a diminution her male comrades, who were granted surnames and initials in the literature, rarely had to survive. The masks she chose for herself are more instructive. Junius, the Roman name on the pamphlet written from her cell, invoked the republican tradition of resistance to tyranny and let a prisoner's argument circulate as a citizen's. The Spartacus letters took the name of a slave who had made an empire tremble, a collective signature for a collective authorship she insisted on against the cult of her own byline. And in the Polish movement she wrote for years under party pseudonyms that the scholarly editions have had to reattach to her laboriously, article by article. The pattern across the names is the pattern of the whole biography in miniature: identity continually offered to her as a banner, by birth, by nation, by sex, by fame, and continually declined in favour of the work and the collective subject she believed the work served. A reader who understands why she signed herself Junius and Spartacus rather than Rosa has understood the woman better than a reader of the wreaths, and the four claimants, each of whom needs a Rosa of its own, might begin their next editions there.

The private signatures tell the complementary story, and the correspondence preserves them in abundance. To Jogiches she is by turns formal and furious, signing with initials in the conspiratorial manner of the Polish years; to her women friends she is Rosa entire, with the diminutives of Warsaw girlhood surfacing in the Polish letters; to Sophie Liebknecht, from the fortresses, she signs herself your Rosa in letters that the recipient kept like relics and posterity reads like literature. The distribution is a small map of her doctrine of the person: the public self dissolved into the collective signature, the private self guarded for the few, and no intermediate self manufactured for the crowd of admirers who already, in her lifetime, wanted a personality to consume. Movements that market their founders have rarely forgiven a founder who withheld the merchandise. Hers withheld it on principle, and the withholding is one more element of the combination, the personal form of her refusal to let any banner, even her own face, stand between the class and its work. The legend manufactured the face anyway, from the police photographs and the funeral portraits. The biography's task, now complete in its main lines, has been to give the face back its mind.

One consequence of the withholding deserves a final sentence, because it disciplines every future biographer of this subject, the present one included. A thinker who refuses to supply a personality cult with materials leaves her interpreters unusually dependent on the arguments, and arguments, unlike personalities, can be checked. Her positions stand or fall in the open: the counter-schemes against her economics are printed, the record of the January days is documented, the manuscript's occasion is known, the votes at her last congress are counted. She arranged, whether by instinct or by design, to be judged exactly there, and the four claimants' continuing need to edit her is the tribute that arrangement extracts. The persona sections that follow are written for readers who intend to take up the judgement in their several capacities, as students, as teachers, and as investigators of the record.

## For students and exam candidates

Rosa Luxemburg rewards the candidate who learns her as a system rather than as a set of quotations, and the study method follows from that fact. Build your notes around the unheld combination: one page for each element, revolutionary against reformism, democratic against substitution, organised against spontaneity, internationalist against self-determination, with the text that proves each element and the claimant who drops it. The four claimants table in this biography converts directly into a revision grid, and a durable way to keep it is to reproduce the grid as a note set in the [marxism study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) format, one card per claimant with its citation, its omission, and her actual position, so that the comparison is in front of you when a question asks you to evaluate her legacy. Learn three dates with their arguments attached: 1899 for the reform pamphlet, 1906 for the mass strike, 1913 for the accumulation book, and treat 1918 as the year of two documents, the prison audit and the December programme, which must always be cited together. The errors that cost marks are the legend's errors: calling her a spontaneist, crediting her with coining the barbarism alternative, presenting the prison manuscript as an attack on the Russian revolution, and asserting casualty figures for the January rising that this biography deliberately withholds. The dedicated practice material in the [luxemburg trotsky exam answers](/2014/12/15/luxemburg-trotsky-exam-answers/) works these discriminations as model answers, and an hour spent there after this biography will fix the combination in the form examiners reward.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

Luxemburg teaches best in confrontation, and the seminar design should exploit the fact that every major text of hers is one half of an argument whose other half survives. Set the reform pamphlet against Bernstein's preconditions and let the room discover that she concedes his facts before she contests his conclusion; set the mass strike pamphlet against the Mannheim congress resolution it failed to move, and ask what a theory owes a movement that will not enact it; set the accumulation book's central chapters against Bauer's counter-scheme, distributed in advance, so that the technical objection is worked by the students rather than announced by the tutor; and set the prison manuscript against the December programme, her own two hands, before any claimant's edition is allowed into the room. The set extract for classroom use is the reform pamphlet, and the [reform or revolution readers guide](/2014/08/15/reform-or-revolution-readers-guide/) provides the passage by passage support for it. Two cautions govern the whole design. Keep the murder out of the first session, since its pathos colonises discussion and converts every subsequent text into a relic; introduce it in its place, as a problem in evidence and contested complicity, where it teaches source criticism better than any manufactured exercise. And police the quotations: require every quoted sentence to arrive with its year, its addressee, and the argument it served, a rule that takes ten minutes to establish and permanently disables the extract habit the biography exists to correct.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research frontier on Luxemburg is archival and Polish, and the biography's emphases indicate where the returns now lie. The Polish writings, still appearing in scholarly editions, are revising the national question from a slogan attributed to her into an argued corpus, and any new work on her internationalism that does not reckon with the 1908 and 1909 essays will be obsolete on arrival. The correspondence in its complete editions supports a proper intellectual biography of the prison years, in which the agrarian studies, the letters, and the Russian manuscript are read as one confinement's work. The contested zone remains January 1919: the complicity question, the exact chain of orders inside the Eden Hotel, and Pabst's late claims all await the historian willing to grade the evidence as this biography has graded it, separating the record from the murk without pretending the murk is the record. Journalists covering her commemoration cycle should note the two disciplines this article has modelled: no casualty figures for the January rising without the protocol of the German revolution literature, for which the [german revolution marxist analysis](/2014/11/01/german-revolution-marxist-analysis/) is the series' treatment, and no quotation without its occasion, since half the Luxemburg sentences in circulation have been severed from arguments that reverse their apparent sense. The claims audit remains the field's open secret. Every archive opened in a century has thickened the unheld combination and dissolved none of it, and the researcher who finds the text that breaks it will have a discovery, which is the correct scientific posture toward a thesis this biography has stated as strongly as the evidence allows.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was Rosa Luxemburg?

Rosa Luxemburg was a Polish born Marxist theorist, economist, and revolutionary who became a leading figure in both the Polish and German socialist movements. Born in Zamość in 1871, she took a Zurich doctorate on Polish industrial development, co-founded a Polish socialist party, and from 1898 worked in Germany as a party journalist, teacher, and polemicist. She wrote major works on reform, the mass strike, and imperialism, spent much of the First World War in prison for her opposition to it, co-founded the German communist party at the end of 1918, and was murdered by Freikorps soldiers in Berlin in January 1919.

### Q: What did Luxemburg contribute that was her own?

Her distinctive contributions form a connected set. She gave the critique of reformism its classic statement, arguing that reforms severed from a revolutionary goal change their character. She theorised the mass strike as the characteristic way a working class moves once a period turns revolutionary. She built an economics of imperialism on capitalism's dependence on a non-capitalist environment. She produced, from inside support for the Russian revolution, the classic audit of revolutionary government without political freedom. And she sustained, against nearly all her contemporaries, the case against national self-determination as a socialist slogan. Each contribution survives separately in the literature; their unity in one author is her signature.

### Q: What is The Accumulation of Capital about?

Published in 1913, her largest book asks how accumulation can proceed in a society composed only of capitalists and workers, and who purchases the surplus product in which new capital is embodied. Her answer locates the missing demand beyond the capitalist relation itself, among peasants, artisans, and colonial populations, so that capitalism lives by penetrating and destroying non-capitalist surroundings. Imperialism is then the competitive struggle among capitalist states for the shrinking outside. The book's technical proof has been widely disputed since Bauer's counter-scheme, but its relocation of imperialism from policy choice to structural necessity permanently altered her tradition's agenda.

### Q: Why did Luxemburg oppose Polish independence?

She opposed it on economic and strategic grounds formed by her doctoral research. Polish industry, she argued, had grown through integration with the Russian imperial market, so an independent Polish state would be economically unviable and dependent, while the independence slogan subordinated Polish workers to their own possessing classes and to the diplomacy of the partitioning powers. She demanded instead the fullest cultural and regional autonomy within a democratised multinational state, and she fought for Polish language and Polish organisation throughout her life. Her target was the state project, not the nation's existence, a distinction her Polish opponents rarely granted her.

### Q: Did Luxemburg coin the phrase socialism or barbarism?

No. The alternative appears in the Junius Pamphlet, which she wrote in prison during the First World War, and she attributed the idea to Engels, presenting the choice between a transition to socialism and a regression into barbarism as a judgement the older thinker had framed a generation earlier. Posterity attached the coinage to her because her pamphlet carried the phrase around the world, but her own text gives the credit away with deliberate scholarly honesty. The episode is a tidy example of her extract problem: even her attributions get extracted, and the thinker who refused the slogan is remembered as its inventor.

### Q: Did Luxemburg criticise the Bolsheviks?

She did, in a manuscript composed during her imprisonment in 1918, and the stance behind the criticism defines it. She regarded October as a justified and necessary act and praised its authors for daring it, then examined their emergency measures as a comrade auditing a shared project: the assembly sent home, the franchise cut down, newspapers silenced, land broken into smallholdings, and separatist national claims conceded. Her warning was institutional rather than liberal. Where elections, an unfettered press, and open argument disappear, public bodies empty out, the councils included, and administration by officials fills the space. The pamphlet appeared only after her death, in 1922.

### Q: Did Luxemburg support the January rising?

She judged the Berlin rising of January 1919 premature and argued against attempting to bring down the government at that moment, believing the masses in the capital and the provinces unready and the young communist party too weak to survive the defeat. She was overruled inside the revolutionary camp. Once the fighting had begun, however, she refused flight and silence, stayed in the city, and edited the rising's newspaper through the suppression, including the editorial of 14 January that closed by promising the revolution's return. Her position was therefore dissent before the decision and solidarity after it, a sequence her legend usually simplifies in one direction or the other.

### Q: How was Rosa Luxemburg killed?

She was arrested with Karl Liebknecht on 15 January 1919 at the Berlin apartment where they were hiding and taken to the Eden Hotel, headquarters of the Freikorps Guards Cavalry Rifle Division, which operated with the social democratic government's authority. Beaten with a rifle butt by a soldier as she was led out, she was shot in the car removing her, the shot generally attributed to Lieutenant Kurt Vogel, and the body was tipped from a bridge into the Landwehr Canal, where it was recovered at a lock the following May. The subsequent military proceedings were lenient, Vogel was helped to escape, and her commanding officer was never tried.

### Q: Did Luxemburg lead a party of her own?

Yes, twice over, in two countries. In 1893 she co-founded a Polish party, the Social Democracy of the Kingdom of Poland, and remained one of its leaders, editors, and theoreticians for twenty five years, through illegality, the 1905 revolution, and exile. In Germany she never led the great social democratic party, whose leadership she opposed from its left, but in December 1918 she co-founded the Communist Party of Germany, drafted its Spartacus programme, and gave the founding congress its programme address, losing there the vote on election participation to her own delegates. The caricature of a leaderless spontaneist cannot survive either half of that record.

### Q: Why is Luxemburg claimed by so many different currents?

Because her positions form a combination that no successor current has held entire, each claimant can quote her accurately on the element it keeps while needing to ignore the element that indicts it. Social democrats cite her defence of political freedom and drop her communism. Communists cite the party she founded and the manner of her death and drop her audit of Bolshevik methods. Feminists cite her life and letters and drop her refusal of a separate women's politics. Green movements cite her account of capital devouring its outside and drop her industrial expectations. She is quotable by all of them precisely because she is representative of none.

### Q: How does the unheld combination explain Luxemburg's four claimants?

The combination names the four positions she held together: revolutionary where reformism counselled patience, democratic where bureaucratic substitution beckoned, organised where others trusted impulse, and internationalist where national programmes demanded allegiance. A claimant tradition can house some of these comfortably and none can house all four, so each claim is produced by amputation, and the table of citations and omissions in this biography maps the surgery claimant by claimant. The explanation is mechanical rather than psychological: it does not accuse the claimants of bad faith, since each cites genuine texts. It shows that accuracy of quotation is compatible with falsity of representation whenever the whole from which the quotation is cut is a combination the quoter has declined to hold.

### Q: What should a biographer separate between Luxemburg's economics and her politics?

A biographer should separate the fate of her central proof from the fate of her central investigation. The proof, that Marx's reproduction schemas cannot close without an external buyer, was answered by counter-schemes within months and has not recovered; treating it as sound because her politics command respect is claimant's work. The investigation, her mass of evidence that capitalism expanded by consuming non-capitalist strata and territories through tax, debt, land seizure, and war, established the relation between capital and its environment as a permanent object of study, and treating it as refuted because the proof failed is the mirror error. Separating the two verdicts lets her economics be neither a footnote to her politics nor a hostage to them.

### Q: Why did her murder shape her reception so strongly?

The murder supplied every later tradition with a story stronger than any text: a woman thinker killed by soldiers serving a government of her former party, her body recovered from a canal months later, her killers sheltered by military justice. Martyrdom reorganised the reading of everything she had written, lending her democratic criticisms the pathos of prophecy and her party founding the pathos of a testament, while discouraging the unglamorous attention her economics and her Polish writings require. It also handed rival movements a relic to contest, so that commemoration itself became a battlefield, from the Friedrichsfelde processions to the arrests at the official ceremony seven decades later. The biography's remedy is sequence: life and work first, on their own evidence, and the death in its documentary place.

### Q: How did Luxemburg's Polish work differ from her German work?

Her Polish work was the construction of a small, often illegal cadre party against the dominant national slogan, conducted in Polish through smuggled and exiled newspapers, inside an empire that imprisoned her in its Warsaw citadel. Her German work was conducted inside the largest legal socialist party in the world, through its daily press, its theoretical journal, its congresses, and its central school, where she taught for seven years. The audiences, the legal conditions, and the enemy differed completely: in Poland the temptation was the nation, in Germany it was respectability. What did not differ was the method, the testing of every cherished slogan against the material structure beneath it.

### Q: What evidence would change the standard picture of her spontaneity doctrine?

The standard picture, in serious scholarship, is that the spontaneity doctrine is a legend: she held that mass action arises from struggle rather than from committee decisions, while building and leading parties throughout her career. Evidence that would change that picture would be a text from her hand opposing party organisation as such, counselling reliance on unled action, or refusing an organisational task on principle. No such text has surfaced in a century of archival recovery that has instead produced party programmes, school lectures, editorial labours, and a founding congress address. Until one appears, the burden of proof rests where this biography has placed it: on the legend, which survives by quotation from secondary sources rather than from anything she wrote.
