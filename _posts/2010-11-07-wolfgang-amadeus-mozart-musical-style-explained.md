Two voices sing at the same time and want opposite things. One pleads, the other refuses, and a third enters with a private worry that contradicts both, yet a listener follows every strand without confusion, because each voice keeps its own melodic shape, its own rhythm and its own emotional temperature while the combined sound stays coherent. That effect, simultaneous characterisation inside an opera ensemble, is the key to Mozart musical style as a whole. Mozart's instrumental music is built on operatic logic, and the reason his symphonies and concertos feel like events rather than designs is that he writes every important theme as a character who wants something.

## Two People Wanting Opposite Things at Once

The ensemble effect described at the opening depends on a discipline that can be heard before it can be named. Each singer receives a line that would make sense alone, with rises and falls placed where a breath can support them and with resting points that feel earned rather than imposed. When those complete lines are combined, the composer must manage every vertical meeting between them, deciding which meetings should sound smooth and which should grate productively. The listener registers the result as human complexity rather than as technical display, because wanting, refusing and hesitating are experiences the ear recognises from speech and from staged drama long before any terminology arrives.

A useful first exercise is to follow one voice through an ensemble passage and ignore the rest, then repeat the passage and follow a different voice. The passage changes character with each choice of focus, even though no note has altered. That change proves that the meaning does not live in a single tune with decoration around it. Meaning lives in the relations between complete utterances, in who interrupts, who yields, who repeats a phrase with altered harmony underneath and who falls silent at the moment when an answer was expected. Silence, in this kind of writing, is a dramatic act rather than an absence, and rests are placed with the same care as notes.

Here the central question of this article must be posed and deliberately left open. How can instrumental music have characters? A symphony has no singers, no words and no stage, so the claim that a theme behaves like a person who wants something needs evidence rather than assertion. The evidence will accumulate through melody, phrase balance, harmony and form, and the question receives its full answer when the second subject of a sonata movement steps forward in its own key and its own manner. Until that point, hold the question as a listening tool and test every passage against it.

The biographical facts behind the technique, the childhood tours, the Salzburg employment, the Vienna freelance years and the opera commissions that taught the young composer how voices combine, belong to [the companion account of his life and career](/2010/10/21/wolfgang-amadeus-mozart-life-and-career/), and only the craft consequences concern the present article. Daniel Heartz, the scholar of music and Enlightenment culture whose work placed opera at the centre of eighteenth century musical life, showed how thoroughly the Viennese stage shaped the expectations of audiences who also attended concerts. A listener raised on ensembles brought ensemble ears to the symphony hall, and a composer who wrote for both rooms could rely on those ears.

## What Makes a Mozart Melody Recognisable

### What makes Mozart's music sound like Mozart?

A Mozart melody is recognisable through short answering units, clear breathing points, graceful turns at phrase ends and accompaniment patterns that keep harmony moving beneath the line. The tune seems simple because each gesture is proportioned, singable and placed with exact timing, while small chromatic touches and sudden dynamic changes supply the expression underneath the calm surface.

The first revelation of the article can now be stated with its mechanism attached. His melodies are built from short answering units, which is why they feel inevitable and are hard to imitate. A typical opening gesture lasts only a few beats, states a compact shape with a clear contour, and then receives a reply that mirrors its rhythm while altering its ending. Question and answer, statement and consequence, call and response at the scale of seconds: the pattern gives the ear a constant supply of completed micro events, and the mind reads completion as naturalness. Imitators capture the surface elegance and miss the timing of the replies, so their phrases sag where his stay alert.

Charles Rosen, the analyst who treated form as expressive action in his account of the classical style, argued that this conversational quality in melody connects directly to the larger forms built from it. A theme assembled from answering units can be broken apart in a development section, because its units are already separable, and it can be reassembled in a recapitulation with altered answers that register as new thoughts rather than as repetition. Melody and architecture are therefore the same skill viewed at different scales. A listener who learns to hear the small answers has also learned, without noticing, how the big paragraphs will behave.

Recognisability also depends on what the melody refuses to do. Long unbroken cantabile lines of the kind associated with later romantic writing are rare in the instrumental music, and where a singing line does extend, it is usually punctuated by orchestral interjections that restore the conversational frame. Ornament serves the same discipline. A turn or a trill arrives as punctuation at a joint in the phrase, clarifying structure while decorating it, rather than as display pasted over the surface. Julian Rushton, the opera scholar and editor whose Mozart work stressed the unity of the vocal and instrumental output, observed that the instrumental themes behave like vocal lines even when no voice could sing them comfortably, because their phrasing follows breath and rhetoric rather than keyboard convenience.

Accompaniment completes the signature. The repeated pattern named after Domenico Alberti, in which a chord is broken into a regular rocking figure beneath a melody, appears so often in the piano music that it can sound like wallpaper, yet its function is precise. The figure keeps the harmony audible in every beat, supplies gentle motion during long melodic notes and frees the right hand to shape the phrase without also carrying the harmonic floor. In orchestral writing the same job falls to divided strings, pizzicato touches or quiet wind chords. The melody floats because the floor beneath it never stops moving, and that combination of stillness above and motion below is one of the most reliable fingerprints a new listener can learn.

Dynamics inside a melody carry identifying information too. Swells placed on a rising line and releases placed at its descent follow the behaviour of a sung phrase under emotional pressure, and sudden piano echoes of a forte statement, a favourite device in the piano sonatas and the concertos alike, convert assertion into reflection within a single bar. A theme stated twice at two dynamic levels has effectively been spoken by two temperaments, and the device lets a short movement hold a dialogue before any contrasting theme has arrived. Listeners who track dynamic shape hear characterisation operating at the smallest scale the style permits.

## Phrase Structure and the Sense of Balance

Balance in this music begins as arithmetic that the ear can feel. Phrases group into pairs, pairs into four bar units, and four bar units into eight bar spans whose symmetry resembles the couplets of verse. A listener does not count consciously, yet the body registers the proportions the way it registers the stride of a familiar walk. When the first half of a phrase ends on an unstable harmony that demands continuation, and the second half answers by settling onto stable ground, the exchange produces a sensation of question and resolution that needs no theory to be felt. Teachers call the pattern antecedent and consequent, and the names matter less than the physical experience of leaning forward and then arriving.

The balance is social as well as numerical. Eighteenth century listeners encountered this music in rooms where dancing, conversation and card play shared the evening, and phrase symmetry belonged to a broader culture of measured exchange. A minuet phrase that answers itself models the courtesy of a dance figure performed and then mirrored by a partner. Simon Keefe, the scholar of the piano concertos whose work traced how audiences heard these exchanges in performance, emphasised that contemporaries valued the give and take between soloist and orchestra partly because it dramatised politeness itself. Phrase balance was therefore a shared language between composer and room, not a private habit of construction.

Beneath the symmetry sits a subtler mechanism, the management of phrase endings. A weak ending, where the melody closes on a light beat with harmony still open, invites continuation and keeps a paragraph breathing. A strong ending, squarely placed and harmonically sealed, grants permission to change the subject. By grading endings from tentative to conclusive across a movement, the composer controls the listener's sense of punctuation as precisely as a dramatist controls full stops and commas in dialogue. Long stretches cohere because the punctuation varies. A movement in which every phrase ended with equal finality would feel like a sequence of sealed boxes, and this writing avoids that trap by design.

Asymmetry enters early and often, which keeps the arithmetic from hardening into mechanism. A four bar phrase may stretch to five through a repeated cadence figure, or a consequent may answer at greater length than its antecedent because the harmony takes a detour on the way home. The extensions feel like a speaker adding a clause for emphasis, and they work because the underlying grid remains audible. A listener can practice by tapping the expected arrival point of a phrase and noting when the music arrives early, late or exactly on the predicted beat. The small surprises register as wit when the grid is secure and as confusion when it is absent, which is why establishing the grid comes first in every well built opening movement.

Sentence structure supplies the larger unit in which the pairs assemble. A period, in the language of the period's own theorists such as Heinrich Christoph Koch, joins antecedent and consequent into a complete statement ending in a full cadence, while a sentence built from a short basic idea and its varied repetition drives forward through continuation toward a cadence that liquidates the opening energy. The two designs produce different gaits: the period walks in balanced strides, the sentence leans and accelerates. Koch's treatises, published in the years of the mature works, describe the expectations of practised listeners in vocabulary those listeners shared, which makes the theory evidence for the experience rather than commentary imposed afterward by a later age.

## Why the Balance Is Often Broken Deliberately

The second revelation follows from the first account of balance. The balance everyone hears is a norm he sets up specifically so that breaking it registers. A composer who never established symmetry could not surprise anyone by departing from it, because surprise requires an expectation firm enough to be violated. The opening movements therefore invest heavily in regular phrasing at the start, building credit with the ear, and then spend that credit at chosen moments: a phrase cut short by an orchestral interruption, a cadence evaded at the last instant, a quiet melody suddenly answered at full volume by instruments that had been silent. Each disruption lands as an event precisely because the surrounding paragraphs behaved so predictably.

Interruptions deserve close attention because they reveal the operatic reflex at work. In an opera buffa scene, a character entering mid sentence changes the direction of an argument, and the music marks the entrance with a change of texture or key. The same gesture appears in instrumental movements when a lyrical passage is broken by a stern unison figure or when a development section opens with the main theme stated in a remote key by a single instrument group. The listener reads the gesture as a person arriving, because the grammar of arrival was learned from the stage. No programme is required and none is implied. The mechanism is formal, and form carries the drama.

Deceptive motion supplies a quieter class of breakage. A phrase travels toward the cadence the ear expects, the harmony gathers itself for arrival, and at the final moment the bass steps aside onto a chord that continues the sentence instead of closing it. The effect resembles a speaker who reaches the end of a clause and adds a qualification, and its expressive range runs from playful delay to genuine unease depending on how often the evasion repeats. Repeated evasions before a double bar can stretch a closing section far beyond its expected span, generating a feeling of abundance that listeners often describe as generosity, although the mechanism is simply postponement artfully graded.

Sudden silence performs similar work. A general pause, in which the whole ensemble stops after a rising figure, suspends the listener in a held breath and makes the next entrance, however modest, feel consequential. The pause also exposes the fragility of the balance myth: the proportions that seemed architectural turn out to depend on continuous human attention, and a composer willing to halt the machine entirely demonstrates complete control over it. Performers who rush these silences in the name of momentum erase information, because the length of the silence is part of the sentence. Hearing a pause held to its full value is one of the clearest tests of whether a performance understands the rhetoric it is presenting.

Broken balance also governs the joins between larger spans. A slow introduction may promise a grandeur the Allegro then treats with buffa lightness, so that the movement's argument becomes the relation between its own two tempers. A minuet may admit a trio whose rustic drones mock the courtliness of the frame, and a finale may quote the first movement's sternest theme in a rhythm that converts threat into dance. Each case spends the same currency: a norm established firmly enough that its transformation carries news. The listener's pleasure in recognition depends on the norm, and the composer's reputation for effortlessness depends on how completely the norm's construction has been hidden inside the pleasure.

## Harmony as a Slow Moving Floor

Beneath the melodic conversation, harmony moves at a pace that can feel almost geological by comparison, and that contrast between fast surface and slow floor is structural. A single chord may govern several bars while melodies, ornaments and rhythmic figures animate the foreground, and the listener senses stability not because nothing happens but because the foundation changes rarely and decisively. When the floor does move, the event matters. A shift from the home chord to the chord built on the fifth degree tightens expectation, a move to the sixth degree softens it, and a plunge toward a distant key in a development section can feel like the ground tilting. Tracking floor changes is a learnable skill that requires no score, only patience and a willingness to notice when the air in the music changes pressure.

The floor also explains why sequences, passages in which a short pattern repeats at successive pitch levels, carry such momentum. Each repetition moves the floor by one step, so the listener feels carried along a staircase whose direction is unmistakable even when the destination remains hidden. Sequences in the development sections often descend by step or circle through related keys, and their regularity provides orientation inside the most unstable part of a movement. The pattern on the surface stays constant while the ground shifts beneath it, which reverses the usual arrangement and produces a distinctive sensation of travelling while standing still.

Pedal points extend the principle to its limit. A single bass note sustains through changing harmonies above it, creating friction as chords pass that agree and disagree with the held note in turn. The friction resolves when the harmony returns to alignment with the pedal, and the release can be timed to coincide with a formal arrival such as the return of the main theme or the entry of a soloist after a cadenza. Before final cadences, a long dominant pedal acts as a held breath across the whole orchestra, raising the pressure until the home chord discharges it. A listener who learns to hear held bass notes has acquired a reliable predictor of where the large arrivals will fall.

Cliff Eisen, the source scholar and editor of the composer's letters and documents, demonstrated how much of this harmonic planning can be traced in the physical evidence of autographs, copyists' scores and corrected proofs. The documents show a musician working inside a shared harmonic grammar taught through figured bass exercises and partimento training across Europe, absorbing its routines so completely that the routines became reflexes. Originality, on the evidence Eisen assembled, rarely meant inventing new chords. It meant deploying the common floor with uncommon timing, placing a familiar progression where the phrase structure made it sound like a revelation and withholding it where habit led the ear to expect it.

Cadence types grade the floor's arrivals into a hierarchy a listener can learn as punctuation. A perfect cadence closes with the authority of a full stop, a half cadence suspends the sentence on the dominant like a comma with the voice raised, an interrupted cadence swerves onto the sixth degree like a sentence changing its mind mid clause, and a plagal colouring softens a close into benediction. Whole movements can be mapped by their cadence hierarchy alone, the strongest closes reserved for the ends of expositions, developments and codas, the weaker grades distributed inside phrases. The map explains why some returns feel inevitable and others feel provisional, and the explanation requires no apparatus beyond attention to how each arrival is weighted.

## Chromaticism and Where the Darkness Enters

Chromatic notes, the pitches that fall between the steps of the governing scale, enter this music as guests whose presence changes the behaviour of the household. A single sharpened note inside a major key phrase leans toward its neighbour with unusual intensity, and the lean creates a small pocket of yearning or unease inside an otherwise untroubled sentence. Because the diatonic surroundings are so clearly ordered, each chromatic inflection carries a charge out of proportion to its size. The mechanism resembles a raised eyebrow in a composed face. Nothing in the features has rearranged, yet the expression has altered completely, and everyone in the room registers the change.

The darkness gathers through accumulation rather than through a single device. Chromatic passing notes thicken a bass line until it seems to descend by inexorable steps. Chromatic chords borrowed from the parallel minor cast a shadow across a major key paragraph without abandoning the key itself. A melody that decorates its main notes with sighing semitone neighbours begins to sound as though it is confessing something the words of the movement, had it words, would leave unsaid. Rosen, whose account of the classical style treated chromaticism as a resource rationed for structural moments, noted that the rationing is the point: scarcity preserves expressive value, and the passages where chromatic density suddenly increases mark the places where the argument of a movement turns inward.

A second open loop belongs here, posed plainly and answered in the following section. Why do the minor key works sound like a different composer? The question arises because the chromatic devices that appear as guests in major key movements seem to govern entire minor key works, bending melody, rhythm and orchestration toward a graver rhetoric. Whether the difference lies in the mode itself, in the topics and gestures the mode attracts, or in the expectations listeners bring to it will be tested against the evidence of the works themselves. For the length of one section the question stays open, and the chromatic vocabulary described here supplies the tools for its answer.

Orchestration carries part of the chromatic effect. A chromatic line entrusted to violas and cellos in their lower registers sounds veiled, while the same line given to oboes acquires a keening edge, and a bassoon doubling can turn private unease into public foreboding. The slow introduction of the Prague Symphony and the opening of the Don Giovanni overture exploit such choices, presenting the same descending chromatic idea at different weights so that the listener learns to associate the figure with a dramatic world before any theme in the home key has been established. Colour is never decoration in these passages. Colour decides how seriously the ear takes the shadow, and the decisions are consistent enough across the output to count as style.

Certain chromatic chords carry recognised dramatic passports. The diminished seventh, symmetrical and rootless in effect, can pivot a passage toward almost any key, which made it the period's standard signal for supernatural threat, sudden reversals and scenes of recognition in opera, and its instrumental appearances import the same passport. The Neapolitan chord, a flattened supertonic sonority of unusual plangency, darkens a cadence approach in both major and minor contexts and appears at points of textual grief in the sacred works and at formal crises in the instrumental ones. Augmented sixth sonorities lean toward the dominant with a chromatic intensity that stretches the moment before arrival. A listener need not name any of these chords to register their passports, because the operatic associations trained the registration in advance, yet naming them converts a vague shiver into a repeatable observation, and repeatable observation is the beginning of style knowledge. The rationing principle governs all three: a diminished seventh in every paragraph would signify nothing, while one placed at a movement's single point of maximum instability can govern the memory of the whole design.

## The Minor Key Works and What Changes in Them

### Why do Mozart's minor key works feel different?

Minor key works feel different because the mode changes melody, rhythm, orchestration and expectation together. Angular themes replace songlike ones, syncopation and driving repeated notes replace dance ease, winds darken the colour, and phrases resist comfortable closure. The craftsmanship is identical, yet the expressive contract shifts from conversation toward confrontation, urgency and grief.

The open loop can now close. Why do the minor key works sound like a different composer? They do not, once the mechanisms are separated. The same answering units, the same graded cadences and the same slow harmonic floor operate in the G minor symphonies and the C minor piano concerto, and a listener tracking those mechanisms hears continuity beneath the changed surface. What alters is the inventory of gestures the mode licenses: unison openings that state a theme as a proclamation rather than a song, offbeat accents that disturb the dance grid, tremolando strings that replace Alberti calm with agitation, and cadences that arrive as exhaustion rather than as courtesy. The composer is recognisable by his syntax even when his vocabulary turns severe.

Proportion sharpens the impression of difference because the minor key catalogue is small. Two symphonies among the mature set, two piano concertos, a handful of quartets and sonatas and isolated movements carry the reputation, so each example bears unusual weight in the imagination of listeners. Scarcity invites the works to be read as personal statements, yet the brief for this article carries a warning that must be honoured: the documentary record does not support reading the minor key works as autobiography. Letters from the periods of the G minor Symphony K. 550 and the C minor Concerto K. 491 show professional calculation, commissions and deadlines, not confessional intent, and the style analysis gains nothing by inventing a diary where the sources show a workshop.

What changes most audibly is the treatment of the second subject. In major key movements the contrasting theme often brings lyrical release, a new character whose manner softens the argument. In minor key movements the second subject, usually placed in a major key area, can sound like remembered happiness or like a consolation that the development will withdraw, and its fragility becomes part of the drama. The development then subjects the stern opening material to chromatic sequences that push the floor through distant keys, so that the recapitulation, when it restores the minor theme, feels like a return to a problem rather than to a home. The architecture is identical to the major key plan. The emotional geometry is reversed, and that reversal, achieved with shared tools, is the precise answer to the loop question.

Rhythm completes the transformation. Minor key movements favour driving quaver patterns, sudden fortissimo punctuation and phrases that begin with an upbeat thrust rather than a settled downbeat. The Symphony No. 40 in G minor opens with an accompaniment figure already in motion before the melody enters, so the listener joins an anxiety in progress instead of witnessing its start. Rushton, the opera scholar whose reading of the output stressed continuity between stage and concert room, linked such openings to operatic scenes of agitation, where agitated string figures conventionally signal alarm. The gesture is a shared topic of the period, deployed in the symphony with a concentration the stage rarely matched, and recognising the topic lets a listener hear convention and intensification at once.


## Sonata Form Described as a Process

### What is sonata form?

Sonata form is a dramatic process in three large phases. An exposition presents contrasting themes in contrasting keys, a development destabilises that material through fragmentation and travel through distant keys, and a recapitulation restores the themes in the home key so that contrast is resolved. Expectations, rather than fixed rules, govern each phase.

The process description matters because the template description misleads. Generations of textbooks presented sonata form as a mould into which themes are poured, with prescribed key schemes and obligatory repeats, and listeners armed with the mould hear deviations as errors or as licences. The brief for this article forbids that reading, and the music supports the prohibition. The form behaves as a set of expectations shared between composer and audience, strong enough that fulfilling, delaying or defeating an expectation all carry meaning. A movement is therefore less like a building with a floor plan and more like a plot whose conventions the audience knows well enough to feel every twist.

The exposition earns its name by exposing a conflict. The first subject establishes the home key, a rhythmic profile and a manner of speaking. A transition then does active work, loosening the home key through sequences or chromatic sidesteps until the harmony can plausibly settle elsewhere, usually on the dominant in major key movements or in a related major key in minor key movements. The second subject, examined closely in the following section, occupies that new territory with contrasting material. A closing group confirms the new key with cadential figures whose repetitiveness signals that the argument of the exposition has reached its provisional settlement, and the repeat sign invites the listener to rehear the whole conflict with foreknowledge.

Hearing the process rather than the mould changes what counts as a notable event. A transition that refuses to modulate, generating energy while staying at home, becomes a joke about reluctance. A second subject that quotes the first subject in the new key becomes an argument that the supposed opponents were related all along. A development that opens with a false recapitulation, presenting the main theme in the wrong key with full ceremonial confidence, becomes a dramatic deception whose correction the listener awaits. Teachers who need slides to map such events onto named movements can open a deck in the browser without installing software, since [the presentation viewer at ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) opens a browser based presentation viewer that renders the file directly on screen. The diagram serves the hearing, and the hearing remains the point.

Scale varies enormously within the shared process. A piano sonata exposition may present its conflict in forty bars, while a symphony exposition deploys the same phases across several minutes with orchestral rhetoric amplifying every phase change. The phases remain recognisable because their functions are signalled by cadential punctuation and key arrival rather than by length. Rosen treated this scalability as evidence that the form is a way of thinking about musical time, a method for making contrast, instability and return intelligible at any duration. A listener who internalises the three functions owns a compass that works on a quartet movement, a concerto movement and an operatic finale with equal reliability.

Movements outside the sonata plan clarify the plan by contrast. A rondo returns its refrain between episodes and therefore promises recurrence rather than resolution, a set of variations holds its harmonic floor constant while surface invention changes, and a minuet encloses its argument inside dance symmetry with a trio as its contrasting room. Sonata process is the only design among them built on a problem that must be solved, the foreign key's material awaiting naturalisation, and the sense of necessity that listeners praise in the great first movements comes from that problem's persistence through every phase. Knowing the alternatives by their promises makes the chosen promise audible.

## The Second Subject as a Second Character

The loop opened in the first section can now close in full. How can instrumental music have characters? A theme earns the name of character when it possesses a recognisable manner, a territory it inhabits, desires implied by its phrasing and a fate that the movement then decides. The second subject meets every condition. It enters in a foreign key after a journey the transition has dramatised, it speaks in a contrasting manner that may be lyrical where the first subject was assertive or hesitant where the first was confident, and the whole subsequent plot concerns whether its material will be reconciled with the home key or expelled from it. Character here is a formal fact before it is a poetic metaphor.

The third revelation lands with that closure. Sonata form in his hands is a dramatic process with opposing parties, not a container. The parties are defined by key as much as by melody. In the tonal thinking of the period, the dominant key area functions as a rival pole whose stability must eventually be undone, and the second subject is the citizen of that rival territory whose themes the recapitulation will naturalise into the home key. A listener can hear naturalisation happen: the same lyrical theme that floated in the dominant during the exposition returns grounded in the tonic, its intervals adjusted, its accompaniment reweighted, and the small adjustments register as a changed allegiance. Nothing on stage has moved, yet a political resolution has occurred in sound.

Contrast between the parties is graded with care. Some movements maximise opposition, pairing a unison proclamation with a whispered cantabile answer that shares no melodic material with it. Others minimise opposition, deriving the second subject from the head of the first through inversion or rhythmic reshaping, so that the drama concerns kinship recognised late rather than strangers reconciled. The Jupiter Symphony finale and several quartet movements exploit kinship, weaving themes that appear opposed until a coda combines them and reveals the family resemblance as the point of the plot. A listener tracking derivation hears the argument tighten. A listener tracking only contrast hears a medley. The writing rewards the first kind of attention, and the reward is available without any equipment beyond patience.

Gendered language attached itself to these themes in nineteenth century criticism, which labelled first subjects masculine and second subjects feminine, and the labels survive in older programme notes. The labels distort more than they reveal, because they freeze a flexible dramatic relation into a fixed allegory and they ignore movements where the second subject is the more forceful party. The stronger reading, supported by the operatic parallel, treats the pair as dramatis personae whose relation each movement defines for itself: rivals, lovers, plaintiff and judge, traveller and destination. What matters for hearing is opposition plus consequence, and any specific casting belongs to the individual movement rather than to the form as a law.

Scoring gives the second character its costume and its entrances are staged with a care that rewards visual attention to the score's layout translated into sound. A subject first entrusted to a solo wind over pizzicato strings enters the drama as a private utterance overheard by the ensemble, while the same shape assigned to full strings in unison would enter as a public claim. Transfers of ownership across the movement then read as changes of custody over an idea: the orchestra examines what the piano proposed, the winds return a theme to the strings altered by their accent, and each transfer slightly revises the character's standing. The second subject's biography inside a movement, who held it, in what dress, at what dynamic, is the shortest accurate summary of the movement's plot that prose can give, and compiling that biography is a practical exercise any listener can perform with a notebook and two hearings.

## Development as the Place Where Argument Happens

If the exposition states positions, the development cross examines them. Themes return in fragments, sometimes reduced to a head motif of three or four notes, and the fragments are passed between instruments, sequenced through falling keys, combined with their own accompaniments detached and set against them. The texture thins and thickens, dynamics swing between whispered exchanges and full ensemble protests, and the harmonic floor moves at its fastest rate of the whole movement. A listener lost in a development has usually stopped tracking the fragments, and the remedy is concrete: hold one small shape in memory and follow only that shape, noting each change of key, colour and company it keeps. The argument reappears around the tracked shape like a debate reassembling around a single contested phrase.

Silence and stillness mark the borders of the cross examination. Many developments begin after a decisive cadence and a breath, and many end with a passage of suspended quiet over a dominant pedal, where fragments of the main theme flicker in the winds above an expectant bass. That suspended passage, sometimes called the standing on the dominant, gathers the scattered argument and aims it at the recapitulation. Its rhetoric is pure theatre: the hush before a re entrance, the moment in an ensemble when every character turns toward a door. Composers of the period inherited the device from opera seria practice, where a held dominant under recitative prepared an aria entry, and its concert use shows the traffic between stage and symphony running in both directions at once.

Proportion inside the development carries meaning as well. A short development after a long exposition suggests a composer confident that the exposition contained its own tensions fully stated, while an extended development signals that the material concealed more conflict than its presentation admitted. The first movement of the Prague Symphony and the opening Allegro of the G minor Symphony K. 550 deploy developments whose sequences traverse unusually wide harmonic distances, and the distance travelled measures the seriousness of the argument. Keefe, in his studies of performance and reception in the concertos, showed that Viennese audiences prized such spans as tests of a composer's learning, applauding the ingenuity with which a return was engineered from remote territory. The engineering is audible as pleasure when the home key finally locks back into place.

Counterpoint enters the development as a weapon rather than as a texture. Fragments that coexisted peacefully in the exposition are forced into stretto, entries piling onto one another before the previous statement has finished, so that the theme argues with itself at rising pressure. Invertible combinations, where a melody and its counterline exchange registers, let the same material return upside down with its hierarchy reversed, a transformation the ear registers as a change of power between parties. These devices, examined later in the context of the late style, appear in developments across the mature output, and their presence explains why the middle of a movement can feel more learned and more dramatic at the same time. Learning, deployed as conflict, becomes drama.

Endings of developments repay a final distinction between two kinds of preparation for return. In one, the music exhausts itself into stillness, fragments losing energy until a single held harmony gathers what remains and the recapitulation enters as recovery. In the other, pressure builds without release through sequences and strettos until the return arrives as interruption, the home theme cutting off the argument by authority rather than by persuasion. The two preparations produce opposite meanings for identical notes at the double bar, and a listener who can say which preparation a given movement uses has understood its rhetoric at the deepest level form offers. Conductors decide the matter in performance through pacing and weight, which is why the same development can sound like a collapse in one reading and a siege in another.

## Recapitulation and Why Return Is Not Repetition

The recapitulation restores the exposition with its tonal conflict settled, and the settlement changes the meaning of every theme it touches. Material first heard in the dominant returns in the tonic, which sounds like a homecoming, yet the listener has travelled through the development and cannot hear the familiar phrases innocently. A theme that sounded confident in the exposition may now sound relieved, defiant or exhausted depending on the violence of the argument it survived. Performers who play a recapitulation as an exact repeat of the exposition erase that narrative, and listeners who follow the story can hear the erasure as a flattening of the plot. The notes repeat. The information does not.

Adjustments at the joins reward close attention. Transitions are rewritten because their original job, escaping the home key, must now be performed without leaving it, and the recomposition often produces the most ingenious bars of the movement: sequences that threaten departure and then fold back, chromatic sidesteps that visit the dominant and decline to settle there. Second subjects return with altered scoring, a wind colour replacing strings, a phrase extended by a bar of echo, small changes that register as the difference between meeting a person and meeting them again after a crisis. Codas then decide how much afterlife the argument receives. A brief coda seals the settlement, while the extended codas of the symphonies reopen debate one final time, sometimes combining themes that the movement had kept apart as a demonstration that reconciliation was structural all along.

The repeat structure frames the whole design. Exposition repeats, common in the symphonies and sonatas, train the listener in the conflict before the development destabilises it, and eighteenth century audiences expected the repeat as a courtesy rather than endured it as a formality. Development and recapitulation together sometimes carry their own repeat marks in earlier works, a practice that fades as movements grow and as the recapitulation becomes a resolution that gains nothing from immediate rehearing. A broad map of how such conventions formed across the century is set out in [the pillar survey of classical composers and their shared practices](/2010/03/07/history-of-classical-composers/), which places these formal habits inside the division that produced them. Within the individual movement, the convention that matters is expectation itself, and the recapitulation is where expectation is finally, deliberately, paid in full.

Failure modes clarify the achievement. A recapitulation that merely transposes the second subject without rethinking the transition sounds mechanical, and the juvenilia contain examples where the young composer, working at speed for Salzburg occasions, allowed the machinery to show. The mature movements hide the machinery by recomposing the joins and redistributing the orchestral light, so that necessity feels like choice. That difference, between transposition and recomposition, is one of the most dependable measures of growth across the catalogue, and it can be heard by any listener willing to compare an exposition and its return with the single question of what has changed besides the key. The answer, in the great movements, is always more than the question expects.


## The Piano Concerto as a Staged Dialogue

The fourth revelation concerns the genre where the operatic thesis becomes easiest to test. The piano concerto is staged like an opera scene, with the soloist as a character and the orchestra as the world. The opening ritornello presents the world's themes in full ensemble, establishing keys, manners and the terms of public life in the movement. The soloist then enters, often with a theme the orchestra never stated, asserting a private identity inside the public frame. From that entrance onward the movement alternates between negotiation and display, and every formal event, modulation, dialogue exchange, cadenza, can be read as a turn in the relationship between an individual voice and the collective that surrounds, challenges and finally accommodates it.

Ritornello design supplies the stage machinery. The orchestral exposition is longer and more self sufficient than a symphony exposition, because it must serve as both setting and prophecy: themes presented there will return as the scenery against which the solo drama plays out, and some will be entrusted to the soloist in altered form. The solo exposition then replays the journey to the second key with the piano as protagonist, adding keyboard figuration that converts orchestral statements into personal utterance. A phrase first heard as a unison proclamation returns as a singing line over Alberti patterning, and the conversion teaches the listener the article's central lesson in miniature: identity in this music is a function of manner, and manner changes meaning without changing notes.

Keefe, whose concerto scholarship reconstructed how these works were performed and heard in Vienna, documented the social charge of the dialogue. Subscription concerts placed the composer at the keyboard before an audience of patrons and connoisseurs who followed the exchange as connoisseurs of conversation followed a salon dispute, applauding wit, daring and recovery. The piano's role included genuine improvisatory elements in embellishment and cadenza, so the character on stage was partly the composer himself, thinking in public. That historical frame explains the genre's peculiar intimacy at large scale. A concerto movement addresses hundreds of listeners with the rhetoric of a private negotiation, and the tension between public scale and private manner generates much of its electricity.

Specific movements make the staging concrete. The first movement of the Concerto in E flat K. 271, written when the composer was twenty one, lets the piano interrupt the orchestra in the second bar, breaking the ritornello convention a generation of composers had observed and announcing that the soloist will behave as an interlocutor rather than as a guest awaiting introduction. The C minor Concerto K. 491 opens with a stern orchestral unison that the piano answers only after the full ritornello, entering as a dissenting voice in a world already darkly ordered. The D minor Concerto K. 466 sustains agitated syncopation in the orchestra beneath the piano's calmer entry, so that the solo character appears as composure maintained against surrounding unrest. Each design is a different play built on the same stage.

Finales test the staging under rondo conditions, where the refrain returns as a shared possession and episodes alternate between solo display and orchestral commentary. The soloist who dominated the first movement's negotiation must in the finale behave as a citizen of the refrain's community, leading dances, answering wind choirs phrase for phrase and reserving the largest display for a late episode or a cadenza whose return to the refrain feels like rejoining a festivity rather than escaping an argument. The progression across three movements, from negotiation through aria like confession to communal dance, reproduces the arc of a full opera evening compressed into half an hour, and audiences who surrender to the arc are responding to dramaturgy as much as to pianism. Keefe's reconstruction of the subscription concert world shows listeners applauding individual numbers and demanding encores of single movements, evidence that the arc was felt as theatre by the people for whom the theatre was built.

## The Orchestra as an Opposing Party

Opposition in the concerto is engineered through the distribution of themes. The orchestra guards certain material as its own property, statements too massive or too public for a single keyboard to deliver, and the soloist who attempts such a theme must transform it, breaking it into figuration or answering it phrase by phrase rather than reproducing it whole. Conversely, the soloist owns themes of a confessional cast that the orchestra may accompany but rarely initiates, as though the collective could support a private thought without being able to think it first. A listener can map the movement as a negotiation over property: who owns this idea, who borrows it, who returns it altered, and who concedes it in the recapitration where ownership disputes are settled in the home key.

Timbre enforces the politics of the exchange. Full ensemble passages arrive with trumpets and drums in festive movements, marking the public world at its most ceremonial, while the piano's most revealing utterances often occur against reduced scoring, muted strings or a single wind line in dialogue. The reduction is not merely contrast for its own sake. It stages the difference between what a character says in the square and what the same character says in a room with one trusted companion. Wind instruments frequently mediate between the parties, carrying a phrase from the piano to the strings or commenting on an exchange from a position outside it, and the mediating role prepares the ground for the independent treatment of winds examined in the sections ahead.

Resistance can also be rhythmic. Orchestral ritornellos return at formal joints to reassert the frame, sometimes cutting across the soloist's momentum with a cadential figure that behaves like a chairman restoring order. The soloist's counter strategy is delay: trills that suspend arrival, scales that postpone the cadence by virtuoso detour, and fermatas that hold the entire ensemble hostage to a single player's timing. Heartz placed such exchanges inside the broader Enlightenment fascination with the individual negotiating institutions, and the parallel needs no forcing, because the concert hall audience recognised the drama from the opera house, where arias regularly stage a single voice testing the limits of a chorus, a court or a fate announced by the full ensemble.

The settlement, when it comes, is audible as changed behaviour rather than as surrender by either party. In the finest finales the orchestra adopts figuration first heard in the piano, while the piano accepts thematic discipline first imposed by the ritornello, so that the closing pages enact mutual education. A rondo refrain shared equally between soloist and ensemble can then sound like a community that has absorbed its most restless member without flattening the restlessness. Listeners who follow the property map and the timbre map together will find that the last pages answer the question the first solo entrance posed, which is how a singular voice can belong to a world built without it.

The slow movement relocates the opposition indoors. Where the first movement stages a public negotiation, the slow movement stages a confidence, the orchestra reduced to a sympathetic company whose interjections behave as responses in a conversation held at low volume. The piano's line acquires the obligations of an aria, long breaths, ornamented returns, a middle strain of intensified feeling, and the orchestra's role changes from opposing party to witness, a transformation the scoring announces by thinning tuttis and entrusting commentary to single winds. Opposition, the comparison teaches, was never hostility. It was the public form of a relation whose private form is trust, and the concerto needs both forms to make its portrait of a person among people complete.

## The Cadenza and the Performer's Role

### What is a cadenza?

A cadenza is an unaccompanied passage near the end of a concerto movement where the orchestra falls silent and the soloist plays alone, usually over a held cadential chord, displaying invention and technique before a trill signals the ensemble to return. In the eighteenth century the passage was normally improvised by the soloist.

The cadenza concentrates the concerto's drama into a single suspended moment. Harmony halts on the cadential six four chord, the conductor figure of the period waits, and the soloist steps outside measured time to review the movement's themes in free combination, rather as an operatic character steps forward for a mad scene or a soliloquy in which earlier motifs return transformed by isolation. The convention carried risk, since a weak improvisation exposed invention failing in public, and it carried honour, since a strong one demonstrated that the performer possessed the composer's art and not only the performer's fingers. Surviving written cadenzas by the composer for his own concertos show the expected profile: thematic recall, sequential passage work, a climb to a high point of display, and the long trill over the dominant that summons the orchestra home.

For concertos written for other players, the cadenza space reveals professional judgement. Some movements receive cadenzas composed by the author for the soloist to use, some leave the space open with a fermata whose brevity in the score conceals its size in performance, and the placement of the fermata itself, first movement near the close of the recapitulation, finale sometimes at more than one joint, encodes how much unscripted speech the design can bear. Later generations of editors and performers supplied cadenzas in period manner for works whose originals were lost, and the joins remain audible to a prepared ear: the seams show where thematic recall gives way to generalised display, because the composer's own cadenzas quote the movement with a fidelity that imitators rarely sustain.

Improvisation extended beyond the cadenza into the fabric of performance. Soloists embellished repeated phrases, added lead ins at fermatas inside movements, and realised the keyboard part from a score that sometimes sketched the solo line in shorthand during orchestral tuttis. Keefe assembled the documentary evidence for these practices from letters, annotated parts and early editions, and the evidence reframes the concerto as a collaboration between a written design and a trained spontaneity that the design deliberately provokes. A modern performance that plays every note as fixed text is therefore not wrong in its notes and incomplete in its theatre, because part of the character the soloist plays was always the ability to speak beyond the script at licensed moments.

Judging a cadenza, as listener or as performer, reduces to proportion and recall. A cadenza that introduces brilliant material unrelated to the movement breaks the dramatic contract, however clean the execution, while a cadenza that merely repeats the exposition without transformation wastes the suspension it was granted. The model passages balance both obligations, quoting themes at altered sizes, combining fragments that the movement kept apart and reserving the largest display for the approach to the trill. Length matters too: the suspended chord can hold only so much pressure before the return of the orchestra loses its restorative force. The composer's own examples rarely exceed a fraction of the movement's span, and their discipline is the lesson the convention exists to teach.

## Writing for Wind Instruments

Wind writing separates this output from that of every contemporary, because the winds behave as independent characters rather than as harmonic filler. Earlier eighteenth century orchestration commonly assigned horns, oboes and bassoons to doubling strings, sustaining chords and reinforcing cadences, reserving melodic exposure for ceremonial entrances. In the mature symphonies and concertos the winds carry themes alone, answer the strings in dialogue, sustain long breathed lines over string figuration and form small ensembles inside the orchestra whose internal balance the composer manages like chamber music. A listener can test the claim in the slow movement of almost any late symphony: remove the wind parts in imagination and the argument collapses, while removing a string figuration merely thins the air around an intact conversation.

The mechanism is partly a matter of assigning each wind family its rhetorical register. Flutes brighten and distance, suited to pastoral topics and to lightening a texture at a formal dawn. Oboes speak with a focused, slightly nasal directness that suits pleading, questioning and pastoral melancholy. Bassoons ground the harmony, double cellos at moments of weight and step forward for dry commentary whose humour depends on the instrument's earnest delivery of absurd material. Horns carry distance and ceremony, their open notes limiting harmony to a natural series that the composer exploits when a passage needs the aura of the hunt or the outdoors. Trumpets and drums govern festivity and war. A theme's meaning shifts with its assignment among these registers, and the shifts are systematic enough to be learned as a vocabulary.

Vienna provided players capable of sustaining the experiment. The imperial capital supported virtuoso wind players in court, theatre and private employments, and the Harmonie, the wind octet of pairs of oboes, clarinets, horns and bassoons maintained by aristocratic households for table and garden music, trained both players and audiences in wind sonority as a complete medium. Serenades and divertimentos written for such ensembles, including the great B flat Serenade K. 361, explore wind balance at a scale where every line is exposed, and the knowledge transfers directly into the orchestral scores. Orchestral wind writing of this independence is a chamber musician's knowledge deployed at symphonic scale, which is why the passages repay the same close tracking a listener gives to a quartet.

Practical consequences follow for performance and for hearing. Wind lines written as dialogue require tuning agreements that string dominated textures forgive, since two oboes sustaining a third together expose any deviation without mercy. Dynamics must be graded inside the wind choir so that a bassoon counterline remains present beneath an oboe theme without either forcing. Conductors who treat the winds as colour applied to a string argument consistently flatten the drama, because the argument itself lives in the exchanges. The recording era before the date wall of this article made the point by accident many times over, as balances improved and listeners discovered conversations in famous passages that earlier balances had presented as melody with accompaniment. The notes never changed. The hearing did.

Dialogue among winds also solves a structural problem in long movements, the renewal of attention at the return of known material. A theme the strings have already presented twice can be renewed completely by entrusting its recapitulation to oboes and bassoons in dialogue, because the ear attends to the exchange and forgets to grow tired of the melody. Composers of symphonies before this generation rarely trusted winds with that responsibility at formal joints, reserving them for episodes and effects. Granting the responsibility at joints, where form is decided, amounts to promoting the wind choir from staff to partners in the argument, and the promotion is one of the clearest datable tendencies a listener can track from the Salzburg works through the Prague and Jupiter symphonies. Each stage of the promotion is marked by longer spans in which the strings fall silent and the winds carry the discourse unassisted, spans that grow from a few bars of colour into whole paragraphs of counterpoint among oboes, clarinets, bassoons and horns.

## The Clarinet and What He Heard in It

The clarinet receives the most personal wind writing of all, and the reason can be traced to specific players and specific instruments rather than to abstract preference. The instrument was a relative newcomer to the orchestra, absent from the Salzburg scores, present in Mannheim and Paris experiments, and available in Vienna through the Stadler brothers, Anton and Johann, whose playing the composer knew from Masonic and concert circles. Anton Stadler performed on an extended instrument, built with the maker Theodor Lotz, that descended below the standard range to written C, and the works composed for him exploit the low chalumeau register as a distinct expressive country: veiled, intimate and capable of a gravity the standard instrument cannot reach.

What the composer heard in the instrument can be described through its registers, which behave almost as three voices in one body. The chalumeau register speaks low and reedy, suited to confession and to accompaniment figures that must stay beneath a vocal line without disappearing. The clarion register sings with a rounded clarity that carries over an orchestra at moderate dynamics, making the clarinet a natural partner and rival for the soprano voice in arias and for the piano in concerto slow movements. The break between registers, a physical awkwardness of the fingering, becomes an expressive joint the writing sometimes exposes deliberately, letting a phrase change character as it crosses. The extreme high register appears sparingly, reserved for brilliance rather than for song.

The Clarinet Quintet K. 581 and the Clarinet Concerto K. 622, both written for Stadler, demonstrate the consequences at full scale. Long lines unfold in the clarion register over string textures that leave the middle of the sound spectrum open, a courtesy of orchestration that lets the solo timbre occupy its own air. Arpeggiated figures descend into the chalumeau for answers, so that a single player conducts a dialogue between registers that resembles two characters of different ages exchanging confidences. The concerto omits trumpets and uses divided violas and restrained winds, darkening the orchestral frame so that the solo colour reads as light inside a shaded room. A listener following the register map hears form as geography: departure into brightness, return into shadow, settlement in the middle voice where the instrument is most human.

Basset horns, the alto members of the clarinet family fitted with the same downward extension, extend the palette into choral territory. Their paired use in the Requiem and in the Masonic funeral music K. 477 gives wind writing a liturgical gravity, two like voices moving in thirds beneath or against the choir, while the serenades for wind ensemble use them as the inner voices that bind oboes to bassoons. Rushton noted the consistency with which dark wind choirs accompany texts and occasions of mourning across the output, a topic level habit that orchestration students can verify in score after score. The clarinet family, in this output, is never neutral equipment. Its members arrive with associations the composer could trust an audience to feel before a single theme had declared its intentions.


## Opera as the Organising Skill

Opera is named here as the organising skill rather than as one genre among several, because the habits formed in the theatre govern choices in every other medium the composer touched. Theatre teaches pacing at the scale of an evening: when to advance plot through recitative, when to freeze action into an aria, when to let characters collide in ensemble and when to release accumulated tension in a finale. Theatre teaches characterisation through vocal range, orchestral colour and the length of phrase a particular temperament is allowed. Theatre also teaches the management of an audience's memory, since a motif planted in an overture can govern expectations for three acts. Each of those skills has a precise instrumental analogue, and the analogues, taken together, constitute the thesis of this article tested at the level of a whole career.

Chronology supports the claim without any forcing. Opera commissions punctuate the entire working life, from the childhood La finta semplice through the Salzburg Idomeneo and the Vienna triumphs and experiments that culminate in Die Zauberflöte and La clemenza di Tito in the final year. Instrumental landmarks were composed in the same months as stage works, by the same hand, for overlapping audiences and often for the same singers and players encountered in the pit. Heartz, whose scholarship situated the operas inside the political and literary culture of the Enlightenment stage, showed how libretti from Metastasio onward supplied a rhetoric of entrances, exits and confrontations that composers internalised as musical grammar. The grammar migrated because the grammar was portable: an exit aria's decisive cadence and a symphony movement's closing group solve the same problem of ending a public utterance with authority.

The distinction between opera seria and opera buffa matters to the instrumental transfer, because the two genres contribute different tools. Seria contributes ceremonial scale, the da capo aria's architecture of statement, departure and decorated return, and a rhetoric of noble utterance whose gestures survive in slow movements and in ceremonial concerto ritornellos. Buffa contributes speed of exchange, ensembles of confusion, patter rhythms, sudden reversals and the finale technique of accumulating characters on stage until the texture itself becomes the plot. A listener who knows which parent a passage descends from can predict its behaviour: seria passages broaden and formalise, buffa passages accelerate and entangle. The symphonies and quartets draw on both inheritances, often within a single movement, and the mixture explains shifts of tone that puzzle listeners who expect stylistic unity to mean uniformity of manner.

Librettists and singers imposed constraints that sharpened the transferable skills. A text had to be projected in a theatre, which demanded syllabic clarity at speed and melodic emphasis at the words carrying the sense. A singer's range and agility fixed the shape of an aria as firmly as a clarinettist's extended instrument fixed the shape of a concerto. Working inside such constraints for two decades trained a reflex for writing to the measure of a specific human capacity while keeping the result singable by the imagination of anyone hearing it. The instrumental music inherits the reflex as its famous naturalness: phrases sized to breaths, climaxes placed where energy peaks, difficulties concentrated where display earns its keep. Naturalness, examined closely, is constraint remembered as freedom.

The theatre also supplied a professional ethic that the instrumental works inherit: the obligation to hold an audience whose attention is voluntary. A symphony performed between other items of a mixed concert, a quartet played in a room where conversation competed, an opera competing with the social life of the boxes, each had to earn its hearing minute by minute through variety of incident within unity of design. The famous variety inside movements, the new theme where a repeat would have sufficed, the wind colour arriving exactly where string fatigue threatened, the joke placed at the point of maximum formality, can all be read as an entertainer's discipline absorbed into high art without surrendering ambition. Analysis that treats such variety as pure structure misses its professional origin, while analysis that treats it as mere entertainment misses its structural yield. The organising skill holds both readings together, which is why the stage, rather than the church or the study, is named here as the school of the whole style.

## The Ensemble and Simultaneous Characterisation

The ensemble is the operatic device on which the whole article turns, and its mechanics deserve a full anatomy. In a duet, terzet or quartet of characters, the composer assigns each participant a melodic profile consistent with that character's dramatic position: the pleading line that rises and falls in supplication, the refusing line that repeats its figure with stubborn regularity, the scheming line that moves in short confiding phrases beneath the others. The profiles combine because their rhythms interlock rather than coincide, entrances answering entrances, sustained notes in one voice freeing another to move, and the harmony at each joint is chosen to keep every profile singable. The listener follows the combination not by tracking all lines equally, which exceeds anyone's attention, but by shifting focus as the writing directs it, and the direction is achieved by register, dynamics and the placement of rests.

Idomeneo, Le nozze di Figaro and Don Giovanni supply ensembles at rising levels of complexity, and the Figaro finales extend the principle from a scene to an architecture. A finale begins with two characters in a defined situation, admits a third whose arrival changes the alliances, then a fourth and fifth, until the stage holds a crowd whose members want incompatible things in compatible music. Each arrival is prepared harmonically, often by a modulation that behaves like a door opening, and the texture thickens without losing the thread because earlier material returns as the scaffolding for new combinations. The finales solve, in theatrical form, the exact problem a development section solves in a symphony: many agents, one argument, no confusion. The resemblance is not an analogy imposed by critics. The techniques are interchangeable, and sketches show material migrating between stage and concert projects in the same seasons.

Vocal writing inside the ensemble obeys a discipline of range separation. Characters of similar voice type are distinguished by rhythm and by orchestral doubling when their ranges overlap, while characters of different types occupy strata that keep their lines physically separable in the theatre's acoustic. Orchestral colour underlines identity: a character may carry a wind obbligato whose timbre becomes a badge, so that the instrument's return later in the opera announces the character's influence before the voice enters. Instrumental music adopts the badge principle directly, assigning a theme a characteristic scoring that persists through transformations, so that a listener recognises the returning agent by colour before recognising it by contour. Recognition by colour is faster than recognition by melody, and the speed is dramatically useful wherever an entrance must register instantly.

Failure in an ensemble is instructive because the period produced many ensembles that sprawl or blur, and the comparison isolates the governing skill. Weak ensembles assign every character the same rhythmic profile, so the combination becomes a chord with words. Competent ensembles differentiate profiles but lose harmonic direction, wandering through keys without an accumulating design. The ensembles examined here do neither: profiles stay distinct, the harmony pursues a large plan across the whole number, and cadences are graded so that false endings proliferate while the true ending, when it arrives, discharges the accumulated arrivals in a stretto of shared figuration. A crowd singing one rhythm at the close, after an entire scene of differentiated wanting, enacts reconciliation as a musical fact. The device will reappear, wordless, at the end of symphonic developments and fugal finales.

## Recitative and Pacing a Scene

Recitative, the speech paced delivery that carries plot between sung numbers, looks like the least musical element of opera and functions as its nervous system. Secco recitative, supported by continuo alone, moves at the speed of conversation, harmony punctuating sentences rather than governing them, and its craft lies in timing: the pause before a revelation, the hurried exchange of short phrases in a quarrel, the single sustained chord that lets a line of text land in silence. Accompanied recitative expands the resource, admitting orchestral gestures that illustrate, contradict or overwhelm the voice, and the great accompanied scenes, Donna Anna's narration, the Countess's meditations framed by arias, Idomeneo's confrontations with the divine, show pacing elevated into psychology. A character thinking aloud at orchestral scale is a spectacle the period invented and this composer perfected.

The pacing skill transfers into instrumental music at points listeners feel without naming. Slow introductions that begin with a unison gesture and then hesitate over chromatic harmony behave as accompanied recitative behaves, presenting a speaker gathering thought before the argument proper begins. Transitions inside concerto movements often strip the texture to a solo line over punctuating chords, the recitative texture exactly, and the passage's job is recitative's job: to move the drama from one settled state to another at the speed of thought. Even the fermata, the held pause that invites a cadenza or a lead in, descends from recitative practice, where the singer's held note over a cadential chord gave the continuo player and the audience a shared breath before the plot resumed.

Text setting inside recitative also trained melodic habits that surface in themes. Italian prosody, with its feminine endings and its patterns of accent, shaped the cadential figures and the placement of appoggiaturas across the output, so that instrumental phrases frequently scan like lines of verse even in works with no Italian connection. Eisen's documentary work on the composer's reading and copying habits placed Italian models at the centre of the training the Salzburg years provided, with opera scores studied, arranged and imitated from childhood. The transfer proposed in this article is therefore biographically grounded as well as analytically visible: the stage was the school, the scores were the textbooks, and the instrumental catalogue is the examination passed in public over twenty years.

Judging recitative in performance clarifies what pacing means as a transferable value. A secco passage delivered at uniform speed with mechanically equal chords sounds like information being read aloud, while the same passage shaped with rhetorical pauses, varied chord weight and attention to the sentence structure becomes theatre. The difference lies in treating harmony as punctuation rather than as accompaniment. Conductors and continuo players who grasp the principle transform scenes that weaker performances leave inert, and listeners can hear the transformation as a change in the temperature of attention inside a theatre. Instrumental performances benefit identically: a development's sequences shaped as sentences hold an audience, while the same sequences delivered as pattern alone let attention leak away. Pacing is meaning, in both media, at every scale.

The correspondences extend beyond the table's five rows, and two deserve prose emphasis. The first is the treatment of the slow movement as an aria without words, complete with the aria's obligations: a long breathed line, an accompaniment that behaves as an orchestra behaves beneath a singer, a middle section of intensification analogous to the aria's contrasting strain, and a return whose ornaments increase as a singer's ornaments increase on the da capo. The slow movements of the piano concertos, the Romanze of K. 466 and the Andante of K. 467 most famously, satisfy every clause of that description, and singers have adopted such movements as vocalises in every generation since the works appeared. The second correspondence is the minuet's transformation: a dance movement that admits a trio of contrasting character behaves as a miniature scene change, and the minuets of the late symphonies exploit the change of company with a dramatist's timing.

A caution belongs with the catalogue, because correspondences can harden into a parlour game in which every instrumental gesture is assigned a stage source and the music becomes an illustrated libretto without words. The claim defended here is narrower and stronger. Operatic practice supplied habits of pacing, characterisation and combination that the instrumental works deploy for purely musical ends, and the habits are demonstrable in the notes through rhythm, scoring and formal behaviour. Rosen insisted on that distinction throughout his writing on the classical style, treating dramatic analogies as descriptions of formal function rather than as hidden programmes. Keeping the distinction intact lets the listener use the stage as a hearing aid without mistaking the hearing aid for the music.

The present article cites works only as exhibits for techniques, and the distinction matters for the reader's map of the subject: craft explains how effects are made, while the works survey judges which effects, in which pieces, repay the closest study. Both maps describe the same territory from different altitudes, and using them together prevents the common error of mistaking a list of masterpieces for an understanding of mastery.


## Rhythm, Dance and the Social Body

Beneath the dramatic superstructure, rhythm keeps the body in the music, and the body's knowledge is social before it is musical. Minuet, contredanse, march, gavotte and the German dance supplied the period with a shared dictionary of steps whose metres and phrase lengths every listener had enacted in ballrooms. A theme that behaves as a minuet carries the minuet's implications of ceremony and measured desire, while a finale built as a contredanse promises communal release in quick repeated figures. Composers could invoke an entire social occasion with a rhythm alone, and the invocation worked because the audience's muscles remembered the steps. Instrumental rhythm in this output is therefore never abstract pattern. It is choreography remembered by listeners who may never have analysed a bar in their lives.

The social body also explains the music's characteristic ease of address across ranks of listeners. A contredanse tune could be whistled by a servant and danced by a count in the same week, and the tunes written for Vienna's public ballrooms during carnival season were designed for exactly that double life, catchy enough for immediate pleasure and crafted enough to reward the connoisseur's second hearing. The Redoutensaal dances and the sets of German dances composed for the imperial court balls place the craft at the service of collective movement, and their phrase discipline, four bar units locked to dance figures, feeds back into the symphonies as clarity of pulse. A listener who feels the urge to move during a finale is responding to engineering, not to accident.

Syncopation and hemiola disturb the social grid with intent. A sforzando landing off the beat behaves as a dancer's feint behaves, breaking the expected weight and forcing the body to recalculate, while hemiola, the temporary regrouping of six beats as two groups of three or three groups of two, tilts the floor of a minuet near its cadences so that arrival feels like a recovered balance. The D minor Concerto's opening builds its unrest from orchestral syncopations that deny the soloist a settled grid to enter upon, and the drama of the piano's calmer first statement depends on that denial. Rhythm, in such passages, is the medium in which power relations between soloist and orchestra are negotiated before any theme has been exchanged.

Tempo choice decides whether the social meanings survive performance. A minuet taken too slowly loses the step and becomes a meditation its phrases were not built to support, while a finale taken too fast turns communal release into scramble. The period's tempo indications, reinforced by dance practice, imply speeds at which one beat corresponds to a gesture a body can perform, and performers who anchor tempo in gesture recover rhetoric that metronomic literalism misses. Heartz placed dance at the centre of his account of the period's musical life for precisely this reason: the ballroom was the conservatory in which the audience's rhythmic expectations were trained, and the concert room collected the interest on that training every time a dance rhythm carried a dramatic argument.

## Orchestration and the Clarity Problem

Clarity is the orchestral virtue this writing pursues above richness, and clarity is a problem of information management. Every added doubling thickens the sound while thinning the listener's ability to separate the strands, so the scores ration doublings with unusual discipline: a melodic line is entrusted to one choir at a time, accompaniment figures are assigned patterns that occupy different rhythmic space from the melody, and the full ensemble is reserved for cadences, proclamations and moments where mass itself is the meaning. The result is a transparency that listeners describe as light, although the mechanism is better described as editing. Nothing competes with the event of the moment, because competing material has been scored down, rested or assigned a register where it cannot interfere.

Register separation performs much of the editing. Melodies in the middle register receive accompaniments placed above and below them rather than beside them, inner voices are thinned when a wind solo needs air, and bass lines are kept rhythmically simpler than the parts they support so that the floor reads as floor. The famous opening of the Eine kleine Nachtmusik serenade demonstrates the economy at miniature scale: a unison fanfare, an answering phrase with the lightest string support, and a texture in which every note's function can be named. Large scores extend the same economy rather than abandoning it, which is why the Jupiter Symphony's crowded finale remains intelligible. The crowd is large, and the traffic rules are strict.

Doubling returns as a deliberate effect at moments of public emphasis. A theme first stated by strings alone may return doubled in octaves by winds at a recapitulation, and the thickened line reads as the same thought spoken by a larger assembly. Flutes doubling violins at the octave add brilliance without weight, horns sustaining middle harmonies bind choirs together, and trumpets withheld for most of a movement make their eventual entrance a structural announcement. The discipline can be measured negatively in the scores of imitators, where constant doubling produces a porridge in which themes lose their edges. Clarity, the comparison shows, is not a property of small orchestras. It is a property of decisions, and the decisions remain visible on every page for a listener who learns to ask which instruments are silent and why.

Performance practice can betray the editing. String sections of romantic size overwhelm wind lines balanced for a handful of desks, modern halls with long reverberation smear the rhythmic separation between melody and accompaniment, and conductors who prize blend above articulation iron out the contrasts the scoring created. The period instrument movement, well established in scholarship and recording before the date of this article, demonstrated the alternative by restoring the proportions the scores assume: gut strings speaking quickly and decaying fast enough to leave air around wind entries, natural horns and trumpets whose limits the writing exploits, timpani struck with hard sticks so that rhythmic punctuation stays dry. Hearing a familiar passage in those proportions often feels like a first hearing, because the clarity problem has been solved at the level of equipment rather than merely of intention.

## The String Quartet and Writing for Equals

The string quartet strips the operatic machinery to four voices and reveals how much of it survives without voices, winds or mass. Two violins, viola and cello constitute a company of equals in which any member may carry the theme, supply the floor or comment from the side, and the conversation among equals became the period's favoured image for the genre. Haydn's example created the medium's modern form, and the set of six quartets dedicated to Haydn in 1785 represents the younger composer's public examination in it, published with a dedication that frames the works as children entrusted to a father's judgement. The dedication is a biographical gesture, yet its craft meaning is precise: the quartets claim membership in a tradition of learned conversation, and their pages prove the claim through the distribution of interest among the four parts.

Equality is engineered through motive sharing. A theme stated by the first violin returns in the cello beneath a new counterline from its original owner, the viola receives the fragments that bind a development together, and accompaniment duties rotate so that no player serves a whole movement as furniture. The D minor Quartet K. 421 and the C major Quartet K. 465, whose introduction's chromatic groping earned it the nickname Dissonance, show the range of the conversation from severity to wit. In the K. 465 introduction the four voices enter one by one on notes that contradict the home key being established, and the listener hears four individuals testing a dark room before the Allegro's clear theme switches on the light. The effect depends on equality: a hierarchy would assign the confusion to decoration, while equals make it a shared experience the movement must resolve together.

The quartet also exposes the limits of the operatic analogy, and the limits refine the thesis. Four stringed instruments share a family timbre, so character cannot be assigned by colour as reliably as in an opera ensemble or an orchestral score. Character must instead be created by register, rhythm and function, and the writing accepts the discipline, differentiating the cello's tenor singing from the first violin's brilliance and the viola's inner commentary by behaviour rather than by costume. The lesson travels back to the orchestral works: colour assists characterisation there, yet behaviour defines it, which is why themes remain recognisable when rescored, transcribed for piano or reduced for domestic performance. Identity lives in conduct, and conduct survives costume changes.

Domestic performance shaped the genre's rhetoric in ways worth recovering. Quartets were published for a market of skilled amateurs who played them in rooms where the performers were also the audience, and the writing balances display with sociability accordingly: difficulties concentrate in passages where a capable amateur feels the stretch as pleasure, ensemble traps are placed where friends will laugh and repeat the bar, and learned counterpoint arrives dressed as entertainment. A listener in a concert hall hears the finished surface. A player in the room hears the negotiation that produced it, and the double address, public polish over private negotiation, is the same double address the concertos achieve between soloist and crowd. The quartet is the concerto's drama with the crowd removed and the negotiation left exposed.

First movements of the quartets also revise the sonata contract in a chamber accent. Developments in the Haydn set quartets lean on combination and imitation more heavily than symphonic developments of the same years, because four stringed voices can sustain contrapuntal argument without any change of colour to renew attention, and the necessity becomes a virtue that Haydn himself acknowledged in his reported praise of the set. Minuets in the quartets behave as character scenes in miniature, their trios admitting rustic drones, learned canons or sudden minor key shadows that comment on the dance's courtly surface. A listener following the quartets in sequence watches the ensemble lesson of the operas being relearned under the strictest economy the composer ever accepted, four voices, one family of sound, no scenery, and the completeness of the drama under those conditions is the strongest single proof that character in this music is made by conduct rather than by costume.

## The Quintets and the Extra Voice

Adding a second viola to the quartet changes the company's politics in ways a listener can hear within bars. The string quintets, especially the C major K. 515 and the G minor K. 516, gain a middle register of unusual density, because two violas can sustain inner harmony, divide into dialogue or join the cello to create a dark choir beneath a single violin line. The extra voice also frees the first viola from pure accompaniment into a second protagonist, and the quintets exploit the freedom by staging exchanges between the two violins above and the two violas below, with the cello arbitrating. Ensembles of equals depend on balance, and the fifth voice tilts the balance toward warmth and shadow, which is why the quintets often feel more confessional than the quartets even when their forms match movement for movement.

The G minor Quintet's Adagio, in which muted strings sustain a conversation of fragments over a pulsing accompaniment, shows the extra voice used as a counsellor rather than as reinforcement. Phrases begin in one instrument and are completed in another of a different register, so that thoughts seem to pass between minds before they are fully formed. The technique descends from operatic duet writing, where lovers or conspirators finish one another's phrases, and its appearance in a wordless slow movement for five strings demonstrates how thoroughly the stage habit had become a chamber instinct. A listener tracking who completes each phrase receives a map of intimacies that no analysis of harmony alone would supply.

Here a third question must be opened, in a section whose subject seems far from its answer. Did he really write without correcting? The quintets raise the question because their surfaces feel inevitable, every completion placed as though no alternative had ever existed, and inevitability of surface nourishes the legend of effortless creation. The legend will be tested in the section on composing speed and the sketches, where the physical evidence of drafting survives. Until that evidence is examined, the question stays open deliberately, and the polish of works such as these should be heard as a claim awaiting cross examination rather than as proof of anything about how the pages were produced.

The quintet medium also clarifies the composer's preferences among string colours. The viola's register, neither brilliant above nor foundational below, carries the music's interior life in symphonies and operas alike, and doubling the instrument doubles the interior. Cello writing in the quintets gains freedom in compensation, singing in the tenor register for long spans because the second viola can hold the bass of the harmony with the cello's support only at cadences. The redistribution shows a mind thinking in functions rather than in fixed roles: any voice may carry any duty the moment requires, provided the company's balance survives the exchange. That functional thinking, learned in ensembles where a character's role could invert within a scene, is as close as analysis can come to a single sentence summary of the style. The catalogue of works assigns the quintets their place among the compositions at large, while the present article claims only the lesson their extra voice teaches about how the whole output is organised.


## The Encounter with Bach and Handel Scores

A datable encounter changed the grain of the writing, and the shareable fact of this article belongs here in full. Mozart's counterpoint changes after 1782, when he began studying Bach and Handel scores at Baron van Swieten's Sunday gatherings in Vienna. The mature fugal writing has a date of origin. Van Swieten, the imperial court librarian and a diplomat who had served in Berlin, collected manuscripts of the Well Tempered Clavier and of Handel's oratorios, and his Sunday musicales assembled professional players and serious amateurs to perform the repertory at a time when Bach's keyboard works circulated in manuscript among connoisseurs rather than in print for the public. The younger composer attended, copied, arranged and absorbed, and the absorption shows in the documents: arrangements of Bach fugues for string trio and quartet survive from the period, made as exercises in inhabiting another mind's voice leading from the inside.

The encounter's timing explains its force. By 1782 the composer had settled in Vienna, left Salzburg employment and was building a freelance career on piano concertos and opera, genres that rewarded fluency and brilliance more than learning. Bach's counterpoint presented a competing excellence, a music in which expression arose from the obligations lines owe one another, and the challenge arrived precisely when a maturing artist could meet it as an equal rather than as a pupil. The arrangements for strings are the laboratory record of the meeting. Transcribing a keyboard fugue for trio forces decisions about which voice can rest, which line needs reinforcement and how spacing changes meaning, and each decision teaches the transcriber where the original's strength resides. Learning by reassigning voices is learning by conducting the argument oneself.

Handel contributed a different lesson from the same shelves. The oratorio choruses demonstrated counterpoint at public scale, fugal entries deployed as crowd rhetoric in which learned combination produces collective emotion rather than private intricacy. The lesson mattered for a composer whose operas already used choruses sparingly by Handelian standards, and it mattered equally for instrumental music, because it proved that fugal technique could carry festivity and proclamation as well as meditation. Eisen's documentary research placed the van Swieten circle at the centre of the Viennese Bach and Handel revival, tracing loans of manuscripts, copying payments and the baron's own advocacy in prefaces to performances. The revival was a social fact before it was a stylistic one, and the style changed because a room full of musicians spent Sundays proving that old counterpoint still lived.

An earlier encounter must be distinguished from the decisive one, or the chronology blurs. The boy had met the music of Johann Christian Bach in London and absorbed its galant songfulness permanently, and Salzburg training included fugal exercises in the manner of Fux's Gradus, whose species counterpoint disciplined generations of Austrian composers. Fugue, as a school exercise, was therefore never foreign. What 1782 added was fugue as expression: Bach's keyboard fugues and Handel's choruses treated as living repertory by adult connoisseurs, studied in scores rather than summarised in rules. The distinction between knowing a technique and being changed by its masterpieces is the distinction the following section measures, because the measurement, unusually for stylistic claims, can be made concrete in themes, textures and formal placements before and after the year in question. A fuller comparison with the older master's own procedures appears in [the companion study of Bach's musical style](/2010/08/21/johann-sebastian-bach-musical-style-explained/), where imitation and invertible writing receive the extended treatment they demand.

## What Changed in His Counterpoint Afterwards

The fifth revelation can now be stated as a measurable claim. His counterpoint changes measurably after 1782, which means the mature style has a datable cause. Before the van Swieten Sundays, fugal writing in the output appears chiefly where convention required it: church movements observing the stile antico, finales that borrow learned colour for a closing flourish, exercises whose competence exceeds their expressive ambition. After 1782 the devices migrate into the centre of secular instrumental argument. Subjects are designed for combination from their first bar, developments deploy stretto and inversion as climactic weapons, and entire finales are constructed as negotiations between fugal procedure and sonata or rondo forms that neither procedure could achieve alone.

Measurability has several faces. The density of independent entries inside a single movement increases, so that a listener counting thematic statements in the finale of the C major Quartet K. 387, completed at the end of 1782, encounters fugal expositions embedded within a sonata design whose development returns to combination as its main business. The treatment of dissonance alters as well: suspensions prepared and resolved across barlines, a Bach habit, replace the galant practice of resolving friction within the beat, lengthening the spans over which tension can accumulate. Subjects acquire countersubjects designed to fit above and below them, and the fit is tested in the music itself, the pair returning inverted at later stages as a demonstration the listener can audit. Each face of the change can be pointed to in a score and heard in a performance, which is what separates a datable cause from a vague narrative of deepening maturity.

The C minor Mass K. 427 and the keyboard Suite K. 399, both from the encounter's immediate aftermath, show the new learning addressing old genres with new ambition, while the Fantasia in C minor K. 475 and its companion sonata translate contrapuntal severity into keyboard rhetoric of an almost orchestral darkness. Later, the Requiem's fugal movements and the Masonic works deploy the stile antico as a chosen archaism whose gravity depends on the listener recognising the style as old and therefore as authoritative. Archaism of that kind is a sophisticated resource: the composer quotes the past the way an orator quotes precedent, and the quotation persuades because the audience shares the reference. Van Swieten's Sundays had manufactured precisely that shared reference among Vienna's musical public, so the resource and its audience were created by the same gatherings.

Resistance to the change should be recorded, because a style absorbs influence selectively or not at all. The galant surface never disappears: melodies remain singable, phrases remain balanced, and the learned devices are rationed so that a movement's argument, not its erudition, stays in the foreground. Rosen characterised the achievement as a synthesis in which counterpoint regained dramatic function after decades of serving as churchly habit or academic display, and the characterisation holds because the fugues in this output argue. They begin in disagreement among voices, intensify through combination and resolve through settlement, following the same dramatic curve as the opera ensembles from which the article began. The circle closes: the oldest technique in the catalogue behaves, in the mature works, exactly like the newest social form of the theatre.

A further measurement concerns where the learned writing chooses to live inside movements. Early fugal passages tend to occupy self contained blocks, a fugal section announced and then discharged before the movement resumes its galant business. The mature writing interleaves the manners bar by bar, a homophonic phrase answered by an imitative one, a cadence achieved through a stretto, a rondo refrain returning in contrapuntal dress, so that the listener can no longer say where display ends and argument begins. Interleaving is the technical signature of absorption, distinguishable in any score from the block construction that precedes it, and its arrival in the quartets of late 1782 and the works that follow gives the fifth revelation a second, independent witness beyond the density counts and dissonance spans already described.

## The Fugal Finales and the Synthesis

Finales concentrate the synthesis because the last movement carries the obligation to discharge a whole work's accumulated energy convincingly. A fugal finale answers the obligation with accumulation by design: entries succeed one another until the texture reaches a density that feels like a crowd arriving, after which the composer may thin the texture for contrast or drive the entries into stretto for a climax. The finale of the Symphony No. 41 in C major, the Jupiter, builds its coda by combining five distinct motifs in invertible counterpoint, each motif having been presented separately earlier in the movement, so that the final pages deliver the pleasure of recognition multiplied by combination. The passage is celebrated because the learning is audible as joy. No listener needs to identify the technique to feel the arrival of everyone at once, and every listener who does identify it feels the arrival twice.

The string quartet finales of the Haydn set pursue the same synthesis at chamber scale, where the combination must be achieved by four players whose every entry is exposed. K. 387's finale alternates fugal writing with homophonic episodes in a design that behaves as a debate between learning and sociability, each manner commenting on the other until the closing pages reconcile them in a texture that is contrapuntal in fact and conversational in effect. The Haffner Symphony's finale and the Linz Symphony's Presto deploy fugato energy as brilliance, entries flashing between choirs in a manner closer to concerto exchange than to churchly precedent. The variety of uses matters: fugue in the mature output is a flexible rhetoric, capable of gravity, wit, brilliance and celebration, and the flexibility is the measure of how completely the 1782 encounter had been digested into instinct.

Synthesis also governs movements that never become fugal in form. Development sections across the late symphonies borrow the fugal devices of stretto and inversion for their climaxes and then return to sonata behaviour for the settlement, so that counterpoint functions as an intensifier inside a dramatic plan rather than as an alternative to one. Overtures behave similarly, the Don Giovanni overture reserving its most concentrated imitation for the passage that must convert the introduction's terror into the opera's comic energy. Even the piano concertos admit the resource, the finale of K. 453 and passages of K. 491 combining themes with a freedom that presupposes listeners able to follow two ideas at once and enjoy the feat. The article's thesis receives here its strongest confirmation: simultaneous characterisation, first met in the opera ensemble, governs the most learned instrumental textures as completely as it governs the stage.

A limit should be stated plainly so that the synthesis claim stays honest. The composer never adopted Bach's largest architectures, wrote no equivalent of the great independent organ fugues and allowed dance, song and theatre to interrupt learning whenever a movement's drama required relief. The synthesis is therefore a hierarchy with drama at its head and counterpoint as its strongest servant, not a conversion to the older master's world. Analysts who reverse the hierarchy misread the Jupiter finale as an academic demonstration and then wonder why audiences cheer it. Audiences cheer because the combinations enact a gathering, and gathering is a theatrical event. The servant, however learned, still serves the play, and the play remains the organising skill the article identified in the opera sections.

## The Church Music and Its Constraints

Church composition supplied the training ground where counterpoint was a professional obligation before it became an expressive choice, and the constraints of liturgical employment shaped habits that secular works later spent freely. Salzburg service under Archbishop Colloredo imposed brevity on Mass settings, the famous requirement that a complete Mass with its full apparatus fit inside a short span, and brevity trained economy: themes sized to their function, transitions compressed, learned display rationed to the few movements where the liturgy allowed expansion. The missa brevis settings from the Salzburg years demonstrate the economy as a positive art, their movements achieving completeness at durations that force every device to justify its place within bars of its entrance.

The stile antico, the deliberate cultivation of Palestrina descended counterpoint for sacred texts, functioned inside the church catalogue as a dialect of authority. Fugal Kyries and Cum Sancto Spiritu movements observe the dialect's rules of dissonance and its vocal spacing even when instruments double the voices, and the observance connects the youngest professional works to the Fuxian exercises of training. What the secular catalogue later quoted as archaism was, in the church works, simply the house style of the institution, and recognising the continuity prevents a common misreading in which the late sacred severity appears as a deathbed conversion of manner. The manner was native. The late works differ in scale and in the freedom with which the native dialect is mixed with operatic and symphonic resources, not in allegiance.

Colloredo's reforms also restricted instrumentation and the use of independent wind display in church, which pushed invention toward vocal combination and string writing of unusual refinement. The Vespers settings and the C minor Mass, the latter a votive work composed outside the Salzburg constraints for Viennese performance, show what happened when the economy met freedom: movements expand to operatic dimensions, soprano solos adopt the profile of concert arias while remaining liturgical in function, and choruses deploy Handel's public counterpoint at a scale Salzburg never permitted. The C minor Mass stands unfinished, a fact reserved for the following sections on abandonment, yet its completed movements suffice to show the church style achieving the synthesis the instrumental works pursue: learned choral writing, solo characterisation and orchestral drama inside a single design.

Hearing the church music sharpens the ear for the whole output, because the constraints make the mechanisms visible. A short Salzburg Mass movement cannot hide its phrase structure behind abundance, and a fugal church finale must achieve its climax within a liturgical minute, compressing entries and strettos into spans where every combination counts. Listeners who begin with the concertos and symphonies often find the church works plain. Listeners who begin with the church works hear the concertos as magnificent expansions of decisions the small forms make nakedly. Either direction of travel serves the article's purpose, which is to make decisions audible, and the church catalogue, sized to obligation, may be the clearest classroom the composer ever built for his students of style.


## Composing Speed and What the Sketches Show

### Did Mozart compose without making corrections?

Mozart did not compose without corrections. Surviving sketches, drafts, crossed out passages and abandoned fragments show themes being tested, passages rewritten and movements discarded. Fair copies were often written quickly because the composing had already happened in sketches, at the keyboard and in memory, which are different claims.

The open loop from the quintets section closes here, and the sixth revelation lands with it. The surviving sketches and corrections show drafting, revision and abandonment, which demolishes the legend of music arriving finished. Did he really write without correcting? The documentary answer is no, and the evidence is physical: sketch leaves preserved among the autographs show opening themes tried in several shapes, developments blocked out in shorthand and abandoned when the proportions failed, and completed scores bearing corrections in the composer's hand where a passage was recomposed after the fair copy had begun. Eisen, whose editorial work on the documents and the correspondence established the modern picture of the workshop, catalogued the categories of surviving draft material across the catalogue and showed that sketching intensifies precisely in the genres the legend calls most effortless, the operas and the late instrumental works.

Speed and correction must be separated, because the legend feeds on their confusion. Writing out a fair copy quickly is a copyist's skill joined to a memory trained by performance, and the correspondence documents many occasions when a completed movement was committed to paper at great speed because its composition, in sketch, at the keyboard and in the head during walks and billiards and travel, had already consumed days. Composing quickly is a different claim, and the evidence supports it only in the qualified sense that a master of a shared grammar, working with themes tested at the keyboard for specific players, could draft conventional movements faster than a lesser musician could draft anything. The two claims have different evidence, different meanings and different implications for how the music should be judged, and collapsing them manufactures a miracle where the record shows a method.

The method can be reconstructed in outline. Themes were tested singingly and at the keyboard, where the hand's knowledge of figuration could veto a shape the imagination had proposed. Structural plans were sometimes sketched as bass lines with figures, the partimento habit of the training years persisting into maturity as a way of fixing the harmonic floor before decorating it. Scoring was frequently the last stage, with a particella or short score holding the essential lines while wind details awaited the fair copy, which explains both the speed of the final writing and the occasional incompleteness of works left at the sketch stage when circumstances intervened. Portrait painters of genius invent a figure in a flash. Workshop masters, which the documents reveal, build the flash into a process whose stages can be dated, priced and verified.

Certain famous anecdotes fail the evidence test and must be handled as anecdotes. The letter describing composition as a process of hearing a finished work entire in the imagination, quoted in biographies for two centuries, survives only in a version published after the composer's death whose authenticity scholars have disputed on documentary grounds, and no autograph of it exists. The overture copied on the night before a premiere, the concerto performed from a part the soloist had not finished writing, belong to a family of deadline stories whose cores are documented and whose details swell with retelling. A responsible account keeps the documented core, speed of fair copying under professional deadline pressure, and declines the swelling. The music loses nothing by the declension. Craft that survives audit is more impressive than magic that requires it.

## Revision, Abandonment and the Unfinished Works

Revision operated at every scale, from the single corrected note to the recomposed movement, and its most instructive examples are the works revised for new circumstances. The Haffner Symphony began as a serenade whose movements were selected, rescored and supplemented for concert use, a recycling that reveals how flexibly material could migrate between functions. The G minor Symphony K. 550 exists in two versions, the second adding clarinets and reworking the wind parts throughout, and comparing the versions shows revision as reconsideration of colour and balance rather than as correction of error: the first version is complete and performable, the second reimagines its sonority for a different ensemble and perhaps a different room. A composer who revised in that spirit treated a finished score as a strong draft of an idea that new circumstances could legitimately reopen.

Abandonment is revision's negative image, and the catalogue preserves abandoned movements, incomplete concertos, opera projects dropped after substantial drafting and fragments whose quality forbids the explanation that they were left because they failed. Commissions lapsed, singers departed, librettists stalled, payment failed to arrive and a theatre's season changed, and the workshop record shows projects suspended for reasons of business far more often than for reasons of inspiration. The C minor Mass lacks its completion because the occasion and the circumstances that sustained its composition dissolved, not because its completed movements exhausted the design. Understanding abandonment as an economic event protects the listener from a romantic error, the assumption that every fragment conceals a tragedy of perfectionism, when the documents usually reveal a calendar and a ledger instead.

The Requiem's incompleteness belongs to biography and to the circumstances of the final illness rather than to any habit of abandonment, and its completion by Franz Xaver Süssmayr from sketches, drafts and reported instruction raises the workshop questions in their sharpest form: which passages survive in the composer's hand, which in sketch, which in a pupil's realisation guided by materials no longer extant. Scholars working before the date of this article, examining the autograph and the copyists' hands, separated those layers with increasing precision, and performances differ according to the edition chosen. For the student of style the case teaches a final lesson about evidence. Attribution is a gradient, documents decide what can be decided, and honest listening admits the gradient instead of demanding a certainty the archive cannot supply.

Fragments also serve performers and listeners as workshop exhibits of unusual candour. A movement abandoned after eighty bars shows a theme's potential being tested against its development in real time, and the point where the drafting stops often coincides with the point where the material's implications turned unmanageable or uninteresting, which is information no finished work can provide about its maker's judgement. The surviving fragments for piano, for strings and for opera have been catalogued, edited and in some cases completed by later hands for performance, and their study belongs to any serious account of the style because they reveal the road system behind the finished cities. Inevitability, the quintets' surface suggested, is an achievement built on discarded alternatives, and the discards, fortunately for the truth of the matter, were not all burned.

## The Too Many Notes Story and What It Is Worth

The exchange in which Emperor Joseph II is said to have greeted Die Entführung aus dem Serail with a complaint about too many notes, and the composer is said to have replied that the work contained exactly as many notes as were necessary, must be assessed before it is used, and the assessment is brief. The story survives through a late secondhand source, published decades after the alleged conversation by a writer reporting what others remembered, and no contemporary letter, diary or court record confirms it. The brief for this article states the verdict that the evidence supports: the exchange is almost certainly apocryphal, and it is worth nothing as evidence about the opera, the emperor or the composer's opinions. Its value lies elsewhere, in what its persistence reveals about the needs of posterity.

Posterity needs the story because it dramatises a real tension in portable form. Viennese taste did divide between listeners who prized the new density of the German operas and the concertos and listeners who experienced that density as excess, and the division is documented in reviews, in Salieri era polemics and in the composer's own letters reporting factions at premieres. A single quotable exchange gives the documented division a hero, a foil and a punchline, and memory prefers the exchange to the archive. Resisting that preference is the discipline this article has practised at every stage: the named scholar over the floating anecdote, the dated document over the deathless quotation, the mechanism over the myth. The discipline costs colour and buys truth, and the purchase is the correct one wherever the two conflict.

The emperor of the record is a more interesting figure than the emperor of the anecdote. Joseph II founded the German Singspiel company that commissioned Die Entführung, employed the composer for court dances and chamber works, set the institutional terms under which the Da Ponte operas reached the Burgtheater and appears in the correspondence as a connoisseur whose judgements, favourable and critical alike, carried professional consequences. Compressing that documented relationship into a joke about arithmetic insults both parties and obscures the institutional history that made the operas possible. Heartz placed imperial policy at the centre of his account of Viennese opera in the period for exactly this reason: stages are built by decisions about money, language and censorship before they are graced by masterpieces, and a style history that ignores the building mistakes decoration for architecture.

What, then, should a listener do with the density the anecdote gestures toward? Count functions rather than notes. A passage feels crowded when several events compete for the same attention without a hierarchy, and it feels rich when many events arrange themselves into foreground, middle ground and floor. The Entführung score and the mature concertos feel rich at densities that crush imitators into crowding, because the hierarchy analysed in the orchestration sections governs every bar. The emperor, had he attended with the hierarchy in mind, would have found the arithmetic exact. The reply attributed to the composer survives because it says, in legend's shorthand, what analysis confirms at length: the notes are as many as the drama requires, and the requirement can be audited bar by bar by any listener trained to hear who wants what, and when, and against whom.

## Writing for Specific Performers

Tailoring to individual performers is a craft discipline that runs through the catalogue, and its traces are legible in the music itself. A singer's range, agility, breath and dramatic temperament shape an aria's tessitura, its placement of climaxes and the distribution of its coloratura, so that the great roles preserve the measurements of the voices that first sang them as a tailored garment preserves a figure. Caterina Cavalieri's agility is written into the roles created for her, Aloysia Weber's exceptional range challenged the young composer in concert arias that remain tests of the soprano instrument, and Nancy Storace's comic intelligence shapes Susanna's music in Figaro, where characterization through quicksilver phrasing replaces display as the measure of a singing actor. The roles outlived the voices because the measurements were taken from human capacities pushed to eloquent limits, and later singers inherit both the opportunity and the examination.

Instrumental tailoring followed the same logic with the same permanence. The piano concertos fit the composer's own hands and habits first, their figuration lying where his technique was strongest, and the works written for pupils and colleagues adjust the balance between display and lyricism to the recipient's powers. The horn concertos for Joseph Leutgeb exploit the natural horn's available notes with wit that presupposes a player of complete security, the jokes in the autographs confirming a friendship inside the professionalism, while the clarinet works for Stadler, examined in their own section, rebuilt the genre around an extended instrument that one man owned. Even the orchestral parts respect known players: the Mannheim orchestra's famous discipline shaped the Paris Symphony's effects, and Viennese wind principals received solos calibrated to reputations the composer had heard in person.

Tailoring carried risks that the letters occasionally record. A singer who demanded alterations could bend an aria away from its dramatic function, a soloist who failed to learn a part could shrink a premiere, and a patron's taste could commission a genre the composer valued less than the fee required. The workshop solution visible across the catalogue is negotiation preserved in variants: alternative arias for different singers and revivals, substitute movements for concertos, insert numbers for other composers' operas that demonstrate professional courtesy and compositional opportunism in equal measure. Variants are therefore documents of social life and of style alike, and editions that print them restore a dimension of the craft that the single authoritative text conceals. The style, examined at this resolution, includes diplomacy among its techniques.

A listener can learn to hear tailoring as clearly as any other mechanism. Passages where a voice or instrument is granted exactly the figure it performs best, repeated with variation until the gift is unmistakable, alternate with passages of deliberate neutrality where the argument belongs to the ensemble. In the concertos the soloist's first entrance often presents material of a simplicity that establishes the character before any display begins, because character, once fixed in the ear, converts later display into speech. The principle unifies the article's subjects at a final level: themes behave as characters because they were so often written for characters, for specific throats, hands and temperaments whose measures the composer held in memory while he worked. Abstraction, in this output, is biography that has been generalised by genius of craft, and the generalisation never quite erases the original measurements.

## Five Things a New Listener Can Learn to Hear

A method is worth more than a list of masterpieces, so this section distils the article into five hearings that any listener can practise in order, each one building the attention the next requires. The first hearing is the answering unit: choose any opening theme and mark where its small question ends and its answer begins, then notice how the answer alters the ending. The second hearing is the floor: in the same movement, ignore the melody for a full minute and track only when the harmony changes ground, feeling the rarity and weight of each shift. The third hearing is the second subject's arrival: identify the moment the music settles in its contrasting key and ask what manner of character has entered, in what colour, and with what degree of confidence.

The fourth hearing is the wind conversation: select a slow movement and follow one wind instrument through it, noting every entrance, every answer given to the strings and every passage where the chosen instrument mediates between the soloist and the ensemble. The fifth hearing is the return: in a movement whose exposition has been repeated, listen to the recapitulation solely for differences, the rewritten transition, the rescored theme, the extended or shortened phrase, and let the differences narrate what the development has done to the material. Five hearings, practised across a month of listening, will convert the vocabulary of this article from description into perception, which is the only conversion that matters. A listener who completes them owns the style in the ear, and ownership in the ear precedes every other kind of musical knowledge worth having.

Supporting habits multiply the value of the five hearings. Attend the same work twice in a season with different performers and compare the handling of a single pause, cadence or wind entry, since interpretation reveals structure by varying it. Read the letters alongside the listening, in the translations Eisen edited, because the workshop voice in the correspondence corrects the marble statue of posterity more effectively than any criticism. Keep a listening journal in whatever form survives contact with habit, noting the movement, the hearing practised and the single event that surprised the ear. Programmes and ticket stubs pasted beside the entries will matter less than the sentences, and the sentences, reread after a year, will measure the growth of the listener more honestly than any examination.

Formal study extends the method for those who want it, and the order of study matters. Begin with the quartets and the piano sonatas, where the mechanisms are exposed at small scale, proceed to the concertos for the drama of the individual and the world, and reserve the late symphonies and the great operas for the stage at which every earlier hearing can be practised simultaneously. Singers should study the ensembles before the arias, pianists the slow movements before the finales, and all listeners should return to a work that defeated them after an interval spent with its neighbours, since the style teaches itself across the catalogue by recurrence with variation. The works themselves, ranked and weighed as compositions, are the business of the works survey already linked in these pages, and the reader who wants a guided sequence through the catalogue will find the itinerary there.

Enthusiasm should be rationed honestly at the end of a method. Not every movement rewards every hearing equally, the juvenilia contain routine pages beside prophetic ones, and even the mature catalogue includes occasional works whose commissions constrained them below the level of the best. Discrimination is part of the method rather than a betrayal of it, because a listener who must praise everything hears nothing in particular. The five hearings protect against that flattening by attaching admiration to events that can be pointed to and defended. Praise with a mechanism attached is criticism. Praise without one is weather, pleasant or unpleasant and equally uninformative, and the style examined across thirty three sections deserves the first kind from everyone who claims to love it.

## Why the Music Sounds Easy and Is Not

Ease is the final illusion the style constructs, and its construction can now be itemised from the evidence of the preceding sections. Melodies built from answering units feel inevitable because each unit completes a small obligation the ear can verify. Balance feels natural because proportions learned from dance and verse govern phrases until deliberate breakage renews attention. Harmony feels calm because the floor moves slowly and decisively beneath a busy surface. Orchestration feels light because editing removes every event that would compete with the event of the moment. Counterpoint, after 1782, feels joyful because combination is staged as gathering rather than as examination. Each effect is manufactured, the manufacture is documented in sketches and revisions, and the finished surface conceals the workshop as thoroughly as a great actor conceals technique inside a character.

Performance exposes the difficulty that listening conceals. The exposed wind entries forgive no tuning compromise, the Alberti patterns expose any unevenness of touch across an entire movement, the ensemble writing in the operas demands that singers act, count and blend at speeds that leave no margin for recovery, and the quartets distribute their traps democratically so that the weakest player in the room sets the ceiling for four reputations. Teachers assign the sonatas to students early because the notes are few, and then spend years correcting the phrasing, the balance and the rhetoric that the few notes require. Is the music easy to play? The notes often are. The music, as the preceding sections have defined music, is among the most demanding in the repertory, because nothing in it can be hidden behind volume, speed or mass.

Comparison sharpens the verdict without diminishing the neighbour. Beethoven, whose style the next cluster of this series examines, built a rhetoric of struggle in which difficulty is displayed as part of the meaning, themes wrestled into form before the listener's ears. The contrast is real and the kinship is equally real, since the younger composer studied these scores, copied passages and inherited the second subject's drama, the development's argument and the recapitulation's settlement as the grammar his own rhetoric would strain against. Hearing both grammars clearly, the conversational and the wrestling, is the reward for the attention this article has demanded, and [the companion account of Beethoven's musical style](/2011/01/21/ludwig-van-beethoven-musical-style-explained/) takes up the second grammar where the first leaves off. Neither grammar is a refutation of the other. They are two answers, separated by a generation, to the question of how instrumental music can mean.

The article closes where it opened, with two voices wanting opposite things and a listener understanding both. That understanding, effortless in the theatre seat, is the product of answering units, balanced and broken phrases, a slow harmonic floor, chromatic shadows rationed for turning points, sonata processes staged as negotiations between characters, concertos built as encounters between a person and a world, winds promoted from filler to dramatis personae, dances remembered in the muscles, counterpoint absorbed from old masters on Sunday afternoons in 1782 and thereafter deployed as joy, and a workshop of sketches and corrections concealed beneath the finished page. Mozart musical style, named as a whole at the end of the enquiry, is operatic logic become instrumental habit, and a listener who has learned to hear the logic owns a key that opens not only this catalogue but the century that followed it, where every composer of ambition had first to answer the ease that is not easy.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What are the features of Mozart's style?

The features combine melody built from short answering units, balanced phrases that are broken deliberately for effect, a slow moving harmonic floor beneath a lively surface, and themes that behave as characters inside sonata form. Wind instruments act as independent voices rather than filler, chromatic notes are rationed for moments of shadow, rhythm draws on dance, and counterpoint absorbed after 1782 supplies learned combination for climaxes and finales. Opera organises the whole: pacing, characterisation and simultaneous singing in ensembles migrate into symphonies, concertos and quartets. The surface sounds easy because every mechanism is edited until nothing competes with the event of the moment. A listener who learns to separate those mechanisms, following the floor, the answers inside a melody and the conversations among winds, hears the style as a set of decisions rather than as a perfume, and the decisions can be practised one hearing at a time.

### Q: What is a second subject in sonata form?

A second subject is the contrasting theme group presented in a new key during the exposition of a sonata form movement, reached after a transition has loosened the home key. It usually differs from the first subject in melody, rhythm, scoring and mood, sounding lyrical where the first subject was assertive or hesitant where the first was confident. In dramatic terms it behaves as a second character, a citizen of a rival key whose material the recapitulation must bring home into the tonic. Hearing a movement means tracking what happens to that character through the development and recognising the small adjustments that mark its change of allegiance on its return. The transition that originally escaped the home key must be recomposed so the same theme can stay at home, and those rewritten joins often contain the most ingenious bars of the movement.

### Q: How does a development section work?

A development section works by taking themes from the exposition apart and testing them under pressure. Fragments, sometimes only three or four notes, pass between instruments, repeat in sequences that travel through distant keys, combine with detached accompaniments and collide in stretto, where entries pile up before earlier statements finish. Dynamics swing between whispered exchanges and full ensemble protest, and the harmonic floor moves faster than anywhere else in the movement. The section usually ends standing on the dominant, a held and expectant passage that aims the scattered argument at the recapitulation. Following one small fragment throughout gives a listener a reliable thread through the argument. Length is a clue to meaning as well: a brief development suggests confidence that the exposition stated its tensions fully, while a long one signals conflict the presentation had concealed.

### Q: Why does Mozart use the clarinet so often?

Mozart uses the clarinet so often because Vienna gave him players and instruments that rewarded the attention. Anton Stadler, for whom the Clarinet Quintet and the Clarinet Concerto were written, played an extended instrument descending below the standard range, opening a low chalumeau register of veiled and intimate colour. Above it the clarion register sings with rounded clarity that carries over an orchestra and partners a soprano voice or a piano naturally. The instrument can therefore whisper, confess and shine inside a single movement, changing character as it crosses registers. Basset horns extend the same palette into choral and ceremonial writing, giving wind sections a gravity no other family supplied. Frequency in the catalogue therefore records an artistic judgement about one instrument's expressive range, confirmed by players, makers and audiences across the Vienna years.

### Q: How does opera technique appear in symphonies?

Opera technique appears in symphonies through pacing, characterisation and combination. Themes behave as characters with wants, the second subject entering as a second party whose fate the movement decides. Transitions strip down to a solo line over punctuating chords, the texture of recitative, moving the drama at the speed of thought. Developments combine fragments the way ensembles combine opposed singers, each strand keeping its profile while the combined sound stays coherent. Codas accumulate themes as buffa finales accumulate people on stage. Wind instruments carry badges of identity, so a returning theme is recognised by colour before contour, exactly as an operatic character is announced by an obbligato timbre. Even the minuet behaves theatrically, its trio admitting a contrasting company of instruments the way a scene change admits new characters to a stage.

### Q: What is an operatic ensemble?

An operatic ensemble is a number in which two or more characters sing at the same time, each keeping a melodic profile fitted to a dramatic position while the combined texture remains coherent. One voice pleads with rising phrases, another refuses through stubborn repetition, a third schemes in short confiding figures beneath them, and the harmony at every joint keeps each line singable. The listener follows by shifting attention as register, dynamics and rests direct it, because no ear can track every strand with equal weight for more than a few bars at a stretch. Ensembles solve the problem of many agents inside one argument, which is why the technique transfers so completely into instrumental developments and fugal finales, where themes replace singers and the same discipline of combination governs the result.

### Q: How did Mozart write for specific singers and players?

Mozart wrote for specific singers and players by measuring each part to a known human capacity. An aria fits its first singer's range, breath, agility and dramatic temperament, placing climaxes where that voice peaked and coloratura where it was most secure. Concertos fit the hands and habits of their soloists, from his own keyboard technique to Anton Stadler's extended clarinet and Joseph Leutgeb's command of the natural horn. Alternative arias and substitute movements survive for revivals with different casts, showing tailoring as a continuing negotiation rather than a single decision. The results outlast the original performers because the measurements were taken from capacities pushed to eloquent limits, so later performers inherit a part that examines them.

### Q: When did Mozart start writing fugues?

Mozart wrote fugues from his training years onward, since Salzburg church employment and study in the tradition of Fux made fugal writing a professional obligation for Mass movements and exercises. The decisive change came in 1782, when the Sunday gatherings at Baron van Swieten's house in Vienna placed the scores of Bach and Handel in his hands as living repertory. From that year fugue stops being mainly a church requirement and becomes an expressive resource inside secular instrumental music: subjects designed for combination, developments using stretto and inversion, and finales built as negotiations between fugal procedure and sonata or rondo forms. The mature fugal writing therefore has a datable origin. Church fugues from the Salzburg years observe the older obligation competently, while the quartet and symphony fugues written after the encounter treat combination as drama, which is the difference the date explains.

### Q: What did Mozart learn from Bach and Handel?

From Bach, Mozart learned that expression can arise from the obligations independent lines owe one another: imitation, invertible counterpoint, suspensions prepared and resolved across barlines, and subjects fertile enough to generate a whole movement. Arranging Bach fugues for string trio and quartet taught the lessons from inside, voice by voice. From Handel he learned counterpoint at public scale, fugal entries deployed as crowd rhetoric in oratorio choruses, where learned combination produces collective emotion and festivity. Together the two masters converted a school technique into a dramatic resource. After 1782 the devices move to the centre of quartets, symphonies, Mass settings and concerto finales, always rationed so that argument stays ahead of erudition. The lesson was absorbed through the hands, in copying and arranging, rather than through treatises alone.

### Q: How fast did Mozart actually compose?

The honest answer separates two claims that legend merges. Writing out a fair copy could be very fast, because composition had often already happened in sketches, at the keyboard and in memory over preceding days, and a trained memory supported by shorthand drafts made the final writing fluent. Composing itself, the testing of themes, the planning of harmonic floors and the revision of joins, took the time the surviving sketches and corrected autographs show it taking. Deadline stories about overtures copied overnight have documented cores of professional speed surrounded by swelling detail. The record supports a master working quickly inside a shared grammar, not a miracle of dictation from a finished inner performance. Speed, properly understood, is evidence of preparation meeting fluency under professional deadline pressure.

### Q: Did Mozart revise his music?

Yes, at every scale from a corrected note to a recomposed movement. Sketches show themes tried in several shapes, developments blocked out and abandoned, and fair copies bearing recomposition in the composer's hand. Works were revised for new circumstances: the Haffner Symphony was selected and rescored from a serenade, and the G minor Symphony K. 550 exists in two versions, the second adding clarinets and reworking the wind writing throughout, a reconsideration of colour rather than a repair of error. Projects were also abandoned when commissions, singers or seasons disappeared, which is an economic fact about a freelance workshop rather than evidence of perfectionism. Revision and abandonment together demolish the legend of effortless arrival. The archive, not the anecdote, is where the working method survives for inspection.

### Q: How is a Mozart concerto structured?

A Mozart concerto first movement usually opens with an orchestral ritornello that presents the public world of the movement, its themes and keys complete enough to stand alone. The soloist enters, often with new material, and replays the journey to the contrasting key as a personal utterance, converting orchestral statements through keyboard figuration. A development negotiates between the parties, the recapitulation settles thematic ownership in the home key, and a cadenza suspends time for unaccompanied review of the themes before the orchestra closes the movement. Slow movements behave as arias, and finales usually adopt rondo form, where a shared refrain alternates with episodes of dialogue and display. Across all three movements the governing relation stays constant: a singular voice negotiating with a collective world that can oppose, support and finally accommodate it.

### Q: Should I listen to opera before the symphonies?

Listening to one great opera ensemble before the symphonies is a genuine shortcut, though not a requirement. A finale from Le nozze di Figaro teaches the ear to follow several opposed wants inside one coherent texture, and that skill transfers directly to developments, concerto dialogues and fugal finales. Don Giovanni adds the darker register, showing how chromatic harmony and orchestral colour build dread beneath formal elegance. A listener who begins with symphonies can still acquire the same hearings from the music itself, using the second subject's entrance and the wind conversations as guides. The opera simply supplies the clearest first lesson, because words name the wants that instrumental themes can only imply. Either route converges on the same skill, which is hearing simultaneous lines as simultaneous intentions.

### Q: Is Mozart easy to play?

The notes often are, and the music is not. Sonatas are assigned early because the textures are clear and the note counts modest, yet the phrasing, balance and rhetoric those notes require take years to command. Alberti patterns expose uneven touch across whole movements, exposed wind entries forgive no tuning compromise, quartet writing distributes traps equally among four players, and operatic ensembles demand acting, counting and blending at speed. Nothing can be hidden behind volume or mass, because the scoring leaves every participant audible. Ease in this repertory is an effect manufactured for the listener. For the performer it is among the most examining assignments in the catalogue, which is why teachers pair the assignment of a sonata with years of correction to its phrasing and balance.

### Q: How do I hear the two subjects in a first movement?

Begin with key and manner rather than with melody alone. The first subject establishes the home key and a confident manner, often rhythmic or proclamatory. A transition then loosens that key through sequences or chromatic sidesteps, and the arrival of the second subject is marked by settlement in a new key, usually the dominant, together with a change of manner toward lyricism, softer dynamics or new scoring. Mark that arrival once and the exposition gains a plot. On the repeat, confirm the journey. In the recapitulation, listen for the same second subject adjusted into the home key, which is the settlement the whole movement exists to achieve. Colour helps throughout, since the second subject often arrives with softer dynamics or a change of instrumental dress.

### Q: How do I follow a development section by ear?

Choose one small fragment before the development begins, ideally the head of the first subject, and follow only that fragment. Note each change of key as it sequences downward or circles through related areas, each change of colour as instruments pass it between them, and each change of company as it combines with other fragments or collides with itself in stretto. Ignore everything else on a first attempt. As the fragment's journey becomes clear, the surrounding argument assembles around it. The section usually ends with a quieter passage standing on the dominant, fragments flickering over an expectant bass, and recognising that hush tells you the recapitulation is being prepared. Repetition helps more than talent here, since the same development followed three times yields more structure at each pass.

### Q: Which works best show Mozart's wind writing?

The B flat Serenade K. 361, written for a full wind ensemble, shows the writing at its most exposed, with every line entrusted to winds alone. Among orchestral works, the slow movements of the late symphonies stage dialogues in which winds carry themes and mediate between strings and soloists. The Clarinet Concerto K. 622 and the Clarinet Quintet K. 581 demonstrate the favourite instrument across its registers, while the piano concertos from K. 453 onward give winds independent conversational roles against the soloist. The Sinfonia Concertante for violin and viola adds wind colour around string soloists. Listening across that sequence teaches the vocabulary of registers faster than any description. Begin with the serenade, where no strings compete for attention, and the later orchestral balances will read as variations on a known conversation.

### Q: What should I listen for in a Mozart ensemble scene?

Listen first for profiles: which character pleads, which refuses, which schemes, and how rhythm and phrase length mark each position. Listen next for entrances, since every arrival changes the alliances and is usually prepared by a harmonic shift that behaves like a door opening. Track the wind colours attached to characters, because an obbligato timbre can announce an influence before a voice enters. Notice false endings, cadences that promise closure and dissolve into new arrivals, and save your fullest attention for the close, where differentiated rhythms often merge into one shared figuration. That merger, after a scene of opposed wanting, is reconciliation enacted as sound. A finale extends the same listening across twenty minutes of arrivals, so start with a single duet or terzet and enlarge the scale gradually as the ear learns to hold more agents at once without losing any of them.

### Q: How does Mozart's style compare with Haydn's?

Haydn, the older composer and the dedicatee of the six quartets of 1785, builds drama from wit, surprise and the exhaustive development of single themes, often deriving a whole movement from one motif whose possibilities he tests with rustic humour and learned patience. Mozart builds drama from character and conversation, multiplying themes with contrasting manners and letting opera govern pacing and combination. Haydn's phrases delight in irregularity learned from folk sources, while Mozart's balance leans toward vocal proportion even when broken for effect. The two men admired and influenced each other directly, Haydn praising the younger composer's operas and quartets in terms the correspondence preserves. The comparison is between two grammars of the same language, and fluency in either one sharpens hearing of the other. Direct acquaintance, documented admiration and exchanged influence keep the comparison a family matter rather than a contest.

### Q: Why does simple-sounding music take such skill?

Simple sounding music takes skill because every effect has been manufactured and then concealed. A melody feels inevitable when its answering units complete small obligations the ear can verify. Balance feels natural when proportions borrowed from dance and verse govern phrases until a deliberate break renews attention. Harmony feels calm when the floor moves slowly beneath a busy surface, and orchestration feels light when editing removes everything that would compete with the event of the moment. Sketches and revisions show the manufacturing that the surface hides. The performer then faces exposed lines with nowhere to hide. Skill, in this style, is measured by how completely difficulty can be converted into clarity for a listener who may know none of the mechanisms involved. The conversion, repeated across a whole catalogue, is the achievement the simplicity advertises.
